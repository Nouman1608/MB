## Section A — Understanding Texts (25 marks)

**1.** Explain what is meant by **audience** when analysing a text, giving **two** ways a text can signal who it is aimed at. **[4]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a text's **purpose** and its **genre**. **[3]**

**3.** Read the two texts below, both concerned with the same theme: a company's move to a four-day working week.

**Text A** (an internal staff announcement, posted on the company intranet):

> "Effective from 1 September, all staff will move to a four-day working week, with Fridays no longer a standard working day. This trial follows extensive consultation with department heads and is intended to improve staff wellbeing while maintaining current levels of client service. Employees are asked to direct any questions to their line manager in the first instance."

**Text B** (a social media post written by an employee):

> "Guess what?? No more Friday shifts for us from September!! Honestly can't believe it took this long lol. A bit worried about fitting everything into 4 days tbh, but I'll take a 3-day weekend any day 😅"

**(a)** Identify the intended audience of each text and explain **one** linguistic feature that reveals it in each. **[4]**
**(b)** Compare the purpose of Text A and Text B. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain how the genre of each text shapes its structure and layout. **[4]**
**(d)** Explain what mode each text belongs to, identifying **one** feature in Text B that shows written language behaving like speech. **[4]**

## Section B — Directed Writing (25 marks)

Answer **one** of the following, using ideas and information from Text A and/or Text B above. Credit
is given for sustaining a style appropriate to the task throughout -- there is no separate commentary
on this unit.

**EITHER**

**4.** Using ideas from **both** texts, write the opening of a short spoken induction talk, to be
delivered to new starters, explaining the new four-day week policy and how existing staff feel about
it. **[25]**

**OR**

**5.** Using ideas from **Text A**, write a diary entry from the perspective of an employee describing
their first four-day week. **[25]**

---

## Answers

**1.** **Audience** is the reader, listener or viewer a text is designed for -- who a text is "aimed
at" [1]. This can be signalled through **register and vocabulary choice** (technical jargon signals a
specialist audience; simple, everyday lexis signals a general one) [1] [1], or through **assumed
shared knowledge** -- a text that explains basic terms is written for non-specialists, while one that
uses them without explanation assumes prior expertise [1].

**2.** **Purpose** is what a text is trying to achieve -- to inform, persuade, entertain, or some
combination of these [1]. **Genre** is the category or type of text it belongs to, recognisable
through conventional features such as headlines, salutations or hashtags [1]. The same purpose can be
pursued through different genres, and the same genre can serve different purposes, so the two must be
analysed separately [1].

**3. (a)** Text A is aimed at **all staff at the company**, a mixed but institutionally-defined
audience; this is signalled by the **formal, impersonal address** ("Employees are asked to...") and
the assumption of shared workplace context (referring to "department heads" and "client service"
without explanation) [1] [1]. Text B is aimed at the **poster's own social circle or followers**, a
personal and informal audience; this is signalled by the **casual, conversational opening** ("Guess
what??") and the assumption that the reader already knows who "us" refers to [1] [1].

**(b)** Text A's purpose is primarily **to inform** staff of a decision and its practical
implications, with an underlying purpose **to reassure** (framing the change as the result of
consultation and aimed at wellbeing) [1] [1]. Text B's purpose is primarily **to share a personal
reaction and entertain** its readers, mixing genuine relief with a joke about the practical difficulty
ahead [1] [1] -- both texts address the same policy, but one manages an institutional relationship
while the other maintains a social one.

**(c)** Text A follows the **structure of a formal announcement**: a clear statement of the change,
followed by its rationale, followed by a practical next step (who to contact), reflecting its genre's
expectation of clarity and completeness [1] [1]. Text B follows the **loose, associative structure of
a social media post**: an attention-grabbing opening, an exclamatory reaction, and a closing aside,
reflecting a genre that prioritises immediacy and personality over completeness [1] [1].

**(d)** Text A is in **written mode**, using the complete sentences, formal connectives and impersonal
tone typical of planned institutional writing [1] [1]. Text B is **written but shows features of
speech** -- for example, the **non-standard punctuation and multiple exclamation marks** ("Guess
what??", "September!!") mimic spoken emphasis and intonation, and "tbh" and "lol" are written
abbreviations that originate in, and imitate the informality of, spoken conversation [1] [1].

## Model approach: Section B

**Question 4** (spoken induction talk) needs a register that is **formal enough to represent the
company but warmer than Text A's memo**, since it will be delivered aloud to a room of new starters.
A workable shape: open with a direct, welcoming address to the audience; state the policy change
clearly, drawing on Text A's factual content; then acknowledge, in a lighter register drawn loosely
from the spirit of Text B, that existing staff have had mixed practical reactions to the change, so
new starters know what to expect. Because this is a spoken text, techniques such as direct address
("you'll notice..."), rhetorical questions and a clear signposted structure ("First... Second...")
help it read aloud naturally, in a way that simply reading Text A's memo aloud would not.

**Question 5** (diary entry) needs a **personal, reflective voice** distinct from both source texts:
more introspective than Text B's public post, and far less formal than Text A's announcement. Draw on
Text A only for the factual detail of what has changed, then invent plausible personal detail (how the
first four-day week actually felt) rather than lifting Text A's institutional phrasing into a diary
entry, where it would sit awkwardly against the form's private, first-person conventions.

In both cases, the credited skill is **sustaining** the chosen register and form consistently across
the whole response, not switching back toward the source text's own register partway through.

## A note on register: field, tenor and mode

When a text's language use switches register mid-way -- for example, an otherwise formal announcement
suddenly using a casual aside -- identify precisely which register variable has shifted. **Field** is
the subject matter or activity a text is about (a four-day week policy, a graduation ceremony); it
does not itself carry a formal or informal charge. **Tenor** is the relationship between the
participants, and it is tenor -- not field -- that governs how formal or informal the language is.
**Mode** is whether the language is spoken, written, or a hybrid of the two. A sudden shift from formal
to casual phrasing within one text is a **tenor shift** (the writer is treating the reader with less
formal distance than the surrounding text implies), possibly combined with a **mode shift** if the new
phrasing borrows features of speech -- it is not a shift in field, since the underlying subject matter
has not changed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing purpose with genre, or describing one while claiming to analyse the other.
- Identifying a feature of audience, purpose, genre or mode without explaining how the specific
  language of the text signals it.
- Treating an informal register shift as a shift in field rather than tenor.
- In Section B, lifting phrasing from the source text into a form where it does not fit the new
  register.
- Writing a commentary or justification of choices -- this unit credits a sustained, consistent style
  in the writing itself, not an explanation of it afterwards.
- Losing the chosen register partway through a Section B response.
