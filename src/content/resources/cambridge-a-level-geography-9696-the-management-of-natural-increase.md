This guide covers **4.4 The management of natural increase** in Topic 4, Population, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The whole subtopic is one required case study:

> Candidates must study one country's population policy regarding natural increase, showing the difficulties faced and evaluate the attempted solution(s). (The case study must include attempts to alter the natural increase rate and to manage the results of population change.)

Three demands are packed into that wording: **attempts to alter the natural increase rate**, **attempts to manage the results** of population change, and an **evaluation** that includes the difficulties faced. The case study is examined in Paper 2, most often as the final, extended part of the Population structured question in Section B. The [4.4 management of natural increase revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-management-of-natural-increase-revision-notes/) give a worked outline of China and brief contrasts with Kerala and Singapore. This guide explains how to build and evaluate any case study, and develops Singapore more fully as a second option. Practise with the [4.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-natural-increase-practice/).

## Types of policy

Population policies regarding natural increase aim either to lower or to raise the birth rate.

**Anti-natalist policies** aim to reduce fertility. Methods range from:

- **education and persuasion:** campaigns, family planning services, free or subsidised contraception, raising the minimum age of marriage;
- **incentives and disincentives:** payments, housing or school priority for small families, or loss of benefits for large ones;
- **coercion:** legal limits on births, enforced with fines or other penalties.

**Pro-natalist policies** aim to raise fertility, for example with cash payments for births, tax relief, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare and housing priority for families with children.

**Managing the results** of population change is a separate set of measures that accepts the existing age structure and adapts to it. For an **ageing** population these include raising the retirement age, reforming pensions, expanding health and elderly care, encouraging older people to keep working, and using immigration to supplement the workforce. For a **youthful** population they include expanding schools, health services and job creation.

## Difficulties governments face

Use these categories to discuss the "difficulties faced" in any case study:

- **Behaviour is hard to change:** fertility decisions depend on income, housing, careers, values and religion as well as policy.
- **Time lags:** effects take a generation to show in the age structure, so results outlast the governments that introduced them.
- **Population momentum:** even after fertility falls below replacement, a large young population keeps numbers growing for decades.
- **Cost:** incentives, services and enforcement are expensive.
- **Ethics and rights:** coercive measures raise serious human rights concerns.
- **Unintended consequences:** for example, imbalanced sex ratios where son preference meets birth limits, or rapid ageing after a sharp fall in fertility.
- **Attribution:** it is hard to separate the effect of a policy from that of wider development.

## Worked example: can we credit the policy?

*Fictional country P introduced an anti-natalist incentive policy in 2000. Its neighbour Q, at a similar level of development, had no such policy.*

| Total fertility rate | 1990 | 2000 | 2010 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Country P (policy from 2000) | 2.9 | 2.6 | 2.1 | 1.9 |
| Country Q (no policy) | 2.8 | 2.5 | 2.2 | 2.0 |

Between 2000 and 2020, fertility in P fell by 0.7 children per woman, but in Q, without a policy, it fell by 0.5. Fertility was already falling in P before 2000, at a similar rate to Q. The policy may explain at most the extra 0.2 fall, and even that could reflect other differences. This kind of comparison is the strongest evidence for an evaluation, and it is the reason examiners reward answers that question whether a policy, rather than development, caused a change.

## Worked case study outline: Singapore (HIC)

- **Context:** after independence in 1965, Singapore was a small, crowded city-state with high fertility and concerns about housing, jobs and services.
- **Attempts to lower natural increase:** from the late 1960s and especially in the 1970s, the government promoted family planning and ran the **"Stop at Two"** campaign. Disincentives for third and later children included lower priority in school allocation and higher costs for delivery in public hospitals, alongside legal abortion and voluntary sterilisation.
- **Outcome:** fertility fell quickly and dropped below replacement level by the late 1970s, faster than planners had expected.
- **Change of direction:** by the 1980s the government was concerned about future labour shortages and ageing. In 1987 it replaced "Stop at Two" with **"Have Three or More (if you can afford it)"**. Later pro-natalist measures included a **Baby Bonus** scheme (from 2001), tax relief, paid maternity and paternity leave and childcare subsidies.
- **Difficulties faced:** fertility has stayed very low, falling below 1.0 in recent years, because of high housing and education costs, long working hours, later marriage and changing attitudes; incentives have not offset these pressures.
- **Managing the results:** Singapore has relied on immigration to supplement its workforce and has raised retirement and re-employment ages, and it has expanded support for elderly care.
- **Evaluation:** the anti-natalist phase was arguably too successful, contributing to a sharp fall that was hard to reverse. The pro-natalist phase shows how difficult it is to raise fertility once low-fertility norms are established. Managing the results through immigration has sustained the economy but raises questions about social integration.

## How to build your own case study

Whichever country you choose, prepare notes under these headings, matching the syllabus wording:

1. **Context:** the population problem as the government saw it, with data.
2. **Attempts to alter the natural increase rate:** policies, methods and dates.
3. **Difficulties faced:** social, economic, political and ethical.
4. **Outcomes:** what happened to birth rate, fertility and natural increase, with figures.
5. **Attempts to manage the results:** measures for ageing, a youthful structure or a sex imbalance.
6. **Evaluation:** effectiveness, attribution, costs, equity, ethics and timescale, ending in a judgement.

## Annotated model answer

*"With reference to a country you have studied, evaluate the success of its policies to manage natural increase." [extract]*

> Singapore's anti-natalist policies of the 1970s, including the "Stop at Two" campaign and disincentives for larger families, were successful in their own terms, since fertility fell below replacement by the late 1970s **(effectiveness with evidence)**. However, fertility was falling across fast-growing East Asian economies at the time, so rising incomes, education and housing pressure also played a part **(attribution)**. The later pro-natalist measures have been far less successful, as fertility has fallen below 1.0 despite cash incentives and leave **(effectiveness of a second phase)**. Because births remain low, the government has had to manage the results through immigration and longer working lives **(managing results)**. Overall the policies show that governments can speed a fall in fertility but find it very hard to raise fertility again **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the policy without evaluating it.
- Covering only attempts to change the birth rate and omitting management of the results.
- Crediting all of a fall in fertility to the policy.
- Using undated, unsupported figures.
- Choosing a regional example (such as an Indian state) when the syllabus asks for one country's policy, unless it is used only as a contrast.
- Treating pro-natalist and anti-natalist measures as interchangeable.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** and **assess** need a judgement supported by evidence: say how far the policy worked, for whom and at what cost.
- Use precise, located and dated detail: names of policies, years and data.
- Plan answers to show the three syllabus demands: altering natural increase, managing results and evaluation.
