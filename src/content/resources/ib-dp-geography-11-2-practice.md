## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a node and a link in a network. **[2]**

**2.** Define chain migration. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between offshoring and reshoring. **[2]**

**4.** Outline one disadvantage of a hub-and-spoke network. **[2]**

**5.** State one type of financial flow that goes directly to households. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A ferry network links 8 islands with 7 routes. A new plan would add 5 routes.

**(a)** Calculate the beta index before and after the plan. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what the change shows about the network. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one limitation of the beta index as a measure of connection. **[1]**

**7.** Country G has a GDP of 25 billion dollars. It receives 4 billion dollars in remittances, 1 billion dollars in aid and 2 billion dollars in foreign direct investment.

**(a)** Calculate each flow as a percentage of GDP. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest one reason why the largest flow may be less stable than it appears. **[1]**

**8.** Explain how migrant networks influence the direction of future migration. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why many firms have reconsidered long global supply chains in recent years. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine why global flows connect some places far more strongly than others. **[10]**

**11.** To what extent do gatekeepers control global flows? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A node is a place or point in a network, such as a city, port or airport [1]; a link is the route or connection that joins two nodes, along which flows move [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Migration in which earlier migrants help later migrants from the same origin to move, by providing information, money, jobs or housing [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Offshoring is moving production or services to another country, usually to cut costs [1]; reshoring is bringing that production back to the home country [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Flows must pass through the hub, so journeys are less direct and take longer [1]; if the hub is disrupted, the whole network can be affected because it is a single point of failure [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Remittances [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Before: 7 / 8 = **0.875** [1]. After: 12 / 8 = **1.5** [1].

**(b)** Before the plan the index is below 1, so the network branches with no loops and losing one route could cut off an island [1]; after the plan it is above 1, so alternative routes exist and the network is more connected and more resilient [1].

**(c)** For example, it counts routes but not their capacity, frequency or cost [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Remittances: 4 / 25 x 100 = **16%** [1]. Aid: 1 / 25 x 100 = **4%** [1]. FDI: 2 / 25 x 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** Remittances depend on jobs and migration rules in host countries, which can change suddenly, for example in a recession [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Earlier migrants send back information about jobs, housing and routes, reducing uncertainty for others [1]. They also provide money and contacts that lower the cost and risk of moving [1], so later migrants tend to follow the same corridor to the same destination, and flows become concentrated between particular places rather than spreading by distance alone [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic showed that long, just-in-time supply chains can break when one place shuts down [1]. Rising trade tensions and tariffs increased the cost and risk of depending on distant suppliers [1], so firms have weighed resilience against cost and some have moved production nearer to home through reshoring or nearshoring [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines flows and networks and identifies types of flow [1].
- Hubs gain cumulative advantages as concentrated flows attract further investment and services [1].
- Location matters: coastal and strategic positions favour ports and transport hubs [1].
- Infrastructure investment, such as ports, airports and fibre-optic connections, raises connection [1].
- Policy and trade agreements open or restrict flows [1].
- Migrant and diaspora networks create strong corridors between particular places [1].
- TNC investment decisions concentrate production in some places [1].
- Some places are bypassed or switched off because of conflict, remoteness or weak infrastructure [1].
- Uses accurate examples, such as Singapore or Dubai as hubs [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that initial advantages are amplified by cumulative processes and policy [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines gatekeepers as governments, institutions and corporations that decide who can access a network [1].
- Governments control borders, visas and capital controls [1].
- Governments can filter information flows, as with China's internet censorship [1].
- Corporations control platforms, app stores and search results [1].
- Institutions such as the WTO set trade rules; credit-rating agencies influence who can borrow [1].
- Employers in kafala systems control migrant workers' residence [1].
- Limits: informal flows, such as some remittances and smuggling, escape gatekeepers [1].
- Limits: digital technology can bypass some controls [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that gatekeepers shape most formal flows strongly but control them incompletely [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" questions on control reward naming the limits of control as clearly as its reach.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing nodes by links instead of links by nodes.
- Giving percentages without showing what they were calculated from.
- Explaining migration flows without mentioning networks or corridors.
- Treating gatekeepers as only governments.
