## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Maximisation | Choosing the option that gives the greatest value of an objective, subject to a constraint |
| Utility | The satisfaction a consumer gets from consumption |
| Consumer surplus | The difference between what a consumer would pay and what they do pay |
| Profit | Total revenue minus total cost |
| Profit maximisation | Producing the output that gives the greatest profit |
| Revenue maximisation | Producing the output that gives the greatest total revenue |
| Sales maximisation | Producing the largest output possible while covering costs |
| Satisficing | Aiming for satisfactory results across several goals rather than the maximum of one |
| Separation of ownership and control | Shareholders own a company while managers run it |
| Corporate social responsibility (CSR) | Voluntary action by a firm for workers, communities or the environment beyond legal duties |
| Social welfare | The well-being of society as a whole |

## Assumed objectives at a glance

| Agent | Assumed objective | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Maximise utility | Income and prices |
| Worker | Maximise utility from income and leisure | Wage rate, hours available |
| Firm | Maximise profit | Costs and demand |
| Government | Maximise social welfare | Budget, information, political pressures |

## Condensed recall

**Consumers**

- Buy an extra unit while it is worth at least its price.
- Each extra unit usually adds less satisfaction, so demand slopes down.
- Example: values of £20, £14, £10, £7 per visit at £8 each: buy 3, consumer surplus £20.

**Workers**

- Higher wages reward extra hours; but conditions, security and satisfaction matter too.

**Firms**

- Profit maximisation is the standard assumption.
- Alternatives: revenue maximisation, sales maximisation subject to covering costs, growth, survival, satisficing, CSR, social aims.
- Reasons: managers' interests differ from owners', many stakeholders, lack of information, reputation and long-run profit.
- Firm Kappa: profit max at 4 thousand units (£60k profit, price £34); revenue max at 6 (TR £156k, price £26); sales max at 7 (profit £15k, price £22).
- A revenue or sales maximiser produces more and charges less than a profit maximiser.

**Government**

- Aims to maximise welfare by balancing growth, jobs, stable prices, fairness and the environment.
- Goals conflict; welfare is hard to measure; politicians face short-run pressures.

## Exam traps

- Confusing revenue maximisation with profit maximisation.
- Calling satisficing "lazy"; it is a deliberate balance of goals.
- Forgetting the constraint when defining maximisation.
- Saying CSR always lowers profit; it can protect long-run profit.
- Choosing the sales-maximising output where profit is negative.
- Giving objectives without the reason an agent might pursue them.
- Assuming government objectives never conflict.

## Self-test

Use this invented table for questions 6 to 9 (output in thousand units, money in £ thousand).

| Output | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TR | 60 | 108 | 144 | 168 | 180 | 180 |
| TC | 40 | 62 | 90 | 122 | 160 | 204 |

1. Define maximisation.
2. What objective is usually assumed for consumers?
3. A consumer values extra cinema trips at £15, £11, £9 and £5. Tickets cost £10. How many trips does she buy, and what is her consumer surplus?
4. Give one reason why managers may not maximise profit.
5. Define satisficing.
6. Find the profit-maximising output.
7. Find the revenue-maximising output.
8. Find the sales-maximising output subject to covering costs.
9. Which output would a satisficer choose if it wanted the largest revenue with profit of at least £20 thousand?
10. What objective is usually assumed for government?
11. Give one reason why government objectives may conflict.
12. Explain how CSR might raise long-run profit.

### Answers

1. Choosing the option that gives the greatest value of an objective, subject to a constraint.
2. Utility maximisation.
3. Two trips (the third is worth £9, less than £10); surplus = (15 - 10) + (11 - 10) = £6.
4. Managers' pay and status may depend on sales or size, not profit; or they lack information about costs and revenue.
5. Aiming for satisfactory results across several goals rather than the maximum of one.
6. Profits are 20, 46, 54, 46, 20, -24, so output 3 (profit £54 thousand).
7. Output 5 or 6 (TR £180 thousand); output 5 gives that revenue at lower cost.
8. Output 5 (profit £20 thousand); at 6 profit is negative.
9. Output 5: profit is exactly £20 thousand and revenue is £180 thousand.
10. Maximising social welfare.
11. A policy that raises growth may increase pollution or inflation.
12. A good reputation can attract customers and staff and avoid fines or boycotts.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do small firms maximise profit more often than large ones?**
Often, because the owner usually runs the business, so there is no separation of ownership and control, although owners may also value independence or lifestyle.

**Is government always acting in society's interest?**
That is the assumption, but politicians and officials have their own objectives and limited information, which is one cause of government failure.
