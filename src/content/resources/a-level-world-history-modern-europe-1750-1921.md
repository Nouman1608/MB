This guide covers the **European Option: Modern Europe, 1774–1924**,
an AS Level route through Papers 1 and 2 for Cambridge International
AS & A Level History 9489, one of three alternative regional options
a centre chooses between at the start of the course. This 2027-2029
series syllabus replaces the previous one, which the board states was
valid for the 2026 series only.

## Where this fits in 9489

At AS Level, candidates study one of three regional options — European
(Modern Europe, 1774–1924), American (The History of the USA, 1820–1941)
or International (International History, 1870–1939) — each built from
**three** named topics, assessed via Paper 1 (a document question) and
Paper 2 (an outline study). The three options share this identical
Paper 1/Paper 2 assessment structure, so the exam technique this guide
covers transfers directly to a candidate studying either of the other
two options, even though the historical content itself is entirely
different.

The syllabus publishes the rotation as a fixed table, not an
unpredictable one: each topic serves as the Paper 1 (document
question) topic once every three years, and in any given year the
remaining two topics are examined only on Paper 2. Paper 1 and Paper 2
examine **disjoint** topics — the topic prescribed for Paper 1 in a
given year is never also assessed on Paper 2 that year.

| Year | Paper 1 topic | Paper 2 topics |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | France, 1774–1814 | Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71; Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924 |
| 2028 | Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71 | France, 1774–1814; Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924 |
| 2029 | Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924 | France, 1774–1814; Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71 |

Because the rotation is fixed and published, always confirm the
current year against the syllabus table above (or the live syllabus
PDF) rather than treating it as something to be announced closer to
the exam — but still prepare all three topics for both essay writing
and document-analysis technique, since which paper a topic lands on
determines the skill it needs, not whether it needs revising at all.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE AS & A LEVEL HISTORY 9489 — EUROPEAN OPTION: MODERN
EUROPE, 1774–1924**

- 1 France, 1774–1814 — the causes and course of the French
Revolution and the rise and rule of Napoleon, spanning the fall of the
Ancien Régime through to Napoleon's eventual defeat
- 2 Liberalism and Nationalism in Germany, 1815–71 — the movement
towards German unification and the political forces driving it, including
the competing influence of liberal and nationalist ideas
- 3 Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924 — the strains on
Tsarist autocracy from the assassination of Alexander II, the causes
and course of the 1917 revolutions, and the consolidation of
Bolshevik power through to Lenin's death

## How to approach it

Because Paper 1 (document question) and Paper 2 (outline study) test
all three topics in different ways — and which topic lands on which
paper follows the fixed three-year rotation above — revision needs to
cover all three periods with both document-analysis skills and
extended-essay writing skills, rather than assuming a topic studied in
depth for one paper style is safe to under-revise for the other. The
strongest essay answers explain causation as a chain of connected
factors rather than a list — for France 1774–1814, tracing how
financial crisis, social inequality and Enlightenment ideas combined
to produce revolution, then how revolutionary instability created the
conditions for Napoleon's rise, is a more convincing answer structure
than treating each stage in isolation. The same connected-chain
principle applies just as strongly to the German unification period,
where liberal and nationalist pressures interacted with Prussian
political and military strategy rather than running as separate,
parallel threads.

Russia from autocracy to revolution (topic 3) rewards similarly
connected reasoning across its now-longer span: linking the strains
already visible on Tsarist autocracy from 1881 onward, through the
specific failures that produced the 1917 revolutions, to the
conditions that allowed the Bolsheviks to seize and then consolidate
power through to 1924 — a wider sweep than "the Russian Revolution"
alone, so revision needs to cover the whole 1881–1924 span, not just
1917 and its immediate aftermath. Across all three topics, practise
essay planning that opens with a clear line of argument, since AS/A
Level History marks essay-writing skill and structure alongside
factual accuracy, not factual accuracy alone. A brief, five-minute
plan before writing — setting out the intended line of argument and
the two or three key pieces of evidence that will support it —
consistently produces a stronger essay than starting to write
immediately and discovering the argument along the way.

## Paper 1 versus Paper 2 — different skills, same content

Paper 1 (the document question) tests source-analysis skills: reading a set of historical documents
on the year's prescribed topic, and evaluating them for reliability, usefulness,
and how they relate to each other and to wider contextual knowledge. Paper 2 (the outline study)
tests extended essay-writing skills across a broader sweep of the other two topics, without the
document-analysis component. Because which topic lands on Paper 1 follows the fixed three-year
rotation, a candidate cannot safely prepare document-analysis technique for only one topic and essay technique
for only the others — both skill sets need to be practised against all three topics, since the rotation
determines which topic needs which skill in the candidate's own exam series.

## Building connected causation across each period

| Period | A weak answer treats causes as... | A strong answer treats causes as... |
|---|---|---|
| France, 1774-1814 | A list: financial crisis, inequality, Enlightenment ideas | A chain: financial crisis exposed by war debt, worsened by social inequality, given ideological shape by Enlightenment ideas, together producing revolution |
| Liberalism and Nationalism in Germany | A list of unification events | A chain showing how liberal and nationalist pressures combined with Prussian political and military strategy to drive unification |
| Russia from autocracy to revolution | Tsarist failures and Bolshevik success as separate stories | The strains of autocracy from 1881, Tsarist failure, and Bolshevik success as one connected chain running through to 1924 |

This "chain, not list" approach is the single most consistently rewarded essay-writing habit across
all three periods, and it transfers directly regardless of which specific period an exam question
happens to draw on.
