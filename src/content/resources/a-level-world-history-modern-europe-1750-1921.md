This guide covers the **European Option: Modern Europe, 1750–1921**,
an AS Level route through Papers 1 and 2 for Cambridge International
AS & A Level History 9489, one of three alternative regional options
a centre chooses between at the start of the course.

## Where this fits in 9489

At AS Level, candidates study one of three regional options — European
(Modern Europe, 1750–1921), American (History of the USA, 1820–1941)
or International (International History, 1870–1945) — each built from
four named topics, assessed via Paper 1 (a document question) and
Paper 2 (an outline study). The three options share this identical
Paper 1/Paper 2 assessment structure, so the exam technique this guide
covers transfers directly to a candidate studying either of the other
two options, even though the historical content itself is entirely
different. Within the European Option, all four
periods below are studied; which specific topics are examined on Paper
1 versus Paper 2 rotates year on year, so candidates need secure
knowledge across all four rather than being able to specialise in only
some of them. Confirm with your teacher, or your board's published exam
timetable for your series, which periods are actually being examined
on which paper that year, since this rotation is announced in advance
rather than being predictable purely from a fixed pattern.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE AS & A LEVEL HISTORY 9489 — EUROPEAN OPTION: MODERN
EUROPE, 1750–1921**

- 1.1 France, 1774–1814 — the causes and course of the French
Revolution and the rise and rule of Napoleon, spanning the fall of the
Ancien Régime through to Napoleon's eventual defeat
- 1.2 The Industrial Revolution in Britain, 1750–1850 — the causes and
social, economic and political consequences of industrialisation, and how
these consequences interacted with one another
- 1.3 Liberalism and Nationalism in Germany, 1815–71 — the movement
towards German unification and the political forces driving it, including
the competing influence of liberal and nationalist ideas
- 1.4 The Russian Revolution, 1894–1921 — the causes and course of the
1917 revolutions and the consolidation of Bolshevik power in the years
that immediately followed

## How to approach it

Because Paper 1 (document question) and Paper 2 (outline study) test
the same four topics in different ways — and which topics land on
which paper rotates each year — revision needs to cover all four
periods with both document-analysis skills and extended-essay writing
skills, rather than assuming a topic studied in depth for one paper
style is safe to under-revise for the other. The strongest essay
answers explain causation as a chain of connected factors rather than
a list — for France 1774–1814, tracing how financial crisis, social
inequality and Enlightenment ideas combined to produce revolution, then
how revolutionary instability created the conditions for Napoleon's
rise, is a more convincing answer structure than treating each stage in
isolation. The same connected-chain principle applies just as strongly to the Industrial Revolution and German unification periods, where economic, social and political factors are similarly intertwined rather than running as separate, parallel threads.

The Russian Revolution (1.4) rewards similarly connected
reasoning: linking the failures of the Tsarist regime directly to the
conditions that allowed the Bolsheviks to seize and then consolidate
power. Across all four periods, practise essay planning that opens
with a clear line of argument, since AS/A Level History marks
essay-writing skill and structure alongside factual accuracy, not
factual accuracy alone. A brief, five-minute plan before writing —
setting out the intended line of argument and the two or three key
pieces of evidence that will support it — consistently produces a
stronger essay than starting to write immediately and discovering the
argument along the way.

## Paper 1 versus Paper 2 — different skills, same content

Paper 1 (the document question) tests source-analysis skills: reading a set of historical documents
on a topic drawn from one of the four periods, and evaluating them for reliability, usefulness,
and how they relate to each other and to wider contextual knowledge. Paper 2 (the outline study)
tests extended essay-writing skills across a broader sweep of one of the four periods, without the
document-analysis component. Because which topics land on which paper rotates year to year, a
candidate cannot safely prepare document-analysis technique for only one period and essay technique
for only the others — both skill sets need to be practised against all four periods.

## Building connected causation across each period

| Period | A weak answer treats causes as... | A strong answer treats causes as... |
|---|---|---|
| France, 1774-1814 | A list: financial crisis, inequality, Enlightenment ideas | A chain: financial crisis exposed by war debt, worsened by social inequality, given ideological shape by Enlightenment ideas, together producing revolution |
| Industrial Revolution in Britain | Separate economic, social, political consequences | Interconnected consequences, where economic change (factories, urbanisation) directly drove social and political change |
| Liberalism and Nationalism in Germany | A list of unification events | A chain showing how liberal and nationalist pressures combined with Prussian political and military strategy to drive unification |
| Russian Revolution | Tsarist failures and Bolshevik success as separate stories | Tsarist failure creating the specific conditions the Bolsheviks then exploited to seize and consolidate power |

This "chain, not list" approach is the single most consistently rewarded essay-writing habit across
all four periods, and it transfers directly regardless of which specific period an exam question
happens to draw on.
