These questions cover section 3.8 Nuclear physics (3.8.1.1 to 3.8.1.8) of the AQA AS and A-level Physics (7407/7408) specification, version 1.4 (AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards). The section is **A-level only** and is assessed in Paper 2. Sections 3.9-3.13 are options (you study one, assessed in Paper 3 Section B); this section is compulsory.

Learn the content first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-nuclear-physics/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-nuclear-physics-revision-notes/). Course pages: [AQA A-level Physics hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/physics/), [printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/physics/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/).

Use: e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, Nᴀ = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, 1 u = 931.5 MeV, proton mass 1.007276 u, neutron mass 1.008665 u, electron mass 0.000549 u, nucleon mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s.

## Questions

**1.** Describe the main observations of the α-particle scattering experiment with thin gold foil, and state what each tells you about the structure of the atom. **[4]**

**2.** A student tests a source with a GM tube. The background count rate is 25 min⁻¹. Count rates with different absorbers between source and tube are: none 1240 min⁻¹; paper 530 min⁻¹; 5 mm aluminium 26 min⁻¹; 25 mm lead 25 min⁻¹. Deduce which radiations the source emits. Explain your answer. **[4]**

**3.** A γ source gives a measured count rate of 1650 min⁻¹ at 0.15 m from a GM tube. The background count rate is 30 min⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the measured count rate expected at 0.45 m. **[3]**
**(b)** A graph of corrected count rate against 1/d², where d is the measured distance, is not quite a straight line through the origin. Suggest why. **[1]**

**4.** A sample contains 5.0 mg of a radioactive isotope of molar mass 60.0 g mol⁻¹ and half-life 5.3 years.

**(a)** Calculate the number of nuclei in the sample. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the activity of the sample. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the time, in years, for the activity to fall to 10% of its starting value. **[2]**

**5.** A best-fit line of ln(A/Bq) against time t passes through (0, 13.60) and (10.0 h, 11.29).

**(a)** Determine the decay constant. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the half-life. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the initial activity. **[1]**
**(d)** State one advantage of a log graph over a decay curve for finding half-life. **[1]**

**6.** The nuclide ¹⁸₉X lies below the line of stable nuclei on a graph of N against Z.

**(a)** State what this tells you about its neutron-to-proton balance, and name a decay mode it could undergo. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation for β⁺ decay of ¹⁸₉X to a daughter Y. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the equation for electron capture by ¹⁸₉X. **[1]**
**(d)** State the change in N and in Z for both decays. **[1]**

**7.** After β⁻ decay, a daughter nucleus can be left in excited states 0.35 MeV and 0.90 MeV above its ground state.

**(a)** State all the possible γ-photon energies as the nucleus returns to its ground state. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortest wavelength emitted. **[2]**

**8.** An α particle of kinetic energy 6.0 MeV is aimed head-on at a nucleus with Z = 50 and A = 120.

**(a)** Calculate the distance of closest approach. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the radius of the nucleus using R = R₀A^(1/3) with R₀ = 1.2 fm. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why your answers to (a) and (b) differ, and why electron diffraction gives a better value of the radius. **[2]**

**9.**

**(a)** Use R = R₀A^(1/3) to show that the density of nuclear material does not depend on A. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate this density for R₀ = 1.3 fm. **[2]**

**10.** The atomic mass of carbon-12 is 12.000000 u. Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of the carbon-12 nucleus in MeV. **[5]**

**11.** In a fusion reaction ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n. Atomic masses: ²₁H 2.014102 u, ³₁H 3.016049 u, ⁴₂He 4.002603 u. Neutron mass 1.008665 u.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released in MeV. **[3]**
**(b)** Convert this energy to joules. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the nuclei must be at a very high temperature for fusion to happen. **[1]**

**12.** A thermal reactor uses uranium fuel enriched in U-235, with heavy water as moderator.

**(a)** Explain why a moderator is needed, and why a chain reaction needs at least the critical mass of fuel. **[2]**
**(b)** A neutron (mass 1 u) collides head-on and elastically with a stationary deuterium nucleus (mass 2 u). Show that the neutron keeps 1/9 of its kinetic energy. **[3]**
**(c)** Name a suitable material for the control rods and explain how the rods control the reaction. **[2]**
**(d)** State one property a good coolant needs. **[1]**
**(e)** Describe two safety features that protect workers from spent fuel and from an accident in the core. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Most α particles pass straight through, so most of the atom is empty space. [1]
Some are deflected through large angles, so there is a small, concentrated region of positive charge. [1]
A very few rebound (deflected by more than 90°), so this nucleus is very small [1]
and contains most of the atom's mass. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each observation with its conclusion; a list of observations alone is likely to earn little.

**2.** Corrected rates: 1215, 505, 1 and 0 min⁻¹. [1]
The large fall with paper shows **α** is emitted. [1]
The fall to background with aluminium shows **β** is emitted. [1]
The aluminium reading is already at background, so **no γ**. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Claiming γ because the raw lead reading is non-zero ignores background and loses the final mark.

**3. (a)** Corrected rate = 1650 − 30 = 1620 min⁻¹ [1]
Distance × 3, so corrected rate ÷ 9 = 180 min⁻¹ [1]
Measured = 180 + 30 = **210 min⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The true distance differs from d by an unknown offset (source inside holder, detection inside tube). [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 1650 by 9 without removing background gives 183 min⁻¹ and loses the method mark for the correction.

**4. (a)** Moles = 5.0 × 10⁻³ g / 60.0 g mol⁻¹ = 8.33 × 10⁻⁵ mol [1]
N = 8.33 × 10⁻⁵ × 6.02 × 10²³ = **5.0 × 10¹⁹** [1]
**(b)** λ = ln2 / (5.3 × 3.16 × 10⁷) = **4.1 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** A = λN = **2.1 × 10¹¹ Bq** [1]
**(d)** 0.10 = e^(−λt), so t = ln10 / λ [1]
t = 3.32 × 5.3 = **17.6 years** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An error carried from (a) or (b) is usually allowed through (c) and (d) if your equation is shown.

**5. (a)** Gradient = (11.29 − 13.60) / 10.0 = −0.231 h⁻¹ [1]
λ = −gradient = **0.231 h⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** T½ = ln2 / 0.231 = **3.00 h** [1]
**(c)** A₀ = e^13.60 = **8.1 × 10⁵ Bq** [1]
**(d)** Uses all the data in one best-fit line, so random scatter is averaged (and λ comes straight from the gradient). [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative decay constant is a physics error; state λ as the magnitude of the gradient, with units.

**6. (a)** Too many protons for stability (too few neutrons); it can decay by β⁺ or electron capture. [1]
**(b)** ¹⁸₉X → ¹⁸₈Y + ⁰₊₁e [1] + νₑ [1]
**(c)** ¹⁸₉X + ⁰₋₁e → ¹⁸₈Y + νₑ [1]
**(d)** N increases by 1 and Z decreases by 1. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The neutrino in β⁺ decay and electron capture is an electron neutrino, not an antineutrino; the wrong one loses the mark.

**7. (a)** 0.90 MeV and 0.35 MeV [1]
and 0.55 MeV (0.90 − 0.35) [1]
**(b)** E = 0.90 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.44 × 10⁻¹³ J; λ = hc/E [1]
λ = **1.4 × 10⁻¹² m** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Shortest wavelength means largest energy; using 0.35 MeV gives the longest wavelength.

**8. (a)** Eₖ = 6.0 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 9.6 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]
Eₖ = (2e)(50e) / (4πε₀r) [1]
r = **2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m** [1]
**(b)** R = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 120^(1/3) = **5.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ m** [1]
**(c)** The α stops and turns back before touching the nucleus, so (a) is only an upper limit. [1]
Electrons are leptons and do not feel the strong force, and diffraction measures the radius directly. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the α's charge is 2e halves the answer; check both charges in Qq.

**9. (a)** Mass ≈ A × nucleon mass and volume = (4/3)πR₀³A [1]
so ρ = m/((4/3)πR₀³), with A cancelling. [1]
**(b)** ρ = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ / ((4/3)π(1.3 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) [1]
= **1.8 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" needs the cancellation of A written out; stating "density is constant" alone does not earn credit.

**10.** Nuclear mass = 12.000000 − 6(0.000549) = 11.996706 u [1]
Nucleons: 6(1.007276) + 6(1.008665) = 12.095646 u [1]
Mass difference = 0.098940 u [1]
Binding energy = 0.098940 × 931.5 = 92.2 MeV [1]
Per nucleon = 92.2 / 12 = **7.68 MeV** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Skipping the electron-mass correction gives 7.42 MeV per nucleon and loses the first mark.

**11. (a)** Mass before = 5.030151 u; mass after = 5.011268 u [1]
Mass lost = 0.018883 u [1]
Energy = 0.018883 × 931.5 = **17.6 MeV** [1]
**(b)** 17.6 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.8 × 10⁻¹² J** [1]
**(c)** The nuclei need enough kinetic energy to overcome their electrostatic repulsion and get close enough for the strong force to act. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Atomic masses work here because each side has two electrons; say so if asked to justify the method.

**12. (a)** Fission neutrons are fast; U-235 is far more likely to absorb thermal neutrons. [1]
Below the critical mass too many neutrons escape, so on average fewer than one fission follows each fission. [1]
**(b)** Momentum: u = v₁ + 2v₂; kinetic energy: u² = v₁² + 2v₂² [1]
Solving: v₁ = −u/3 (and v₂ = 2u/3) [1]
Fraction kept = (v₁/u)² = (1/3)² = **1/9** [1]
**(c)** Boron (or cadmium) [1]
absorbs neutrons; inserting the rods further leaves fewer neutrons, so the fission rate falls. [1]
**(d)** High specific heat capacity (or flows easily, or low neutron absorption). [1]
**(e)** Spent fuel is moved by remote handling (or stored behind shielding); [1]
in an emergency the control rods drop fully into the core and stop the chain reaction. [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "control rods slow neutrons down" earns no credit; slowing is the moderator's job.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using raw count rates without correcting for background.
- Mixing units for λ: activity in Bq needs λ in s⁻¹.
- Quoting the ln A gradient as λ with its minus sign.
- Taking atomic mass as nuclear mass in binding-energy calculations.
- Using charge e instead of 2e for the α particle in closest-approach work.
- Calling the closest-approach distance the radius.
- Writing an antineutrino in β⁺ or electron-capture equations.
- Confusing moderator, control rods and coolant roles.
- Swapping the fission and fusion regions of the binding energy per nucleon graph.
