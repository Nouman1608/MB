This guide covers subtopic **32.2, Phenol**, from Topic 32, Hydroxy compounds, of Cambridge International AS & A
Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. [Phenol: Reactions and Acidity](/resources/a-phenol-reactions-and-acidity/)
introduces each reaction and the acidity order. This page focuses on the two outcomes that examiners use to
set unfamiliar questions: **predicting ring substitution in other phenolic compounds** (7) and **explaining
acidity and reactivity** with a chain of reasoning (3 to 5). Afterwards, use the
[phenol revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-phenol-revision-notes/) and the
[phenol practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-phenol-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

1. recall how phenol is produced: phenylamine with HNO₂ (or NaNO₂ and dilute acid) **below 10 °C** to form the
   diazonium salt, then **warming** the diazonium salt with water;
2. recall phenol's reactions: (a) with NaOH(aq) to give sodium phenoxide; (b) with Na(s) to give sodium
   phenoxide and H₂; (c) in NaOH(aq) with diazonium salts to give azo compounds; (d) nitration with **dilute
   HNO₃(aq) at room temperature** to give 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol; (e) bromination with **Br₂(aq)** to
   give 2,4,6-tribromophenol;
3. explain the acidity of phenol;
4. describe and explain the relative acidities of **water, phenol and ethanol**;
5. explain why the reagents and conditions for nitration and bromination of phenol differ from benzene's;
6. recall that the –OH group of a phenol directs to the **2-, 4- and 6-positions**;
7. apply knowledge of phenol's reactions to **other phenolic compounds**, e.g. naphthol.

## Producing phenol

    NaNO₂ + HCl → HNO₂ + NaCl
    C₆H₅NH₂ + HNO₂ + HCl → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O        (below 10 °C)
    C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + H₂O → C₆H₅OH + N₂ + HCl            (warm)

The same diazonium salt has two different uses depending on temperature. Kept cold, it survives long enough to
couple with phenol to make an azo dye. Warmed, it decomposes deliberately, releasing nitrogen gas, to give
phenol. A question that asks for "the conditions" for each step expects both temperatures.

## The five reactions at a glance

| Reagent | Product | Type of reaction | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaOH(aq) | C₆H₅O⁻Na⁺ + H₂O | acid–base | phenol is acidic enough to react with a strong base |
| Na(s) | C₆H₅O⁻Na⁺ + ½H₂ | redox (acid + reactive metal) | O–H bond present |
| C₆H₅N₂⁺ in NaOH(aq), cold | azo compound | electrophilic substitution | ring activated at position 4 |
| dilute HNO₃(aq), room temperature | 2- and 4-nitrophenol | electrophilic substitution | ring activated; mild conditions |
| Br₂(aq), room temperature | 2,4,6-tribromophenol, white precipitate | electrophilic substitution | three positions activated |

Adding a strong acid to sodium phenoxide solution re-forms phenol, which is only slightly soluble in water, so the
solution turns cloudy. This reversibility is a useful observation in practical questions.

## Explaining acidity: build the chain

A complete answer for "explain the relative acidities of water, phenol and ethanol" has four links:

1. **Acidity depends on the stability of the anion** left after H⁺ is lost. The more stable the anion, the
   further the equilibrium ROH ⇌ RO⁻ + H⁺ lies to the right.
2. **Phenoxide:** a lone pair on O overlaps with the delocalised π system, so the negative charge is spread
   over the ring. The ion is stabilised: phenol is the strongest acid of the three.
3. **Hydroxide:** the charge stays on O, with no delocalisation and no alkyl group. Intermediate.
4. **Ethoxide:** the ethyl group is electron-donating and **increases** the charge density on O. The ion is
   less stable: ethanol is the weakest acid.

**Order: phenol > water > ethanol.**

**Evidence you can quote:** phenol reacts with NaOH(aq) but ethanol does not; phenol does not release CO₂ from
carbonates, so it is a weaker acid than carboxylic acids.

## Explaining the milder conditions

Benzene needs concentrated HNO₃ with concentrated H₂SO₄ for nitration, and Br₂ with AlBr₃ for bromination.
Phenol needs only dilute HNO₃ and Br₂(aq). The reasoning:

- a lone pair on the oxygen of –OH is partly **delocalised into the ring**;
- this **increases the electron density** of the ring, especially at the 2-, 4- and 6-positions;
- the ring therefore attracts electrophiles more strongly: it is **activated**;
- the ring can **polarise Br₂ itself**, so no halogen carrier is needed, and a lower concentration of
  electrophile from dilute HNO₃ is enough;
- because three positions are activated, bromination continues to **tribromination**.

## Applying the reactions to other phenolic compounds

This is outcome 7, and it turns a recall topic into a problem-solving one. Use this routine:

1. Find the carbon bearing –OH; call it C1.
2. List the **2-, 4- and 6-positions**.
3. Cross out any already occupied.
4. Each free position takes one Br (one Br₂, one HBr) with bromine water. For nitration, the free 2- and
   4-positions give the mononitro products.

**Worked example 1: predicting bromination products.**

| Compound | Free 2-, 4-, 6-positions | mol Br₂ per mol | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-methylphenol | 2, 4, 6 (CH₃ is on 3) | 3 | 2,4,6-tribromo-3-methylphenol |
| 4-nitrophenol | 2, 6 | 2 | 2,6-dibromo-4-nitrophenol |
| 2,4-dimethylphenol | 6 only | 1 | 2-bromo-4,6-dimethylphenol |
| 4-chlorophenol | 2, 6 | 2 | 2,6-dibromo-4-chlorophenol |

For 2,4-dimethylphenol the bromine enters at position 6, but the product is renumbered so that the
substituents are listed with the lowest locants and alphabetically: 2-bromo-4,6-dimethylphenol.

**Worked example 2: a quantity calculation.**

*1.08 g of 3-methylphenol reacts completely with bromine water. Calculate the mass of product and the mass of
Br₂ used. Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Br = 79.9.*

    Mr(CH₃C₆H₄OH) = 108.0           n = 1.08 / 108.0 = 0.0100 mol
    3 free positions                n(Br₂) = 0.0300 mol
    mass Br₂ = 0.0300 × 159.8 = 4.79 g
    Mr(C₇H₅Br₃O) = 7(12.0) + 5(1.0) + 3(79.9) + 16.0 = 344.7
    mass product = 0.0100 × 344.7 = 3.45 g

**Naphthol.** Naphthols have an –OH on a naphthalene ring system. They react like phenol: they dissolve in
NaOH(aq) to give a naphthoxide ion, react with sodium to release hydrogen, and couple with diazonium salts in
alkaline solution to give intensely coloured azo dyes. With 2-naphthol, coupling takes place at the 1-position,
next to the –OH group, rather than at a position numbered "4" as in phenol. In a naphthol question, reason from
the structure you are given rather than copying phenol's locants.

## Worked example 3: telling three compounds apart

*Describe simple tests to distinguish phenol, ethanol and ethanoic acid.*

| Test | Phenol | Ethanol | Ethanoic acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| add Na₂CO₃(aq) | no effervescence | no effervescence | **effervescence of CO₂** |
| add Br₂(aq) | **decolourised; white precipitate** | no change | no change |
| add NaOH(aq) | reacts (phenol dissolves) | no reaction | reacts (no visible change) |

Two tests are enough: sodium carbonate picks out the carboxylic acid, and bromine water then picks out phenol.

## Common mistakes

- **Using benzene's conditions** for phenol, or adding a catalyst for bromination.
- **Giving one nitrophenol**: dilute HNO₃ gives a mixture of the 2- and 4-isomers.
- **Explaining acidity by O–H bond strength** rather than anion stability.
- **Saying the ethyl group "stabilises" ethoxide**: it destabilises it.
- **Forgetting the temperatures** in the production route: below 10 °C, then warm.
- **Counting bromines without checking occupied positions** in substituted phenols.
- **Claiming phenol fizzes with carbonates**: it does not.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Recall / state**: reagent, concentration (dilute or aqueous), temperature, product names.
- **Explain** acidity: anion stability, delocalisation into the ring, alkyl group effect; give the order.
- **Explain** different conditions from benzene: lone pair delocalised, higher ring electron density,
  activated ring, Br₂ polarised without a carrier.
- **Predict** or **apply**: number the ring, mark the free 2-, 4- and 6-positions, then write the product and
  balanced equation.
