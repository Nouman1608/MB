This guide covers the **email**, one of the five forms named for **Section A, Directed Writing**, of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2. The syllabus says candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, **email**, report, letter or article", writing "about 250–350 words". The [directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/) gives the email a single row: "greeting, purpose in first lines, short paragraphs, clear request or recommendation". This page explains each of those features, shows how the email differs from the letter, and works through the register decisions that make or break the form.

## What makes an email different

An email is direct and efficient. The reader may be busy, may read it on a phone and wants to know quickly what it is about and what is being asked. Compared with a letter:

| | Letter | Email |
|---|---|---|
| Typical recipient | an official, an organisation, an editor | often someone you know or work with: a teacher, an organiser, a manager, a friend |
| Register | usually formal | ranges from formal to friendly, depending on the recipient |
| Opening | "Dear Sir or Madam" or a formal name | "Dear Ms Patel", "Hello Mr Chen", "Hi Leila" |
| Closing | "Yours faithfully" or "Yours sincerely" | "Kind regards", "Best wishes", "Thanks, Sam" |
| Paragraphs | full, developed | shorter, each with one clear purpose |
| Pace | can build up to the point | gets to the point in the first two sentences |

Shorter paragraphs do not mean thinner content. The reading marks (R3 and R5) still depend on using, developing and evaluating the sources.

## Register: the central decision

**W4**, "use register appropriate to context", is where emails are most often marked down, because the range of possible registers is wide. Decide three things before writing:

1. **Who is the recipient, and what is your relationship?** A head teacher and a close friend need very different language.
2. **What is the purpose?** Asking for something from someone with authority needs more courtesy than sharing advice with a friend.
3. **What does the recipient already know?** If you are replying to their message, refer to it; if not, explain the background briefly.

| Recipient | Greeting | Register | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head teacher or principal | "Dear Mrs Okoye" | formal, respectful | "Kind regards" or "Yours sincerely" |
| A teacher who organises an activity | "Dear Mr Tan" | polite, clear, slightly less formal | "Kind regards" |
| A company or organisation | "Dear Customer Services team" | formal and precise | "Kind regards" |
| A friend or cousin | "Hi Leila" | warm, conversational, still organised | "Speak soon" or "Love, Sam" |

**Informal is not careless.** An email to a friend can use contractions and a relaxed tone, but it still needs accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar (W5), clear paragraphs (W2) and no text-speak or emojis.

## Getting to the point

The first two sentences should do three jobs: greet, give the reason for writing, and signal your view or request.

- **Weak:** "Hello. I hope you are well. I am writing this email because I wanted to write to you about something."
- **Strong:** "Dear Mr Tan, Thank you for asking students for their views on the end-of-year trip. I would like to explain why I think the science museum is the better choice."

A specific subject line, if you choose to include one, can do some of this work: "End-of-year trip: why I recommend the museum" is clearer than "Trip".

## Organising the body

Each short paragraph should have one job. A useful order for a discursive or recommending email:

1. **Purpose** (one or two sentences).
2. **Option or argument one**, with source material used and developed.
3. **Option or argument two**, with evaluation of the source's claims.
4. **Recommendation**, with your reasons drawn together.
5. **Request or next step**: what you want the reader to do, or an offer of help.
6. **Sign-off**.

Because paragraphs are short, linking words matter: "On the other hand", "However", "For these reasons", "With that in mind".

## Worked example

Sources (original, shortened):

> **Text 1, from a teacher's notice:** Year 10 can choose their end-of-year trip: a day at a theme
> park or a visit to the science museum with a robotics workshop. The theme park is more popular in
> surveys of students. The museum trip costs less and links to the science curriculum.
>
> **Text 2, from a student's comment on the school website:** The theme park would be the only time
> all year that the whole year group relaxes together after examinations. But last year's trip there
> involved three hours on a coach and long queues, and some students could not afford the ticket.

**Task:** *Write an email to Mr Tan, the teacher organising the trip, giving your views on which trip should be chosen.*

**Model email (extract):**

> Dear Mr Tan,
>
> Thank you for asking Year 10 for our views on the end-of-year trip. Having thought about both
> options, I would recommend the science museum.
>
> I understand why the theme park is popular. After examinations, a day out together would be a
> real reward, and the student comment on the website is right that this is a rare chance for the
> whole year group to relax.
>
> However, the popularity of the theme park in surveys does not tell us everything. Last year's trip
> meant three hours on a coach and much of the day in queues, so students spent less time together than
> it might seem. More importantly, the cost meant some students could not go at all, and a trip that
> leaves people out cannot really be called a year-group celebration.
>
> The museum trip is cheaper, so more students could take part, and the robotics workshop would be
> genuinely enjoyable as well as linked to our science course. If the school added a picnic in the
> park next to the museum, we would have both the learning and the time together.
>
> I would be happy to help collect students' views on the picnic idea if that would be useful.
>
> Kind regards,
>
> Amina Yusuf (10C)

**Commentary.** The opening thanks the recipient and gives the recommendation at once. Each paragraph has a single job. The writer **uses** points from both texts, **evaluates** the survey result (popular is not the same as best) and the student comment, and **develops** a compromise (a picnic) that neither source mentions. The register is polite and slightly less formal than a letter to an official: "Thank you for asking", "I would be happy to help". The final offer gives a clear next step. A full answer would be a little longer, around 300 words.

## Common mistakes

- **Letter conventions** such as "Yours faithfully" after a named greeting, or "Dear Sir or Madam" to a teacher you know.
- **Text-speak and emojis**, or no capital letters, in an attempt to sound like an email.
- **Overly casual register** to a head teacher or organisation.
- **Slow openings** that delay the purpose.
- **Bullet-point lists** instead of developed sentences, which leave no room for evaluation.
- **No request or next step**, so the email trails off.
- **Retelling the sources** in order.

## Exam technique

Underline the recipient, your role and the purpose in the question. Decide the greeting, register and sign-off before you start. Plan five or six short paragraphs, each with a job, and write the opening two sentences carefully, because they set both the register and the point. Aim for about 300 words, and finish by checking spelling, punctuation and grammar.
