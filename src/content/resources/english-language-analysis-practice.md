## Section A

**1.** Define the following and give an example of each: metaphor, personification, sibilance, juxtaposition. **[4]**

**2.** Explain the difference between analysing **language** and analysing **structure**. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** A writer describes a storm: *"The wind clawed at the shutters; the sea heaved itself against the wall, again and again, refusing to be refused."*

**(a)** Identify **two** language techniques and explain the effect of each. **[6]**
**(b)** Comment on the effect of the repetition in "again and again" and the final clause. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how a writer can create tension through structure, giving **three** techniques. **[6]**

**5.** Explain how each of the following shapes tone, giving an example: sentence length, verb choice, punctuation. **[6]**

**6.** You are asked to write a letter to a local newspaper arguing against the closure of a library.

**(a)** Identify **three** features of form you must include. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **three** persuasive techniques you would use and why each suits this audience. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. Metaphor** — describing something **as if it were something else**, without "like" or "as", e.g. "he was a wall of silence" [1]. **Personification** — giving **human qualities to something non-human**, e.g. "the fog crept" [1]. **Sibilance** — the **repetition of s and sh sounds**, e.g. "the soft hiss of the surf" [1]. **Juxtaposition** — placing **two contrasting ideas or images side by side** for effect, e.g. laughter in a graveyard [1].

**2. Language** analysis examines **word and phrase choices — imagery, diction, sound patterns** — and their effect [1]. **Structure** analysis examines **how the whole text is organised** — where the focus shifts, the order of information, paragraph and sentence patterning, beginnings and endings [1].

**3. (a)** Any two, 3 marks each: **Personification** — "clawed" gives the wind **animal aggression**, so it becomes a predator rather than weather [1]; the verb suggests **repeated, tearing violence** [1], making the shutters — and the people behind them — seem vulnerable [1]. **Personification/metaphor of the sea** — "heaved itself" suggests **enormous deliberate effort by a living body** [1]; the reflexive "itself" gives the sea **agency and will** [1], making the assault feel intentional rather than accidental [1]. (Also accept sibilance in "sea"/"itself", or the semicolon's structural effect.)
**(b)** "Again and again" **enacts the relentlessness it describes**, so the reader feels the rhythm of the waves [1]. "Refusing to be refused" uses **polyptoton — the same verb turned back on itself** [1], creating a sense of **deadlock between sea and wall** in which the sea is clearly the more determined [1].

**4.** Any three, 2 marks each: **withholding information** and revealing it only at the end, so the reader reads on to resolve the uncertainty [1] [1]; **shortening sentences and paragraphs** as the crisis approaches, which quickens the pace [1] [1]; **shifting focus from wide to narrow** — from the landscape to a single detail such as a door handle — which narrows the reader's attention as the threat closes in [1] [1]; a **cyclical structure** that returns to the opening image, so the reader recognises how much has changed [1] [1]; **a sudden time shift or flashback** that interrupts at the moment of greatest suspense [1] [1].

**5. Sentence length** — a **succession of short sentences creates urgency or bluntness**, while long multi-clause sentences slow the pace and suggest reflection, e.g. "He stopped. He listened. Nothing." [1] [1]. **Verb choice** — verbs carry the energy of a sentence; "strolled" and "stormed" convey opposite attitudes to the same action [1] [1]. **Punctuation** — a **dash creates an abrupt interruption**, an ellipsis creates hesitation, and a colon sets up an expectation the following clause fulfils [1] [1].

**6. (a)** An **address and date**; a **formal salutation and sign-off** appropriate to a newspaper ("Dear Editor" / "Yours faithfully"); a **clear headline or subject line and paragraphed argument** [1] [1] [1].
**(b)** Any three, 2 marks each: **direct address and inclusive pronouns** ("our library", "we") build shared ownership with local readers [1] [1]; **statistics and evidence** — visitor numbers, exam results — give the argument credibility with an audience that includes councillors [1] [1]; **anecdote** about a specific user makes the abstract closure emotionally concrete for a local readership who may recognise themselves [1] [1]; **rhetorical questions** invite readers to reach the conclusion themselves rather than being told [1] [1]; **counter-argument and rebuttal** acknowledges the budget pressure, which makes the writer seem reasonable rather than merely angry [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technique without explaining its effect on the reader.
- Writing about structure but only discussing word choices.
- Feature-spotting: listing four techniques briefly instead of analysing two well.
- Forgetting the conventions of form in directed writing.
