> **HL only.** These are original questions written for Marlbridge on the technology extension of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. The sources are constructed for practice and the data are invented. Questions 6 to 9 follow the pattern of the four Paper 3 questions (3, 6, 6 and 15 marks); the others use practice tariffs.

Related: [Technology study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Outline why much research on technology and behaviour is open to bidirectional ambiguity. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by publication bias in research on technology. **[3]**

**3.** Describe one way in which tele-therapy could be evaluated for effectiveness. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one ethical consideration in an online experiment that changes what social media users see. **[3]**

**5.** Distinguish statistical significance from the size of an effect. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the sources (constructed for practice; data are invented). They concern health and well-being and the role of technology.

> **Source A (survey).** A researcher asked 600 students aged 14 to 16 to report their average hours per day on social media and completed a well-being scale (0 to 50, higher = better). The correlation coefficient was r = -0.30. Mean daily use was 3.2 hours (standard deviation 1.4 hours); mean well-being was 31 (standard deviation 8). The students were recruited through an online advert for a "digital life" study.
>
> **Source B (randomised trial).** Eighty adults with mild anxiety were randomly allocated to eight weekly sessions of therapy by video call (40 adults) or in person (40 adults). Anxiety was measured with a 0 to 63 scale before and after therapy.
>
> | | Video call | In person |
> |---|---|---|
> | Mean score before | 28.0 | 27.5 |
> | Mean score after | 17.5 | 16.5 |
> | Mean reduction | 10.5 | 11.0 |
> | Drop-outs | 7 of 40 | 3 of 40 |
>
> **Source C (interview extract).** A student from Source A said: "I post more when I feel low, because it is the only time anyone replies. My friends say the same. I am not sure the app is the problem."

**6.** Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of Source A. **[3]**

**7.** Analyse the findings in Source B and state a conclusion. **[6]**

**8.** Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings in Source A. **[6]**

**9.** Using Sources A, B and C and your knowledge of the role of technology, discuss the claim that "technology has a harmful effect on mental health". **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Many studies are correlational and measure the association between technology use and a behaviour such as mood **[1]**. The correlation does not show direction, so technology use may affect mood, or mood may affect technology use **[1]**. Example, such as heavy use leading to low mood or low mood leading to heavier use **[1]**.

**2.** Studies that find a clear effect (positive or negative) are more likely to be published than studies that find none **[1]**. Because technology is a new topic, with strong public interest, striking findings may be published more often **[1]**, so the published evidence may overstate the effect and give a distorted picture **[1]**.

**3.** Any one design, three marks: compare outcomes for clients treated by video call with a comparison group treated in person **[1]**; use the same validated symptom scale before and after treatment, and compare the change **[1]**; random allocation, a follow-up and records of drop-out would strengthen the evaluation **[1]**.

**4.** Any one, three marks: users may not have given informed consent **[1]**; seeing changed content could affect mood, so there is a risk of harm, especially for vulnerable users **[1]**; there is no debrief or easy right to withdraw, so the benefits would have to be weighed against costs **[1]**.

**5.** Statistical significance means the result would be unlikely if chance alone were operating **[1]**. The size of an effect shows how big the difference or relationship is **[1]**. In a very large sample, a tiny effect can be statistically significant but have little practical importance **[1]**.

**6.** Any one, three marks: the study is correlational (r = -0.30), so it cannot show whether social media use lowers well-being or low well-being increases use **[1]**; the correlation is modest and explains only a small share of the differences in well-being (about 9 per cent of the variation, since 0.30 squared is 0.09) **[1]**; recruitment by an online advert means a self-selected sample who may not represent all teenagers **[1]**. Also creditable: self-reported hours of use may be inaccurate.

**7.** Analysis, up to 4: the mean reduction in anxiety was 10.5 points for video call and 11.0 for in person, a difference of only 0.5 points **[1]**. Both groups improved by about 10 or 11 points on a 63-point scale **[1]**. Drop-outs were higher for video call (7 of 40, which is 17.5 per cent) than in person (3 of 40, which is 7.5 per cent) **[1]**. No standard deviations or significance test are given, so we cannot tell whether the 0.5-point difference is meaningful **[1]**. Random allocation supports internal validity **[1]**. Conclusion, up to 2: video therapy and in-person therapy produced similar reductions in anxiety in this sample **[1]**; higher drop-out for video therapy suggests access and engagement issues, and the small sample of adults with mild anxiety limits generalisation **[1]**. Maximum **[6]**.

**8.** Markband guide: 5 to 6 for detailed discussion with explicit reference to the source; 3 to 4 for some reference to the source; 1 to 2 for generic points. Indicative content, up to two marks each:
- Triangulation: add an objective measure such as logged app use or a diary to check the self-reported hours **[2]**.
- Use a validated well-being scale and report its reliability **[2]**.
- Recruit a more representative sample, for example by random sampling of schools, instead of an online advert **[2]**.
- Collect data at more than one time (a longitudinal design), to test whether use predicts later well-being **[2]**.
- Report effect sizes and the limits of a correlational design honestly, and avoid causal language; pre-register the analysis to reduce confirmation bias **[2]**.
Maximum **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for detailed, well-developed critical analysis that interprets at least three sources, uses own knowledge, evaluates different points of view and reaches a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good but less developed analysis. Indicative content, up to the markband:
- Source A gives a negative relationship (r = -0.30), which is consistent with the claim, but the design is correlational, the sample is self-selected and the effect is modest **[3]**.
- Source B shows that technology can deliver benefit: video-call therapy reduced anxiety by 10.5 points, nearly the same as in-person therapy, which is evidence against "technology is harmful" for mental health, although drop-out was higher **[3]**.
- Source C suggests reverse causation (low mood leads to more posting), which supports bidirectional ambiguity **[2]**.
- Own knowledge: studies with large samples and careful analysis (for example Orben and Przybylski, 2019) found only a very small association between technology use and well-being; other studies reported larger links; and replication has been mixed **[3]**.
- Perspective and different points of view: the claim treats "technology" as one thing, but the type and context of use (supportive messaging, passive scrolling, tele-therapy) matter **[2]**.
- Conclusion consistent with the arguments, for example that the evidence does not support a general claim of harm and that effects depend on how technology is used and who uses it **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 6:** writing "correlation is not causation" without referring to the figures or the sampling method in the source.
- **Question 7:** failing to compare the drop-out figures, or to say that no test of significance is given.
- **Question 9:** treating the claim as true or false without weighing Source B against Source A.
