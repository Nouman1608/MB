## 4.1.2.1 How the table is arranged

The elements are arranged in order of **atomic (proton) number**. They are placed so that elements with similar
properties fall in the same column, called a **group**. Rows are called **periods**. The table is "periodic"
because similar properties recur at regular intervals as atomic number increases.

The link to electrons is the key idea. For the first 20 elements:

- the **period number** equals the number of occupied shells
- the **group number** (Groups 1 to 7) equals the number of electrons in the outer shell
- Group 0 elements have a full outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 for helium).

Elements in a group have the **same number of outer electrons**, which decide how an atom reacts, so they have
similar chemical properties.

**Worked example 1.** An atom has electronic structure 2,8,6. Identify the element and its position.

```
Total electrons = 2 + 8 + 6 = 16
Neutral atom, so protons = 16, atomic number = 16 -> sulfur
Three occupied shells -> period 3
Six outer electrons -> Group 6
```

Position also predicts reactivity: lower in Group 1 means more reactive, lower in Group 7 means less reactive.

## 4.1.2.2 Development of the periodic table

Before protons, neutrons and electrons were discovered, scientists could only sort elements by their
**atomic weights** (what we now call relative atomic masses). The steps you need to describe are these.

1. **Early tables used strict atomic-weight order.** They were incomplete, and strict order forced some
   elements into groups they did not belong in.
2. **Mendeleev (1869) left gaps** for elements he thought had not yet been discovered, so that each column kept
   elements with similar properties.
3. **He changed the order in some places.** Tellurium has a higher atomic weight than iodine, but he placed
   tellurium first so that iodine sat with chlorine and bromine, which it resembles.
4. **His predictions were tested.** He used the gaps to predict the properties of missing elements. Gallium
   (1875), scandium (1879) and germanium (1886) were discovered and fitted his gaps.
5. **Isotopes explained the swaps.** The table is really in order of atomic number. Atomic weight is an
   average over isotopes, so an element with heavy isotopes can outweigh the next element.

**Worked example 2.** Decide whether this data supports Mendeleev's table. He predicted an element he called
eka-silicon, below silicon. Germanium was later found to fill that gap.

| Property | Mendeleev's prediction | Germanium (measured) |
|---|---|---|
| Relative atomic mass | 72 | 72.6 |
| Density in g/cm³ | 5.5 | 5.32 |
| Oxide | a dioxide | GeO₂, a dioxide |

```
Mass:    (72.6 - 72) / 72.6 x 100 = 0.8% difference
Density: (5.5 - 5.32) / 5.32 x 100 = 3.4% difference
```

The predictions are close and the oxide formula matches. A prediction made before a discovery that turns out
correct **supports** the idea behind it; a badly wrong one would **refute** it. This is how testing a prediction
works.

**Worked example 3 (isotopes).** Tellurium (atomic number 52) has relative atomic mass 127.6. Iodine (atomic
number 53) has 126.9. Explain why tellurium comes first.

Elements are ordered by atomic number, and 52 is less than 53. Iodine has only one natural isotope, iodine-127.
Tellurium's most common isotopes are tellurium-128 and tellurium-130, both heavier than 127, so tellurium's
average mass is higher even though it has fewer protons. The same reasoning places argon (39.95) before
potassium (39.10): almost all argon is argon-40, while most potassium is potassium-39.

## 4.1.2.3 Metals and non-metals

The specification defines the two groups by ions:

- **metals** are elements that react to form **positive ions**
- **non-metals** are elements that **do not form positive ions**.

Most elements are metals. Metals are on the left and towards the bottom of the table; non-metals are on the
right and towards the top.

| Property | Typical metal | Typical non-metal |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical and thermal conduction | Good | Poor (graphite is an exception) |
| Appearance | Shiny | Dull |
| Melting and boiling points | Mostly high | Mostly low |
| Solid shape change | Malleable and ductile | Brittle |
| Reaction in compounds | Loses electrons, forms positive ions | Gains electrons (negative ions) or shares electrons |
| Oxide | Reacts with acids (a base) | Often acidic |

**Why position matches atomic structure.** Metal atoms have only a few outer electrons (usually 1, 2 or 3). They
lose these to reach a stable arrangement, forming positive ions, so metals sit on the left. Non-metal atoms
have nearly full outer shells (4 to 7 outer electrons). They gain or share electrons instead, so they sit on
the right. Going down a group, the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and are lost more easily, so
elements become more metallic. In Group 4, carbon at the top is a non-metal, while tin and lead lower down are
metals.

**Reactions follow electrons.** Sodium (2,8,1) loses one electron to form Na⁺; chlorine (2,8,7) gains one to
form Cl⁻, giving ionic sodium chloride. For bonding, see the [ionic bonding revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-ionic-bonding-revision-notes/).

## 4.1.2.4 Group 0: the noble gases

The noble gases are **unreactive** and do not easily form molecules. They exist as single atoms, because
their atoms already have a **stable arrangement of electrons**: eight outer electrons, except helium, which has
two (a full first shell). There is nothing to gain by losing, gaining or sharing electrons.

Their **boiling points increase with relative atomic mass**, going down the group.

| Element | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|
| Helium | −269 |
| Neon | −246 |
| Argon | −186 |
| Krypton | ? |
| Xenon | −107 |

**Worked example 4.** Predict the boiling point of krypton.

```
Midpoint of argon and xenon = (-186 + -107) / 2 = -146.5 °C
```

A sensible prediction is about −150 °C (the measured value is −152 °C). Any value between the two neighbours
fits the trend; a value outside them does not.

## 4.1.2.5 Group 1: the alkali metals

All Group 1 elements have **one electron in the outer shell**, which gives them their characteristic
properties. They lose that electron to form ions with a **1+ charge**. Their reactivity **increases down the
group**.

**Explaining the trend.** Going down the group there are more shells, so the outer electron is further from
the nucleus and more shielded. It is less strongly attracted, so it is lost more easily and the reaction is
more vigorous.

You must describe the reactions of the first three alkali metals with three substances.

**With oxygen.** Freshly cut lithium, sodium and potassium are shiny but tarnish quickly in air as an oxide
forms, so they are stored under oil. Heated in oxygen they burn (lithium red flame, sodium yellow-orange,
potassium lilac). At GCSE the product is written as the oxide.

```
4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O    lithium oxide
4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O    sodium oxide
4K + O₂ → 2K₂O      potassium oxide
```

**With chlorine.** Each metal reacts vigorously when heated in chlorine to form a white solid chloride, more
vigorously going down the group.

```
2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl    sodium chloride
```

**With water.** Each metal forms a metal hydroxide and hydrogen. The hydroxide dissolves, so the solution is
alkaline and turns universal indicator purple.

| Metal | Observations |
|---|---|
| Lithium | Floats; fizzes steadily; slowly gets smaller |
| Sodium | Floats; melts into a ball; fizzes rapidly; moves about on the surface |
| Potassium | Floats; melts; fizzes violently; hydrogen ignites with a lilac flame |

```
2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂
```

**Worked example 5 (predicting).** Rubidium is below potassium. Predict its reaction with water.

It forms rubidium hydroxide and hydrogen, 2Rb + 2H₂O → 2RbOH + H₂, and reacts **more vigorously than
potassium**, because its outer electron is further from the nucleus and lost more easily.

## 4.1.2.6 Group 7: the halogens

The halogens are **non-metals** made of **diatomic molecules** (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). They react in similar ways
because each atom has **seven outer electrons** and needs one more.

**Trends down the group:**

- relative molecular mass, melting point and boiling point all **increase**
- reactivity **decreases**.

At room temperature chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a grey-black solid.
Larger molecules mean stronger forces between molecules, so more energy is needed to separate them.

**Explaining the reactivity trend.** A halogen reacts by gaining one electron. Further down, the outer shell is
further from the nucleus, so an incoming electron is attracted less strongly and is gained less easily.

**Compounds with metals and non-metals.** This is a separate learning outcome, so learn both cases.

- With **metals**, chlorine, bromine and iodine form **ionic compounds** (salts) containing halide ions with a
  **1− charge**: chloride Cl⁻, bromide Br⁻, iodide I⁻. Example: 2K + Br₂ → 2KBr, potassium bromide.
- With **non-metals**, they form **covalent molecules**, sharing electrons. Example: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, hydrogen
  chloride, a gas made of small molecules. It dissolves in water to form an acidic solution.

**Displacement.** A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its
salt. If the halogen added is less reactive than the one in the salt, nothing happens.

| Halogen added | Potassium chloride (aq) | Potassium bromide (aq) | Potassium iodide (aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine water | -- | Turns orange (bromine formed) | Turns brown (iodine formed) |
| Bromine water | No reaction | -- | Turns brown (iodine formed) |
| Iodine solution | No reaction | No reaction | -- |

**Worked example 6.** Write the equation for chlorine reacting with potassium bromide solution.

```
Word:     chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine
Symbol:   Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂
```

**Higher tier only.** Potassium ions do not change, so the ionic equation leaves them out:
Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂. Check charges: 2− on each side.

**Predicting.** Astatine, below iodine, should be a dark solid with a higher melting point, less reactive than
iodine, and unable to displace iodine from potassium iodide.

## Common errors

- Saying Mendeleev "discovered" gallium and germanium. He predicted them; others found them.
- Explaining tellurium and iodine without isotopes. The mark is for "tellurium has heavier isotopes, so a
  higher average mass".
- Writing that noble gases are unreactive "because they are gases". The reason is a stable electron arrangement.
- Giving bromine water and potassium chloride a colour change. There is no reaction.

## Frequently asked questions

**Did Mendeleev discover gallium and germanium?**
No. Mendeleev left gaps in his table and predicted the properties of undiscovered elements; other scientists later found elements such as gallium and germanium that fitted those gaps.

**Why is tellurium placed before iodine even though it has a higher relative atomic mass?**
The modern table orders elements by atomic number, and tellurium has fewer protons than iodine. Tellurium has heavier isotopes, which gives it a higher average relative atomic mass.

**Is there a reaction when bromine water is added to potassium chloride?**
No. A halogen only displaces a less reactive halogen from its salt, and bromine is less reactive than chlorine, so there is no reaction and no colour change.

**What is the formula of a Group 1 halide such as sodium chloride?**
Group 1 ions have a 1+ charge and halide ions have a 1− charge, so the formula is NaCl, not Na₂Cl or NaCl₂. The same pattern gives KBr and LiI.
