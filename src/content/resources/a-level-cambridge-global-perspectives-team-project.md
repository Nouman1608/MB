Component 3 – Team Project is worth 40 marks and is externally marked
coursework, sitting alongside Component 1 (Written Exam) and Component
2 (Essay) as one of the three compulsory AS Level components in
Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research
(9239). Where the [Written Exam guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-written-exam/)
and [Essay guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-essay/)
cover individually-produced components, Team Project is the one
component built explicitly around collaborative research, even though
the final marks are entirely individual.

## What makes Team Project different

Unlike the Essay, which is a single piece of independent work, Team
Project requires candidates to work in **teams of two to five
members** to identify a local problem with global relevance. The team
works together to agree a single problem statement broad enough that
each member can research and argue a distinct, contrasting perspective
on it -- but from that point on, assessment is entirely individual.
Team presentations are not accepted for submission; only individual
work is marked.

Each candidate produces two pieces of individually authored,
individually assessed work from the shared research process:

- a **presentation** (30 marks) on their own research and preferred solution
- a **reflective paper** (10 marks, maximum 800 words) on the team's collaboration and their own learning

## Forming the team and the problem statement

Teachers take an active role in creating teams -- candidates do not
simply self-select. The syllabus notes that teams of three or four
tend to work best, since they are large enough to generate genuinely
different perspectives on a problem but small enough to reach
agreement on what to research. Once formed, the team's central task
is to identify a **local problem that has global relevance** and
express it as a single statement broad enough in scope that different
members can explore it from different angles -- some more local, some
more global -- while still addressing the same underlying issue.
Teachers support the team in formulating this statement, but the
research direction and argument for each member's chosen angle must
be the candidates' own.

Once the problem statement is agreed, each team member is allocated
(or chooses) a distinct area of the problem to research, and is
expected to argue from that distinct perspective towards an effective,
workable solution -- not to summarise the team's collective findings,
but to build and defend an individual position.

## The presentation (30 marks)

Each candidate delivers a presentation, **maximum 10 minutes**, about
their own research and their preferred solution. It can take either
of two forms:

- a **live recording** of the candidate presenting to an audience of at least three people, one of whom must be the class teacher, recorded in one session in a quiet setting
- a **presentation with a recorded voiceover**, using presentation software with the candidate's own narration attached, which does not require a live audience but must still be recorded in a quiet setting

Whichever format is used, the presentation is assessed against both
AO1 (Research, analysis and evaluation) and AO3 (Communication),
specifically:

- individual analysis of the team's problem (AO1)
- range of research undertaken (AO1)
- evaluation of sources (AO1)
- justification for the candidate's own proposed solution (AO1)
- a well-argued, well-organised presentation (AO3)
- effective use of visual information (AO3)
- language pitched appropriately for the audience (AO3)

The syllabus is explicit that the presentation should be engaging to a
**non-specialist audience**, use audio and visual elements
purposefully rather than extensively, and cite references clearly and
accurately. Work beyond the 10-minute limit is simply not assessed, so
timing the presentation precisely during preparation matters as much
as its content.

Alongside the recording itself, candidates must submit the supporting
audio/visual materials and a **verbatim transcript** of the
presentation.

## The reflective paper (10 marks)

The reflective paper is a maximum of 800 words and is assessed
entirely against **AO2 (Reflection)**. It focuses on two things:

- how effectively the team worked together, and what could have been improved
- the candidate's own learning -- which may be about the problem itself, the research skills they developed, the presentation skills they developed, or how the collaborative experience shaped their personal views

This is not a second presentation of research findings. Examiners are
assessing genuine, specific reflection on process and collaboration,
not a restatement of the problem or solution already covered in the
presentation.

## What must be submitted

For each candidate, four pieces of work go to Cambridge International:

1. the recording (live presentation or presentation with recorded voiceover)
2. the audio and/or visual materials supporting it
3. a transcript of the presentation
4. the reflective paper

A statement of originality is required for each candidate, and all
materials must be submitted electronically by the deadlines set out in
the Administrative Guide.

## The teacher's role and its limits

Teachers may support candidates in understanding the task, formulating
a research topic, developing ways to explore different perspectives,
building organisational and referencing skills, and developing clear
oral arguments -- but the syllabus draws a firm line once research and
preparation begin. Teachers must not offer detailed subject guidance,
carry out research on a candidate's behalf, write or correct any part
of a candidate's notes, prepare any part of a presentation, produce
any part of the transcript, or suggest amendments to a candidate's
presentation or reflective paper. Advice must stay general enough that
candidates lead their own decisions -- which matters for candidates to
understand up front, since it shapes how much support they can
realistically expect once the project is under way.

## How to approach it

Choose a problem statement broad enough to support genuinely different
individual angles, but specific enough that "local relevance" and
"global relevance" are both concrete rather than asserted in the
abstract -- a strong problem statement names a real place, group or
situation, not just a general global issue. Because AO1 (research,
analysis, evaluation) carries the most marks in the presentation,
prioritise the range and evaluation of sources over polish in the
delivery itself, though both are assessed. Draft the reflective paper
only after the presentation is finished, since genuine reflection on
"what could be improved" is far more convincing -- and far easier to
write honestly -- once the whole process, including its difficulties,
has actually happened.
