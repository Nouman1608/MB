This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 12.2 of Unit 5, Human development and diversity, part of the Higher Level extension of IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019), examined in Paper 3. The [12.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-changing-identities-and-cultures-revision-notes/) already define culture, identity, diffusion, homogenisation, glocalisation and diaspora, and give examples such as the Korean Wave and the Welsh and Māori language revivals. This page adds tools for analysing cultural change: the difference between material and non-material culture, the idea of placelessness, contrasting models of how migrant and host cultures relate, a further diaspora example, and a worked example of reading survey data. Practise with the [12.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-12-2-practice/); the [Human development and diversity study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-human-development-diversity-study-guide/) gives the unit overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how global interactions spread cultural traits and change identities.
- Evaluate the evidence for cultural homogenisation against the evidence for new, hybrid forms.
- Examine how migration and diasporas change both origin and destination places.
- Recognise that cultural change affects groups and places unevenly.

## Material and non-material culture

Splitting culture into two kinds sharpens an argument about homogenisation.

| Type | Examples | How quickly it spreads |
|---|---|---|
| Material culture | Clothing, food products, buildings, consumer goods | Fast: goods travel easily through trade and retail |
| Non-material culture | Language, religion, values, family structures, beliefs | Slower: rooted in upbringing, community and institutions |

Global brands and fashions often spread quickly, so a city centre can *look* homogenised. Values, faith and family life usually change more slowly, so people may wear global clothing and use global apps while keeping distinctive beliefs. An answer that makes this distinction can argue that homogenisation is real but shallow in some places, which is more convincing than claiming it is total or absent.

## Sense of place and placelessness

Identity is tied to place as well as to groups. The geographer Edward Relph, in *Place and Placelessness* (1976), argued that standardised landscapes, such as identical shopping malls, chain hotels and airport terminals, weaken the distinctive character of places and create **placelessness**. Places that feel the same everywhere offer fewer anchors for local identity.

The idea helps explain why communities sometimes resist global chains or redevelopment: they are defending a sense of place, not only jobs or prices. It also helps you evaluate glocalisation: a global chain that adapts its menu but keeps an identical building may still contribute to placelessness.

## How migrant and host cultures relate

Migration brings cultures into contact. Three broad models describe what can follow.

- **Assimilation.** Migrants are expected to adopt the language and customs of the host society, and distinctive traits fade over generations.
- **Multiculturalism.** Different cultural groups keep distinct identities side by side, with public recognition of that diversity.
- **Integration.** Groups share common civic values and institutions while keeping aspects of their own culture.

Real places mix these, and policies change over time. The models are useful because they turn "migration changes culture" into a question of *how* and *on whose terms*.

## A diaspora example: carnival in London

The Notting Hill Carnival in west London grew from the Caribbean community that settled in Britain after the Second World War; it is usually dated from the street festival of 1966. Drawing on traditions such as Trinidad's carnival, it has become one of the largest street festivals in Europe. It shows several processes at once:

- **Diffusion**: Caribbean music, costume and food carried by migration.
- **Hybridisation**: new musical forms and audiences that blend Caribbean and British influences.
- **Identity**: an assertion of community presence, especially important at times when Caribbean residents faced discrimination.
- **Change in the host society**: a migrant tradition becoming part of the city's identity.

Examples like this are powerful evidence against simple homogenisation, because diffusion runs from a former colony to the former colonial centre and creates something new.

## Worked example: reading survey data on language use

A survey asks 200 residents of a bilingual region whether they mainly use the regional language at home. Figures are invented.

```
Age group    Respondents    Use regional language at home    Percentage
Under 25         80                    30                    30 / 80 x 100 = 37.5%
25 to 59         90                    54                    54 / 90 x 100 = 60.0%
60 and over      30                    27                    27 / 30 x 100 = 90.0%
All ages        200                   111                   111 / 200 x 100 = 55.5%
```

Use of the regional language falls sharply with each younger age group, from 90 per cent among older residents to 37.5 per cent among the young. The overall figure of 55.5 per cent hides this pattern. The interpretation: the language may be in decline between generations, consistent with homogenisation, unless young people are learning it at school but not using it at home. Evaluate the evidence: the sample is small, only 30 people are aged 60 and over, and home language is one measure of identity among many.

## Indigenous identity and political recognition

For indigenous peoples, identity is closely tied to land, language and livelihoods that global interactions such as mining, tourism and forestry can threaten. Political recognition is one response. Norway opened a Sámi Parliament in 1989, giving the Sámi people an elected body with responsibilities for their language and culture, and Sámi languages are taught in schools in parts of northern Norway. Recognition of this kind shows identity being protected through institutions as well as through everyday practice, and it links this sub-topic to the local responses examined in 12.3.

## Uneven change and backlash

Cultural change rarely affects a whole place in the same way. Younger, urban, educated and online groups usually adopt global influences fastest. At the same time, rapid change can provoke a **backlash**: stronger assertion of national, religious or local identity, sometimes expressed through politics. This means globalisation can intensify identity as well as dilute it. A well-balanced answer notes both responses, and the groups most likely to show each.

## Annotated model answer

*To what extent does globalisation lead to cultural homogenisation?* **(sample paragraph)**

"Material culture shows clear convergence: the same brands, clothing and shopping malls appear in cities worldwide, creating what Relph called placelessness **[evidence for, with a concept]**. Non-material culture changes more slowly, and diasporas often create new forms rather than losing old ones, as London's Notting Hill Carnival shows **[counter-evidence, located]**. Change is also uneven: survey evidence often shows young urban people adopting global habits faster than older residents **[differentiation by group]**. Homogenisation is therefore strongest in visible consumer culture and weakest in deeper values and in places where communities actively assert their identity **[differentiated judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating the appearance of global brands as proof that whole cultures have converged.
- Using culture and identity as if they meant the same thing.
- Describing migration's cultural effects only in destination places, ignoring origins.
- Quoting an overall survey figure without examining differences between groups.
- Forgetting that globalisation can strengthen local identity through backlash or revival.

## Exam technique

- **To what extent** questions on homogenisation need a position that distinguishes types of culture and groups of people.
- **Examine** rewards showing how a process works, for example how a diaspora both keeps and changes its culture.
- **Discuss** needs evidence for convergence and for diversity, from named places.
- **Analyse** data by comparing groups, quoting percentages and noting limits of the sample.
- Use precise terms: diffusion, hybridisation, placelessness, assimilation, multiculturalism.
