This guide covers section 3.2.2.2, Challenges of rapid economic development, in Section B (The changing economic world) of Unit 2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The key idea is: "Some LICs and NEEs are experiencing rapid economic development which leads to significant social, environmental and cultural change." The specification requires **a case study of an LIC or NEE**. It does not name a country, so this guide uses China as a worked framework. If your class studied another country, such as India, Nigeria, Brazil or Bangladesh, keep the same headings and replace the facts. This page goes deeper than the earlier theme on [global variations in development](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-global-variations-economic-development/).

## What the specification lists

The case study must cover:

- the **location and importance** of the country, regionally and globally;
- the wider **political, social, cultural and environmental context**;
- the **changing industrial structure**, the balance between sectors, and how manufacturing can stimulate development;
- the **role of transnational corporations (TNCs)**, with advantages and disadvantages to the host country;
- **changing political and trading relationships** with the wider world;
- **international aid:** types of aid and impacts on the receiving country;
- the **environmental impacts** of economic development;
- the **effects on quality of life** for the population.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Newly emerging economy (NEE) | A country with rapid industrial growth, between LIC and HIC |
| Primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary | Raw materials; manufacturing; services; knowledge and research |
| Transnational corporation (TNC) | A company that operates in more than one country |
| Special economic zone | An area with special rules, such as low taxes, to attract foreign investment |
| Foreign direct investment (FDI) | Money invested in a business in another country |
| Bilateral aid | Aid from one government to another |
| Multilateral aid | Aid pooled from many countries through an organisation such as the World Bank |
| Tied aid | Aid that must be spent on goods or services from the donor |
| Quality of life | The level of well-being: health, education, income, housing, environment |
| Multiplier effect | Growth in one industry creating jobs and demand in others |

## Case study: China

### Location and importance

China lies in East Asia, bordered by many countries and by the Pacific Ocean to the east. It has the world's second-largest population (more than 1.4 billion, having been overtaken by India in 2023) and is one of the largest countries by area. Since the economic reforms that began in 1978, it has grown from a poor, mostly rural economy into the world's second-largest economy by total output and the largest manufacturing country. It is a permanent member of the UN Security Council and a leading trading nation. **Regionally**, it is the dominant economy in Asia. **Globally**, it supplies a huge share of the world's manufactured goods and is a major investor in other countries.

### Wider context

- **Political:** a one-party state, with strong central planning. The government set the direction of reform, for example by choosing where to place special economic zones.
- **Social:** a rapidly urbanising society with large movements of people from villages to cities. The one-child policy (1979 to 2015) slowed population growth but contributed to an ageing population and a gender imbalance.
- **Cultural:** an ancient culture with many ethnic groups and traditions. Rapid change has brought tension between modern urban lifestyles and traditional rural ones.
- **Environmental:** the country has varied physical geography, with mountains, deserts, large rivers and a densely populated east coast. Water is unevenly distributed, with the south wetter than the north.

### Changing industrial structure

Before reform, most people worked in farming. Since 1978:

- The share of **primary** employment (farming) has fallen sharply, from around 70% of workers in 1978 to under a quarter today.
- The **secondary** (manufacturing) sector grew very rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s, and China became known as the "workshop of the world".
- The **tertiary** (service) sector is now the largest in terms of output, and the **quaternary** sector, including technology and research, is growing.

Agriculture, which made up about a third of output in 1980, now contributes less than a tenth. Check the exact shares in your own notes.

**How manufacturing stimulated growth.** Factories created millions of jobs, raising incomes. Exports earned foreign currency, which paid for machinery and technology. Workers moved from low-productivity farming to higher-productivity factories. Demand for steel, power, transport and services created further jobs (the multiplier effect). Tax income paid for roads, railways and ports.

### Role of TNCs

The government set up **special economic zones** from 1980, beginning with Shenzhen, to attract foreign companies. TNCs invested in factories, bringing capital, technology and export markets. A well-known example is the assembly of electronic products for brands such as Apple by the firm Foxconn, in large factory cities such as Shenzhen and Zhengzhou.

| Advantages to China | Disadvantages to China |
|---|---|
| Millions of jobs | Wages often low, and working conditions sometimes poor |
| Investment in factories and infrastructure | Profits may be sent abroad |
| Transfer of technology and skills | Factories may relocate to cheaper countries when wages rise |
| Exports and foreign currency | Pollution from industry; dependence on foreign companies |
| Tax revenue | Local firms may find it hard to compete |

### Changing political and trading relationships

China was relatively closed to the world before 1978. The "open door" policy ended that. Key steps include joining the World Trade Organization in 2001, which opened more markets, and rapid growth in trade with the USA, Europe, Japan and elsewhere. China is now the world's largest exporter of goods. It has also become a major investor abroad, notably through the Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, which funds ports, railways and roads across Asia, Africa and Europe. Relationships are not all smooth: trade disputes with the USA, including tariffs from 2018, show how interdependence can bring tension.

### International aid

Aid is help given by governments, organisations or individuals to another country.

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bilateral | Government to government |
| Multilateral | Through organisations such as the World Bank or UN |
| Voluntary (NGO) | Charities and non-governmental organisations |
| Tied | Money must be spent on the donor's goods |
| Emergency (short-term) | Food, shelter and medicine after a disaster |
| Development (long-term) | Projects such as schools, roads and clinics |

China itself received loans and technical help from other governments and the World Bank in the 1980s and after, which helped fund infrastructure such as ports, railways and power. **Impacts on a receiving country** can be positive (infrastructure, health and education, jobs, saving lives) or negative (dependence, conditions attached, corruption, projects that do not suit local needs). China has now become a major donor and lender to other countries, which shows how a recipient can change roles as it develops.

### Environmental impacts

- **Air pollution:** coal supplies a large share of China's electricity, and heavy industry and traffic add to smog. Cities such as Beijing have suffered serious episodes.
- **Water pollution and shortages:** industrial waste and fertiliser pollute rivers; northern China is short of water.
- **Land degradation:** soil loss, desertification in the north-west, and the loss of farmland to cities.
- **Carbon emissions:** China is the world's largest emitter of carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change.
- **Responses:** large investment in wind, solar and hydropower; closure of old coal plants; tree-planting; stricter pollution laws.

### Effects on quality of life

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Hundreds of millions lifted out of extreme poverty since the 1970s | Inequality between coast and interior and between rich and poor |
| Higher life expectancy and better healthcare | Long working hours and low pay in some factories |
| Wider access to education | Migrant workers often lack access to city services; children left behind in villages |
| Better housing, electricity and consumer goods | Air and water pollution harm health |
| Improved transport and communications | Loss of traditional neighbourhoods and ways of life |

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Assess the effects of economic development on the quality of life of people in a country you have studied. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "In China, rapid industrial growth since 1978 has raised incomes and lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty. Life expectancy has risen, more children go to school, and many families now have electricity, better housing and consumer goods. However, the benefits are uneven. Coastal cities have grown much faster than inland areas, so there is a large income gap. Millions of migrant workers in cities work long hours for low pay and cannot always use city schools and health services, so their children are often left behind in villages. Air pollution in cities such as Beijing harms health. Overall, quality of life has improved for most people, but the gains are shared unevenly and come with environmental costs."

The answer balances positives and negatives and ends with a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a country that is not an LIC or NEE.** Check with your teacher.
- **Writing general statements about development.** Name the country and give facts.
- **Confusing the sectors.** Primary is raw materials, secondary is manufacturing, tertiary is services.
- **Only listing TNC advantages.** Both advantages and disadvantages are needed.
- **Forgetting dates.** Say when reforms or events happened.
- **Treating aid as always good or always bad.** Weigh the types and effects.
