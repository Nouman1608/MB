These questions cover **section 3.2.3.3 Supply-side policies** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: the named policies, their advantages and disadvantages, and how they help achieve government objectives. There are no tiers. All firms, countries and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [supply-side policies study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-supply-side-policies-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [supply-side policies revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-supply-side-policies-aqa-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a supply-side policy? **[1]**

A: A cut in the Bank Rate

B: A rise in unemployment benefits

C: Government-funded apprenticeships

D: A rise in VAT

**2.** A government sells its state-owned railway company to private shareholders. This is an example of: **[1]**

A: Deregulation

B: Privatisation

C: Nationalisation

D: Trade union reform

**3.** State what is meant by productivity. **[1]**

**4.** A factory's 80 workers produce 2,000 units a week. After a training scheme, the same 80 workers produce 2,240 units a week.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker before and after the training. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[1]**

**5.** A firm makes a profit of £3.2 million. The rate of tax on business profits is cut from 28% to 22%.

**(a)** Calculate the tax the firm pays at each rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the extra profit the firm keeps after tax. **[1]**

**6.** A worker is offered 6 extra hours at £15 an hour. The share of extra earnings taken in tax and deductions falls from 42% to 32%. Calculate how much more of the extra earnings the worker keeps. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** way in which deregulation could benefit consumers. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how investment in education and training could help a government keep inflation low as the economy grows. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** disadvantages of supply-side policies. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how trade union reform could increase an economy's productive capacity. **[3]**

**11.** An invented country has shortages of skilled engineers and IT workers, low business investment, and high structural unemployment in towns where factories have closed.

**(a)** Identify the supply-side policy most suited to reducing the structural unemployment, and explain why. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why a cut in the tax on business profits may fail to raise investment. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate whether supply-side policies are better than demand-side policies for achieving economic growth. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Apprenticeships improve the quality of labour; the other options change demand. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Ownership moves from the state to private shareholders. **[1]**

**3.** Output per worker, or per hour worked, over a period [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Before: 2,000 ÷ 80 = **25 units** [1]. After: 2,240 ÷ 80 = **28 units** [1].

**(b)** 3 ÷ 25 × 100 = **12%** [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** At 28%: 0.28 × 3,200,000 = **£896,000** [1]. At 22%: 0.22 × 3,200,000 = **£704,000** [1].

**(b)** 896,000 − 704,000 = **£192,000** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Extra earnings = 6 × 15 = £90. Kept before: 0.58 × 90 = £52.20; kept after: 0.68 × 90 = £61.20 [1]. The worker keeps **£9.00** more [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Removing rules that block new firms allows more competitors to enter the market [1], so firms must lower prices or improve quality to keep customers [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Training raises workers' skills and productivity [1], so firms' costs per unit fall and they have less need to raise prices, easing cost-push pressure [1]. Productive capacity also rises [1], so output can grow to meet rising demand without shortages that push up prices (demand-pull inflation) [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Any two developed: long time lags [1], because training or new investment takes years to raise output, so they do not help in a recession [1]; high cost to the government [1], either through spending or lost tax revenue, which may mean higher borrowing or cuts elsewhere [1]; some policies widen inequality [1], for example lower income tax gives the largest cash gains to high earners [1]; uncertain results [1], because workers and firms may not respond as hoped [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Limits on union powers, such as ballots before strikes, reduce the number of days lost to strikes [1]. Firms can change pay and working practices more flexibly to match demand [1], which lowers costs and encourages investment, so the economy can produce more [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Investment in education and training (retraining) [1]. Structural unemployment comes from a mismatch between workers' skills and the jobs available [1]; retraining former factory workers in engineering or IT skills lets them fill the vacancies, cutting unemployment and the skills shortage together [1].

**(b)** Firms may pay the extra profit to shareholders instead of investing [1], or they may not invest if they expect weak demand for their products, whatever the tax rate [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *For supply-side policies:*

- They raise productive capacity, so growth can be sustained over the long run [1].
- Growth comes without the inflation that extra demand can cause near full capacity [1].
- They can also improve competitiveness and the balance of payments [1].

*For demand-side policies:*

- Lower interest rates or higher government spending can raise output quickly when the economy has spare capacity, such as in a recession [1].
- Supply-side policies take years and cost money, with uncertain results [1].
- Extra capacity is wasted if there is not enough demand to buy the output [1].

*Judgement:* supply-side policies are better for long-run growth because they raise what the economy can produce [1], but in a recession demand-side policy is needed first to use spare capacity [1]; the most effective approach uses both, chosen by whether the economy is short of demand or short of capacity [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Distinguish the short run from the long run; it is the strongest basis for a judgement here.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an interest-rate cut a supply-side policy.
- Dividing total output by the new number of workers when the number did not change, or forgetting to divide at all.
- Mixing up privatisation and deregulation.
- Giving "it costs money" as the only disadvantage.
- Recommending a policy that does not match the problem in the case.
