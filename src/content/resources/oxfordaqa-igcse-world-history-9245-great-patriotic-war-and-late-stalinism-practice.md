## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three economic problems the USSR faced during the Great Patriotic War. **[3]**

**2.** Give three ways in which women were used in Soviet wartime propaganda. **[3]**

**3.** Give three features of High Stalinism after 1945. **[3]**

**4.** Give three features of living standards in the USSR in the years 1945-53. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which the relocation of industry in 1941 affected the Soviet war effort. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which Stalin's leadership weakened the USSR in 1941. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the terror affected Soviet people in the years 1945-53. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the Great Patriotic War changed Soviet society. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why Soviet agriculture stagnated after 1945. **[9]**

**10.** "Stalin's leadership was the main reason for Soviet victory in the Great Patriotic War." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "The USSR recovered successfully from the Great Patriotic War by 1953." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**12.** "Fear was the main way Stalin controlled the Soviet people in the years 1945-53." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: loss of Ukraine and other occupied regions; factories had to be dismantled and moved east; food shortages and strict rationing; shortages of labour as men joined the army; damage to transport; the need to produce weapons rather than consumer goods.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: the poster *The Motherland Calls!* (1941) showed a woman as the nation; Lyudmila Pavlichenko was presented as a heroic sniper; she toured Allied countries in 1942; women factory and farm workers were shown as patriotic heroines; women pilots such as the "Night Witches" were celebrated; images of women showed Soviet equality.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: the cult of personality at its height, including the seventieth birthday celebrations (1949); Zhdanov's attacks on writers such as Akhmatova; the campaign against "cosmopolitans"; returning prisoners of war sent to the Gulag; the Leningrad Affair; the Doctors' Plot (1953); Beria's security apparatus.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: severe housing shortage, with families in communal flats, barracks or dugouts; scarce consumer goods; famine in 1946-47; rationing until December 1947; a currency reform that wiped out savings; low pay on collective farms.

**5.** Way identified [1]: it allowed weapons production to continue and grow. Precise knowledge [2]: in 1941 over 1,500 factories were dismantled and moved by rail to the Urals, Siberia and Central Asia, where they were rebuilt and restarted, often before buildings were finished. Explanation [2]: because the German advance captured much of the western industrial area, without relocation the Red Army would have run short of tanks, aircraft and guns. Further development [1]: by 1943 Soviet factories were producing more tanks and aircraft than Germany, helping victories at Stalingrad and Kursk.

**6.** Way identified [1]: the Red Army was poorly prepared and suffered huge early losses. Precise knowledge [2]: Stalin ignored warnings of the invasion; the purges of 1937-38 had removed many experienced officers; his orders to hold ground led to vast encirclements such as at Kiev in September 1941. Explanation [2]: because the army was surprised and badly led in the first months, the Germans advanced deep into the USSR, capturing millions of soldiers and key industrial and farming regions. Further development [1]: this explains the scale of the economic crisis of 1941 and the need to relocate industry.

**7.** Way identified [1]: returning soldiers and prisoners faced suspicion and punishment. Precise knowledge [2]: Soviet prisoners of war who returned in 1945 were screened by the security services, and many were sent to the Gulag, whose population peaked in the early 1950s. Explanation [2]: because Stalin regarded surrender and contact with the West as signs of disloyalty, people who had suffered for the country were treated as possible traitors, so victory did not bring safety. Further development [1]: fear grew again in 1953 with the Doctors' Plot, which many saw as the start of a new purge.

**8.** Way one [4]: huge loss of life and family breakdown [1]; around 27 million Soviet citizens died, and millions of children were orphaned [1]; so many families lost their main earners, and women far outnumbered men in the post-war population [2]. Way two [4]: women's roles expanded [1]; women did most collective-farm work and filled factory jobs, and hundreds of thousands served in the armed forces [1]; so women gained new skills and responsibilities, though after 1945 the state again stressed motherhood [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the deportation of the Chechens and Crimean Tatars in 1944 or the Church's revival in 1943.

**9.** Reason one [4]: the war's destruction and loss of labour [1]; occupied areas were devastated, and millions of men and horses were lost [1]; so collective farms lacked workers and animals and could not produce enough [2]. Reason two [4]: state policies [1]; grain procurement continued during the drought of 1946, peasants were heavily taxed on private plots, and collective farmers were paid little [1]; so peasants had little incentive to work harder, and the famine of 1946-47 showed the system's failure [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example that industry received priority under the Fourth Five-Year Plan.

**10.** Given factor [3]: Stalin chaired the State Defence Committee; stayed in Moscow in October 1941; used patriotic appeals; from 1942 trusted commanders such as Zhukov; Order No. 227 enforced discipline. Other factors [4]: the industrial base built under the Five-Year Plans and relocated east; the sacrifice of Soviet soldiers and civilians, including women; the Russian winter and the size of the country; German mistakes; Lend-Lease aid from the USA and Britain; the Allies' war in the west. Comparison [3]: Stalin's early mistakes made victory harder, and his later leadership depended on the industry and people he mobilised; the economy and the people's endurance were the resources Stalin's direction used. Judgement [2]: for example, the industrial base and the sacrifices of the Soviet people were more important than Stalin, whose leadership improved after 1942 but who was partly responsible for the disasters of 1941.

**11.** Given factor, case for recovery [3]: heavy industry was rebuilt under the Fourth Five-Year Plan, with official claims that output had passed pre-war levels by 1950; the Dnieper dam was rebuilt; the USSR tested an atomic bomb in 1949 and became a superpower. Case against [4]: agriculture stagnated and there was famine in 1946-47; housing remained desperately short; consumer goods were scarce; the currency reform of 1947 wiped out savings; living standards were little better than in 1940; terror increased again. Comparison [3]: recovery depended on what is measured: the state and its heavy industry recovered, but ordinary people's lives did not, because resources went to industry and defence rather than to food and housing. Judgement [2]: for example, the USSR recovered as a military and industrial power, but not as a society in which people lived better.

**12.** Given factor [3]: returning prisoners of war sent to the Gulag; the Leningrad Affair (1949-50); the Doctors' Plot (1953); Beria's security apparatus; the Gulag at its peak in the early 1950s. Other factors [4]: propaganda and the cult of personality, which credited Stalin with victory and peaked in 1949; cultural control under Zhdanov; genuine pride in victory and patriotism; the party's control of jobs, housing and education. Comparison [3]: propaganda and pride in victory gave many people reasons to support Stalin, while fear silenced anyone who doubted; fear was most intense for particular groups, such as returning soldiers, Jews and party officials. Judgement [2]: for example, fear was the main method because terror reached every level of society, but propaganda and victory explain why most people did not need to be coerced.
