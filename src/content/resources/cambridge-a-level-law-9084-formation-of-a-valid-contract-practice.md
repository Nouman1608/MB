## Section A -- short answers and scenarios

**1.** Distinguish a bilateral contract from a unilateral contract. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by a collateral contract, using a case. **[2]**

**3.** State **three** ways in which an offer may be terminated. **[3]**

**4.** Ana offers to sell her bike to Ben for £200. Ben replies, "Would you take £150?" Ana refuses. Ben then says, "All right, I accept £200." Explain whether a contract exists. **[4]**

**5.** A college invites tenders for catering, stating that "the contract will be awarded to the lowest tender received by noon on 1 March". Cara submits the lowest tender at 11 am, but the college awards the contract to a friend of the principal. Advise Cara. **[5]**

**6.** Dan, 17, buys a gaming laptop on credit for £1,500. He already owns a laptop that meets all his college needs. He refuses to pay. Advise the seller. **[5]**

**7.** Eve posts a letter accepting Finn's written offer to sell his car. The letter is lost. Finn, hearing nothing, sells the car to someone else. Explain whether Eve has a contract with Finn. **[4]**

## Section B -- short answer and essay

**8.** Explain the presumptions about intention to create legal relations and how they may be rebutted. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the rules on performance of an existing duty as consideration. **[6]**

**10.** Explain the requirements of promissory estoppel and its limits. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how the rules of offer and acceptance apply at an auction. **[4]**

**12.** "The law on minors' contracts strikes the right balance between protecting young people and protecting those who deal with them." Assess this view. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A bilateral contract is an exchange of promises, so both parties are bound from the moment of agreement [1]. A unilateral contract is a promise in exchange for an act; only the promisor is bound, and acceptance is by performing the act (*Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* (1893)) [1].

**2.** A collateral contract is a separate contract alongside a main contract, often between a party to the main contract and a third party [1]. In *Shanklin Pier v Detel Products* (1951) the paint maker's assurance about durability was enforceable by the pier owners, whose instruction to their contractors to buy the paint was consideration [1].

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: communicated revocation [1]; rejection or counter offer [1]; lapse of time [1]; failure of a condition; death in some circumstances.

**4.** "Would you take £150?" might be a counter offer, which would end the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench* (1840)) [1], or a request for information, which leaves the offer open (*Stevenson, Jacques v McLean* (1880)) [1]. As a question rather than a new proposal, it is more likely a request for information [1]. If so, Ben's later acceptance of £200 forms a contract; if it was a counter offer, his "acceptance" is only a new offer that Ana may reject [1].

**5.** An invitation to tender is normally an invitation to treat (*Spencer v Harding* (1870)) [1]. But a promise to award the contract to a particular tender can be a binding unilateral offer, as with the promise to accept the highest bid in *Harvela Investments v Royal Trust Co of Canada* (1986) [1]. In *Blackpool and Fylde Aero Club v Blackpool BC* (1990) a body inviting tenders was bound at least to consider a conforming tender submitted on time [1]. Cara submitted the lowest tender before the deadline, so she accepted the unilateral offer by performance [1]. The college is in breach and Cara may claim damages for the lost contract [1].

**6.** Dan is a minor, so a contract binds him only in limited categories [1]. A contract for necessaries binds him to pay a reasonable price under s3 Sale of Goods Act 1979, but goods must suit his condition in life and his actual needs at the time [1]. As in *Nash v Inman* (1908), where a student already well supplied with clothing was not liable for waistcoats [1], Dan already has a laptop that meets his needs, so the gaming laptop is not a necessary and the contract is unenforceable against him [1]. Under s3 Minors' Contracts Act 1987 the court may, if just and equitable, order Dan to return the laptop [1].

**7.** Acceptance must normally be communicated, but the postal rule makes an acceptance complete when posted where post is a reasonable method (*Adams v Lindsell* (1818)) [1]. It applies even if the letter is lost (*Household Fire Insurance v Grant* (1879)) [1]. Finn made a written offer, so post is reasonable and nothing suggests he required actual notice, which would exclude the rule (*Holwell Securities v Hughes* (1974)) [1]. A contract was formed on posting, so Finn's sale of the car to another is a breach of contract with Eve [1].

**8.** In **social and domestic** agreements there is a presumption that the parties do not intend legal relations (*Balfour v Balfour* (1919)) [1]. It can be rebutted, for example where spouses have separated (*Merritt v Merritt* (1970)) [1] or where there is a shared financial arrangement (*Simpkins v Pays* (1955)) [1]. In **commercial** agreements there is a presumption that legal relations are intended (*Edwards v Skyways* (1964)) [1]. It can be rebutted by clear words such as an honour clause (*Rose and Frank v Crompton* (1925)) [1], and a letter of comfort may be a statement of present policy only (*Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining* (1989)) [1].

**9.** Performing a **public duty** is not consideration (*Collins v Godefroy* (1831)) [1] unless the claimant goes beyond the duty, as with the extra police protection in *Glasbrook Brothers v Glamorgan CC* (1925) [1]. Performing an existing **contractual duty owed to the promisor** is not consideration (*Stilk v Myrick* (1809)) [1] unless extra is done (*Hartley v Ponsonby* (1857)) or the promisor gains a practical benefit without duress (*Williams v Roffey Brothers* (1990)) [1]. Performing a contractual **duty owed to a third party** is good consideration (*Scotson v Pegg* (1861)) [1]. The practical benefit idea does not apply to part payment of a debt (*Re Selectmove* (1995)) [1].

**10.** **Requirements**: a clear promise by the creditor not to enforce strict legal rights [1]; reliance on the promise by the debtor [1]; and it must be inequitable for the creditor to go back on it (*Central London Property Trust v High Trees House* (1947)) [1]. **Limits**: it is a shield, not a sword, so it cannot found a claim (*Combe v Combe* (1951)) [1]; it is not available where the debtor has acted inequitably, as in *D & C Builders v Rees* (1966) [1]; and it often suspends rather than extinguishes rights, which can be revived on reasonable notice (*Tool Metal v Tungsten Electric* (1955)) [1].

**11.** The auctioneer's request for bids is an invitation to treat [1]. Each bid is an offer, which may be withdrawn before acceptance (*Payne v Cave* (1789)) [1]. Acceptance occurs on the fall of the hammer (Sale of Goods Act 1979 s57) [1]. In an auction "without reserve", the auctioneer is bound by a collateral contract to sell to the highest genuine bidder (*Barry v Davies* (2000)) [1].

**12.** **Protection of minors**: most contracts are not enforceable against minors, guarding against exploitation and inexperience [1]; even contracts of service bind only if beneficial overall, so oppressive terms fail (*De Francesco v Barnum* (1890)) [1], and a footballer's agency contract did not bind him in *Proform v Proactive* (2006) [1]. **Protection of adults**: minors must pay a reasonable price for necessaries [1]; voidable contracts with continuing obligations bind unless repudiated, and money paid after receiving a benefit cannot be recovered (*Steinberg v Scala* (1923)) [1]; s2 Minors' Contracts Act 1987 makes guarantors liable, and s3 allows return of property [1]. **Weaknesses**: an adult who sold non-necessary goods that have been consumed or spent has no remedy [1]; distinctions about what is "necessary" depend on the minor's condition in life, which is uncertain [1]; a 17-year-old in work may be over-protected, which is why lowering the age to 16, as in Scotland, has been suggested [1]. **Conclusion**: the balance broadly favours minors, which is defensible, but the uncertainty of the categories means reform could make the law clearer [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not deciding whether a reply is a counter offer or a request for information.
- Treating an invitation to tender as never binding.
- Forgetting the minor's actual needs when discussing necessaries.
- Listing promissory estoppel requirements without its limits.
