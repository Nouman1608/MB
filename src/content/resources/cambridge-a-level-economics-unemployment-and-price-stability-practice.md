## Questions

**1.** Define unemployment. **[2]**

**2.** Country B has 7.20 million people employed, 0.45 million unemployed and a working-age population of 10.2 million.

**(a)** Calculate the labour force. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the unemployment rate, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the participation rate. **[2]**

**3.** Region R has 9,200 people in work and 800 unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate its unemployment rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Next year employment is unchanged, but 400 of the unemployed stop looking for work. Calculate the new rate, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the fall in the rate overstates the improvement. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the type of unemployment in each case.

**(i)** A trainee accountant takes eight weeks to find a better-matched job. **[1]**
**(ii)** A shipyard closes, and its welders cannot do the digital work on offer in distant cities. **[1]**
**(iii)** Falling consumer spending leads firms across the economy to cut staff. **[1]**
**(iv)** Fruit pickers lose work when the harvest ends. **[1]**
**(v)** A warehouse installs robotic pickers. **[1]**

**5.**

**(a)** Explain why structural unemployment tends to last longer than frictional unemployment. **[3]**
**(b)** Using the AD/AS model, explain how a fall in aggregate demand causes cyclical unemployment. **[3]**

**6.** Country C has 0.6 million unemployed. Each would produce 25,000 cu a year if employed. The government pays each 5,000 cu in benefits and loses 3,500 cu in tax from each.

**(a)** Calculate the output lost per year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the annual cost to the government budget. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one consequence for the unemployed themselves, other than lower income. **[3]**

**7.**

**(a)** Define inflation, deflation and disinflation. **[3]**
**(b)** A consumer price index is 100 in year 1, 104 in year 2, 106.1 in year 3 and 105.0 in year 4. Calculate the inflation rate for years 2, 3 and 4, to one decimal place, and say which year shows disinflation and which deflation. **[5]**

**8.** A CPI has four categories with weights of 40, 25, 20 and 15. In year 0 every index is 100. In year 1 the indices are 104, 110, 95 and 102 in that order.

**(a)** Calculate the CPI for year 1. **[3]**
**(b)** State the inflation rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one reason why the CPI may not reflect the price changes a pensioner faces. **[1]**

**9.** A worker earns 1,800 cu a month in year 0, when the CPI is 100, and 1,872 cu in year 1, when the CPI is 106.

**(a)** Calculate the real wage in year 1 at year 0 prices. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the real wage, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** State what this shows about the worker's living standards. **[1]**

**10.** In Economy X the price level rises from 100 to 106 and real output from 600 to 620. In Economy Y the price level rises from 100 to 106 and real output falls from 600 to 575.

**(a)** Identify which economy is experiencing demand-pull and which cost-push inflation, with a reason for each. **[4]**
**(b)** Give one possible cause of each. **[2]**

**11.** A pensioner receives a fixed pension of 1,500 cu a month. Prices rise by 8%.

**(a)** Calculate the real value of the pension after the rise, to the nearest cu. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether inflation harms all households and firms equally. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Unemployment is where people are able and willing to work at the going wage [1] and actively seek a job but do not have one [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "people without jobs" omits the conditions, so state that they are willing, able and looking.

**2. (a)** 7.20 + 0.45 = **7.65 million** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 0.45 ÷ 7.65 × 100 [1] = **5.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 7.65 ÷ 10.2 × 100 [1] = **75.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The base for the rate is the labour force, not the population, and showing that base is where the working earns credit.

**3. (a)** Labour force = 10,000 [1]; rate = 800 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **8.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Unemployed = 400 and labour force = 9,200 + 400 = 9,600 [1]; rate = 400 ÷ 9,600 × 100 = **4.2%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The 400 still have no job [1], but they have left the labour force, so they are no longer counted as unemployed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Remember to shrink the labour force as well as the unemployed total; changing only the numerator is a common slip.

**4. (i)** **Frictional** [1]. **(ii)** **Structural** [1]. **(iii)** **Cyclical** [1]. **(iv)** **Seasonal** [1]. **(v)** **Technological** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* One-word answers are enough here, but check the cause in the scenario: a skills mismatch points to structural, a fall in demand to cyclical.

**5. (a)** Frictional unemployment is a delay in matching workers to vacancies [1] that better information shortens quickly [1]. Structural unemployment needs retraining or a move to another area [1], which takes longer and costs more. **[3]**
**(b)** Aggregate demand shifts left along SRAS [1], so equilibrium real output falls [1] and firms need fewer workers, so employment falls and unemployment rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An AD/AS answer gains from naming the axes and the direction of each shift, and from finishing on employment.

**6. (a)** 0.6m × 25,000 [1] = **15,000 million cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Benefits = 0.6m × 5,000 = 3,000 million [1]; tax forgone = 0.6m × 3,500 = 2,100 million [1]; total = **5,100 million cu** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Skills become outdated while out of work [1], so employers prefer other applicants [1], which lengthens the spell and lowers later earnings [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs both benefits and lost tax, and the lost output belongs in part (a) only, not in the budget cost.

**7. (a)** Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level [1]. Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level [1]. Disinflation: a fall in the rate of inflation, with prices still rising [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Year 2: (104 − 100) ÷ 100 = **4.0%** [1]. Year 3: (106.1 − 104) ÷ 104 = **2.0%** [1]. Year 4: (105.0 − 106.1) ÷ 106.1 = **−1.0%** [1]. Year 3 is disinflation, because inflation slowed while prices still rose [1]. Year 4 is deflation, because the price level fell [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign in year 4, and give the reason for each label as well as the label.

**8. (a)** Weighted products: 40×104 = 4,160; 25×110 = 2,750; 20×95 = 1,900; 15×102 = 1,530 [1]. Sum = 10,340 [1]. Divide by 100: **103.4** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** (103.4 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = **3.4%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The weights reflect an average household [1], whereas a pensioner may spend more on heating or health, so their basket differs. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason must be specific: say how the pensioner's spending differs from the weights, not just that people differ.

**9. (a)** Real wage = 1,872 ÷ 106 × 100 [1] = **1,766 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (1,766.04 − 1,800) ÷ 1,800 × 100 [1] = **−1.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The worker's purchasing power fell, although the money wage rose 4% [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the index, not multiply, and finish by interpreting the figure, not just stating it.

**10. (a)** Economy X is demand-pull [1] because output rises with prices, as when AD shifts right [1]. Economy Y is cost-push [1] because output falls as prices rise, as when SRAS shifts left [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Demand-pull: a cut in income tax raises consumer spending [1]. Cost-push: a rise in energy costs or in wages above productivity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The change in output is the evidence that separates the two, so quote the figures.

**11. (a)** 1,500 ÷ 1.08 [1] = **1,389 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The pensioner's purchasing power falls about 7.4% [1], so fixed incomes lose. Savers lose if interest is below inflation [1], while borrowers with fixed repayments gain [1]. Workers whose pay rises with prices are protected [1], but those with weaker bargaining power are not [1]. Firms face menu costs [1] and uncertainty that may delay investment [1]. The harm depends on the rate and on whether inflation is anticipated [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names who loses and what the outcome depends on tends to gain more credit than a list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the population, not the labour force, as the base for the unemployment rate.
- Forgetting that discouraged workers leave the labour force, so the rate can fall without new jobs.
- Naming a type of unemployment without linking it to its cause.
- Writing the inflation formula with the new CPI as the denominator.
- Calling disinflation a fall in prices.
- Multiplying by the index instead of dividing to get a real value.
- Describing cost-push as a rise in demand, or leaving out the change in output.
- Listing consequences of inflation without saying who gains or loses.
