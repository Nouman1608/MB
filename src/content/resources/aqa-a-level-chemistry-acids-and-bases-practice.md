These questions cover section **3.1.12 Acids and bases** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification
(7404/7405), version 1.2, July 2026, for exams from June 2016 onwards: 3.1.12.1 to 3.1.12.6 and
Required practical 9. All of it is **A-level only** and is placed on A-level Paper 1; Paper 3 can test any
content.

Learn the ideas first in the [acids and bases study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/)
and the [acids and bases revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases-revision-notes/).
The course hub is [AQA A-Level Chemistry](/boards/aqa/a-level/chemistry/), and the
[printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/chemistry/) lists every specification point. Use
Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K unless a question says otherwise. Give pH values to 2 decimal places.

## Questions

**1.** Ammonia reacts with water: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. Identify the species acting as a Brønsted–Lowry
acid in the forward reaction and in the reverse reaction. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate:

**(a)** the pH of a solution with [H⁺] = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ **[1]**
**(b)** [H⁺] in a solution of pH 9.72 **[1]**
**(c)** the pH of 0.0063 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. **[1]**

**3.** This question is about the ionic product of water.

**(a)** Write an expression for Kw. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of 0.0350 mol dm⁻³ KOH at 298 K. **[2]**
**(c)** At a temperature above 298 K, Kw = 3.80 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water at this
temperature and state whether the water is acidic, neutral or alkaline. **[2]**
**(d)** Use the data in (c) to deduce whether the dissociation of water is exothermic or endothermic. **[1]**

**4.** HA is a weak monoprotic acid with Ka = 6.30 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

**(a)** Write an expression for Ka. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of a 0.250 mol dm⁻³ solution of HA. **[2]**
**(c)** State one assumption you made in (b). **[1]**

**5.** A 0.0450 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid HB has pH 3.62. Calculate Ka, with units, and pKa for HB. **[4]**

**6.** Acid X has pKa = 3.86. Acid Y has Ka = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Calculate Ka for X and pKa for Y, and
state which is the stronger acid. **[3]**

**7.** A student titrates 25.0 cm³ of a weak monoprotic acid with 0.0850 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The titres are
22.60 cm³ (rough), 22.15 cm³, 22.10 cm³ and 22.20 cm³.

**(a)** Calculate the mean titre. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the concentration of the acid. **[3]**

**8.** Three indicators change colour over these ranges: methyl orange pH 3.1–4.4; bromothymol blue pH
6.0–7.6; phenolphthalein pH 8.3–10.0.

**(a)** Choose an indicator for titrating a weak acid with a strong base, and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(b)** Choose an indicator for titrating a strong acid with a weak base, and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why none is suitable for a weak acid–weak base titration. **[2]**

**9.** In Required practical 9, a student adds 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH to 25.0 cm³ of a weak acid HX and
plots a pH curve. The equivalence point is at 24.0 cm³. The pH at 12.0 cm³ is 4.20.

**(a)** Describe how the student should collect the data for the pH curve. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate Ka for HX. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the pH at 12.0 cm³ is equal to pKa. **[2]**

**10.** 30.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 0.180 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 298 K. Calculate
the pH of the mixture. **[5]**

**11.** This question is about buffers.

**(a)** A buffer contains ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate. Explain how it resists a fall in pH when a
small amount of acid is added. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the same buffer resists a rise in pH when a small amount of alkali is added. **[2]**
**(c)** A basic buffer contains ammonia and ammonium chloride. Write an equation for the reaction that
removes added H⁺ and one for the reaction that removes added OH⁻. **[2]**

**12.** A weak acid HA has Ka = 1.50 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. 15.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is added to 40.0 cm³
of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ HA.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of the buffer formed. **[4]**
**(b)** 1.00 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl is added to the buffer. Calculate the new pH. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the pH if the same HCl were added to 55.0 cm³ of water instead, and comment on the
difference. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Forward: **H₂O** donates a proton to NH₃ [1]. Reverse: **NH₄⁺** donates a proton to OH⁻ [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each direction is a separate mark, so naming only water earns half the credit.

**2. (a)** pH = −log₁₀(4.5 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.35** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = 10^(−9.72) = **1.91 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** HCl is fully dissociated, so [H⁺] = 0.0063; pH = **2.20** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A pH given as 2.2 rather than 2.20 can lose the mark when 2 decimal places are asked for.

**3. (a)** Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0350 = 2.86 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **12.54** [1]
**(c)** [H⁺] = √(3.80 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.95 × 10⁻⁷; pH = **6.71** [1]. **Neutral**, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻] [1]
**(d)** Kw increases with temperature, so the equilibrium shifts right on heating: **endothermic** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Neutral" without the reason [H⁺] = [OH⁻] is unlikely to score the second mark in (c).

**4. (a)** Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA] [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = √(6.30 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.250) = 3.97 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **2.40** [1]
**(c)** Any one: [H⁺] = [A⁻]; or [HA] at equilibrium = 0.250 mol dm⁻³ because dissociation is slight [1]
*Examiner insight:* An expression with [H₂O] on the bottom, or with square brackets missing, loses (a).

**5.** [H⁺] = 10^(−3.62) = 2.40 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]
Ka = [H⁺]² / [HB] = (2.40 × 10⁻⁴)² / 0.0450 [1]
Ka = **1.28 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³** [1]
pKa = −log₁₀(1.28 × 10⁻⁶) = **5.89** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write [H⁺] down before squaring it; if it is wrong, the Ka and pKa steps here can still earn method credit.

**6.** Ka(X) = 10^(−3.86) = 1.38 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pKa(Y) = −log₁₀(2.4 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.62 [1]
X has the larger Ka (smaller pKa), so **X is stronger** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The comparison mark needs a reason tied to Ka or pKa, not just the name.

**7. (a)** Mean of concordant titres = (22.15 + 22.10 + 22.20) / 3 = **22.15 cm³** [1]
**(b)** n(NaOH) = 0.0850 × 22.15/1000 = 1.88 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
n(acid) = 1.88 × 10⁻³ mol (1 : 1) [1]
concentration = 1.88 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = **0.0753 mol dm⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Including the rough titre in the mean costs the mark in (a), though later marks can follow through.

**8. (a)** Phenolphthalein [1]; its range lies within the steep section, which is in the alkaline region
(equivalence above pH 7) [1]
**(b)** Methyl orange [1]; its range lies within the steep section, which is in the acidic region
(equivalence below pH 7) [1]
**(c)** The curve has no steep (vertical) section [1], so any indicator changes colour gradually over a
large volume and no sharp end point is seen [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Changes colour at the right pH" is too vague; link the range to the steep section.

**9. (a)** Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH [1]. Add NaOH from a burette in small
portions, swirling and recording the pH after each addition [1]. Use smaller additions near the end point,
then plot pH against volume [1]
**(b)** At 12.0 cm³ (half the equivalence volume), pH = pKa = 4.20 [1]
Ka = 10^(−4.20) = **6.31 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** Half of HX has been converted to X⁻, so [HX] = [X⁻] [1]. These cancel in
Ka = [H⁺][X⁻]/[HX], so Ka = [H⁺] and pKa = pH [1]
*Examiner insight:* Writing "pH = pKa" in (c) without saying [HX] = [X⁻] restates the answer and gains little.

**10.** n(OH⁻) = 0.200 × 0.0300 = 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol; n(H⁺) = 0.180 × 0.0250 = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
Excess OH⁻ = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
[OH⁻] = 1.50 × 10⁻³ / 0.0550 = 0.0273 mol dm⁻³ [1]
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0273 = 3.67 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **12.44** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by 30.0 cm³ instead of the total 55.0 cm³ loses the third mark; the rest can follow through.

**11. (a)** The added H⁺ reacts with ethanoate ions: H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃COOH [1]. There is a large amount
of CH₃COO⁻, so the ratio [CH₃COOH]/[CH₃COO⁻] changes little and pH stays nearly constant [1]
**(b)** The added OH⁻ reacts with ethanoic acid: OH⁻ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O [1]. There is a large amount
of CH₃COOH, so the ratio changes little [1]
**(c)** NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ [1]; NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O [1]
*Examiner insight:* Saying the buffer "neutralises" the acid without naming the species that reacts earns no credit.

**12. (a)** n(HA) start = 0.250 × 0.0400 = 0.0100 mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.200 × 0.0150 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
HA left = 7.00 × 10⁻³ mol; A⁻ = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
[H⁺] = 1.50 × 10⁻⁵ × 7.00/3.00 = 3.50 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **4.46** [1]
**(b)** n(H⁺) added = 0.500 × 0.00100 = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol; HA = 7.50 × 10⁻³ mol, A⁻ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
[H⁺] = 1.50 × 10⁻⁵ × 7.50/2.50 = 4.50 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **4.35** [1]
**(c)** Total volume 56.0 cm³: [H⁺] = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.0560 = 8.93 × 10⁻³; pH = **2.05** [1]. The buffer pH falls by only 0.11,
but in water the pH falls to about 2 [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), adding H⁺ to [H⁺] instead of changing the moles of HA and A⁻ scores only the first mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a weak acid as fully dissociated, so [H⁺] = [HA].
- Using √(Ka[HA]) for a buffer, where [H⁺] ≠ [A⁻].
- Dividing excess moles by one solution's volume instead of the total.
- Forgetting Kw when OH⁻ is in excess.
- Omitting units for Ka, or giving Kw in mol dm⁻³.
- Including the rough titre in the mean.
- Choosing an indicator without linking its range to the steep section.
- Saying hot pure water is acidic.
- Writing buffer explanations without equations or without naming the reacting species.
