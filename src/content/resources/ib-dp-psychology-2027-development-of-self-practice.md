> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the development of self strand of the human development context (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [development of self study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-development-of-self/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-development-of-self-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain the role of attachment in the development of self. **[4]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by enculturation, and give one example related to the self. **[4]**

**3.** Outline one way in which peers can influence the development of self. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one way in which childhood experiences can affect the development of self. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Imran, 14, grew up in a family where decisions are made together and children are expected to put family needs first. At his new school, teachers praise students who "speak up and stand out". His classmates often tease him for staying quiet. Imran says, "I don't know who I am here."

**(a)** Use enculturation to explain Imran's experience. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how peer influence might change Imran's self-concept. **[3]**

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Two researchers observe 18-month-olds in a laboratory. A caregiver leaves the room for three minutes and returns. Anna cries on separation, goes to her caregiver on return, is comforted and returns to play. Ben shows little distress and ignores his caregiver on return.

**(a)** Identify the attachment type of each child. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one way this early pattern might relate to the development of self. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one limitation of drawing conclusions about attachment from this procedure. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one limitation of using retrospective reports to study the effect of childhood experiences on the self. **[3]**

**8.** Evaluate the role of attachment in the development of self. **[6]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate research on the development of self with regard to the role of childhood experiences. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Attachment is a strong emotional bond between infant and caregiver **[1]**. Early caregiving forms an internal working model, expectations about whether others are reliable and whether the self is worth care **[1]**. Secure attachment supports a positive self-concept and self-esteem, and confidence to explore **[1]**; insecure attachment may lead to expectations of rejection or low self-worth **[1]**. Credit evidence such as the Strange Situation or longitudinal research.

**2.** Enculturation is the process of learning the norms, values and practices of one's own culture, mainly in childhood **[1]**, through family, school, language and media **[1]**. Example linked to the self, such as a child in a collectivist culture learning to define themselves by family roles and cooperation **[1]**, with an explanation of how this shapes self-concept (for instance, interdependent self-construal) **[1]**.

**3.** Any one way. For example social comparison: adolescents judge their abilities or appearance against friends **[1]**, so positive feedback may raise self-esteem and negative feedback may lower it **[1]**. Link to the self-concept changing over time **[1]**. Equally creditable: conformity to group norms, social learning from peers, looking-glass effects.

**4.** Any one experience, for example childhood adversity. Adverse experiences such as neglect or abuse are linked to later emotional and health difficulties **[1]**; they may lead to negative beliefs about the self and others **[1]**. Evidence such as Felitti et al. (1998) found more adverse experiences linked to poorer outcomes **[1]**. A qualification, such as that supportive adults or resilience reduce the effect, or that the evidence is retrospective **[1]**.

**5.** (a) Imran has been enculturated into norms of cooperation and putting the family first **[1]**, which shape an interdependent self-concept **[1]**. His new school values independence and speaking up, so he faces conflicting norms and feels uncertain about his identity **[1]**.

(b) Any one process, up to **[3]**: normative influence, in which classmates' teasing pressures him to conform to the group's style of behaviour **[1]**; social comparison, in which he sees himself as different and may judge himself negatively **[1]**; links to the changing self-concept, such as speaking up more or hiding his background **[1]**. Credit an answer that notes supportive peers could strengthen his self-esteem.

**6.** (a) Anna: secure attachment **[1]**. Ben: insecure-avoidant **[1]**.

(b) Anna's responses suggest a working model of caregivers as reliable and of herself as worthy of comfort **[1]**, which may support self-esteem and confidence **[1]**; Ben's pattern may relate to expecting a lack of response, and to a more self-reliant but guarded self-concept **[1]**. Award up to **[3]** for a developed link.

(c) Any one limitation, up to **[3]**. The procedure is brief and artificial, so behaviour may not reflect everyday attachment **[1]**. Classification may reflect temperament or cultural parenting norms, not attachment quality **[1]**. One observation at 18 months cannot show how the child will develop **[1]**.

**7.** Retrospective reports rely on recalling the past **[1]**, so memory may be inaccurate or influenced by current mood **[1]**, which could exaggerate or underestimate the effect of childhood experiences **[1]**.

**8.** Description (up to 2): attachment as a bond, internal working model, types of attachment **[1]**; link to self-concept and self-esteem **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4): longitudinal evidence supports links with later social competence **[1]**; the Strange Situation is artificial and culturally biased **[1]**; temperament and later experience also matter, so the claim of determinism is too strong **[1]**; evidence is correlational so cause is uncertain **[1]**; animal research (Harlow) raises ethical issues and generalisation questions **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Childhood adversity is linked to poorer outcomes, with large surveys, but data are retrospective and correlational **[2]**.
- Early deprivation studies show lasting effects but also recovery, which shows variation and resilience **[2]**.
- Attachment and the internal working model link early care to self-concept, but classification is limited by culture and procedure **[2]**.
- Self-control research shows how early behaviour is linked to later outcomes, but later replication with controls found a weaker link, illustrating confounds **[2]**.
- Causality: confounding variables such as income, genes and temperament make causal claims hard **[2]**.
- Change: protective factors and later relationships mean the self can change, which limits determinism **[2]**.
- Measurement and bias: self-report, retrospective data and Western samples limit validity and generalisation **[2]**.
- Responsibility: ethics of studying distress, avoiding stigma and blaming parents **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the importance of childhood experiences, for example that they matter but interact with culture, peers and later experience **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** describing attachment types without linking them to the self.
- **Question 5:** writing about acculturation instead of enculturation.
- **Question 6(c):** saying "it is artificial" without explaining the effect on the conclusion.
- **Question 9:** describing studies without discussing confounds or using only one concept.
