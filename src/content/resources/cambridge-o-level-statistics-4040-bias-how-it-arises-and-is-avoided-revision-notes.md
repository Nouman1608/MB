## The core idea in three lines

1. Bias is a **systematic** error: the method pushes results the same way every time.
2. Sampling variation is a **chance** error: random samples differ, scattering both sides of the truth.
3. A bigger sample shrinks sampling variation but leaves bias exactly where it was.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bias | A tendency of a method to give results that are consistently too high or too low |
| Sampling frame | The list of the population from which the sample is drawn |
| Selection bias | Bias from the way members are chosen, so some groups are over- or under-represented |
| Self-selection | People decide for themselves whether to take part, as in phone-in or online polls |
| Non-response bias | Bias when those who do not reply differ from those who do |
| Response bias | Answers are pushed away from the truth by the question or the situation |
| Leading question | A question worded to suggest the answer wanted |
| Interviewer bias | The interviewer's manner, presence or recording affects the data |
| Measurement bias | An instrument or recording rule that errs in one direction |
| Pilot survey | A small trial of the survey used to find faults in questions before the main survey |
| Representative sample | A sample whose make-up reflects the population on the features that matter |

## Four places bias gets in

| Stage | Typical cause | Typical cure |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing the sample | Frame missing groups; investigator picks; volunteers only; periodic list | Complete current frame; random numbers; random start; stratify in proportion |
| Getting replies | Busy or uninterested people do not respond | Reminders; call-backs at other times; short form; report response rate |
| Asking | Leading, loaded or sensitive questions; unbalanced options | Neutral wording; balanced options; anonymity; pilot survey |
| Recording | Interviewer influence; uncalibrated instrument; rounding one way | Trained interviewers or self-completion; calibrate; record to stated accuracy |

## How to write a full answer

Use the chain **cause, reason, effect, cure**.

- **Cause:** quote the feature of the method ("only members of the gym are asked").
- **Reason:** say how that group differs from the population ("gym members probably exercise more than adults in general").
- **Effect:** give the direction ("so the mean hours of exercise will be too high").
- **Cure:** name a specific method ("choose a simple random sample from a list of all adults in the area").

A cause with no effect, or a cure with no method, usually earns only part of the credit.

## Short worked reminders

**Systematic sample from a periodic list.** A factory tests every 6th item from a production line with 6 machines feeding it in turn. With a fixed start, every item tested comes from the same machine. Cure: random start and an interval that does not match the machine cycle, or a stratified sample of each machine's output.

**Self-selected poll.** A website asks visitors to vote on whether a new road should be built. Only visitors who feel strongly and happen to use that website take part. The result cannot be generalised to residents. Cure: random sample of residents from a full list.

**Sensitive question.** "Have you ever parked illegally?" asked aloud by a traffic warden gets fewer honest yes answers. The proportion admitting it will be too low. Cure: anonymous self-completed form returned in a sealed box.

**Measurement.** A stopwatch started only when the timer hears the whistle, a moment late, gives times that are all slightly too short. Cure: electronic timing, or the same timer starting on a visible signal, checked against a reference.

## Exam traps

- "The sample is too small" is about precision, not bias. Do not offer it as a source of bias unless the question is about something else.
- "Ask more people" is never a cure for bias.
- A random sample from an incomplete frame is still biased: randomness cannot reach people who are not on the list.
- Stratified sampling reduces bias only if the strata are sampled **in proportion** and members are chosen **at random** within each stratum.
- Quota sampling looks representative but is not random; the interviewer chooses within each quota.
- A pilot survey tests questions; it does not repair a poor sampling frame.
- A high response rate does not prove the sample is unbiased if the frame itself was incomplete.
- Name the direction only when you can justify it. If the direction is unclear, say the results may not represent the population and explain which group is missing.

## Quick self-test

1. Define bias in one sentence.
2. Why does increasing the sample size not remove bias?
3. A pupil surveys the first 30 pupils to arrive at school about how they travel. Give one cause of bias.
4. In question 3, which way might the proportion of pupils who come by car be pushed? Explain.
5. Name the type of bias when the people chosen for a postal survey do not return the form.
6. Rewrite "Do you agree that the fantastic new library deserves more funding?" neutrally.
7. Why is an online poll on a news website likely to be biased?
8. Give one way to reduce non-response in a postal survey.
9. A list of houses alternates between houses with gardens and flats. Why could a systematic sample with an even interval be biased?
10. What is a pilot survey, and which kind of bias does it help to avoid?
11. A bathroom scale reads 1.5 kg when nothing is on it. What effect does this have on the mean mass of a sample of people?
12. State one way to reduce bias when a question asks about a sensitive topic.

### Answers

1. Bias is a systematic tendency of a data collection method to give results that are too high or too low.
2. A larger sample reduces chance (sampling) variation only; the systematic cause, such as a missing group, is still there however many are asked.
3. Early arrivals are not typical: for example, pupils dropped off by parents on the way to work, or those living nearest, may arrive first.
4. It could be pushed up, if early arrivals are mostly dropped off by car on a parent's way to work. Any direction given needs a reason.
5. Non-response bias.
6. "How should funding for the library change? Increase / Stay the same / Decrease."
7. Respondents select themselves, so only people who visit that site and feel strongly about the issue reply.
8. Any one: send reminders; include a prepaid return envelope; keep the form short; follow up by phone.
9. With an even interval and a fixed start, the sample may contain only houses with gardens or only flats.
10. A small trial run of the survey on a few people; it helps avoid response bias from unclear or leading questions.
11. Every reading is 1.5 kg too high, so the mean is 1.5 kg too high.
12. Any one: make the form anonymous; use a sealed collection box; avoid a face-to-face interviewer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is bias the same as a mistake in the calculation?**
No. Bias comes from the way data are collected, so it is present before any calculation starts and cannot be removed by careful arithmetic.

**Can a census be biased?**
Yes. A census has no sampling variation, but leading questions, non-response or a faulty instrument can still push results one way.
