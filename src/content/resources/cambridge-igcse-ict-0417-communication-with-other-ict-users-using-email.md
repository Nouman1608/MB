Section 10.1 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 asks you to know the "characteristics, uses and constraints of email communication including acceptable language, guidelines set by an employer, the need for security, netiquette, email groups, carbon copy (cc), blind carbon copy (bcc), forward, attachments", and the "characteristics and effects of spam email including the methods which can be used to help prevent spam". The [Communication study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-communication-study-guide/) introduces the parts of an email. This guide goes further into each constraint, into when each feature should and should not be used, and into spam.

For quick recall use the [email revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-communication-with-other-ict-users-using-email-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [email practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-communication-with-other-ict-users-using-email-practice/).

## Characteristics and uses of email

An email is a message sent electronically from one account to one or more other accounts. Its characteristics explain why it is used so widely:

- It is **asynchronous**: the recipient does not need to be online when it is sent and can read it later.
- It is **fast**: a message usually arrives within seconds, anywhere in the world.
- It leaves a **written record** that can be stored in folders, searched and used as evidence of what was agreed.
- One message can go to **many recipients** at once.
- **Files** can be attached.
- It is **cheap**: there is no cost per message beyond the internet connection.

Typical uses include businesses sending quotes, invoices and confirmations to customers; schools sending newsletters and reports to parents; organisations sending meeting invitations and documents to staff; and individuals keeping in touch with family abroad.

## Constraints on using email

A constraint is a rule or limit on how something may be used. The syllabus names four.

### Acceptable language

Emails must not contain offensive, abusive, threatening or discriminatory language, and many countries have laws that make sending such messages an offence. Because an email is a written record, an offensive message can be forwarded to others and used as evidence long after it was sent. Formal emails, such as a job application, also need formal language: no slang, text-speak or emojis.

### Guidelines set by an employer

Most organisations have an email or acceptable use policy that staff must follow. It typically:

- limits or bans personal use of work email;
- forbids sending confidential company information to people outside the organisation;
- sets rules for tone, such as a formal greeting and an approved signature;
- may add a legal disclaimer automatically to every message;
- states that emails sent and received on work accounts may be **monitored**;
- explains what disciplinary action follows misuse.

The reason for these rules is that a member of staff's email represents the organisation. A careless or offensive email could damage its reputation or lead to legal action against it.

### The need for security

Email accounts hold personal and business information, so they must be protected:

- use a **strong password** and change it if it may have been seen;
- use **two-factor authentication** where it is offered;
- **log out** on shared or public computers;
- do not open **attachments** or click **links** in unexpected emails, as they may contain malware or lead to a phishing site;
- check that the **sender's address** really belongs to the organisation it claims to come from;
- **encrypt** emails that contain sensitive data, so they cannot be read if intercepted.

### Netiquette

Netiquette is the set of good-manners rules for communicating online. Beyond the familiar rules (no capitals, a clear subject line, polite language), remember: reply within a reasonable time; keep to one topic per email so it is easy to file and search; quote only the relevant part of a previous message; and do not forward someone's private message without their permission.

## Features: when to use them and when not to

### Email groups

An **email group** (or distribution list) is a single name in the address book that stands for many addresses, such as "Year 10 parents" or "Sales team". Benefits: one name is typed instead of many addresses, and no one in the group is accidentally missed. Risks: the group must be kept up to date as people join and leave, and choosing the wrong group sends the email to the wrong people.

### To, cc and bcc

| Field | Who goes here | Can other recipients see this address? |
|---|---|---|
| To | People who need to read and act on the message | Yes |
| cc | People who need to be kept informed but need not act | Yes |
| bcc | People whose addresses should be hidden from the others | No |

A useful test: if a recipient would be unhappy that the other recipients can see their address, use bcc. If the main recipient should know that someone else has been told, use cc.

### Forward

**Forward** sends a received email, including its attachments, to a new recipient. **Reply** goes only to the sender and does not include the original attachments; **reply all** goes to the sender and everyone in the To and cc fields. Before forwarding, check that the content may be shared and remove earlier addresses that the new recipient does not need to see.

### Attachments

Attachments let you send documents, images and other files. Constraints to know:

- Email systems set a **maximum attachment size**, so a large file may be rejected. The fix is to compress it into a .zip file or share a link to a cloud copy.
- Large attachments take longer to send and download, and fill the recipient's storage.
- Attachments are a common way of spreading **malware**, so many systems block executable files.
- The recipient needs suitable software to open the file, so a **generic format** such as PDF is often a safer choice.

## Spam

### Characteristics

**Spam** is unsolicited (unrequested) email sent in bulk to large numbers of addresses. It is usually sent automatically, often from false addresses, and typically advertises products, offers fake prizes or tries to trick the reader into giving personal details.

### Effects

- Fills inboxes, so important emails may be missed.
- Wastes time deleting it.
- Uses storage space and network bandwidth.
- May contain phishing links or attachments carrying malware.
- Replying, or clicking a link, can confirm that the address is active, which leads to even more spam.

### Prevention

- Use a **spam filter**, which moves suspected spam to a junk folder.
- **Block** addresses that send spam and mark messages as spam so the filter learns.
- Do not reply to spam or click its links, including "unsubscribe" links in suspicious emails.
- Do not publish your email address on public websites or forums.
- Untick boxes that let organisations share your address with third parties.
- Use a separate address for online sign-ups.

A spam filter can occasionally move a genuine email to the junk folder, so the folder should be checked from time to time.

## Worked example: improving a poorly written email

*An employee sends this to 60 customers, all in the To field:*

> Subject: (blank)
>
> HI ALL, our new prices r attached, plz read asap!!! The file is 25 MB.

*Problems and fixes:*

1. **Blank subject**: add one, such as "New price list from 1 March".
2. **Capitals and text-speak**: unprofessional and seen as shouting; write in sentence case with full words.
3. **All 60 customers in To**: every customer sees every other address, a privacy breach and a risk of spam. Put the customers in **bcc**, or use a mailing group in bcc.
4. **25 MB attachment**: may exceed size limits; save the price list as a PDF, compress it, or send a link.
5. **No signature**: add the employee's name, role and the company's contact details, as the employer's guidelines would require.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Describe two effects of spam on a company.* **[4]**

> Spam fills employees' inboxes (1), so important emails from customers may be missed or take longer to find (1). Spam can also contain links to phishing websites or attachments carrying malware (1), which could infect the company's computers or lead to staff giving away passwords (1).

*Why it scores:* each effect is named and then developed with its consequence for the company, which is what **describe** needs.

## Common mistakes

- Saying bcc recipients cannot see the message. They receive it; it is their address that is hidden.
- Saying spam is a virus. It is unwanted bulk email that may carry malware.
- Confusing forward with reply all.
- Writing "be polite" as the only netiquette point when two different rules are needed.
- Claiming email is completely secure. It can be intercepted unless encrypted.

## Exam technique

**State** needs a short answer, such as "a spam filter". **Describe** needs the feature and what it does. **Explain** needs a reason: "use bcc **because** it hides the customers' addresses from each other". In scenario questions, use the people and numbers given rather than general statements.

## Check your understanding

1. Why is email described as asynchronous? *The recipient does not need to be online when it is sent.*
2. Give two items an employer's email policy might contain. *Limits on personal use; monitoring; no confidential data sent outside.*
3. What is the difference between reply all and forward? *Reply all goes to existing recipients; forward sends the message and attachments to someone new.*
4. Why is clicking "unsubscribe" in a spam email risky? *It confirms the address is active and the link may be malicious.*
