- **the importance of capacity**, including how to utilise capacity efficiently

- **how to choose the optimal mix of resources**, including labour-intensive and capital-intensive processes

## Capacity

**Capacity** is the maximum output a business can produce in a period. Its importance comes from the link to costs and to customer service. Too little capacity means lost sales, late delivery and a poor reputation. Too much means idle resources and high unit costs because fixed costs are spread over few units.

### Utilising capacity efficiently

| Problem | Possible responses |
|---|---|
| **Under-utilisation** (spare capacity) | Find new customers or markets; run promotions in quiet periods; take on subcontract work for other firms; hire out or share space or machines; rationalise (close a line or site) if the fall in demand is permanent; develop products with different seasonal peaks |
| **Over-utilisation** (demand above capacity) | Overtime and extra shifts; temporary or part-time staff; outsource some production; better scheduling to cut idle time; invest in more capacity if demand is lasting |
| **Uneven demand** | Flexible contracts and multi-skilled staff; build inventory in quiet times; pricing to move demand to off-peak; produce to order |

Efficient use also needs good scheduling, maintenance that prevents breakdowns, and accurate forecasts of demand. Rationalising is cost-saving but can mean redundancies, loss of skills and bad publicity.

## Efficiency and labour productivity

**Efficiency** means using resources with as little waste as possible to produce a given output. A business is more efficient when it produces the same output with fewer inputs, or more output with the same inputs. **Labour productivity** (output per worker) is a key measure, though a business can be efficient in other ways too, such as using less energy or less material.

### Why it matters

Higher efficiency and productivity reduce unit costs, which supports lower prices or higher profit. They improve competitiveness and allow higher wages to be paid without raising unit labour costs. They can also help the environment when less material and energy are wasted.

### How to increase them

- **Training and development** so workers use better methods.
- **Motivation**: better pay, recognition, team working and job enrichment can raise effort (see the HR section).
- **Better equipment and technology**, including automation.
- **Better organisation**: improved layout, work scheduling, and removal of bottlenecks.
- **Cutting waste**: lean methods, better maintenance, recycling.
- **Better management** and communication, setting clear targets and measuring progress.

### Difficulties

- **Cost**: training and new equipment need investment before benefits appear.
- **Resistance to change**: workers may fear job losses or extra pressure.
- **Time lag**: improvements may take months to show.
- **Quality risk**: pushing for speed can increase errors.
- **Limits of the process**: in some services (such as teaching or care) output cannot be raised greatly without harming quality.
- **Labour market**: difficulty recruiting skilled workers.
- **Redundancy costs** if higher productivity means fewer staff are needed.

## Lean production

**Lean production** aims to produce with the minimum of waste: no unnecessary inventory, time, movement, defects or space. It is associated with continuous improvement (**kaizen**), where all workers suggest small improvements; efficient layouts; quality built in at each stage; and close links with suppliers.

### Just in Time and Just in Case

| | Just in Time (JIT) | Just in Case (JIC) |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Materials arrive only when needed for production; finished goods are made to order | Hold buffer inventory of materials and goods in case of problems or demand surges |
| Benefits | Lower inventory holding costs; less storage space; less risk of stock becoming obsolete; money released for other uses; problems exposed early | Protects against supply delays, strikes or demand surges; orders can be filled at once; bulk buying discounts |
| Difficulties | Relies on reliable suppliers and accurate demand forecasts; any delay stops production; may cost more from small, frequent deliveries; loses bulk discounts | Costs of storing, insuring and financing stock; risk of damage, theft, waste or obsolescence; ties up cash |

*Worked example.* Hallam Electronics holds average raw material inventory of £120,000. The annual cost of holding inventory (storage, insurance, finance) is 15% of its value, so £120,000 × 0.15 = **£18,000** a year. By moving to JIT it reduces average inventory to £30,000, so the holding cost is £30,000 × 0.15 = **£4,500**. The saving is £18,000 − £4,500 = **£13,500** a year. Against this, Hallam must pay for more frequent deliveries and risks a production stop if a supplier is late. JIT suits Hallam if it has dependable suppliers nearby and steady demand; it would suit it less if its parts come from far away or demand is unpredictable.

### Benefits and difficulties of lean production overall

Benefits: lower costs, higher quality, quicker response to customers, less waste, a more engaged workforce. Difficulties: demands strong supplier relationships and good forecasts; staff need training and a culture of improvement; the change can be disruptive; a lean business has little slack to deal with shocks, as some firms found when supply chains were disrupted.

## Choosing the optimal mix of resources

A **labour-intensive** process uses a high proportion of labour compared with capital (for example a hairdresser, a craft bakery). A **capital-intensive** process uses mainly machinery and equipment (for example a car assembly line, a bottling plant). The **optimal mix** is the combination of labour and capital that produces the required output at the lowest cost while meeting the quality and flexibility required.

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Cost pattern | Lower fixed costs; higher variable costs | High fixed costs (machines); low variable costs |
| Best for | Low volume, bespoke or personal products; uncertain demand | High volume, standard products; steady demand |
| Flexibility | High: workers can change tasks | Lower: machines are set up for specific tasks |
| Quality | Variable; personal touch | Consistent |
| Risk | Rising wages; absence | Machine breakdown; large debt |

*Worked example.* Marlowe Cases can make a product by hand (A) or on a machine line (B).

- Process A: fixed costs £10,000 a month and variable cost £10 per unit (labour £8, materials £2). Total cost = 10,000 + 10 × output.
- Process B: fixed costs £42,000 a month (machine lease and five staff) and variable cost £2 per unit (materials). Total cost = 42,000 + 2 × output.

At 6,000 units: A costs 10,000 + 60,000 = £70,000 (**£11.67** per unit); B costs 42,000 + 12,000 = £54,000 (**£9.00** per unit).
At 10,000 units: A costs £110,000 (**£11.00**); B costs £62,000 (**£6.20**).
At 3,000 units: A costs £40,000 (**£13.33**); B costs £48,000 (**£16.00**).

The costs are equal where 10,000 + 10x = 42,000 + 2x, so 8x = 32,000 and x = **4,000 units**. Below 4,000 units, hand production is cheaper; above it, the machine line is cheaper. If Marlowe expects steady demand of 8,000 units or more, process B is the better choice on cost. If demand is uncertain or products are often customised, A's flexibility could outweigh the cost.

Factors in choosing the mix: volume and stability of demand, product type, finance available, skills of the workforce, wage levels, quality required, and the image (a handmade product may be a selling point).

## Using technology to improve efficiency

- **Automation and robotics** carry out repetitive tasks quickly, accurately and without breaks, raising productivity and consistency.
- **Computer-aided design and manufacture (CAD/CAM)** speed up design and reduce errors.
- **Inventory and supply chain software**, including links with suppliers, support JIT by showing stock levels and triggering orders.
- **Data and sensors** allow predictive maintenance, cutting breakdowns.
- **Online systems** let customers place orders directly, cutting processing time and errors.
- **Digital tools in services**, such as booking apps and online self-service, reduce staff time per customer.

Costs and difficulties: high set-up cost; need for new skills and training; the risk of breakdown or cyber attack; resistance from staff who fear job loss; and the danger of reduced flexibility or personal service. The benefits are greater where volumes are high and tasks are standard.

## Technique for exam questions

- Link each method to the business in the case, for example JIT for a firm with reliable local suppliers.
- Use calculations to support arguments (unit costs at different outputs, holding cost savings).
- Evaluate by weighing cost against flexibility and risk, and say what the decision depends on, such as demand, finance or the skills available.

## Common mistakes

- Treating JIT as simply "no inventory" without noting its need for reliable suppliers.
- Assuming capital-intensive is always cheaper.
- Ignoring the cost and time needed to raise productivity.
- Recommending automation without comment on job losses and staff reaction.
- Confusing efficiency with productivity: productivity is one measure of efficiency.
- Describing methods without linking them to capacity or unit costs.
