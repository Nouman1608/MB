## Questions

**1.** The H–Cl bond is more polar than the H–I bond. Explain why, in terms of electronegativity. **[3]**

**2.** A student writes: "Fluorine boils at a lower temperature than iodine because the F–F bond is weaker
than the I–I bond." Explain why the student is wrong and give the correct explanation. **[3]**

**3.** A colourless solution contains one sodium halide, Z. Bromine water and cyclohexane are added and the
mixture is shaken. The upper organic layer turns violet.

**(a)** Identify the halide ion in Z. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an ionic equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why bromine reacts with the ion in Z but does not react with chloride ions. **[2]**

**4.** Concentrated sulfuric acid can be reduced to SO₂, S or H₂S by halide ions.

**(a)** State the oxidation state of sulfur in each of SO₂, S and H₂S. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the sodium halide that reduces sulfuric acid as far as H₂S. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why sodium chloride gives only steamy fumes. **[1]**

**5.** Solid sodium bromide reacts with concentrated sulfuric acid to form sodium hydrogensulfate,
bromine, sulfur dioxide and water.

**(a)** Write a balanced equation for this reaction, starting from NaBr. **[2]**
**(b)** Use oxidation states to identify the element oxidised and the element reduced. **[2]**

**6.** A solution contains both chloride ions and iodide ions. It is acidified with dilute nitric acid and
silver nitrate solution is added in excess. Excess dilute ammonia solution is then added.

**(a)** Describe what is seen after each addition. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the observation after adding the ammonia. **[1]**
**(c)** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the change that takes place when ammonia is added. **[1]**

**7.** 20.0 cm³ of 0.0450 mol dm⁻³ magnesium chloride solution, MgCl₂, is acidified with dilute nitric acid.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ silver nitrate solution that reacts exactly with all the
chloride ions. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of precipitate formed. **[2]**

**8.** A student claims that chlorine is simply reduced when it dissolves in water.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reversible reaction of chlorine with water that forms chloride ions and
chlorate(I) ions. **[1]**
**(b)** Use the oxidation states of chlorine in the products to show that the student is wrong, and name
this type of reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** In sunlight the reaction is 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂. Use oxidation states to explain why this
is not the same type of reaction as in (b). **[2]**

**9.** A works absorbs 1.50 kg of chlorine in excess cold, dilute sodium hydroxide, making 40.0 dm³ of
solution.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum concentration of sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, in this solution, in
mol dm⁻³. **[2]**
**(b)** State one use of the solution produced. **[1]**

**10.** A town council is deciding whether to chlorinate a new drinking-water supply.

**(a)** State why chlorine is added to drinking water. **[1]**
**(b)** Give two disadvantages of adding chlorine. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why chlorination is still used despite these disadvantages. **[1]**
**(d)** Fluoride is added to water in some places. Give one reason for adding it and one reason against. **[2]**

**11.** Four unlabelled bottles contain white solids: sodium carbonate, sodium sulfate, sodium iodide and
ammonium chloride. Describe one test for each solid that would identify it, and give the positive result
for each. **[8]**

**12.** A white solid, W, is a single sodium halide. With concentrated sulfuric acid, W gives steamy fumes
and an orange-brown vapour, but no purple vapour, no yellow solid and no bad-egg smell.

**(a)** Identify W, explaining how the observations rule out the other halides. **[2]**
**(b)** A solution of W is acidified and silver nitrate solution is added. Predict the colour of the
precipitate and how it behaves in dilute and in concentrated ammonia. **[2]**
**(c)** A 0.618 g sample of W is dissolved and treated with excess acidified silver nitrate. Calculate the
mass of precipitate expected. **[3]**
**(d)** A student records 1.16 g. Suggest one practical reason why this is higher than expected. **[1]**
**(e)** State the role of the halide ion in W in its reaction with sulfuric acid. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Chlorine is more electronegative than iodine [1]. The Cl atom is smaller, with fewer shells and less
shielding, so the bonding pair is closer to its nucleus and attracted more strongly [1]. The difference in
electronegativity between H and Cl is therefore larger, so the electron density is pulled further towards
Cl, giving a larger δ− [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Chlorine has a bigger nuclear charge" is wrong (iodine has more protons); credit
goes to distance and shielding.

**2.** Boiling does not break the covalent X–X bond; only the forces between molecules are overcome [1].
I₂ has more electrons (106) than F₂ (18), so the van der Waals forces between I₂ molecules are stronger [1].
Separating I₂ molecules therefore takes more energy, so iodine boils at the higher temperature [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say plainly that no covalent bond breaks; otherwise "why the student is wrong" is
left unanswered.

**3. (a)** **Iodide**, I⁻ [1]
**(b)** **Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂** [1]
**(c)** Bromine is a stronger oxidising agent than iodine but a weaker one than chlorine [1]. So bromine can
take electrons from iodide ions, but cannot take them from chloride ions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), compare bromine with both iodine and chlorine, not just one.

**4. (a)** SO₂ **+4**, S **0**, H₂S **−2** (all three for the mark) [1]
**(b)** **Sodium iodide** [1]
**(c)** Chloride ions are too weak a reducing agent to reduce sulfuric acid, so only the acid–base reaction
forming HCl happens [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "chloride is unreactive" is too vague; the answer needs the idea that Cl⁻ is not
a strong enough reducing agent.

**5. (a)** Correct formulae of all products from NaBr and H₂SO₄ [1];
**2NaBr + 3H₂SO₄ → 2NaHSO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O** [1]
**(b)** Bromine is oxidised, −1 → 0 [1]; sulfur is reduced, +6 → +4 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* One H₂SO₄ is reduced and two more form NaHSO₄; two H₂SO₄ cannot balance.

**6. (a)** After silver nitrate: a pale yellow / mixed precipitate (white AgCl and yellow AgI) [1]. After
dilute ammonia: some precipitate dissolves and a **yellow** precipitate remains [1].
**(b)** AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia but AgI does not [1].
**(c)** **AgCl(s) + 2NH₃(aq) → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c) the equation must be for the silver halide that dissolves; writing it for AgI
contradicts the answer to (b).

**7. (a)** n(MgCl₂) = 0.0200 × 0.0450 = 9.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol, so n(Cl⁻) = 1.80 × 10⁻³ mol [1];
volume = 1.80 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.120 = 0.0150 dm³ = **15.0 cm³** [1]
**(b)** Mr(AgCl) = 107.9 + 35.5 = 143.4 [1]; mass = 1.80 × 10⁻³ × 143.4 = **0.258 g** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each MgCl₂ supplies two chloride ions; a 1 : 1 ratio halves both answers.

**8. (a)** **Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HClO + HCl** (or Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ 2H⁺ + ClO⁻ + Cl⁻) [1]
**(b)** Cl⁻ (HCl) −1 but ClO⁻ (HClO) +1, so chlorine is oxidised as well as reduced [1];
**disproportionation** [1]
**(c)** Chlorine only goes from 0 to −1, so it is only reduced [1]; oxygen goes from −2 to 0 and is
oxidised, so two different elements change oxidation state [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Redox" on its own does not name the reaction in (b); the specific term
disproportionation is what gains the mark.

**9. (a)** n(Cl₂) = 1500 ÷ 71.0 = 21.13 mol = n(NaClO) [1];
concentration = 21.13 ÷ 40.0 = **0.528 mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(b)** **Bleach** (or a disinfectant for cleaning) [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert 1.50 kg to grams before dividing by 71.0.

**10. (a)** To kill bacteria (and so prevent water-borne disease) [1]
**(b)** Chlorine is toxic [1]; with organic matter in water it forms chlorinated organic compounds that
may be harmful [1]
**(c)** The health benefits of killing bacteria outweigh the toxic effects at the small amounts used [1]
**(d)** For: helps prevent tooth decay [1]. Against: too much causes fluorosis (or people disagree about
adding it to everyone's water) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), "to clean the water" or "to purify it" is too vague; the answer needs bacteria
or microorganisms.

**11.** Ammonium chloride: warm with sodium hydroxide solution and test the gas given off with damp red
litmus paper [1]; litmus turns **blue** [1].
Sodium carbonate: add dilute acid and bubble the gas through limewater [1]; **fizzing and limewater turns
milky** [1].
Sodium sulfate: dissolve and add acidified barium chloride solution [1]; **white precipitate** [1].
Sodium iodide: dissolve in water, acidify with dilute nitric acid and add silver nitrate solution [1];
**yellow precipitate**, which stays even in concentrated ammonia [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For the iodide, write "dilute nitric acid", not just "acid"; an unnamed acid could be
hydrochloric, which adds chloride ions, so the method is less likely to gain credit.

**12. (a)** **Sodium bromide**: orange-brown vapour is Br₂, so the halide was oxidised to bromine, which
chloride or fluoride cannot do [1]. No purple vapour, sulfur or H₂S rules out iodide [1].
**(b)** **Cream** precipitate [1]; stays in dilute ammonia but dissolves when concentrated
ammonia is added [1].
**(c)** Mr(NaBr) = 102.9; n = 0.618 ÷ 102.9 = 6.006 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(AgBr) = 6.006 × 10⁻³ mol [1];
Mr(AgBr) = 187.8, mass = 6.006 × 10⁻³ × 187.8 = **1.13 g** [1]
**(d)** The precipitate was not fully dried (water still present) [1].
**(e)** **Reducing agent** (electron donor) [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), naming bromide without using the missing iodide signs gains only part of the
credit, because the question asks you to rule out the other halides.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining electronegativity with nuclear charge instead of distance and shielding.
- Leaving NaHSO₄ out of an equation that starts from the solid sodium halide.
- Calling every chlorine reaction disproportionation, including the sunlight one.
- Giving a test with no result, or a result with no reagent, in Required practical 3 questions.
