## Section A

**1.** Define the term *trade payables*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** reasons why a new business needs finance before it starts trading. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between bankruptcy and liquidation. **[3]**

**4.** Ingram Electrical has inventory of $26 000, trade receivables of $31 000 and cash of $5 000. Its trade payables are $22 000 and its bank overdraft is $12 000. Calculate its working capital. **[3]**

**5.** State whether each is capital or revenue expenditure: (a) a new oven for a bakery; (b) flour; (c) an extension to the shop; (d) repainting the shop. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why a business should not hold too much working capital. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** In July, Pollard Garden Centre sells $48 000 of plants, of which $30 000 is on credit to landscapers who will pay in September. It pays $36 000 in cash for stock, wages and rent. It also receives a $20 000 bank loan.

**(a)** Calculate Pollard's profit for July.

**(b)** Calculate the change in Pollard's cash in July.

**(c)** Explain why the two figures differ. **[6]**

**8.** Darnell Printing gives customers 60 days' credit and is paid by none of them in cash. Its annual credit sales are $438 000. Assume 365 days in a year.

**(a)** Calculate average daily credit sales.

**(b)** Calculate the cash released if Darnell cuts its credit period to 40 days. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which a business could improve its management of trade payables without damaging its relationship with suppliers. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse why a fast-growing business might face a shortage of cash. **[4]**

**11.** Wexley Builders owes $400 000 to suppliers and its bank, and cannot pay its debts as they fall due. Explain **two** differences between Wexley going into administration and going into liquidation, for its employees. **[4]**

**12.** Marston Wholesale is considering offering customers a 3% discount for paying within 10 days instead of 60. Its customers owe $90 000 at any time. Evaluate whether Marston should offer the discount. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Money a business owes to suppliers [1] for goods or services bought on credit and not yet paid for [1].

**2.** Any two: to buy premises or equipment [1]; to buy the first inventory [1]; to pay wages and other costs before sales revenue arrives.

**3.** Bankruptcy is the legal process for an individual, such as a sole trader, who cannot pay debts [1], and personal assets may be used to pay creditors [1]. Liquidation applies to a business, usually a company, which stops trading and has its assets sold to pay creditors [1].

**4.** Current assets = 26 000 + 31 000 + 5 000 = $62 000 [1]. Current liabilities = 22 000 + 12 000 = $34 000 [1]. Working capital = 62 000 − 34 000 = $28 000 [1].

**5.** (a) Capital [1]. (b) Revenue [1]. (c) Capital [1]. (d) Revenue [1].

**6.** Cash tied up in idle inventory or unpaid customer debts is not earning a return [1], when it could be used for investment, such as new equipment, or to repay expensive borrowing [1].

**7.** **(a)** Profit = 48 000 − 36 000 [1] = $12 000 [1].

**(b)** Cash in = 18 000 (cash sales) + 20 000 (loan) = $38 000 [1]. Cash out = $36 000. Change = +$2 000 [1].

**(c)** $30 000 of sales count towards profit but the cash will not arrive until September [1]. The loan increases cash but is not revenue, so it does not affect profit [1].

**8.** **(a)** 438 000 ÷ 365 [1] = $1 200 a day [1].

**(b)** Reduction of 20 days × 1 200 [1] = $24 000 released [1].

**9.** Any two, explained. Negotiate longer credit terms in advance [1], so the business keeps cash longer with the supplier's agreement rather than paying late [1]. Pay on the last agreed day rather than early [1], which uses the full credit period without breaking terms [1]. Allow: concentrate purchases with fewer suppliers to gain better terms; pay early only where a discount is worth more than the cash held.

**10.** As sales grow, the business must buy more materials and pay more wages before it sells the extra output [1]. If it sells on credit, cash from the extra sales arrives weeks later [1], so cash outflows rise before inflows [1]. It may also need new equipment or premises, which use large amounts of cash at once, so profit can rise while the bank balance falls [1].

**11.** Any two, explained. In administration, the administrator tries to keep the business trading [1], so some or all employees may keep their jobs [1]. In liquidation, the business stops trading and its assets are sold [1], so employees lose their jobs and may receive only part of what they are owed [1].

**12.** For the discount: cash arrives 50 days sooner [1], so Marston could reduce its overdraft or pay suppliers on time [1]; customers who pay quickly are less likely to become bad debts [1]; it may attract customers who value the discount [1].
Against: the discount costs 3% of $90 000 = $2 700 each time the debts are collected [1]; over a year, if this happens about six times (365 ÷ 60), the cost could be about $16 200 [1]; customers who already pay promptly gain a discount for no change in behaviour [1].
Judgement: Marston should offer the discount only if it urgently needs cash or faces expensive borrowing [1], because 3% for 50 days is a high cost compared with typical overdraft interest [1]. The decision depends on its cash position and its customers' payment record [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting a loan or credit sale in the wrong figure when comparing cash with profit.
- Leaving out the overdraft from current liabilities.
- Applying "bankruptcy" to a company.
- Calculating the cash released without first finding daily sales.
- Evaluating a discount without comparing its cost with the benefit of faster cash.
