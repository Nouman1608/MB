## Section A

**1.** Which Article of the Convention allows derogation in time of war or public emergency? A: Article 13. B: Article 14. C: Article 15. D: Article 17. **[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is an absolute right? A: Article 3. B: Article 5. C: Article 8. D: Article 11. **[1]**

**3.** State what Article 1 of the Convention requires of states. **[2]**

**4.** Identify two Articles from which no derogation is permitted under Article 15. **[2]**

**5.** State two features of the composition of the European Court of Human Rights. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the difference between limited and qualified rights in the Convention, using one example of each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the purpose of Article 14 and its limitation. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what the decision in Al-Skeini v UK (2011) shows about the meaning of "jurisdiction" in Article 1. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the role of protocols to the Convention, referring to three protocols. **[5]**

**10.** *A political group whose stated aim is to remove the right to vote from members of a religious minority complains to Strasbourg that a national ban on its leaflets breaches Article 10.* Suggest how the Convention's general provisions are relevant to the group's complaint. **[4]**

**11.** *A state faces a wave of terrorist attacks and declares a public emergency. It passes a law allowing detention without trial for up to 60 days and allowing interrogators to use treatment that causes severe suffering.* Advise whether the state can rely on Article 15. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss whether the structure of the Convention strikes an appropriate balance between protecting individuals and allowing states to protect the public interest. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: Article 15. **[1]**

**2.** A: Article 3. **[1]**

**3.** States must **secure the Convention rights** [1] to **everyone within their jurisdiction**, not only their own citizens [1].

**4.** Any two of: **Article 2**, except deaths resulting from lawful acts of war [1]; **Article 3**; **Article 4.1**; **Article 7** [1].

**5.** Any two of: **one judge per state party** [1]; judges are **elected by the Parliamentary Assembly** of the Council of Europe; a **single nine-year term**; judges sit **as individuals**, not state representatives; formations include a **Grand Chamber of 17** [1].

**6.** A **limited right** can be restricted only in the situations **listed in the Article itself** [1], such as Article 5, which lists the grounds for lawful detention in 5.1(a) to (f) [1]. A **qualified right** has a second paragraph allowing restrictions that are **prescribed by law, pursue a listed aim and are necessary in a democratic society** [1], such as Article 10.2 [1].

**7.** Article 14 prohibits **discrimination** on grounds such as sex, race, religion or "other status" [1]. It applies only to the **enjoyment of the other Convention rights**, so it must be argued **with another Article** [1]. **Protocol 12** creates a free-standing prohibition, but the **UK has not ratified** it [1].

**8.** Jurisdiction is **primarily territorial** [1], but it can extend **abroad** where a state exercises **authority and control**, here public powers in Basra [1]. The UK therefore had to secure Article 2 to Iraqi civilians killed by its soldiers and breached the **duty to investigate** [1].

**9.** Protocols are **additional treaties** that bind only states that ratify them [1]. Some **add rights**: **Protocol 1** adds property, education and free elections, and is in the HRA [1]. **Protocol 13** abolishes the death penalty in all circumstances [1]. Others **change procedure**: **Protocol 11** created a full-time Court and abolished the Commission [1]; **Protocol 15** added subsidiarity and the margin of appreciation to the preamble (or Protocol 14) [1].

**10.** **Article 10** is qualified, so a ban on leaflets may be justified under 10.2, for example to protect the **rights of others** [1]. **Article 17** prevents anyone using Convention rights for activity **aimed at destroying the rights of others** [1]. A group seeking to remove the vote from a religious minority may be **unable to rely on Article 10 at all** [1]. **Article 14** and the rights of the minority support the ban rather than the group [1].

**11.** Article 15 allows derogation in **war or a public emergency threatening the life of the nation** [1]; a wave of terrorist attacks may qualify, as the House of Lords accepted in **A v SSHD (2004)** [1]. Measures must go only **as far as strictly required** by the situation [1], and must be proportionate and non-discriminatory, as A v SSHD shows [1]. Detention without trial is a derogation from **Article 5**, which is possible if strictly required [1]. Treatment causing severe suffering engages **Article 3**, from which **no derogation is ever permitted** [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **Absolute rights** such as Article 3 give the strongest protection where the harm is gravest [1].
- **Limited rights** set exhaustive exceptions, giving certainty about when life or liberty may be taken [1].
- **Qualified rights** let states pursue legitimate aims, but only if necessary and proportionate [1].
- **Article 15** gives flexibility in emergencies but excludes the most fundamental rights [1].
- **Article 17** stops the Convention being used to destroy democracy [1].
- **Article 18** guards against restrictions used for hidden purposes [1].
- The **margin of appreciation**, now in the preamble after Protocol 15, may give states too much room, especially on moral questions [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that the tiered structure is a sensible balance, with the Court's interpretation deciding how it works in practice [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating all rights as qualified.
- Saying Article 3 can be derogated from in an emergency.
- Treating Article 14 as a free-standing right.
- Claiming the UK is bound by every protocol.
- Naming provisions without explaining their effect.
