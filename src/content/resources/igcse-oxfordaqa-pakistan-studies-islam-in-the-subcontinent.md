**Islam in the Subcontinent (c700–c1831) is topic 3.1.3 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Pakistan
Studies 9236, one of two optional topics examined in Paper 1, Section C.** It follows the spread
of Islam from the Arab conquest of Sindh, through the Sufi orders and the reform thinkers of the
Mughal period, to the revival movements of Shah Waliullah and Syed Ahmad Barelvi. Section C carries
21 marks of short and extended responses, so you need both precise facts and the ability to judge
each person's importance.

## What is Sufism and why did it matter in the subcontinent?

Sufism is the inner, spiritual dimension of Islam. It developed in the early centuries of Islam
among Muslims who stressed personal devotion, remembrance of Allah (*dhikr*), self-discipline and
love of Allah, alongside obedience to the Sharia. Sufis gathered disciples (*murids*) around a
master (*pir* or *shaikh*) in orders called *silsilas*.

In the subcontinent Sufi saints spread Islam through example, service to the poor and welcoming
people of all backgrounds at their *khanqahs* (lodges). Important figures include **Ali Hujwiri
(Data Ganj Bakhsh)** of Lahore, author of *Kashf al-Mahjub*; **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti** of Ajmer,
founder of the Chishti order in India; and **Bahauddin Zakariya** of Multan of the Suhrawardi order.
Later the **Qadiri** and **Naqshbandi** orders also spread. Their shrines remain centres of
devotion and culture today.

## What was the importance of Muhammad bin Qasim?

In 711–712, under the Umayyad Caliphate, the governor Hajjaj bin Yusuf sent the young commander
**Muhammad bin Qasim** to Sindh. He captured the port of Debal and defeated Raja Dahir, bringing
Sindh and southern Punjab, including Multan, under Muslim rule. **Religious impact:** Sindh became
the first part of the subcontinent under Muslim rule; mosques were built; local Hindus and
Buddhists were granted protected status and allowed to keep their places of worship on payment of
jizya; and trade and learning linked Sindh to the wider Muslim world. This is why Sindh is
sometimes called *Bab-ul-Islam*, the gateway of Islam.

## Why was al-Ghazali important?

**Imam Abu Hamid al-Ghazali** (1058–1111) was a Persian scholar who taught in Baghdad. In his great
work *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (*The Revival of the Religious Sciences*) he argued that true faith needs
both correct practice under the Sharia and inner sincerity. He brought Sufism within mainstream
Sunni Islam, criticised philosophers who put reason above revelation, and gave later scholars a
model of combining law and spirituality. His ideas shaped the teaching of madrasas and Sufi
orders in the subcontinent for centuries.

## Who was Ahmad Sirhindi?

**Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi** (1564–1624), born in Sirhind in Punjab, was a scholar of the
**Naqshbandi** order and is known as **Mujaddid Alf Thani**, "renewer of the second millennium". He
opposed the religious syncretism of Emperor Akbar's court, including the *Din-i-Ilahi*, and argued
that Sufism must follow the Sharia strictly. Through hundreds of letters (*Maktubat*) to nobles he
urged a return to orthodox practice. He was imprisoned for a time by Jahangir in Gwalior fort,
then released. His influence helped revive orthodox Sunni Sufism and is often linked to the more
orthodox policies of **Aurangzeb**, under whom the legal compilation *Fatawa-e-Alamgiri* was produced.

## Why were Muslims weak and divided by the early 18th century?

After Aurangzeb's death in 1707, the Mughal Empire declined: weak emperors, rival nobles, Maratha
and Sikh expansion, and invasions undermined Muslim political power. Religious life was divided
between Sunnis and Shias, and between scholars who stressed law and those who followed popular
customs at shrines. Many Muslims had little direct knowledge of the Qur'an, which was mainly
available in Arabic.

## What were Shah Waliullah's reforms?

**Shah Waliullah** (1703–1762) of Delhi was the son of Shah Abdur Rahim, founder of the Madrasa
Rahimiya, where he studied and later taught. After the Hajj he studied hadith in Madinah. His reforms
aimed to unite Muslims and return them to the Qur'an and Sunnah:

- He translated the **Qur'an into Persian**, the educated language of the day, so more Muslims could understand it; his sons later produced Urdu translations.
- He stressed respect for the **Sharia** and the study of hadith, and wrote *Hujjat Allah al-Baligha* explaining the reasons behind Islamic law.
- He tried to reduce Sunni–Shia and sectarian conflict and to reconcile the different schools of thought.

**Political impact:** he wrote to Muslim rulers urging them to defend Muslim power. His letters to
**Ahmad Shah Abdali** of Afghanistan are linked to Abdali's victory over the Marathas at the **Third
Battle of Panipat (1761)**. His family and students, notably his son **Shah Abdul Aziz**, carried
his ideas into the 19th century.

## Who was Syed Ahmad Barelvi and what did his Jihad movement achieve?

**Context:** by the early 1800s the British controlled much of India, and in Punjab and the
north-west **Ranjit Singh's Sikh state** placed restrictions on Muslim worship, including the call to
prayer in some areas.

**Syed Ahmad Barelvi** (1786–1831) was born at Rae Bareli, studied under Shah Abdul Aziz in Delhi
and served for some years in the cavalry of the Pathan leader Amir Khan, gaining military
experience. He preached a purified Islam, the *Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya*, opposing practices he saw as
un-Islamic. With **Shah Ismail Shaheed**, grandson of Shah Waliullah, he launched a **jihad**
against Sikh rule in the north-west from 1826, aiming to restore Muslim freedom of worship and set
up a state run on Islamic lines.

His forces won some battles and briefly held Peshawar in 1830, but rivalries among local Pathan
leaders, tax disputes and Sikh military strength weakened the movement. Syed Ahmad and Shah Ismail
were killed at the **Battle of Balakot (1831)**. **Importance:** the movement kept the reform ideas
of Shah Waliullah's family alive, inspired later reformers, and influenced the **Ahl-i-Hadith** and
the **Deobandi** movement founded at Deoband in 1867.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Sindh called Bab-ul-Islam?**
Because Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest of 711–712 made it the
first region of the subcontinent under Muslim rule.

**What does Mujaddid Alf Thani mean?**
"Renewer of the second millennium", the title given to Ahmad
Sirhindi for reviving orthodox Islam around the thousandth year of the Islamic calendar.

**Why did Shah Waliullah translate the Qur'an into Persian?**
So that Muslims who did not read Arabic
could understand its message directly, which he saw as the basis for reform.
