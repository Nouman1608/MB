All angles in this subtopic are measured **from the normal**, the line drawn at 90° to the surface where the ray meets it. This one habit decides more marks than any equation.

## Reflection at a plane mirror

The law of reflection: **angle of incidence = angle of reflection**, both measured from the normal. If a question gives the angle between a ray and the mirror surface, subtract it from 90° first. A ray striking a mirror at 25° to its surface has i = 65°, so r = 65°, and the angle between the incident and reflected rays is 130°.

The image in a plane mirror is **the same size** as the object, **the same distance behind** the mirror as the object is in front, and **virtual**: the reflected rays only appear to come from it, so it cannot be caught on a screen.

**Constructing the image (Extended only).** To locate the image of a point object O:

1. Draw a line from O at right angles to the mirror and continue it behind the mirror.
2. Mark the image I on that line, the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as O is in front.
3. To show how an eye at E sees the image, draw a straight dashed line from I to E. Where it crosses the mirror is the point of reflection. Draw the real ray from O to that point (solid, with an arrow) and the reflected ray from there to E.
4. Dashed lines are for virtual rays behind the mirror; solid lines are for real light.

Distance calculations follow from the image rule. A person standing 1.5 m from a mirror is 3.0 m from their image. If they walk 0.5 m towards the mirror, they are 1.0 m from it and 2.0 m from the image: the gap closes by twice the distance walked.

## Refraction and refractive index

Light changes speed when it crosses a boundary between two materials. Entering a material where it travels more slowly (air to glass, air to water), a ray bends **towards** the normal; leaving it, the ray bends **away** from the normal. A ray travelling along the normal changes speed but not direction. Through a parallel-sided block, the ray that emerges is parallel to the ray that entered, but shifted sideways.

Inside a block, some light is always reflected at the boundary as well: this is **internal reflection**. As the angle of incidence inside the denser material increases, the refracted ray bends further from the normal until, at the **critical angle**, it travels along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°). For any larger angle there is no refracted ray at all: **total internal reflection**. It needs two conditions: the light must be travelling in the denser (slower) material towards the less dense one, and the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

**Refractive index (Extended only).** The syllabus defines n as the ratio of the speeds of a wave in two different regions. For light entering a material from air (or a vacuum):

- n = speed of light in air ÷ speed of light in the material
- n = sin i / sin r, with i in air and r in the material
- n = 1 / sin c, where c is the critical angle of the material

Because light is slowest in the material, n is always greater than 1 for these cases. A larger n means more bending and a smaller critical angle.

**Optical fibres (Extended only).** A fibre is a thin strand of very pure glass. Light (or infrared) enters one end and meets the wall of the fibre at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected again and again and follows the fibre round bends. In telecommunications, fibres carry signals as pulses of light over long distances with little loss.

### Worked example 1 (Extended)

Light in air strikes a plastic block of refractive index 1.60 at an angle of incidence of 50°. Find the angle of refraction, the speed of light in the plastic and the critical angle. Speed of light in air = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

```
sin r = sin i / n = sin 50° / 1.60 = 0.766 / 1.60 = 0.479
r = 28.6°   (29° to 2 s.f.)
speed in plastic = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.60 = 1.9 × 10⁸ m/s
sin c = 1 / n = 1 / 1.60 = 0.625,  so c = 38.7°
```

## Thin lenses

A **converging** (convex) lens refracts a parallel beam so that it meets at the **principal focus** (focal point), F. A **diverging** (concave) lens spreads a parallel beam out so that it appears to come from a principal focus on the same side as the incoming light. The **principal axis** is the line through the centre of the lens at right angles to it; the **focal length** is the distance from the centre of the lens to the principal focus.

**Three construction rays** for a converging lens (you need any two):

1. A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through F on the far side.
2. A ray through the centre of the lens passes straight through, undeviated.
3. A ray through F on the near side emerges parallel to the principal axis.

Where the refracted rays actually cross, a **real** image forms; it can be projected on a screen. If the refracted rays spread apart, extend them backwards as dashed lines: where these meet is a **virtual** image, which cannot be shown on a screen.

| Object position (converging lens) | Image | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| Beyond 2F | Between F and 2F; real, inverted, diminished | Camera, eye |
| At 2F | At 2F; real, inverted, same size | -- |
| Between F and 2F | Beyond 2F; real, inverted, enlarged | Projector |
| Between F and the lens (Extended only) | Same side as object; virtual, upright, enlarged | Magnifying glass |

Describe every image with three words: enlarged / same size / diminished; upright / inverted; real / virtual.

### Worked example 2 (scale drawing)

An object 2.0 cm tall stands 15 cm from a converging lens of focal length 5.0 cm. On a scale of 1 square = 1 cm, draw the principal axis, the lens as a vertical line, and mark F at 5.0 cm on each side. From the top of the object, draw the parallel ray (refracting through the far F) and the ray through the centre. They cross 7.5 cm beyond the lens, 1.0 cm below the axis. The image is **real, inverted and diminished**, 1.0 cm tall.

**Extended only.** A magnifying glass is a single converging lens held so that the object is closer than F. The rays leaving the lens diverge; traced back, they meet behind the object, giving a virtual, upright, enlarged image. A short-sighted eye focuses distant light in front of the retina and is corrected with a diverging lens; a long-sighted eye would focus near light behind the retina and is corrected with a converging lens.

## Dispersion

White light passed through a glass prism splits into a spectrum because each colour refracts by a slightly different amount: violet bends most and red least. This separation is **dispersion**. The seven traditional colours are:

**red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet**

In this order frequency **increases** and wavelength **decreases**, so red has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency. **Extended only:** light of a single frequency is called **monochromatic**; a monochromatic beam passed through a prism is refracted but not split.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
- Calling the plane-mirror image "behind the mirror, real".
- Drawing total internal reflection for light going from air into glass: it only happens inside the denser material.
- Writing n = sin r / sin i (upside down), which gives n less than 1.
- Describing an image with only one or two of the three describing words.
- Listing the spectrum colours in the wrong order, or saying red has the highest frequency.

## Exam technique

- **State:** a short answer, no explanation, for example "angle of incidence = angle of reflection".
- **Describe:** say what happens step by step, for example the path of a ray through a block, boundary by boundary.
- **Explain:** give the reason, usually a change of speed or the critical-angle condition.
- **Calculate:** write the equation, substitute, and give the answer with a unit (degrees or m/s) to 2 or 3 significant figures.
- **Draw / complete a ray diagram:** use a ruler, put arrows on rays, use dashed lines only for virtual rays, and show where you read the image position.
