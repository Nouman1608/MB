These questions test the section 3.2 row on property offences: theft and robbery. In Section B, take the theft elements in a fixed order (ss3, 4, 5, 2, 6), then robbery.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the definition of theft in s1 Theft Act 1968. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by appropriation, using **one** case. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** things that cannot be stolen, with an authority for one. **[3]**

**4.** State the three situations in s2(1) in which a defendant is not dishonest. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the test for dishonesty in *Ivey v Genting Casinos* (2017). **[3]**

**6.** Explain when borrowing amounts to an intention to permanently deprive. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Kim is given £50 by her flatmates to pay the electricity bill. She spends it on clothes. Explain whether the money belonged to another for the purposes of theft. **[4]**

**8.** Lars finds a ring in a park. He keeps it without making any enquiries, although it has the owner's name engraved on it. Advise Lars on dishonesty. **[5]**

**9.** Maya borrows her classmate's season ticket for a football club, uses it for every remaining match of the season and returns it after the last match. Advise Maya on intention to permanently deprive. **[5]**

**10.** Nico grabs a bag from Ola's shoulder. Ola holds on and is pulled to the ground; Nico runs off with the bag. Advise Nico on robbery. **[7]**

**11.** Pia takes goods from a shop without paying. As she leaves, a security guard grabs her arm and she pushes him away and escapes. Explain whether she has committed robbery. **[5]**

**12.** Evaluate the law on appropriation in theft. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A person is guilty of theft if he dishonestly appropriates property belonging to another [1] with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it [1].

**2.** Appropriation is any assumption by a person of the rights of an owner (s3(1)); assuming any one right is enough [1], as in *R v Morris* (1984), where switching price labels on goods was an appropriation (credit *Gomez* or *Hinks*) [1].

**3.** Confidential information [1], as in *Oxford v Moss* (1979) [1]; electricity, which is covered by a separate offence of abstracting electricity (credit land, with the s4(2) exceptions, or wild plants picked for personal use) [1].

**4.** He believes he has in law the right to deprive the other of the property [1]; he believes he would have the other's consent if the other knew of the appropriation and its circumstances [1]; he believes the person to whom the property belongs cannot be discovered by taking reasonable steps [1].

**5.** First, the jury ascertains the defendant's actual state of knowledge or belief as to the facts, which need not be reasonable [1]. Second, it decides whether, given that belief, the conduct was dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people [1]. It is not necessary for the defendant to have realised that ordinary decent people would regard it as dishonest; this replaced *R v Ghosh* and was confirmed in *R v Barton and Booth* (2020) [1].

**6.** Borrowing is an intention to permanently deprive if it is for a period and in circumstances making it equivalent to an outright taking (s6(1)) [1], for example where the property is returned with its goodness, virtue and practical value gone (*R v Lloyd*, 1985) [1].

**7.** The flatmates handed over ownership of the money, so on its own s5(1) might suggest it belongs to Kim [1]. But s5(3) provides that where a person receives property under an obligation to deal with it in a particular way, it is treated as belonging to the person who gave it [1]. Kim was obliged to use the money for the electricity bill [1], so it is treated as belonging to her flatmates, as in *Davidge v Bunnett* (1984), and spending it on clothes is theft if the other elements are met [1].

**8.** Lars is not dishonest if he believes the owner cannot be discovered by taking reasonable steps (s2(1)(c)) [1]. The belief need only be honest, not reasonable [1], but the engraved name makes it hard to believe he honestly thought the owner could not be found [1]. If s2(1) does not apply, the *Ivey* test asks what he actually knew or believed, which includes knowing the owner's name [1], and whether keeping the ring without enquiry was dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people, which a jury is likely to find [1].

**9.** Maya intends to return the ticket, so she does not intend to permanently deprive in the ordinary sense [1]. Under s6(1), borrowing counts if it is equivalent to an outright taking [1]. In *R v Lloyd* (1985) this was explained as returning the property with its goodness, virtue and practical value gone [1]. A season ticket returned after the last match of the season has no practical value left [1], so Maya is likely to have an intention to permanently deprive [1].

**10.** Robbery requires a theft (s8(1)) [1]. Grabbing the bag is an appropriation of property belonging to Ola (ss3–5) [1]; running off with it shows dishonesty and an intention to permanently deprive (ss2, 6) [1]. Force: pulling Ola to the ground is force on a person; even force on a bag can count where transmitted to the victim (*R v Clouden*, 1987) [1], and the amount is for the jury (*R v Dawson and James*, 1976) [1]. Timing and purpose: the force was used at the time of stealing [1] and in order to steal [1]. Nico is likely to be guilty of robbery.

**11.** Pia committed theft when she appropriated the goods in the shop [1]. Robbery requires force immediately before or at the time of stealing [1]. Under *R v Hale* (1979) appropriation can be a continuing act, and in *R v Lockley* (1995) force used on a shopkeeper while escaping with goods was robbery [1]. Pia is still in the course of the theft when she leaves the shop with the goods [1], and she pushed the guard in order to get away with them, so she is likely to be guilty of robbery [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Breadth:** s3 defines appropriation as any assumption of the rights of an owner, and *R v Morris* (1984) held that one right is enough [1]. *Lawrence* (1972) and *R v Gomez* (1993) held that consent is irrelevant [1], and *R v Hinks* (2000) extended this to valid gifts [1]. **Criticisms:** after *Hinks*, conduct lawful in civil law can be criminal, so the criminal and civil law conflict [1]. Almost any dealing with property is an appropriation, so the actus reus does little work and liability depends on dishonesty [1], which, after *Ivey*, is judged by a variable standard [1]. Ordinary shopping can technically be appropriation (*Morris*) [1]. **In its favour:** a broad rule avoids technical arguments about the moment of appropriation and catches fraudsters who obtain consent by deception, as in *Gomez* [1]; the requirement of dishonesty protects honest people [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that the breadth is workable but shifts too much onto dishonesty [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Skipping s5(3) when money is handed over for a purpose.
- Treating a s2(1) belief as needing to be reasonable.
- Calling any borrowing theft, or ignoring *Lloyd*.
- Forgetting continuing appropriation in shop-escape scenarios.
