This study guide teaches the identification of ions for OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202). It follows the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards, and covers every statement in section 3.4.3 (statements a to f) plus the required practical on flame testing. There are no tiers in this qualification, so everything here is for every candidate. The specification says content from any part of the course may be assessed on Paper 1 or Paper 2.

## Two words you will use all the time

- An **ion test** tells you which ion is present. Ionic compounds contain a positive ion (usually a metal ion) and a negative ion, so a full identification needs one test for each.
- A **precipitate** is a solid that cannot dissolve, produced when two solutions react. The mixture turns cloudy, and the solid slowly settles. Always give its colour.

Most tests here use a **solution** of the unknown. Dissolve a solid in distilled water first; tap water contains ions of its own.

## 3.4.3 a: flame tests

Some metal ions give a hot flame a colour typical of that metal. The colour depends on the metal ion, not the negative ion, so lithium chloride and lithium sulfate give the same result.

| Flame colour | Metal ion in the compound |
|---|---|
| green | barium (Ba²⁺) |
| red | calcium (Ca²⁺) |
| crimson | lithium (Li⁺) |
| lilac | potassium (K⁺) |
| yellow | sodium (Na⁺) |

Crimson (lithium) and red (calcium) are the pair most often confused. Crimson is a deep red; the specification simply calls calcium "red". Learn the words exactly as in the table.

### Required practical: identifying the metal ion in an unknown compound

The specification links this practical to designing a procedure, risk assessment, collecting and analysing data, making conclusions and evaluation. A sound procedure:

1. Light a Bunsen burner and open the air hole fully so the flame is blue and roaring. A yellow, smoky flame would hide the colours you are looking for.
2. Clean a loop of nichrome wire. Dip it in dilute hydrochloric acid, then hold it in the flame. Repeat until the wire gives no colour of its own.
3. Moisten the loop with acid once more and touch it on the solid so a few grains cling to it.
4. Hold the loop at the edge of the blue flame and note the colour straight away.
5. Repeat the cleaning between samples, or give each sample its own loop.

**Risk assessment.** The wire and the burner get very hot; hold the wire by its handle and let it cool on a heat-proof mat. Wear eye protection because acid can spit. Tie back long hair. Barium compounds are harmful if swallowed, so wash your hands afterwards.

**Collecting data.** Record the colour in words, in a table with one row per sample. A blank run with the clean wire on its own shows what "no colour" looks like.

**Evaluation.** The commonest problem is contamination. Sodium compounds are everywhere (fingers, dust, unwashed wire) and give a strong yellow colour that can swamp a weaker colour. Potassium's lilac is pale and is easily masked this way. A flame test also only works well for a single metal ion; if a solid contains two of these metals, one colour may hide the other. Repeating each test with a freshly cleaned wire makes the conclusion more reliable.

### Worked example 1: reading flame test results

A technician has five unlabelled bottles. Each holds a chloride of a different metal: lithium, sodium, potassium, calcium or barium. A student records these flame colours:

| Bottle | V | W | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | green | crimson | yellow | lilac | red |

Identify each metal ion and name each compound.

1. Match each colour to the table above. Green = barium; crimson = lithium; yellow = sodium; lilac = potassium; red = calcium.
2. Write the names, with the negative ion as chloride: **V barium chloride, W lithium chloride, X sodium chloride, Y potassium chloride, Z calcium chloride**.

## 3.4.3 b and c: sodium hydroxide solution

Add sodium hydroxide solution a few drops at a time to a solution of the unknown. Many metal ions form an **insoluble metal hydroxide**, which appears as a precipitate. Then keep adding sodium hydroxide until it is **in excess** and watch whether the precipitate disappears.

| Colour of precipitate | Does it dissolve in excess NaOH? | Metal ion |
|---|---|---|
| white | **yes**, leaving a colourless solution | aluminium (Al³⁺) |
| white | no | calcium (Ca²⁺) or magnesium (Mg²⁺) |
| blue | no | copper(II) (Cu²⁺) |
| green | no | iron(II) (Fe²⁺) |
| brown | no | iron(III) (Fe³⁺) |

Key points:

- Aluminium, calcium and magnesium all give **white** precipitates. Only **aluminium hydroxide** dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide. That is how you separate aluminium from the other two (statement b).
- Calcium and magnesium cannot be told apart with sodium hydroxide alone. Use a **flame test**: calcium gives red, while magnesium compounds give no characteristic colour.
- The coloured precipitates (statement c) identify copper(II), iron(II) and iron(III) at once, and tell you the charge on an iron ion.
- A green iron(II) hydroxide precipitate slowly darkens towards brown if left standing in air, as the iron(II) is oxidised to iron(III). Describe the colour you see as soon as it forms.

If you are asked for an equation, the metal ion and hydroxide ions form the hydroxide. For copper(II) chloride solution:

CuCl₂(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

The (s) state symbol shows the precipitate.

### Worked example 2: a white precipitate that stays

A colourless solution of a nitrate gives a white precipitate with a few drops of sodium hydroxide solution. The precipitate does not dissolve in excess. A flame test on the solid gives no characteristic colour. Identify the metal ion.

1. White precipitate: the ion is aluminium, calcium or magnesium.
2. It stays in excess, so it is **not aluminium**.
3. Calcium would give a red flame. No colour was seen, so it is **not calcium**.
4. The metal ion is **magnesium, Mg²⁺**, and the compound is magnesium nitrate.

Notice that two tests were needed. If one test leaves two possibilities, say which further test would decide between them.

## 3.4.3 d: carbonate ions

Carbonates react with dilute acids to form carbon dioxide gas, along with a salt and water.

1. Add a few cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid to the solid or solution.
2. A carbonate **fizzes** (effervescence) as carbon dioxide forms.
3. Bubble the gas through **limewater** (calcium hydroxide solution) using a delivery tube, or draw it up with a teat pipette and bubble it through.
4. Carbon dioxide gives a **white precipitate** of calcium carbonate, so the limewater **turns cloudy white**.

Fizzing alone is not enough: a reactive metal in acid also fizzes, giving hydrogen. The limewater result confirms carbon dioxide.

A symbol equation, for copper(II) carbonate and dilute nitric acid:

CuCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

## 3.4.3 e: halide ions (chloride, bromide, iodide)

1. To a solution of the unknown, add a few drops of **dilute nitric acid**.
2. Then add a few drops of **silver nitrate solution**.
3. A precipitate of the silver halide forms. Its colour tells you which halide is present.

| Halide ion | Precipitate | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | silver chloride | white |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | silver bromide | cream |
| Iodide, I⁻ | silver iodide | yellow |

**Why add nitric acid first?** The acid removes carbonate ions. Carbonate ions would also give a precipitate with silver ions, which could be mistaken for a halide result.

**Why nitric acid and not hydrochloric acid?** Hydrochloric acid contains chloride ions. They would give a white silver chloride precipitate every time, even if the unknown had no chloride in it.

### Worked example 3: putting a flame test and a halide test together

A white crystalline solid gives a crimson flame. A solution of it, acidified with dilute nitric acid, gives a cream precipitate with silver nitrate solution. Name the solid.

1. Crimson flame: lithium ion, Li⁺.
2. Cream silver halide precipitate: bromide ion, Br⁻.
3. Li⁺ and Br⁻ have charges of +1 and −1, so they combine 1 : 1. The solid is **lithium bromide, LiBr**.

## 3.4.3 f: sulfate ions

1. To a solution of the unknown, add a few drops of **dilute hydrochloric acid**.
2. Then add a few drops of **barium chloride solution**.
3. If sulfate ions are there, **white** barium sulfate precipitates.

Without the acid, any carbonate ions in the sample would react with barium ions too, and the white barium carbonate formed would look like a positive result. Here hydrochloric acid is the right choice, because the test reagent is a chloride anyway. Do not use sulfuric acid: it adds sulfate ions and would always give a positive result.

## Planning a full identification

When an exam question gives you an unknown ionic compound, plan one test for the positive ion and one for the negative ion. Use a **fresh portion** of the unknown for each test, so the reagents from one test cannot affect the next.

### Worked example 4: planning the tests for an unknown sulfate

A student thinks a white powder is aluminium sulfate. Describe the tests that would confirm this, with the expected result of each.

1. Dissolve some powder in distilled water and split the solution into three test tubes.
2. **Tube 1, the positive ion.** Add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop: a white precipitate forms. Keep adding until in excess: the precipitate dissolves. This shows aluminium ions.
3. **Tube 2, the negative ion.** Add a little dilute hydrochloric acid, followed by some barium chloride solution; a white precipitate appears. This shows sulfate ions.
4. **Tube 3, ruling out a carbonate.** Add dilute hydrochloric acid: no fizzing, so no carbonate is present.

Why separate tubes? If silver nitrate were added to the solution from tube 2, the chloride ions from the barium chloride and hydrochloric acid would give a white precipitate and suggest a chloride that was never in the powder.

## Common errors

- Writing "orange" or "brick red" for lithium. The answer for lithium is crimson.
- Swapping the green flame (barium) with the green precipitate (iron(II) hydroxide). One is a flame colour, the other is a solid in a test tube.
- Saying all white hydroxide precipitates dissolve in excess. Only aluminium hydroxide does.
- Writing "limewater turns milky" with no mention of the gas test. Give both: fizzing, and the gas turns limewater cloudy white.
- Choosing hydrochloric acid for the halide test, or sulfuric acid for the sulfate test.
- Describing a precipitate without its colour. "A precipitate forms" will not identify the ion.
