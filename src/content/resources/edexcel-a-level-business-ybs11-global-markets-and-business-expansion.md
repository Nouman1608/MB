This guide covers section **4.3.2 Global markets and business expansion** in Unit 4 (Global business) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. It asks why businesses go global, how they choose where to sell and where to produce, why they combine with overseas firms, and what can go wrong. All businesses, countries and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Global business study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-global-business/). Condensed notes are in the [Global markets and business expansion revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-markets-and-business-expansion-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Global markets and business expansion practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-markets-and-business-expansion-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Conditions that prompt trade:** push factors (saturated markets, competition); pull factors (increased sales and profitability, risk spreading and economies of scale); cost competitiveness through off-shoring and outsourcing; extending the product life cycle.
2. **Assessing a country as a market:** disposable income levels and growth; ease of doing business; infrastructure; political stability; exchange rates; Porter's five forces applied to potential markets.
3. **Assessing a country as a production location:** costs of production; skills and availability of labour; infrastructure; location in a trade bloc; government incentives; ease of doing business; political stability; natural resources; likely return on investment.
4. **Reasons for global mergers, takeovers or joint ventures:** ten reasons listed in the specification (see below).
5. **Global expansion and uncertainty:** the effect of exchange rate movements; skill shortages and their effect on international competitiveness.

## Push and pull factors

**Push factors** drive a business out of its home market. **Saturated markets** are those where most potential customers already own the product, so growth is slow. **Competition** from many rivals squeezes prices and margins.

**Pull factors** draw a business towards overseas markets: **increased sales and profitability** from reaching more customers; **spreading risk** across several countries so a fall in one market matters less; and **economies of scale** from producing and selling in larger volumes.

**Cost competitiveness** can be improved by **off-shoring** (moving the firm's own production or services to another country, usually where costs are lower) or **outsourcing** (paying an outside firm to carry out an activity, at home or abroad). Off-shoring keeps control but needs investment; outsourcing is more flexible but can lose control of quality. **Extending the product life cycle:** a product in decline at home may be at the growth stage in an overseas market, so a business can lengthen its life and spread development costs.

**Worked example 1: off-shoring.** A furniture maker pays $12 an hour in its home country and needs 3 hours per unit. In an overseas location wages are $4 an hour, but lower productivity means 5 hours per unit. Materials cost $40 per unit in both; transport to the customer costs $4 per unit from home and $14 per unit from overseas.

- Home: labour 12 x 3 = 36; total = 36 + 40 + 4 = **$80** per unit.
- Overseas: labour 4 x 5 = 20; total = 20 + 40 + 14 = **$74** per unit.
- Saving = $6 per unit; for 100,000 units = **$600,000** a year.

The saving is only 7.5% of the home cost despite a 67% lower wage, because productivity and transport costs eat most of the advantage. The quality of work, delays in the supply chain, and the risk of changes in wages or exchange rates would also have to be weighed.

## Assessing a country as a market

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Level and growth of disposable income | Can customers afford the product, and will they be able to in future? |
| Ease of doing business | How hard is it to register, hire, get permits and enforce contracts? |
| Infrastructure | Are transport, power and communications reliable enough to deliver and sell? |
| Political stability | Is there a risk of conflict, expropriation or sudden policy change? |
| Exchange rates | Are they stable? Will the currency weaken against the firm's own? |

**Worked example 2.** Market M has disposable income per head of $8,000, growing at 6% a year; Market N has the same income but growth of 3%. After five years:

- M: 8,000 x 1.06^5 = **$10,706**.
- N: 8,000 x 1.03^5 = **$9,274**.

M's customers have $1,432 more to spend, which is 15% more than N's. Over time, the faster-growing market has a bigger gap in buying power, but if M is politically unstable or has weak infrastructure, the better income growth may be outweighed by risk.

**Porter's five forces** helps a firm judge the profit potential of a market: how strong is local **rivalry**; how easily can **new entrants** arrive; how likely are **substitutes**; how powerful are **suppliers**; how powerful are **buyers** (such as large retail chains)? A market with strong local rivals and large buyers offers lower profit than one with few competitors and many small customers.

## Assessing a country as a production location

| Factor | What to consider |
|---|---|
| Costs of production | Wages, land, energy, materials, taxes |
| Skills and availability of labour | Education, training, size of the labour pool |
| Infrastructure | Ports, roads, power, broadband |
| Location in a trade bloc | Tariff-free access to neighbouring markets |
| Government incentives | Grants, tax holidays, subsidised land |
| Ease of doing business | Regulation, bureaucracy, legal protection |
| Political stability | Safety of assets and contracts |
| Natural resources | Access to materials and energy |
| Likely return on investment | Profit compared with the cost of the investment |

**Worked example 3.** A factory costs $8m and is expected to earn $1.2m a year in profit: ROI = 1.2 / 8 x 100 = **15%**. If the host government offers a $1m grant, the net investment is $7m, so ROI = 1.2 / 7 x 100 = **17.1%**. If the firm requires at least 12%, both pass, but the incentive improves the margin of safety if profit turns out lower than forecast. At $0.84m profit (30% below forecast), the return would be 0.84 / 7 = 12%, which only just meets the target, whereas without the grant it would be 10.5%.

The best location balances all factors. The cheapest location may fail on skills or infrastructure, while a location in a trade bloc offers access to a large market even if its wages are higher.

## Reasons for global mergers, takeovers or joint ventures

A **joint venture** is when two firms set up a new business together and share its costs, risks and profits. The specification lists these reasons for global mergers, takeovers and joint ventures:

- **Spreading risk and economies of scale** across several markets.
- **Entering new markets and trading blocs** quickly, avoiding barriers.
- **Acquiring national or international brand names and patents.**
- **Securing resources and supplies.**
- **Maintaining or increasing global competitiveness.**
- **Reducing competition.**
- **Using local knowledge** of customers, law and culture.
- **Meeting government or legal requirements**, such as rules that foreign firms must partner with a local firm.
- **Accessing supply chains and distribution networks.**
- **Sharing costs and risks.**

Compared with setting up alone, these routes are faster and can be cheaper, but they carry risks: cultural clashes, disagreements between partners, overpaying, and loss of control. A joint venture shares profit as well as risk.

## Global expansion and uncertainty

**Exchange rate movements.** A firm with overseas operations earns revenue in foreign currency. A subsidiary earning EUR 5m in profit is worth 5m / 0.90 = **$5.56m** when $1 = EUR 0.90, but 5m / 0.80 = **$6.25m** if the dollar depreciates to $1 = EUR 0.80, a gain of $694,000 with no change in the subsidiary's performance. If the dollar appreciates, the effect is reversed. Exchange rate movements also change the cost of imported inputs and make a country's exports more or less competitive. Firms can reduce the risk by producing and selling in the same currency area, borrowing in local currency or using forward contracts.

**Skill shortages.** If a country lacks workers with the skills a firm needs, wages rise, projects are delayed and quality can fall. A firm can respond by training, recruiting abroad (migration), using more automation, or locating where skills are available. A persistent shortage lowers **international competitiveness** because costs rise and the firm may lose orders to rivals with a better workforce.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing off-shoring (moving the firm's own activity abroad) with outsourcing (paying another firm to do it).
- Judging a location on wage costs alone, ignoring productivity, transport and skills.
- Treating all push and pull factors as equally important for every firm.
- Listing the market-assessment factors without applying them to the country in the source.
- Describing joint ventures and takeovers as the same thing; a joint venture creates a separate shared business.
- Assuming a weaker home currency always helps: it helps exporters but raises the cost of imports.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions, give one factor and develop it with evidence from the source, such as a wage rate, an incentive or a political risk. For **analyse**, link cause and effect: "lower wages cut unit costs, but poor infrastructure raises transport costs, so the net saving is smaller". For **discuss** and **assess**, compare options (for instance exporting, FDI or a joint venture), weigh risk, cost and control, and judge in terms of the firm's resources and the market's stability.
