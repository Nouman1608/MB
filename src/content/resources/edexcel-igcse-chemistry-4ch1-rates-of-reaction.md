## What is meant by rate

The **rate** of a reaction tells you how fast reactants are used up or products are formed. We cannot see rate directly, so we measure a change over time: the volume of gas collected, the loss in mass of a flask (as gas escapes), or the time for a colour or cloudiness to appear.

```
Mean rate = change in quantity (volume or mass) / time taken
```

**Worked example 1: reading a gas volume table.** Marble chips react with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. The volume of carbon dioxide collected is measured every 30 s.

| Time / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume / cm³ | 0 | 24 | 36 | 43 | 46 | 48 | 48 |

| Interval / s | Gas made / cm³ | Mean rate / cm³ per s |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 30 | 24 | 24 / 30 = **0.80** |
| 30 to 60 | 12 | 12 / 30 = **0.40** |
| 60 to 90 | 7 | 7 / 30 = **0.23** |
| 90 to 120 | 3 | 3 / 30 = **0.10** |
| 120 to 150 | 2 | 2 / 30 = **0.07** |
| 150 to 180 | 0 | **0** |

The rate is highest at the start and falls to zero at 150 s, when the reaction has finished, because the reactant has been used up. When the data are plotted as a graph, the line is steepest at the start and then flattens to a plateau. The final volume (48 cm³) depends only on the amount of the limiting reactant, not on the rate. Over the first 60 s, the mean rate = 36 / 60 = **0.60 cm³ per s**.

## Collision theory (3.11)

Reactions happen only when particles **collide** with enough energy. The minimum energy needed is the **activation energy**. A collision with less than this energy bounces apart without reacting. So the rate depends on two things: how often particles collide, and what proportion of collisions have enough energy.

**Template for an explanation.** Name the factor, say what changes at the particle level, then state the effect on the frequency of successful collisions and so on the rate.

- **Greater surface area:** more particles of the solid are exposed to the reactant, so there are more frequent collisions per second, so a faster rate. (Powder reacts faster than lumps.)
- **Higher concentration:** more particles of the reactant in each cm³, so the particles are closer together and collide more often, so a faster rate.
- **Higher gas pressure:** the same number of gas particles in a smaller volume, which is the same as a higher concentration, so more frequent collisions.
- **Higher temperature:** particles move faster, so they collide more often, **and** a larger proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so there are many more successful collisions. The second reason is the larger effect. A marking point on temperature is only complete if it mentions both.

Two cautions. First, a higher concentration does not give particles more energy. It gives more collisions. Second, "more collisions" alone is not enough for temperature; the energy of the collisions must be mentioned.

**Worked example 2: temperature and rate.** Sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give a cloudy yellow precipitate of sulfur. A cross under the beaker disappears when enough sulfur forms. The time taken is recorded at three temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | Time for cross to disappear / s | Rate = 1 / time / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 80 | 1 / 80 = 0.0125 |
| 30 | 38 | 1 / 38 = 0.0263 |
| 40 | 19 | 1 / 19 = 0.0526 |

Because the same amount of sulfur forms each time, the rate is proportional to 1 / time. From 20 °C to 30 °C the rate rises by 0.0263 / 0.0125 = **2.1 times**, and from 30 °C to 40 °C by 0.0526 / 0.0263 = **2.0 times**. Each 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate in this reaction, which fits with collision theory but is a pattern in this data, not a rule for every reaction.

## Catalysts (3.12 and 3.13)

A **catalyst** is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction but is chemically unchanged at the end. It is not used up, so a small amount can be used repeatedly. It may be recovered, although it can be physically changed (for example, a lump may become powdery).

A catalyst works by providing an **alternative pathway** for the reaction with a **lower activation energy**. More collisions then have enough energy to react, so the rate is faster. The catalyst does not change the overall energy change of the reaction (ΔH) or the amount of product made. Examples: manganese(IV) oxide for hydrogen peroxide; iron in the Haber process; silica or alumina in catalytic cracking.

## Reaction profile diagrams (3.14C, Paper 2 only)

A reaction profile plots energy (vertical) against progress of reaction (horizontal).

- Draw a line for the **reactants** at the left, rising to a **peak**, then falling to a line for the **products** at the right.
- The height from the reactants up to the peak is the **activation energy**.
- The difference in height between reactants and products is ΔH.
  - **Exothermic:** products below reactants, ΔH negative (arrow down).
  - **Endothermic:** products above reactants, ΔH positive (arrow up).
- **With a catalyst:** draw a second, lower peak on the same diagram. The start and end levels do not change, so ΔH is the same. Only the activation energy is smaller.

Typical marking points: correct labelled axes; reactants and products at the right levels; a peak above both; activation energy shown from the reactant level to the peak; ΔH shown between reactant and product levels; catalysed route drawn with a lower peak.

## Required practicals

### Marble chips and hydrochloric acid (3.15)

Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with dilute hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂.

**Method.** Put acid in a conical flask on a balance (or connect the flask to a gas syringe). Add a fixed mass of marble chips and start a stopwatch. Record either the loss in mass or the volume of gas at regular times. Repeat with (a) smaller marble chips (same mass), and (b) acid of a different concentration (same volume).

**Control variables.** To test surface area, keep the mass of marble, the volume and concentration of acid, and the temperature the same. To test concentration, keep the mass and size of the chips, the volume of acid and the temperature the same. Change only one variable at a time.

**Worked example 3: the expected volume of gas.** 1.0 g of calcium carbonate (Mr = 100) is 0.010 mol. By the equation, 1 mol of CaCO₃ gives 1 mol of CO₂, so 0.010 mol of gas is made. At room temperature and pressure, 1 mol of gas occupies 24 dm³, so the volume = 0.010 x 24,000 = **240 cm³**. If the acid is in excess, 240 cm³ is the final volume whatever the chip size or acid concentration. This is why curves for faster and slower runs differ in steepness but reach the same plateau.

In the practical, "loss in mass" works because carbon dioxide leaves the flask; a cotton wool plug stops acid spray escaping.

### Catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (3.16)

Hydrogen peroxide decomposes slowly: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Different solids (for example manganese(IV) oxide, copper(II) oxide, iron(III) oxide) may speed it up.

**Method.** Measure a fixed volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide into a flask connected to a gas syringe. Add a fixed mass of a solid and start the stopwatch at once. Record the volume of oxygen at regular intervals, or the time to collect a fixed volume. Repeat with each solid, then with no solid as a control.

**Conclusions.** The solid that gives the steepest curve (or the shortest time) is the best catalyst. After the reaction, the solid can be filtered, dried and weighed to show that its mass is unchanged. The test for oxygen is a glowing splint that relights.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that a higher temperature "gives particles more energy, so they collide more", without the point that a larger fraction of collisions exceeds the activation energy.
- Claiming that a catalyst "gives particles more energy" or "is used up".
- Saying a more concentrated acid makes more product. The amount made is set by the limiting reactant.
- Drawing a catalysed reaction profile that starts or ends at different energies.
- Writing "more particles" when the correct idea is "more particles per unit volume".
- Plotting a rate graph with the wrong axis. Volume of gas goes on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal.
- Forgetting a control variable in a "describe an experiment" answer.

## Exam technique

When asked to **explain** a rate change, use the three-part chain: the particle-level change, the frequency or energy of successful collisions, and then the rate. For rate calculations, show the intervals and the units (cm³/s or g/s). For graph questions, link steepness to rate and the plateau to the amount of limiting reactant. Mark "Paper 2 only" on reaction profile questions in your revision notes, because they are only tested on that paper.
