This guide covers topic 1.5 Settlements (rural and urban) and service provision in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks you to explain patterns of settlement (dispersed, linear and nucleated); to describe and explain the factors that influence the sites, growth and functions of settlements, including physical factors (relief, soil, water supply) and others (accessibility, resources); and to give reasons for the hierarchy of settlements and services, using high-, middle- and low-order settlements and services, sphere of influence and threshold population. A case study of settlement and service provision in an area is required. The [Settlements and Service Provision revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-settlements-service-provision-revision-notes/) give the definitions; this guide shows how to apply them to map and data resources, with worked examples. Practise with the [Settlements and Service Provision practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-settlements-service-provision-practice/).

## Settlement patterns and how to recognise them

| Pattern | What it looks like on a map | Common reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **Dispersed** | Single farms or houses spread out, with fields between them | Large farms; extensive grazing; even spread of water and fertile land; a safe area where people do not need to group together |
| **Linear** | Buildings in a line along a road, river, canal, valley floor, coast or ridge | Access to transport; flat land in a narrow valley; dry land along a raised embankment; a spring line |
| **Nucleated** | Buildings clustered around a centre such as a crossroads, well, church, mosque or market | Shared water source; defence; access to services; a route centre where roads meet |

To identify a pattern on a map extract, look at the spacing of buildings and what they are aligned with or grouped around, then name that feature in your answer. Patterns can change: a nucleated village may grow outwards along its main roads, becoming partly linear.

## Sites, growth and functions

The **site** is the land a settlement is built on; the **situation** is its position in relation to the surrounding area.

**Physical site factors named in the syllabus**

- **Relief**: flat or gently sloping land is easy to build on and farm; a hilltop gave defence; a south-facing slope in the northern hemisphere receives more sunshine; land above the flood plain avoids flooding (a dry point site).
- **Soil**: fertile soils supported the farming that early settlements depended on; settlements often grew on the boundary between different soils so that people could farm both.
- **Water supply**: a river, spring or well for drinking and washing (a wet point site, important in dry regions and on permeable rock).

**Other factors named in the syllabus**

- **Accessibility**: a bridging point or ford, a gap through hills, a natural harbour or a meeting of routes lets people travel in and goods be traded; accessible settlements grow faster.
- **Resources**: building materials, fuel (wood or coal) and minerals; many towns grew around a mine or quarry.

**Growth.** A good site lets a settlement start; a good **situation** lets it grow. A village at a crossroads becomes a market town because people from many directions can reach it. A port grows with trade, then industries locate there to process imports. Growth also depends on later factors, such as a railway arriving or a government office being located there.

**Functions** are the main activities of a settlement: market town, port, mining town, administrative centre, resort, residential or commuter settlement. Functions change. Many former mining or fishing villages now act mainly as commuter or tourist settlements, and their services change with them.

## Hierarchy, threshold and range

A **settlement hierarchy** ranks settlements by size and by the number and order of their services: isolated dwelling, hamlet, village, town, city, and in some countries conurbation or megacity. As you go up the hierarchy, settlements become larger, fewer in number, further apart, offer more and higher-order services, and have larger spheres of influence.

| Order | Examples of services | Threshold population | Range | How often used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Small food shop, primary school, place of worship, bus stop | Small | Short: people will not travel far | Daily or weekly |
| **Middle** | Supermarket, secondary school, bank, health centre | Medium | Moderate | Weekly or monthly |
| **High** | Department store, specialist hospital, university, airport, sports stadium | Large | Long: people travel a long way | Rarely |

- **Threshold population** is the minimum number of people needed to support a service and keep it running.
- **Range** is the maximum distance people are willing to travel to use a service.
- **Sphere of influence** is the area served by a settlement or service.

**Why the hierarchy exists.** A service can only survive where its threshold is met within its range. A specialist hospital needs a very large population, so it can only be found in a large city with a large sphere of influence. A small food shop needs few customers and people want it nearby, so it appears in almost every village. This is why there are many small settlements with low-order services and few large ones with high-order services.

## Worked data example (fictional area, invented data)

The table shows settlements in the fictional Marrow District.

| Settlement | Population | Number of services | Highest-order service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kelby | 48,000 | 312 | General hospital |
| Ashwell | 6,500 | 58 | Secondary school |
| Bramley | 1,900 | 14 | Health centre |
| Corran | 620 | 4 | Primary school |
| Dunholt | 85 | 0 | None |

- **Ranking**: Kelby (town), Ashwell (small town or large village), Bramley and Corran (villages), Dunholt (hamlet).
- **Services per 1,000 people**: Kelby 312 ÷ 48 = 6.5; Corran 4 ÷ 0.62 = 6.5. The ratio is similar, but Kelby's services are of a much higher order and include ones whose thresholds Corran could never meet.
- **Threshold reasoning**: if a supermarket chain needs 5,000 people within 10 km, Ashwell can support one only if its surrounding villages are counted; Bramley cannot on its own.

A data question might then ask why Corran residents travel to Kelby for clothing: clothing shops are middle- to high-order comparison services with thresholds far above Corran's population, and people are willing to travel the range to Kelby for them, perhaps combining several errands in one trip.

## Measuring a sphere of influence

Fieldwork or Paper 2 questions may ask how to measure a sphere of influence:

1. Choose a service, such as a supermarket or secondary school.
2. Ask a sample of users where they have travelled from (a questionnaire), or use a postcode or address list provided by the service.
3. Plot each origin on a map and draw desire lines to the service.
4. Draw a boundary around most of the origins to show the sphere of influence.

High-order services will show long desire lines; low-order services short ones.

## Changing service provision

In many high-income countries, rural low-order services have declined: village shops, post offices and bank branches have closed because more people own cars and shop at large supermarkets in towns, and more people shop and bank online. Responses include mobile libraries and shops, community-run shops and services shared in one building.

In many lower-income countries, rural service provision is increasing but remains limited. Periodic markets move between villages on different days, so each village is visited often enough for traders to meet their threshold. Mobile phones have brought some services to remote areas: mobile money, such as M-Pesa, launched in Kenya in 2007, lets people send and receive money without a bank branch.

## Building your case study

For the area you study, prepare:

- the settlements in the area, ranked as a hierarchy, with populations if you know them;
- the site and situation of the main settlement, linked to its growth and functions;
- named services at low, middle and high order, and where they are found;
- the sphere of influence of the largest settlement, with evidence;
- how service provision has changed and one response, with a judgement on how well it has worked.

## Model answer: explain the hierarchy of services

*Question: Explain why high-order services are found only in large settlements.*

> High-order services such as a university have a large threshold population (knowledge). They need many thousands of users to make enough money or justify the cost, so they can only survive where a large population lives within reach (application). People will travel a long way to use them because they are used rarely and offer specialised goods or services, so the service draws users from a wide sphere of influence (analysis). Only large settlements combine a big local population with good transport links, which is why these services are rare and found at the top of the hierarchy (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Confusing threshold (minimum people) with range (maximum distance).
- Giving a site factor ("a bridging point") without explaining why it helped the settlement.
- Naming patterns without the feature they follow or cluster around.
- Describing the hierarchy only by population, with no reference to services.
- Case studies with no named settlements or services.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a pattern from a map: name it and give evidence from the map.
- **Explain** a site: factor → advantage for early settlers.
- **Suggest** why a service is located somewhere: threshold, range and accessibility.
- **Describe** a sphere of influence: its size, shape and direction, with distances if given.
