## Section A

**1.** Compare the open circulatory system of an insect with the closed circulatory system of a mammal. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the advantage to a mammal of a double circulatory system. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the structure of a capillary suits it to its function. **[3]**

**4.** A person at rest has a stroke volume of 64 cm³ and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute.

**(a)** Calculate the cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** After a period of training, the same cardiac output is produced with a stroke volume of 90 cm³. Calculate the new resting heart rate. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Insect: haemolymph is pumped by a tubular heart and flows freely in the body cavity, bathing the organs; mammal: blood stays inside vessels [1]. Insect haemolymph flows at low pressure and slowly; mammalian blood flows at high pressure and quickly [1]. Insect haemolymph does not carry oxygen (the tracheal system does); mammalian blood carries oxygen bound to haemoglobin [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Blood returns to the heart after the lungs and is pumped again, so it reaches the body at high pressure and flows quickly, supplying a high metabolic rate [1]. The lungs receive blood at lower pressure, which does not damage their capillaries [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The wall is one endothelial cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short [1]. The lumen is narrow, about the diameter of a red blood cell, so cells pass close to the wall and flow is slow, giving time for exchange [1]. Gaps between endothelial cells let plasma escape to form tissue fluid; capillaries form large networks with a large total surface area [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 64 × 78 = 4992 cm³ min⁻¹ [1] = **4.99 dm³ min⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** 4992 ÷ 90 = 55.5, so about **55 beats per minute** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The **bicuspid** (mitral) valve [1]. Tendinous cords (chordae tendineae) attached to the valve and the ventricle wall [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Any three [1 each]: tissue fluid has no red blood cells; very few plasma proteins; fewer white blood cells; less glucose and oxygen and more carbon dioxide and waste than arterial plasma.

**(b)** Oncotic pressure in the blood is lower [1], so less water returns to the capillaries at the venule end by osmosis and tissue fluid builds up [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** At 0.10 s the bicuspid valve is **closed** and the semilunar valve is **closed** [1]: ventricular pressure is above atrial pressure but below aortic pressure [1]. At 0.15 s the bicuspid valve is **closed** and the semilunar valve is **open**, because ventricular pressure is now above aortic pressure [1].

**(b)** The elastic tissue in the aorta wall stretched during systole [1]. It recoils during diastole, keeping pressure up and pushing blood onward [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* For each valve, compare the pressures on its two sides; the higher pressure behind a valve opens it.

**8.** The SAN in the wall of the right atrium sends out a wave of excitation, as cardiac muscle is myogenic [1]. The wave spreads across the atria, which contract; non-conducting tissue stops it reaching the ventricles directly [1]. The AVN passes it on after a short delay, down the bundle of His to the apex and into the Purkyne tissue in the ventricle walls [1]. The ventricles contract from the apex (the lowest point of the heart) upwards, squeezing blood up and out through the arteries at the top [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** An **ectopic heartbeat**: an early beat (0.38 s) followed by a longer pause (0.84 s) [1].

**(b)** Mean of regular intervals = (0.62 + 0.60 + 0.61 + 0.61 + 0.60) ÷ 5 = 0.608 s [1]. Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.608 = **99 beats per minute** [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Fetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult haemoglobin [1]. At the low partial pressure in the placenta, adult haemoglobin releases oxygen [1] and fetal haemoglobin takes it up, so oxygen passes from mother to fetus [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The curve is shifted to the right (the Bohr effect) [1], because exercising muscle produces more carbon dioxide (and the blood is more acidic) [1]. Haemoglobin has a lower affinity for oxygen, so at the same partial pressure more oxygen is released to the muscle [1]. **[3]**

**12.** H⁺ ions from carbonic acid bind to haemoglobin, which causes oxyhaemoglobin to release oxygen to the tissues [1]. Removing H⁺ from solution stops the pH of the blood falling, so haemoglobin acts as a buffer [1]. **[2]**

**13.** The left ventricle has the thicker muscular wall (and leads to the aorta) [1]. Any one precaution: cut away from the body on a dissecting board; wear gloves or wash hands afterwards; handle scalpels with care [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the insect system closed or saying insect blood carries oxygen.
- Describing capillary walls as "thin" without "one cell thick" and the link to diffusion distance.
- Using "pressure" without saying which chambers or vessels are being compared.
- Leaving out the AVN delay or the non-conducting tissue.
- Including an ectopic interval when averaging the heart rate.
- Saying a right shift "increases" affinity.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does training lower resting heart rate?**
Training can increase the stroke volume, so the heart delivers the same cardiac output with fewer beats per minute, as Question 4 shows.

**How do I read valve events from a pressure graph?**
Find where the lines cross. When ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure the AV valve closes; when it rises above arterial pressure the semilunar valve opens; the reverse crossings give the other two events.

**What is the difference between an ectopic beat and fibrillation?**
An ectopic beat is a single extra early beat in an otherwise regular rhythm. Fibrillation is continuous irregular, uncoordinated activity with no clear repeating pattern.
