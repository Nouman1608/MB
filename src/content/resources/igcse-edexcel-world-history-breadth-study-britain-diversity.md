Breadth Study B8, "Diversity, rights and equality in Britain, 1914–2010", is one of eight breadth studies
in change in Section B of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It traces changing
attitudes to, and opportunities for, women, ethnic minorities, disabled people, sexual minorities and the
working class over nearly a century — through two world wars, mass immigration from the Commonwealth, the
civil rights legislation of the 1960s and 1970s, and campaigns that culminated in the Equality Act 2010.
The specification notes that some of the legislation named applies to England and Wales only.

## Syllabus coverage

The specification (Issue 5, February 2026) names five **themes**: changing attitudes and opportunities for
race and gender; for disability and class; the influence of immigration and diversity; the role of protest
and pressure groups; and the role of government. The (c) essay normally draws on two or more key topics.
The five **key topics** are:

1. **The fight for recognition, 1914–28** — diversity in 1914; the First World War's impact on women, ethnic
   minorities, the working class and disabled people; changes in voting rights, the Sex Disqualification
   (Removal) Act (1919), the Education Act (1918) and the Aliens Restriction (Amendment) Act (1919).
2. **The impact of Depression and war, 1928–45** — eugenics, unemployment and the Jarrow Crusade, immigration
   and the BUF, the Second World War, national service, aliens, the "colour bar" and Learie Constantine, and
   the Disabled Persons (Employment) Act (1944).
3. **Consequences of war and the end of empire, 1944–62** — Windrush and Commonwealth immigration, Claudia
   Jones and the Caribbean Carnival, the Commonwealth Immigrants Act (1962), changing attitudes to women,
   sexual minorities and disabled people (Ludwig Guttmann and the Paralympic movement, the Wolfenden Report
   1957), Butler's Education Act (1944) and the NHS (1948).
4. **Changes in civil rights, c1962–86** — feminism and the Dagenham strike, the Bristol Bus Boycott,
   comprehensive schools and university grants, the Warnock Report (1978), disability and sexual-minority
   rights; Enoch Powell, the anti-immigration movement and the Immigration Act (1971); the Race Relations
   Act (1965), Sexual Offences Act (1967), Chronically Sick and Disabled Persons Act (1970), Equal Pay Act
   (1970) and Sex Discrimination Act (1975).
5. **Changes in opportunity and culture, 1986–2010** — the National Council for Civil Liberties, the
   Stephen Lawrence case, third-wave feminism, the LGBT Foundation and the British Council of Organisations
   of Disabled People; diversity in the arts; education; barriers including religious intolerance,
   institutional racism, sexism, Section 28 (1988), disability discrimination and inequality; and the
   Equality Act (2010).

## What changed between 1914 and 1928?

In 1914 women could not vote in parliamentary elections, many working men had no vote either, disabled
people relied largely on charity, and Britain's small black and Asian communities faced prejudice. The
**First World War** brought women into munitions factories, transport and the services, and hundreds of
thousands of men returned disabled, including from shell shock. The **Representation of the People Act
1918** gave the vote to all men over 21 and to women over 30 who met a property qualification; equal voting
at 21 followed in **1928**. The **Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act 1919** opened the professions and juries
to women, and the **Education Act 1918** raised the school-leaving age to 14. But after riots in port towns
in 1919, the **Aliens Restriction (Amendment) Act 1919** tightened controls on foreigners.

## How did Depression and war affect equality, 1928–45?

**Eugenics** — the idea of "improving" the population by discouraging disabled and poor people from having
children — was influential. Mass **unemployment** hit industrial areas hardest; the **Jarrow Crusade** (1936)
marched to London to petition for work. Jewish refugees arrived from Nazi Europe, and Oswald Mosley's
**British Union of Fascists** stirred antisemitism, leading to the Battle of Cable Street (1936). In the
**Second World War**, conscription (**national service**) extended to unmarried women from 1941; many
"enemy aliens" were interned in 1940. A "**colour bar**" excluded black people from some hotels and dance
halls; the West Indian cricketer **Learie Constantine** sued the Imperial Hotel in London after it refused to
accommodate him in 1943, and won. The **Disabled Persons (Employment) Act 1944** required larger employers to
employ a quota of registered disabled people.

## How did the end of war and empire change Britain, 1944–62?

**Butler's Education Act (1944)** made secondary education free for all, and the **NHS (1948)** provided free
healthcare. The arrival of the *Empire Windrush* from the Caribbean (1948) symbolised **Commonwealth
immigration**; migrants from the Caribbean, Africa and South Asia filled labour shortages in transport, the
NHS and industry but often faced discrimination in housing and work, and violence such as the Notting Hill
riots (1958). **Claudia Jones** founded the *West Indian Gazette* and organised an indoor Caribbean Carnival
(1959), a forerunner of the Notting Hill Carnival. The **Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962** introduced work
vouchers, restricting immigration for the first time. **Ludwig Guttmann** organised the Stoke Mandeville Games
for disabled ex-servicemen (1948), the origin of the **Paralympic** movement. The **Wolfenden Report (1957)**
recommended decriminalising private homosexual acts between consenting adult men.

## What civil rights changes happened, c1962–86?

The **Bristol Bus Boycott (1963)** forced a bus company to end its ban on black crews. In 1968 women sewing
machinists at Ford's **Dagenham** plant struck for equal recognition, helping lead to Barbara Castle's **Equal
Pay Act (1970)**. Feminist campaigners pressed for further change, achieved in part by the **Sex Discrimination
Act (1975)**. The **Race Relations Act (1965)** banned discrimination in public places (strengthened in 1968 and
1976). The **Sexual Offences Act (1967)** enacted Wolfenden's recommendation in England and Wales. The
**Chronically Sick and Disabled Persons Act (1970)** required councils to provide services for disabled people,
and the **Warnock Report (1978)** promoted including children with special educational needs in mainstream
schools. **Comprehensive schools** spread from 1965 and **university grants** widened access. Barriers remained:
**Enoch Powell**'s "Rivers of Blood" speech (1968) stirred anti-immigration feeling, the National Front grew,
and the **Immigration Act (1971)** further restricted entry.

## How did opportunity and culture change, 1986–2010?

The **National Council for Civil Liberties** (Liberty) campaigned on rights. The racist murder of **Stephen
Lawrence** (1993) and the failed police investigation led to the Macpherson Report (1999), which found the
Metropolitan Police "institutionally racist". **Third-wave feminism** challenged continuing sexism, and the
**LGBT Foundation** and the **British Council of Organisations of Disabled People** campaigned for their
communities. **Diversity in the arts** grew — in music, literature, theatre and the Notting Hill Carnival —
and higher education expanded. Barriers included religious intolerance, institutional racism, **Section 28**
(1988), which banned local councils from "promoting" homosexuality until its repeal (2003 in England and
Wales), disability discrimination (addressed partly by the Disability Discrimination Act 1995) and continuing
inequality. The **Equality Act (2010)** brought earlier anti-discrimination laws into one framework protecting
characteristics including race, sex, disability, sexual orientation and religion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Was government or protest more important?** A classic (c) question: protest (Bristol, Dagenham, Lawrence
campaign) often prompted government action (1965, 1970, 1975, 2010 Acts).

**Did the world wars help equality?** They brought opportunities and votes for women and services for disabled
people, but also internment and the colour bar.

**Which groups gained least?** Answers often weigh class inequality, which persisted despite education reform.
