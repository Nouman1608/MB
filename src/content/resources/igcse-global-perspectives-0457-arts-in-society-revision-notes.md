These notes condense the [Arts in society study guide](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-arts-in-society/); read that first for full explanations and worked paragraphs.

They follow the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). Arts in society is one of the topics in the section 3 topic list, which is used for all three components in section 4: the Written Exam, the Individual Report and the Team Project. **Topic knowledge is not assessed**: the syllabus says "knowledge of content is not assessed in this syllabus". These notes show how to use the arts to practise the assessed skills of research, analysis, evaluation, reflection, communication and collaboration.

Test yourself with the [Arts in society practice questions](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-arts-in-society-practice/). Course links: [IGCSE Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and [IGCSE Global Perspectives checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## The assessment in one table

| Component (section 4) | What it asks you to do with a topic like the arts | Weighting |
|---|---|---|
| Component 1 Written Exam, 1 hour 25 minutes, 70 marks | Four compulsory questions on sources about one global issue: analyse sources; evaluate research and suggest how to test a claim; evaluate arguments; assess actions | 35% |
| Component 2 Individual Report, 60 marks | A 1500–2000 word report on a global issue that divides opinion, titled with your own global research question | 30% |
| Component 3 Team Project, 70 marks | A team identifies a local issue, plans and carries out an action and measures its success; each member writes a Reflective Paper | 35% |

Assessment objectives (section 2): **AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation**; **AO2 Reflection**; **AO3 Communication and collaboration**. Component 1 is all AO1. Command words printed in section 4: Analyse, Describe, Evaluate, Explain, Identify, Suggest.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Issue | "An important subject or problem for discussion" (syllabus definition) |
| Perspective | "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning" (syllabus definition) |
| Evidence | Information about a global issue that helps develop understanding or prove something true or false (syllabus wording, paraphrased) |
| Argument | A series of statements with reasons and evidence supporting a claim (syllabus wording, paraphrased) |
| Claim | A statement presented as true, which may or may not be supported |
| Fact | A statement that can be checked and shown true or false |
| Opinion | A personal view that cannot be proved |
| Generalisation | A broad statement about a whole group based on limited cases |
| Prediction | A statement about what will happen |
| Value | A belief about what matters, such as fairness or access |
| Bias | A one-sided presentation that favours one view |
| Vested interest | Something a person or group gains if a view is accepted |
| Restitution | Returning a cultural object to the people or country it was taken from |
| Cultural heritage | Objects, places and practices a community values and passes on |
| Access to the arts | Whether people can take part, which depends on cost, distance, time and confidence |

## Issues inside the topic

- **Public funding**: should taxes pay for theatres, galleries and community arts?
- **Restitution**: should museums return objects taken during colonial rule?
- **Arts in education**: should arts subjects be compulsory or protected in schools?
- **AI and creative work**: should AI systems be trained on artists' work without permission?
- **Street art**: protect it as public art, or remove it as damage?
- **Censorship**: when, if ever, should a work be withdrawn because it offends?

Each can be seen at **global**, **national** and **local** level. For example, restitution is global (objects held across continents), national (laws on what national museums may give away) and local (one museum's single item).

## Real-world examples at a glance

You are not marked for knowing these, but accurate examples make your research and reasoning stronger.

| Example | What happened | Use it for | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benin Bronzes | Many taken by British forces from the Kingdom of Benin (present-day Nigeria) in 1897; Germany and Nigeria signed a joint declaration on returns on 1 July 2022 | Perspectives on restitution at national and global level | Shows returns can happen, but each museum and country has its own laws, so one case does not prove a general trend |
| Parthenon Marbles | Held in the British Museum; Greece asks for their return; the British Museum Act 1963 limits disposal of objects | How law shapes a course of action | Law explains why a museum acts as it does, but not whether the law itself is fair |
| Guggenheim Museum Bilbao | Designed by Frank Gehry, opened in 1997; linked to the phrase "the Bilbao effect" | Causes and consequences; correlation versus causation | Other investment happened at the same time, so the building cannot be credited with all the change |
| UDHR Article 27 (1948) | States a right "to participate in the cultural life of the community, to enjoy the arts" | Values-based arguments | Supports a value, not a particular level of spending |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue.** "Arts in society" is the topic. "Should governments fund the arts?" is an issue.
- **Opinion vs perspective.** An opinion can be unsupported. A perspective rests on evidence and reasoning and belongs to a person or group with reasons for holding it.
- **Fact vs claim.** A fact can be checked. A claim is presented as true and needs testing.
- **Value vs evidence.** "Culture is a right" is a value. "Ticket prices rose last year" is evidence. Values cannot be proved; evidence can be tested.
- **Correlation vs causation.** Students who take music may get higher grades, but music lessons may not be the cause.
- **Bias vs vested interest.** Bias is in how a source presents material. Vested interest is a reason the author might be biased. Show the bias from the text; do not just name the interest.
- **Global vs local issue.** The Individual Report needs a global issue; the Team Project starts from a local one.
- **Course of action vs outcome.** The action is what someone does (a youth arts pass). The outcome is its effect (more young people attending). Always say how you would measure the outcome.

## Checking a source in four questions

1. **Who wrote it, and what do they gain?** An arts centre writing about arts funding has a vested interest.
2. **What kind of statement is it?** Fact, opinion, claim, prediction, value or generalisation.
3. **What evidence supports it, and how was it gathered?** Who was surveyed, how many, and who was left out?
4. **Does the conclusion follow?** Watch for a jump from one town to a whole country, or from correlation to cause.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** Evaluate the possible courses of action a town council could take to increase young people's participation in the arts. (This mirrors the skill in Component 1 of assessing actions; it is not a past-paper question.)

1. **Set up the issue.** State the problem and the evidence for it, and note any weaknesses in that evidence (for example, a survey of existing users only).
2. **Criteria.** Name the criteria you will use: effectiveness, cost, feasibility, who benefits, sustainability.
3. **Action 1: a one-day festival.** Visible and popular; but a single event may not build lasting habits, and it may draw mostly existing arts-goers.
4. **Action 2: a free or discounted youth pass.** Tackles cost directly; but weak if the real barrier is time, transport or confidence.
5. **Action 3: artist workshops in schools.** Reaches students who never visit a venue; but depends on school time and long-term funding.
6. **Perspectives.** Show how taxpayers, young people, artists and the council might each judge these actions, and why.
7. **Judgement.** Choose one action, or a combination, and justify it against your criteria. Say how success would be measured and what evidence would change your mind.

## Quick self-test

1. Does the 0457 syllabus assess knowledge of the arts?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Is "Arts in society" an issue or a topic?
4. Classify: "If the gallery closes, fewer tourists will visit the town."
5. Classify: "Teenagers never go to the theatre."
6. What is a vested interest? Give an arts example.
7. Why is a survey of people already attending an arts centre weak evidence about the whole town?
8. Rewrite "Street art" as a global research question.
9. What level of issue must a Team Project start from?
10. Name two criteria for judging a course of action.
11. Name two forms of Evidence of Action that the syllabus lists.
12. Why does UDHR Article 27 not prove that a government should increase arts spending?

### Answers

1. No. The syllabus says knowledge of content is not assessed; the topic is a context for practising skills.
2. "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning."
3. A topic. An issue is a specific problem inside it that divides opinion.
4. A prediction.
5. A generalisation (and an opinion presented as fact).
6. Something a person or group gains if a view is accepted, for example a gallery arguing for more public arts funding.
7. It leaves out the people who do not attend, who are the ones whose barriers you need to understand.
8. For example: "Should street art be protected as public art or removed as damage to property?"
9. A local issue.
10. Any two of: effectiveness, cost, feasibility, who benefits, sustainability, unintended effects.
11. Any two of: posters, leaflets, web pages, videos (10 minutes maximum), presentations, photographs of an event.
12. It states a value (a right to take part in cultural life) but does not say how that right should be met or how much should be spent.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about the arts themselves (famous artists, art movements) instead of analysing sources, perspectives and evidence.
- Naming a group's perspective without explaining why they hold it.
- Saying "this source is biased" without quoting the words or omissions that show it.
- Treating figures from an arts organisation or ticket company as neutral.
- Treating a correlation (arts and grades, galleries and growth) as proof of cause.
- Answering a value-based argument with a fact that addresses a different question.
- Proposing an action without saying who does it, how, and how success is measured.
- Using a topic word as an Individual Report title instead of a question that divides opinion.
- Forgetting that the Team Project must start from a local issue.
