Section 7.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks how globalisation changes who people think they are. The syllabus lists three strands: the impact of globalisation on identity, including ethnic revitalisation, cultural defence and hybrid identity; cultural divergence versus cultural convergence/homogenisation; and the role of Western ideology in shaping identity and the concept of Westernisation. It connects to the key concept of Socialisation, culture and identity and is examined in Paper 4 Section A.

The [globalisation debates guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-debates-and-perspectives/) introduces Ritzer, Bhabha, Hall, Bruce, Huntington and Barber. This page adds frameworks that organise the debate, further evidence for each identity response, and a sharper treatment of Westernisation as ideology.

## A framework: three possible outcomes

Stuart Hall, in "The Question of Cultural Identity" (1992), set out three possible consequences of globalisation for identity:

1. **National identities are eroding** as cultural homogenisation spreads.
2. **National and local identities are being strengthened** by resistance to globalisation.
3. **National identities are declining but new hybrid identities are taking their place.**

Jan Nederveen Pieterse, in *Globalization and Culture: Global Mélange* (2004), proposed a similar set of three paradigms: **clash** (cultures in conflict, as in Huntington), **McDonaldization** (convergence, as in Ritzer) and **hybridisation** (mixing). Using either framework gives an essay a clear structure: each outcome becomes a section, and the judgement asks which best fits the evidence.

## Ethnic revitalisation

Ethnic revitalisation is the renewal of pride in, and active revival of, an ethnic group's language, customs or traditions. Globalisation can provoke it in two ways: contact with dominant global culture makes a group aware of what it may lose, and global media and travel connect dispersed members of a group.

**Examples well documented:**

- **Te reo Māori** in Aotearoa New Zealand: after a long decline, revival efforts included Māori-language pre-schools from the early 1980s, and the Māori Language Act 1987 made it an official language.
- **Welsh**: the Welsh Language Act 1993 placed Welsh and English on an equal basis in public business in Wales.

**Evaluation.** Revitalisation can be partly symbolic, practised at festivals rather than in daily life. It may also be shaped by global forces itself, for example through heritage tourism or international funding for minority languages.

## Cultural defence and resistance identity

Cultural defence occurs when a group protects its culture against a perceived external threat, often by strengthening religion or nationalism. Bruce uses the idea to explain religious vitality where religion carries a group's identity.

**Manuel Castells**, in *The Power of Identity* (1997), distinguished three kinds of identity in a networked, globalising society:

- **legitimising identity**, promoted by dominant institutions to extend their authority;
- **resistance identity**, built by groups who feel devalued or excluded, creating "trenches" of resistance — religious fundamentalism, ethnic nationalism and territorial movements are examples;
- **project identity**, where people build a new identity that seeks to transform society, as in feminism or environmentalism.

Giddens, in *Runaway World* (1999), presented fundamentalism as a defence of tradition in a world where tradition is increasingly questioned. Both accounts link identity directly to globalisation: the more global flows unsettle local life, the stronger defensive identities may become.

## Hybrid identity

Hybrid identities combine elements of more than one culture into something new, rather than choosing one or the other.

- **Les Back**, in *New Ethnicities and Urban Culture* (1996), studied young people in south London and found them mixing musical styles, language and identities across ethnic boundaries.
- Hall's work on **diaspora** identities shows how migrants and their descendants develop identities that belong fully to neither the society of origin nor the society of settlement.

**Evaluation.** Hybridity may describe young, urban, mobile groups better than older or rural ones. Critics also note that celebrating hybridity can overlook the racism and inequality that limit how freely people can mix identities.

## Convergence versus divergence

| Convergence / homogenisation | Divergence |
|---|---|
| Global brands, media formats and consumer goods spread | Local adaptation (glocalisation) changes meanings |
| Ritzer's McDonaldization: efficiency, calculability, predictability, control | Resistance identities and cultural defence |
| English as a global language of business and science | Revival of minority languages |
| Cultural imperialism: Herbert Schiller, *Mass Communications and American Empire* (1969) | Contra-flows from non-Western centres |

**Contra-flows.** Daya Kishan Thussu (2007) used "contra-flow" for media flowing from outside the West to global audiences, such as Indian film or East Asian television and music. These flows weaken the claim that convergence simply means Americanisation.

**Active audiences.** John Tomlinson, in *Cultural Imperialism* (1991), argued that the cultural imperialism thesis assumes audiences passively absorb Western meanings, when in practice people interpret imported media through their own culture. This links to active audience models in the media section of Paper 4.

## Western ideology and Westernisation

Westernisation is the spread of Western values, institutions and lifestyles — individualism, consumerism, secularism, liberal democracy — often presented as the natural endpoint of modernisation.

**Ideology.** The concept becomes ideological when Western ways are presented as superior or universal. **Edward Said**'s *Orientalism* (1978) argued that Western scholarship and culture constructed "the East" as backward, irrational and exotic, defining the West by contrast as rational and modern. Such representations shape identity on both sides: Western audiences learn to see themselves as the standard, and others may internalise, resist or rework the image.

**How Western ideology shapes identity in practice:**

- consumer identities built around global brands and lifestyles;
- beauty and body ideals spread through global media and advertising;
- individualistic ideas of choice in marriage, career and religion.

**Evaluation.** Westernisation is uneven and often selective: people may adopt Western technology and consumer goods while rejecting Western values. Non-Western economic powers also export their own cultural products, and resistance identities are a direct response to Westernisation.

## Worked paragraph

> Hall suggests one outcome of globalisation is the strengthening of local identities through resistance. Castells's concept of resistance identity explains this: groups who feel excluded by global economic and cultural change build defensive identities around religion, ethnicity or territory. The rise of movements defending a religious or national way of life against perceived Western influence fits this pattern. However, Back's research on young people in south London shows the opposite tendency, as they mixed cultures rather than defending one. This suggests the response to globalisation varies by age, class and location rather than following a single direction.

## Building a 35-mark essay on convergence

1. Define convergence, divergence and Westernisation.
2. For convergence: Ritzer, Schiller, global brands, English.
3. Against: glocalisation, contra-flows (Thussu), active audiences (Tomlinson).
4. Identity responses: revitalisation, cultural defence (Bruce, Castells, Giddens), hybridity (Back, Hall).
5. Frameworks: Hall's three outcomes or Nederveen Pieterse's three paradigms.
6. Judgement: for example, convergence in consumption alongside divergence in identity.

## Common mistakes

- Treating hybridity, glocalisation and Westernisation as the same thing.
- Assuming cultural defence is always religious; nationalism and ethnicity count too.
- Using Huntington uncritically; his civilisations are criticised as too unified.
- Forgetting the "role of Western ideology" strand, which needs the concept of ideology, not just a list of Western products.
