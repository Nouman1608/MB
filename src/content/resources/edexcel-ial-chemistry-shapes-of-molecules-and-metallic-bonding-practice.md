## Questions

**1.** Define each term.

**(a)** Bond length **[1]**
**(b)** Bond angle **[1]**

**2.** State three principles of electron-pair repulsion theory that are used to predict the shape of a simple
molecule. **[3]**

**3.** This question is about gaseous BeCl₂ and BCl₃.

**(a)** Explain why a molecule of gaseous BeCl₂ is linear. **[2]**
**(b)** State the shape of a BCl₃ molecule and its Cl-B-Cl bond angle. **[2]**

**4.** For each species, predict the shape and bond angle using electron-pair repulsion theory.

**(a)** The amide ion, NH₂⁻ **[1]**
**(b)** The tetrafluoroborate ion, BF₄⁻ **[1]**
**(c)** The hexafluorophosphate ion, PF₆⁻ **[1]**
**(d)** Boron triiodide, BI₃ **[1]**

**5.** Ammonia reacts with hydrogen ions:

NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺

Explain why the H-N-H bond angle increases from about 107° in NH₃ to 109.5° in NH₄⁺. **[4]**

**6.** This question is about ethene, C₂H₄.

**(a)** State the shape around each carbon atom and the H-C-H bond angle. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the C=C bond is treated as a single region of electron density, and state whether all six
atoms lie in one plane. **[2]**

**7.** Phosphorus(V) chloride has different structures as a gas and as a solid.

**(a)** State the shape of a gaseous PCl₅ molecule and give its two bond angles. **[2]**
**(b)** State how many Cl-P-Cl angles of 90° there are in one gaseous PCl₅ molecule. **[1]**
**(c)** Solid phosphorus(V) chloride contains PCl₄⁺ and PCl₆⁻ ions. Predict the shape and bond angle of each ion.
Show how you counted the electron pairs. **[4]**

**8.** Describe the structure of a metal and state what is meant by metallic bonding. **[3]**

**9.** Aluminium is used in overhead power cables.

**(a)** Explain why aluminium conducts electricity. **[2]**
**(b)** Each aluminium atom contributes three electrons to the delocalised electron sea. A 1.00 m length of
one cable's aluminium core has a mass of 1.08 kg. Calculate the number of delocalised electrons in this
length of core. Use Ar(Al) = 27.0 and the Avogadro constant L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Give your answer to
3 significant figures. **[3]**

**10.** Use these melting temperatures.

| Metal | Melting temperature / °C |
|---|---|
| lithium | 181 |
| sodium | 98 |
| potassium | 63 |
| rubidium | 39 |
| calcium | 842 |

**(a)** Describe and explain the trend in melting temperature from lithium to rubidium. **[3]**
**(b)** The Ca²⁺ ion is larger than the Li⁺ ion. Explain why calcium still has a much higher melting temperature
than lithium. **[3]**
**(c)** A student says: "When potassium melts, all of its metallic bonding is broken." Explain why this statement
is wrong. **[2]**

**11.** This question is about aluminium chloride.

**(a)** At high temperatures, aluminium chloride vapour contains AlCl₃ molecules. Predict and explain the shape
of an AlCl₃ molecule and its Cl-Al-Cl bond angle. **[3]**
**(b)** At lower temperatures the vapour contains Al₂Cl₆, in which each aluminium atom accepts a lone pair from a
chlorine atom of the other AlCl₃ unit. State the number of bonding pairs and
lone pairs around each aluminium atom in Al₂Cl₆, and the arrangement of chlorine atoms around each aluminium.
**[3]**
**(c)** Aluminium chloride reacts with chloride ions to form AlCl₄⁻. State the shape of AlCl₄⁻ and its Cl-Al-Cl
bond angle, and name one other species with the same shape. **[2]**

**12.** This question is about molecules that contain multiple bonds.

**(a)** Hydrogen cyanide has the structure H-C≡N. Predict the shape of the molecule and the H-C-N bond angle.
Give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Methanal has the structure H₂C=O. State the shape around the carbon atom and the H-C-H bond angle you
would predict. **[2]**
**(c)** Use these values: N-N bond length in hydrazine, N₂H₄ = 0.145 nm; N≡N bond length in nitrogen,
N₂ = 0.110 nm. Explain why the N≡N bond is shorter than the N-N bond. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage by which the N≡N bond is shorter than the N-N bond. Give your answer to
3 significant figures. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The distance between the **nuclei** of two atoms joined by a covalent bond [1].
**(b)** The angle between two bonds that meet at the same atom [1].
*Examiner insight:* write "nuclei" in the bond length definition; "the distance between two atoms" is vague and risks losing the mark.

**2.** Electron pairs in the outer shell of the central atom repel each other [1]. They take up positions as far apart as possible, so that repulsion is a minimum [1]. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs (lone-lone > lone-bond > bond-bond) [1].
*Examiner insight:* three marks need three distinct ideas; "atoms repel" names the wrong particles and gains nothing.

**3. (a)** Beryllium has **two bonding pairs and no lone pairs** [1]. The pairs repel to opposite sides of the Be atom, so the molecule is linear, **180°** [1].
**(b)** **Trigonal planar** [1]; **120°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a) state both the bonding pairs and the absence of lone pairs; "two bonds" alone is not an explanation.

**4. (a)** N: 5 - 2 + 1 = 4 electrons left, so 2 lone pairs and 2 bonding pairs: **bent, about 104.5°** [1].
**(b)** B: 3 - 4 + 1 = 0, so no lone pairs and 4 bonding pairs: **tetrahedral, 109.5°** [1].
**(c)** P: 5 - 6 + 1 = 0, so no lone pairs and 6 bonding pairs: **octahedral, 90°** [1].
**(d)** B: 3 - 3 = 0, so no lone pairs and 3 bonding pairs: **trigonal planar, 120°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* each mark needs both shape and angle; ignoring the charge gives NH₂⁻ the wrong number of lone pairs.

**5.** In NH₃ nitrogen has **three bonding pairs and one lone pair** [1]. The lone pair repels more strongly than bonding pairs, pushing the N-H bonds closer, so the angle is about 107° [1]. The lone pair forms a **dative covalent bond** to H⁺, so NH₄⁺ has **four bonding pairs and no lone pairs** [1]. These repel equally, giving a regular tetrahedron, **109.5°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* the key link is that the lone pair becomes a bonding pair; "NH₄⁺ has four bonds" alone misses why the angle changes.

**6. (a)** **Trigonal planar** around each carbon [1]; H-C-H about **120°** [1].
**(b)** Both pairs of the double bond lie in the same region between the two carbon atoms, so they act as one region [1]. Yes, all six atoms lie **in one plane** [1].
*Examiner insight:* counting the C=C as two regions leads to a tetrahedral answer of 109.5°, which earns nothing.

**7. (a)** **Trigonal bipyramidal** [1]; **120° and 90°** [1].
**(b)** **6** (each of the two axial bonds is at 90° to each of the three equatorial bonds) [1].
**(c)** PCl₄⁺: 5 - 4 - 1 = 0, giving 4 bonding pairs with 0 lone pairs [1]; the ion is **tetrahedral, 109.5°** [1]. PCl₆⁻: 5 - 6 + 1 = 0, so six bonding pairs and no lone pairs [1]; **octahedral, 90°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* when asked to show your counting, a bare shape is unlikely to earn the counting marks; write the arithmetic, charge included.

**8.** A giant lattice of **positive metal ions** [1] in a **sea of delocalised electrons** [1]. Metallic bonding is the **strong electrostatic attraction** between the metal ions and the delocalised electrons [1].
*Examiner insight:* use "ions", not "atoms", and include "electrostatic"; describing metallic bonding as shared electrons uses covalent language and is not creditworthy.

**9. (a)** Aluminium contains **delocalised electrons** [1]; with a potential difference applied, these mobile electrons drift through the lattice carrying charge [1].
**(b)** n(Al) = 1080 g ÷ 27.0 g mol⁻¹ = 40.0 mol [1]. Moles of delocalised electrons = 3 × 40.0 = 120 mol [1]. Number = 120 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **7.22 × 10²⁵ electrons** [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a) the electrons carry charge, not the ions; in (b) set out each step so correct working stays visible if one line slips.

**10. (a)** Melting temperature **decreases** from lithium to rubidium [1]. All the ions are +1, but the **ionic radius increases** down the group [1]. The delocalised electrons are further from each ion's centre, so attraction is weaker and less energy is needed to overcome it [1].
**(b)** Ca²⁺ has **twice the charge** of Li⁺ [1]. Calcium gives **two delocalised electrons per atom** compared with one for lithium [1]. These effects outweigh the larger ionic radius, so the electrostatic attraction between ions and delocalised electrons is stronger, and much more energy is needed to overcome it [1].
**(c)** Liquid potassium still contains K⁺ ions attracted to delocalised electrons, and it still conducts [1]. Melting only overcomes enough attraction for the ions to move past each other; separating the atoms completely, on boiling, needs much more energy [1].
*Examiner insight:* finish each comparison with "stronger attraction, so more energy needed"; a list of factors with no link to energy usually falls short.

**11. (a)** Al uses all 3 outer electrons in Al-Cl bonds: **three bonding pairs, no lone pairs** [1]. The pairs repel to be as far apart as possible [1]. The molecule is **trigonal planar**, Cl-Al-Cl **120°** [1].
**(b)** **Four bonding pairs** (three covalent bonds plus the dative bond) [1]; **no lone pairs** [1]; the four chlorine atoms are arranged **(approximately) tetrahedrally** around each aluminium [1].
**(c)** **Tetrahedral, 109.5°** [1]; any one of CH₄, NH₄⁺, BF₄⁻ or another four-bonding-pair species [1].
*Examiner insight:* a dative bond counts as a bonding pair exactly like any other covalent bond, so do not leave it out of the count in (b).

**12. (a)** Carbon has two regions of electron density (the C-H bond and the C≡N bond) and no lone pairs [1]; so the molecule is **linear, 180°** [1].
**(b)** **Trigonal planar** [1]; about **120°** [1].
**(c)** N≡N has **three shared pairs** between the nitrogen atoms, not one [1]. The greater electron density between the nuclei attracts both more strongly, pulling them closer [1].
**(d)** (0.145 - 0.110) ÷ 0.145 × 100 = **24.1%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* a triple bond is still one region, so HCN is like CO₂; in (d) divide by the N-N value, the bond being compared against.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the shape of an ion without counting its charge into the electron total.
- Giving "tetrahedral" for a species with lone pairs, such as NH₂⁻ or NH₃.
- Treating a double or triple bond as more than one region.
- Writing "lone pairs take up more space" instead of "lone pairs repel more strongly".
- Leaving the dative bond out of the bonding-pair count in Al₂Cl₆ or NH₄⁺.
- Explaining a melting-temperature difference by listing factors without linking them to stronger attraction
  and more energy.
