> **HL only.** These are original questions written for Marlbridge on the culture extension of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. The sources are constructed for practice and the data are invented. Questions 6 to 9 follow the pattern of the four Paper 3 questions (3, 6, 6 and 15 marks); the others use practice tariffs.

Related: [Culture study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-culture/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-culture-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the ecological fallacy. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish an etic from an emic approach. **[3]**

**3.** Outline one reason why it is difficult to operationalise "culture" as a variable. **[2]**

**4.** Describe two of the four acculturation strategies in Berry's model. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how globalisation may affect one aspect of health or identity. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the sources (constructed for practice; data are invented). They concern human relationships and the role of culture.

> **Source A (survey).** A researcher gave 150 university students in each of two countries (Country P and Country Q) a questionnaire about how willing they would be to say "no" to a request from a friend. Scores range from 1 (very unwilling) to 10 (very willing).
>
> | | Country P | Country Q |
> |---|---|---|
> | Mean score | 7.4 | 5.1 |
> | Standard deviation | 1.6 | 1.9 |
>
> The questionnaire was written in the language of Country P and translated into the language of Country Q.
>
> **Source B (interviews).** The same researcher carried out semi-structured interviews with 12 students in Country Q. Students said they avoided saying "no" directly in order to protect the friendship and said that people "read between the lines". The researcher was born in Country P and carried out the interviews in her first language with an interpreter.
>
> **Source C (field note).** An exchange student from Country P spent a year in Country Q. She reported that at first she thought her friends were unhelpful because they rarely said "no", and that later she understood that "maybe" meant "no".

**6.** Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of Source A. **[3]**

**7.** Analyse the findings in Source A and state a conclusion. **[6]**

**8.** Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias in Source B. **[6]**

**9.** Using Sources A, B and C and your knowledge of the role of culture, discuss the claim that "people in different cultures communicate disagreement in different ways". **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Assuming that an individual member of a cultural group shares the norms or traits of that group **[1]**, even though a group average does not describe every person in it **[1]**.

**2.** An etic approach studies behaviour from outside a culture, using the same measures across cultures to compare them **[1]**. An emic approach studies a culture from the inside, in its own terms and with its own concepts **[1]**. The etic approach risks imposed etic bias; the emic approach gives depth but is harder to generalise **[1]**.

**3.** Culture is not a single measurable variable **[1]**; researchers use proxies such as country, language or a cultural dimension, which may not match individuals' actual cultural experience **[1]**.

**4.** Any two, two marks each: integration (keeping one's own culture while adopting the new culture) **[2]**; assimilation (adopting the new culture and giving up the original) **[2]**; separation (keeping the original culture and avoiding the new one) **[2]**; marginalisation (losing contact with both) **[2]**.

**5.** Any one aspect, for example eating behaviour or identity **[1]**. Cultural diffusion through media and trade can spread norms from one culture to another **[1]**; for example exposure to idealised body images may be linked to changes in attitudes to eating, though correlational evidence cannot show the cause **[1]**.

**6.** Any one, three marks: the questionnaire was translated into the language of Country Q, so words may not carry the same meaning and the groups may not have answered the same question **[1]**; this is a form of imposed etic bias because the instrument was designed in Country P **[1]**; so a difference in scores may reflect the measure, not real differences in willingness **[1]**. Also creditable: the samples are students, so they do not represent each country; scores are self-reported; response styles differ across cultures.

**7.** Analysis, up to 4: Country P students had a higher mean score (7.4) than Country Q students (5.1), a difference of 2.3 points on a 10-point scale **[1]**. The standard deviations are 1.6 and 1.9, so there is variation within each country and the distributions probably overlap **[1]**. The data do not say whether the difference is statistically significant, because no inferential test is reported **[1]**. Both samples are 150 students **[1]**. Conclusion, up to 2: among these students, those in Country P reported greater willingness to say "no" to a friend **[1]**, but this cannot be generalised to everyone in both countries and does not show that culture is the cause **[1]**. Maximum **[6]**.

**8.** Markband guide: 5 to 6 for detailed, explicit reference to the source; 3 to 4 for partly developed use of the source; 1 to 2 for generic points. Indicative content, up to two marks each:
- Researcher bias: she was born in Country P, and her expectations may shape which comments she notices; use reflexivity and a second analyst from Country Q **[2]**.
- Interviewing in her first language with an interpreter may distort meaning; use trained interviewers who speak the participants' language **[2]**.
- Social desirability: students may give the answers they think a visitor wants; build rapport and assure anonymity **[2]**.
- Small sample of 12 students; use purposive sampling to include different backgrounds and report the sampling clearly **[2]**.
- Use of triangulation, for example with observation, and checking interpretations with participants (member checking) **[2]**.
Maximum **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for detailed, well-developed critical analysis that interprets at least three sources and uses own knowledge, evaluates different points of view and reaches a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for the same with less development. Indicative content, up to the markband:
- Source A supports a difference: Country P had a higher mean score (7.4 against 5.1), but the translation, student sample and self-report limit it **[3]**.
- Source B gives a reason from the inside (protecting the friendship), supporting an emic reading that indirect refusal has a different meaning, but the researcher's background and the interpreter are limits on credibility **[3]**.
- Source C is a single anecdote, so it supports the claim only as illustration, but it shows how misunderstanding arises across cultures **[2]**.
- Own knowledge: cultural dimensions such as individualism and collectivism offer a possible explanation of indirectness, and models of cross-cultural communication show strategies such as asking clarifying questions; avoid the ecological fallacy **[3]**.
- Points of view: the claim may be too general, because variation within cultures is large (standard deviations in Source A), and context, such as the closeness of friends, matters **[2]**.
- Conclusion consistent with the arguments, for example that the evidence supports the claim for these samples but that the strength of the claim depends on the quality of the sources and on avoiding stereotypes **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 6:** naming "bias" without saying how the translation matters.
- **Question 7:** repeating the table without a conclusion linked to the figures, or claiming that the difference is significant without a test.
- **Question 9:** using one source only, or using own knowledge with no reference to the sources.
