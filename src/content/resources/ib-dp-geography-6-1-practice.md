## Section A

**1.** Define the crude death rate. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why crude death rates can mislead when comparing two countries. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the shape of the Preston curve. **[2]**

**4.** State how child stunting is defined against international growth standards. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why proportional symbols are more suitable than a choropleth map for showing the number of undernourished people by country. **[2]**

**6.** Outline why a phase classification such as the IPC is used for food crises. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows invented data for two towns.

| Age group | Town P population | Town P deaths per 1,000 | Town Q population | Town Q deaths per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 50 | 80,000 | 2 | 50,000 | 2 |
| 50 and over | 20,000 | 30 | 50,000 | 25 |

**(a)** Calculate the crude death rate for each town. **[2]**

**(b)** Using a standard population of 60,000 aged under 50 and 40,000 aged 50 and over, calculate the age-standardised death rate for each town. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what your answers show about health in the two towns. **[2]**

**8.** Two countries have the same income per person. Country R has a life expectancy well above the Preston curve; Country S is well below it.

**(a)** Suggest two reasons for Country R's position. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons for Country S's position. **[2]**

**9.** An invented district reports: undernourishment 6%; child stunting 12%; child wasting 15%; under-five mortality 90 per 1,000 live births (up from 55 two years earlier).

**(a)** Explain what the wasting and stunting figures together suggest. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest what may have caused the change in under-five mortality, and what action should be prioritised. **[3]**

**10.** Outline two limitations of child anthropometric data. **[2]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of different measures of health for comparing countries at different levels of development. **[10]**

**12.** Examine the view that food security cannot be measured by a single indicator. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of deaths per 1,000 people in a population per year [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The rate depends on age structure as well as health [1]: a country with an older population will have more deaths per 1,000 even if age-for-age its people are healthier [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Life expectancy rises steeply with income per person at low incomes [1] and then flattens at higher incomes, with the whole curve shifting upwards over time [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A child is stunted if their height for age is more than two standard deviations [1] below the median of the World Health Organization Child Growth Standards [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Shading absolute numbers on a choropleth would make large, populous countries look worst simply because of their size [1]; proportional symbols show numbers directly without implying a rate per area or per person [1]. **[2]**

**6.** It grades the severity of a crisis on a common scale from minimal to catastrophe or famine [1], allowing agencies to compare areas and trigger early action before conditions worsen [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Town P: deaths = 160 + 600 = 760 in 100,000, so **7.6 per 1,000** [1]. Town Q: deaths = 100 + 1,250 = 1,350 in 100,000, so **13.5 per 1,000** [1].

**(b)** Town P: 120 + 1,200 = 1,320 in 100,000 = **13.2 per 1,000** [1]. Town Q: 120 + 1,000 = 1,120 in 100,000 = **11.2 per 1,000** [1].

**(c)** Town Q's higher crude rate is caused by its much older population (half aged 50 and over) [1]; once age structure is removed, Town Q has the lower rate because its older residents die at a lower rate (25 against 30 per 1,000), suggesting better health among older people [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Any two, for example: strong, universal primary health care [1]; high female education, improving child health [1]; effective public health such as vaccination, clean water and sanitation [1]. Maximum [2].

**(b)** Any two, for example: conflict damaging health services [1]; a major epidemic such as HIV [1]; high inequality so that average income does not reach the poor [1]; weak governance or low health spending [1]. Maximum [2]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Wasting of 15% is high, showing recent acute food shortage or illness [1], while stunting of 12% is relatively low, suggesting long-term nutrition had been fairly good, so this points to a recent crisis rather than chronic undernutrition [1].

**(b)** The sharp rise from 55 to 90 per 1,000 suggests a recent shock, such as drought, conflict or a disease outbreak, causing acute malnutrition and infection [1] [1]. Priority: emergency nutrition and health care for young children, for example therapeutic feeding and treatment of infections [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Any two, for example: ages are uncertain where births are not registered, affecting height-for-age [1]; measurement needs trained staff and accurate equipment [1]; data cover children only and say little about adults [1]; surveys may miss displaced or remote groups [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Life expectancy:** widely available and comparable; responds strongly to income at low levels [1]; but measures length not quality of life [1].
- **Infant and under-five mortality:** sensitive to basic services, nutrition and sanitation, so useful for lower-income countries [1]; less discriminating between high-income countries where rates are all low [1].
- **Crude death rate:** easy but distorted by age structure; age-standardised rates are needed for fair comparison [1].
- **Healthy life expectancy and DALYs:** capture years lived with illness and the burden of chronic disease, relevant to ageing high-income countries [1]; harder to calculate and depend on disability weights [1].
- **Data quality:** registration of births and deaths is weaker in many lower-income countries [1].
- **Scale:** national averages hide internal inequality [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, mortality measures suit comparisons at low development, morbidity measures at high development, so several are needed [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Four dimensions:** availability, access, utilisation and stability each need different evidence [1].
- **Calorie supply:** measures availability but hides distribution and diet quality [1].
- **Undernourishment prevalence:** better on access but still a modelled national estimate [1].
- **Anthropometric data:** stunting and wasting reveal utilisation and time-scale of undernutrition [1] [1].
- **Experience-based surveys:** capture access problems from households' own reports but rely on honest recall [1].
- **Composite indices:** such as the Global Hunger Index combine indicators, but weightings involve choices and hide which component drives the score [1].
- **Phase scales:** grade sudden crises for action [1].
- **Double burden:** obesity measures are also needed [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, agree, because each indicator captures one dimension or time-scale, so a small set used together gives the most reliable picture [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* both essays reward matching each measure to what it captures and stating clearly where it fails.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing crude rates without considering age structure.
- Forgetting to divide by the total population when calculating a rate.
- Reading high wasting as a long-term problem.
- Naming measures without evaluating them.
