Covered here: sub-topic **C1.1 The particle model** in OCR GCSE (9–1) Chemistry A (Gateway Science), following the J248 specification (Version 4.0, August 2026): learning outcomes C1.1a to C1.1c, the maths outcome CM1.1i and the practical suggestion. ## Questions

**1.** Name the change of state in each example.

**(a)** A puddle dries on a warm pavement. **[1]**
**(b)** Dew forms on grass on a cool morning. **[1]**
**(c)** Solder turns runny when touched by a hot soldering iron. **[1]**
**(d)** Molten glass sets hard as it cools. **[1]**

**2.** A steel cylinder can hold a large mass of oxygen squeezed into a small volume. A bottle already full of water cannot have more water forced in. Explain this difference in terms of particles. **[3]**

**3.** Use the data in this table.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| J | −98 | 65 |
| K | 80 | 218 |
| L | −188 | −42 |
| M | 660 | 2519 |

**(a)** Which substance is a liquid at 25 °C? **[1]**
**(b)** Which substance is a gas at 25 °C? **[1]**
**(c)** The substances are warmed from 25 °C to 100 °C. Name the two substances that change state, and name each change. **[2]**

**4.** For each change, state whether it is physical or chemical, and explain your answer in terms of particles.

**(a)** A piece of gallium metal melts when held in a warm hand. **[2]**
**(b)** A copper roof slowly turns green over many years as the copper forms a new compound. **[2]**
**(c)** The alcohol in hand gel evaporates from your skin. **[2]**

**5.** Liquid ethanol is heated from 20 °C until it boils at 78 °C. Describe what happens to the ethanol particles during this process. **[4]**

**6.** Two students make these statements.

Student 1: "A metal rail gets longer on a hot day because its particles get bigger."
Student 2: "Between the particles of a gas there is air."

Explain what is wrong with each statement. **[4]**

**7.** A student sets up two test tubes.

| Tube | What the student does | Observations |
|---|---|---|
| A | Warms a white waxy solid in a beaker of hot water | Solid turns to a clear liquid |
| B | Adds a piece of magnesium ribbon to dilute hydrochloric acid | Bubbles form, the ribbon gets smaller and disappears, the tube feels warm |

**(a)** State which tube shows a chemical change. Give **two** observations that support your answer. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe what happens to the particles in tube B. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest how the student could show that the change in tube A is physical. **[1]**

**8.** A model of a solid is built as a cube of polystyrene balls, 6 balls along each edge.

**(a)** Calculate the total number of balls. **[1]**
**(b)** State how many balls a 2D drawing of one face would show. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how many balls are completely hidden inside the cube. **[1]**
**(d)** Give **one** way in which a 2D diagram of a solid gives a misleading picture of its structure. **[1]**

**9.** (Higher Tier only) Use these values. 1.0 g of liquid X occupies 1.25 cm³. As a gas, at the conditions used, 1.0 g of X occupies 1000 cm³. A textbook diagram of gaseous X draws each particle as a circle 3 mm across, with centres about 6 mm apart.

**(a)** Calculate how many times larger the volume of the gas is than the volume of the liquid. **[1]**
**(b)** Estimate how many times further apart the particles are in the gas than in the liquid. **[2]**
**(c)** Use your answer to (b) to comment on the textbook diagram. **[2]**
**(d)** Give **two** other limitations of drawing particles as inelastic spheres. **[2]**

**10.** (Higher Tier only) Substance N melts at −114 °C; substance Z melts at 1085 °C. A student explains melting with a model in which every particle is a hard, identical ball. Explain why this model cannot account for the difference in melting points, and state the idea that is needed. **[4]**

**11.** A small amount of white ammonium chloride is heated at the bottom of a test tube. The solid at the bottom disappears, and a white solid collects on the cooler part of the tube near its mouth. Tests show that the gas in the hot part of the tube is a mixture of ammonia and hydrogen chloride.

**(a)** Write a word equation for the change at the bottom of the tube. **[1]**
**(b)** Student A says: "This is sublimation, so it is a physical change." Student B says: "Two chemical changes happen." Evaluate the two claims, using the particle model. **[5]**
**(c)** Explain what this example shows about using reversibility to decide whether a change is chemical. **[1]**

**12.** Smoke in a small glass cell is viewed under a microscope. The bright specks move in a jerky, random way, though the air is still. Explain how this observation supports the particle model. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Evaporation [1]. **(b)** Condensation [1]. **(c)** Melting [1]. **(d)** Freezing (solidifying) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the scientific name of the change; everyday words such as "drying" or "setting" repeat the question rather than answer it.

**2.** Gas particles are far apart with empty space between them [1]. Squeezing the gas pushes the particles closer together into that space, so a large mass fits in a small volume [1]. Water particles are already close together and touching, so there is almost no space to push them into [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe the liquid particles too, not only the gas.

**3. (a)** **J** (25 °C is between −98 °C and 65 °C). [1]
**(b)** **L** (25 °C is above −42 °C). [1]
**(c)** **K melts** (passes 80 °C) [1]; **J boils** (passes 65 °C) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the change as well as the substance; "J changes" is incomplete.

**4. (a)** Physical [1]. The gallium particles gain energy and start to move past each other, but they are still gallium particles; no new substance forms [1].
**(b)** Chemical [1]. Copper atoms are rearranged with atoms from the air to make new particles of a different substance (the green compound) [1].
**(c)** Physical [1]. The alcohol particles gain energy and spread out as a gas, but they are the same alcohol particles; no new substance forms [1].
*Examiner insight:* "It can be reversed" is not a particle explanation; say whether the particles stay the same or new ones form.

**5.** The particles take in energy [1]. They move faster [1]. At 78 °C they move far apart as a gas, moving quickly and randomly [1]. The particles stay the same: still ethanol molecules, no bigger [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A description that covers energy, movement and spacing gains more credit than one that only says "the particles move faster".

**6.** Student 1: the particles do not get bigger [1]. They gain energy and vibrate more, so they take up more room and the rail as a whole expands [1].
Student 2: the space between gas particles is empty [1]. Air is itself made of particles, so there cannot be air between them [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For an "explain what is wrong" question, state the correct idea as well as saying the statement is wrong.

**7. (a)** Tube **B** [1]. Bubbles show a gas is made [1]. The magnesium disappears, so it has been used up to make something new (or: the tube warms up, so energy is given out) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The atoms in the magnesium and in the acid are rearranged [1] to make new particles: hydrogen molecules and magnesium chloride, which are new substances [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Let it cool: it sets back into the same white solid, which melts again at the same temperature [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote observations from the table, not general signs of reaction.

**8. (a)** 6 × 6 × 6 = **216** [1].
**(b)** 6 × 6 = **36** [1].
**(c)** Inner cube is 4 balls along each edge: 4 × 4 × 4 = **64** [1].
**(d)** It shows only one layer, hiding the particles behind and inside (or: it makes a 3D arrangement look flat) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication; a bare wrong number shows no method.

**9. (a)** 1000 ÷ 1.25 = **800** [1].
**(b)** Spacing increases by the cube root of the volume ratio [1]: ∛800 ≈ 9.3, so about **9 times** further apart [1].
**(c)** To scale, the centres should be about 9.3 × 3 mm ≈ **28 mm** apart, not 6 mm [1]. The diagram shows the particles far too close; the real spaces are much larger [1].
**(d)** It ignores the forces of attraction between particles [1]. It shows all particles as the same size, when real particles differ in size (or: real particles are not hard, solid balls) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The spacing comes from the cube root; dividing by 3 or using 800 directly loses the method.

**10.** The model shows no forces of attraction between the particles [1]. Melting needs energy to overcome the attractions holding the particles in place [1]. Z must have much stronger forces between its particles than N, so needs much more energy [1]. The idea needed is that particles, and the forces between them, differ from one substance to another [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the missing idea (forces of attraction) explicitly; "the model is too simple" does not say what is missing.

**11. (a)** ammonium chloride → ammonia + hydrogen chloride [1].
**(b)** Student A is wrong [1]. In sublimation the same particles would just move apart as a gas [1]. Here the gas is ammonia and hydrogen chloride, different substances, so atoms have been rearranged into new particles [1]. Student B is right: the ammonium chloride breaks down into new substances at the bottom of the tube [1], and at the cool end ammonia and hydrogen chloride particles react to form new ammonium chloride particles, which is a second chemical change [1]. **[5]**
**(c)** It shows that a chemical change can be reversed, so reversibility alone does not show a change is physical; the test is whether new substances form [1].
*Examiner insight:* Judge each claim and back it with evidence from the question, here the identity of the gases.

**12.** The smoke specks are hit by air particles that are too small to see [1]. The air particles move fast and in random directions [1]. The specks are hit unevenly from different sides at different moments, so they are pushed in a jerky, random path, which is evidence that tiny moving particles exist [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that invisible air particles do the hitting; "the smoke vibrates" explains nothing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying particles get bigger, melt or get hot, instead of gaining energy and moving faster.
- Describing only one of arrangement, movement and spacing.
- Reading negative melting and boiling points the wrong way round.
- Classifying a change as physical or chemical with no reference to new particles or new substances.
- (Higher Tier only) Using a volume ratio directly as a spacing ratio instead of taking the cube root.
- (Higher Tier only) Writing "the model is too simple" without naming forces, sizes or spaces.
