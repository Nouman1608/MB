Section A of Component 1 is the reading half of Paper 1 in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE
English Language A (4EA1). This guide goes deeper than the
[Component 1 overview](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-language-component-1/): it takes each of the
three reading skills in turn, shows what a mark-earning answer looks like, and works through an
original passage written for this page.

## What Section A covers

The specification sets **a mixture of short- and long-answer questions** on two texts: one non-fiction
text from **Part 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology**, printed in the exam,
and **one previously unseen extract**. You answer every question. The section is worth **45 marks**, and
Pearson advises about **1 hour 30 minutes, including reading time**.

| Assessment objective | What it asks you to do | Marks in Section A |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Read and understand, selecting and interpreting information, ideas and perspectives | 11 |
| AO2 | Understand and analyse how writers use linguistic and structural devices to achieve their effects | 12 |
| AO3 | Explore links and connections between writers' ideas and perspectives, as well as how these are conveyed | 22 |

The specification expects you to have read widely: journalism (such as articles and reviews),
speeches, journals and reference-book extracts, and literary non-fiction such as autobiography,
letters, obituaries and travel writing. It also states that transient texts such as instant news feeds
and advertisements will not be assessed, so an unseen extract will be a sustained piece of writing.

## Skill 1: AO1, selecting and interpreting

AO1 questions reward accurate selection. Some ask for information stated directly; others ask what
you can **infer** about a person, place or attitude. The difference matters:

- **Explicit:** the writer tells you. "The village had no road" gives you a fact.
- **Implicit:** you work it out. If the writer says she "rationed the last of the batteries", you can
  infer the journey had gone on longer than planned and that she felt anxious about it.

Three habits lift AO1 answers:

1. **Stay inside the line range.** If a question names lines, evidence from elsewhere earns nothing.
2. **Make one point per piece of evidence.** Short questions are usually marked point by point, so two
   separate, precise points are worth more than one long sentence.
3. **Use your own words when told to.** Copying a phrase shows you found it, not that you understood it.

## Skill 2: AO2, analysing language and structure

AO2 asks *how* the writer achieves an effect. Non-fiction writers use many of the same tools as
fiction writers, plus some of their own.

| Device | Typical effect in non-fiction |
|---|---|
| Anecdote | Makes an argument personal and believable |
| Direct address ("you") | Draws the reader in and makes them share responsibility |
| Rhetorical question | Prompts the reader to reach the writer's conclusion |
| Contrast or juxtaposition | Sharpens a judgement by placing two ideas side by side |
| Shift in register | Moves from personal to formal, or from humour to seriousness, to change the reader's response |
| Sentence length | Short sentences create emphasis or shock; long ones build detail or momentum |
| Structural shift | A change of time, place or focus that changes how the reader sees what came before |
| Ending that circles back | Returns to an opening image so the reader measures how far the writer has travelled |

**Structure** is the device candidates most often leave out. Ask: How does the text open? Where does the
focus change? Is it chronological, or does it move between past and present? How does it end, and why
there? A single sentence on the effect of the ending is AO2 analysis; a sentence saying "the text has
paragraphs" is not.

The mark-earning pattern is **method, evidence, effect, and the reason behind the effect**: name the
choice, quote a few words, explain the effect on the reader, then explain why the writer wants that
effect for their purpose.

## Skill 3: AO3, comparing ideas and perspectives

The comparison question carries 22 marks, almost half the section. The AO3 wording has two halves:
links between **ideas and perspectives**, *as well as* **how these are conveyed**. A comparison of
content alone covers the first half only.

A **perspective** is the writer's position: insider or outsider, participant or observer, approving or
critical, certain or uncertain. Two writers can describe the same kind of event from very different
perspectives. For example, Kari Herbert's *The Explorer's Daughter* presents a hunt with a mixed,
uneasy viewpoint, while Steven Morris's newspaper article, whose headline asks whether adventurers are
explorers or "boys messing about", takes a sceptical public view of risk-taking.

Build each comparative paragraph around **one shared idea**, then show how the two writers differ in
perspective and method. Connectives such as *whereas*, *similarly*, *by contrast* and *both writers*
make the comparison visible in every paragraph, not only at the end.

## Worked example on an original unseen extract

> *The original passage below was written for this page.*
>
> The call came at 2 a.m., as they always do. Two walkers, no torch, somewhere above the second waterfall.
> I have done this for nineteen years and I still feel the same small anger as I lace my boots: anger at
> them, then at myself for the anger. Nobody plans to be lost. We found them at dawn, sitting on a rock,
> sharing one chocolate bar with great ceremony. The younger one apologised eleven times. I counted.

**AO1 short answer.** *What can you infer about the narrator's attitude to the walkers?*

> She is irritated at first ("small anger"), but she is also fair-minded, because she criticises her own
> reaction and accepts that "nobody plans to be lost".

Two separate inferences, each supported. That is the pattern for short-answer marks.

**AO2 paragraph.** *How does the writer use language and structure to present the rescue?*

> The writer opens with a short, flat sentence, "The call came at 2 a.m., as they always do", so the
> emergency feels routine to the narrator rather than dramatic; the reader immediately senses her long
> experience. Structurally, the passage jumps from the call to the dawn discovery, leaving out the search,
> so the focus falls on the walkers and on the narrator's feelings rather than on heroism. The final two
> sentences use dry humour: the precise detail "eleven times" and the blunt "I counted" show that her
> anger has turned into amused sympathy, which persuades the reader to share her tolerant view.

This names methods (sentence form, omission in the structure, humour, precise detail), quotes briefly,
and links each effect to the writer's purpose.

**AO3 paragraph.** *Compare how the writers present people who need rescuing.* (Pairing the original
passage with Morris's article.)

> Both writers question whether people who need rescuing are to blame, but from different perspectives.
> Morris writes as a journalist reporting on a rescue at public cost, and his headline's choice between
> "explorers" and "boys messing about" invites the reader to judge. The rescuer, by contrast, writes from
> inside the event; her first-person honesty about her "small anger" admits the same suspicion and then
> rejects it. Morris keeps the detached stance of a news report, whereas the rescuer closes the gap with
> a comic, affectionate detail, so the reader ends with sympathy rather than judgement.

Notice that the paragraph compares perspective (journalist and participant), method (headline and
reporting against first-person anecdote) and effect on the reader.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering AO1 questions with evidence from outside the line range.**
- **Feature-spotting.** "The writer uses a rhetorical question" earns little until you explain what the
  question makes the reader think and why.
- **Ignoring structure** in the AO2 answer, even though the objective names structural devices.
- **Writing two separate essays** in the comparison: one paragraph on Text 1, one on Text 2, and a
  sentence of comparison at the end.
- **Comparing topics only.** Saying both texts are about travel is a starting point, not a comparison of
  perspectives.
- **Treating the anthology text as unseen.** It is printed in the exam, but knowing it well beforehand
  saves reading time you need for the unseen extract.

## Exam technique and command words

| Command | What to do |
|---|---|
| Identify / give | Select accurately from the stated lines; brief answers are fine |
| Describe / explain | Show understanding, often of an implicit idea, with a reason |
| Analyse how the writer uses language and structure | Method, evidence, effect and purpose; cover both language and structure |
| Compare how the writers present | Integrated points on ideas, perspectives and methods across both texts |

**Pacing.** Allocate time roughly in proportion to marks within your 90 minutes: read both texts with
care first, keep the short AO1 answers brief, and protect the largest block of time for the 22-mark
comparison. Plan the comparison with a quick three-row grid: shared idea, Text 1 perspective and method,
Text 2 perspective and method.

**Anthology preparation.** For each of the ten Part 1 texts, know the writer's perspective, the form
(speech, article, memoir, travel writing) and two or three methods worth analysing. That preparation
makes the comparison question faster, whichever unseen extract it is paired with. Anthology contents
are fixed by specification issue, so check the current list with your teacher.
