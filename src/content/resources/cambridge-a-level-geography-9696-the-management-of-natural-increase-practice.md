These questions cover **4.4 The management of natural increase** in Topic 4, Population, from Paper 2 Core Human Geography. Data in the tables are fictional; Questions 11 and 12 ask you to use your own case study of one country's population policy. Revise first with the [4.4 management of natural increase study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-natural-increase/) and the [4.4 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-management-of-natural-increase-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between anti-natalist and pro-natalist population policies. **[2]**

**2.** Give **two** examples of non-coercive anti-natalist measures. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by population momentum. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why it is difficult to judge the success of a population policy. **[3]**

**5.** State **two** ways a government can manage the results of an ageing population. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why coercive population policies are controversial. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Fictional country R introduced pro-natalist policies in 2010, including cash payments for births and paid parental leave. Spending on family benefits rose from 1.0% of GDP in 2010 to 2.5% in 2025.

| Year | 2005 | 2010 | 2015 | 2020 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total fertility rate | 1.40 | 1.30 | 1.45 | 1.35 | 1.30 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in the total fertility rate after 2010. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons why the policies had limited success. **[4]**

**8.** A fictional government has introduced four measures:

- A: free contraception at all health centres;
- B: raising the state pension age from 62 to 67;
- C: a cash payment for every third child;
- D: recruiting overseas workers for elderly care homes.

**(a)** For each measure, state whether it is an attempt to alter the natural increase rate or to manage the results of population change. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why a government might use both types of measure at the same time. **[2]**

**9.** Explain the difficulties governments face when trying to raise fertility. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how a rapid fall in fertility can create problems that governments then need to manage. **[5]**

**11.** With reference to a country you have studied, describe the attempts made to alter its rate of natural increase. **[6]**

**12.** With reference to a country you have studied, evaluate the success of its attempts to manage natural increase and its results. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Anti-natalist policies aim to reduce the birth rate or fertility [1]. Pro-natalist policies aim to increase the birth rate or fertility [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Two from: free or subsidised contraception and family planning services [1]; education campaigns promoting smaller families [1]. Accept raising the minimum age of marriage or improving girls' education in place of either. **[2]**

**3.** The continued growth of a population after fertility has fallen to or below replacement level [1], because the large numbers of young people already born will reach childbearing age and have children [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Fertility may have been falling or rising anyway because of wider development, such as education and urbanisation [1]. Effects take decades to show in the age structure [1]. Success can be judged by different criteria, such as the birth rate, costs, human rights or unintended consequences, which may conflict [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Two from: raising the retirement or pension age [1]; encouraging immigration of working-age people [1]. Accept expanding elderly care, reforming pensions or encouraging older people to keep working in place of either. **[2]**

**6.** They restrict personal freedom to choose family size, which many regard as a basic human right [1], and enforcement can involve heavy fines or pressure on medical decisions [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Fertility rose from 1.30 in 2010 to 1.45 in 2015 [1], but then fell back to 1.30 by 2025, so there was no lasting increase [1].

**(b)** Payments are small compared with the long-term costs of raising children, such as housing and education [1]. Long working hours and lack of affordable childcare make it hard to combine work and family [1]. Later marriage and changing attitudes mean many people choose small families or none [1]. The rise to 2015 may reflect couples bringing births forward rather than having more children in total [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** A: alter the natural increase rate [1]. B: manage the results [1]. C: alter the natural increase rate [1]. D: manage the results [1].

**(b)** Measures to change fertility take decades to affect the age structure [1], so the government must also adapt to the existing structure, for example by supporting a growing elderly population now [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Fertility depends on many factors beyond government control, such as housing costs and incomes [1]. Changing attitudes, later marriage and careers mean many people choose small families [1]. Incentives are expensive and often too small to change decisions [1]. Couples may simply have children earlier rather than more children [1]. Results take many years to show, beyond the term of the government that introduced the policy [1]. **[5]**

**10.** A rapid fall in births reduces the number of young people [1], so the working-age population later shrinks, causing labour shortages [1]. The large older cohorts born before the fall reach retirement, raising old-age dependency [1]. Pension and health care costs rise while the tax base falls [1]. Where birth limits met a preference for sons, sex ratios became imbalanced, creating a surplus of young men [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Credit located, dated detail from one country's policy.

Names the country and states the aim of the policy, anti-natalist or pro-natalist [1]. Gives the date the policy began or changed [1]. Describes one method used, such as a limit, campaign or incentive [1]. Describes a second method used [1]. Describes how the policy was enforced or promoted [1]. Describes any exceptions or later changes to the policy [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Credit located, dated detail from one country's policy.

Effectiveness: uses data to show how far the birth rate, fertility or natural increase changed [1]. Considers whether the change was caused by the policy or by wider development [1]. Assesses whether the policy met its stated aims [1].

Difficulties and costs: explains difficulties faced, such as public resistance or cost [1]. Considers ethical or human rights issues [1]. Identifies unintended consequences, such as ageing or sex imbalance [1].

Managing the results: describes measures to manage the results of population change [1]. Assesses how well these measures have worked [1].

Judgement: weighs successes against failures [1] and reaches a clear, supported conclusion on the overall success [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the policy in detail but never evaluating it.
- Omitting attempts to manage the results of population change.
- Crediting the whole change in fertility to the policy.
- Describing data without quoting figures for specific years.
- Case study answers without a named country, dates or data.
