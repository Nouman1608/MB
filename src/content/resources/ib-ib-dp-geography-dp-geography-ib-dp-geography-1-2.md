This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 1.2 of Option A, Freshwater, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): flooding and flood mitigation. The [1.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-freshwater-flooding-and-flood-mitigation-revision-notes/) already cover the physical and human causes of floods, recurrence intervals, primary and secondary impacts, the three families of mitigation and three case studies (Pakistan 2022, Room for the River and Slowing the Flow at Pickering). This page adds the analytical tools that turn those facts into high-level answers: comparing two hydrographs from the same basin, working out flood probability over a lifetime, the idea of residual risk, and the case for moving people rather than water. Practise afterwards with the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-1-2-practice/), and see the [Freshwater option study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-freshwater-study-guide/) for the wider option.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the natural and human factors that increase flood risk, and how they show on a storm hydrograph.
- Use flood frequency ideas to judge how likely a flood is.
- Explain and evaluate attempts to modify floods and reduce their impacts, including structural, natural and planning approaches.
- Apply all of this to flood events and strategies you have studied.

## Reading two hydrographs from one basin

Comparing two storms on the **same** basin is a powerful way to isolate a factor, because geology, relief and basin shape stay constant. Consider this illustrative data for one small basin (invented figures). Both storms delivered 30 mm of rain, with peak rainfall at 09:00.

| | Storm 1 (dry soil, after 3 dry weeks) | Storm 2 (wet soil, after a week of rain) |
|---|---|---|
| Baseflow before storm | 2 cumecs | 5 cumecs |
| Peak discharge | 9 cumecs, at 15:00 | 21 cumecs, at 12:00 |
| Lag time | 6 hours | 3 hours |
| Rise from baseflow to peak | 7 cumecs | 16 cumecs |
| Rate of rise on rising limb | 7 / 6 = about 1.2 cumecs per hour | 16 / 3 = about 5.3 cumecs per hour |

The rain was the same, so the difference must come from **antecedent conditions**. In Storm 2, soil pores were already full, so infiltration was limited and saturation-excess overland flow carried water quickly to the channel. Groundwater was also higher, which is why baseflow started higher. This is why many large floods follow a long wet spell rather than a single storm: the final rainfall event "tips" an already saturated basin.

When you describe a hydrograph in an exam, quote three numbers with units (lag time, peak discharge, and the rise above baseflow), then explain them with a named flow.

## Flood probability over time

The revision notes give recurrence interval = (n + 1) / m. The annual probability of a flood at least that size is 1 divided by the recurrence interval. Planners and householders care about a longer period, so you can extend the idea:

**probability of at least one such flood in N years = 1 - (1 - p) to the power N**, where p is the annual probability.

**Worked example.** A house lies inside the 100-year flood outline, so p = 0.01.

```
Chance of no flood in one year  = 1 - 0.01 = 0.99
Chance of no flood in 30 years  = 0.99 to the power 30 = 0.74
Chance of at least one flood    = 1 - 0.74 = 0.26, about 26%
```

Over a 30-year mortgage, the owner faces roughly a one-in-four chance of at least one "100-year" flood. Two warnings belong in any answer that uses recurrence intervals:

- Records are often short, so the largest floods may be missing from the data, and estimates for rare events are uncertain.
- The calculation assumes conditions stay the same. Land-use change upstream or a changing climate alters flood frequency, so a flood that used to be a 1-in-100-year event can become more likely.

## Design standards and residual risk

Every engineered defence is built to a **design standard**, for example to hold back a 1-in-100-year flood. That leaves **residual risk**: the chance of a bigger flood, or of the defence failing through overtopping, breaching or poor maintenance. The levee effect in the revision notes is one part of this. Two further points add depth:

- **Raised stakes.** When a defence fails, water can enter quickly and deeply, so damage and danger may be greater than in an undefended floodplain where people expected shallow flooding.
- **Upstream and downstream trade-offs.** Levees confine water to the channel, raising water levels and speeding flow, which can pass the problem downstream. Storage reservoirs work only while they have spare capacity; if heavy rain fills a reservoir before the peak, operators may have to release large volumes while the town downstream is already flooding. In January 2011, Wivenhoe Dam upstream of Brisbane, Australia, filled its flood storage during prolonged heavy rain and made large releases while the city flooded; a commission of inquiry later examined how the dam had been operated.

## Moving people rather than water

Planning approaches reduce **exposure** instead of changing the flood.

- **Floodplain zoning** restricts land use by risk: open space, farmland and playing fields closest to the river; buildings designed to tolerate flooding further out; homes, schools and hospitals only on higher ground.
- **Building controls** require raised floor levels, flood-resistant materials and wiring above expected flood depth.
- **Buy-outs and relocation** remove existing buildings from the floodplain. After the 1993 flood on the upper Mississippi and Missouri, the small town of Valmeyer, Illinois, was rebuilt on bluffs above its former site rather than behind repaired levees, and US federal programmes bought out many floodplain properties.

Evaluation of planning approaches:

- They avoid future damage permanently and give space for natural flood storage.
- They are cheap compared with structures, but only for new development; buying out existing homes is costly and politically difficult.
- Communities may lose homes, history and social ties, and those who stay may be the poorest.
- Zoning only works if it is enforced and maps are kept up to date.

## Flood warnings: lead time

A warning's value depends on its **lead time**, the time between the warning and the flood arriving. Large basins give long lead times because the flood peak travels slowly downstream; small, steep or urban basins may give only an hour or two, so warnings must rely on rainfall forecasts rather than river gauges upstream. Warnings reduce loss of life and movable damage, but not damage to buildings.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the effectiveness of floodplain zoning as a flood mitigation strategy.* **(sample conclusion)**

"Floodplain zoning is highly effective at reducing future losses because it limits exposure, which no structure can do completely **[criterion: long-term effectiveness]**. It also keeps the floodplain available to store water, so it reduces flooding downstream rather than passing it on, unlike levees **[comparison with an alternative]**. However, it protects nothing that is already built, so in cities with dense floodplain development it must be combined with defences or buy-outs **[limitation tied to context]**. Its success depends on enforcement: where land is scarce and planning is weak, informal settlement often spreads onto the floodplain regardless **[condition for success]**. Overall, zoning is the most sustainable approach for growing towns, but the least useful on its own for established ones **[supported judgement that depends on context]**."

## Common mistakes

- Comparing hydrographs from different basins and claiming a single factor explains the difference.
- Treating the annual probability as the chance over a lifetime.
- Calling a defended area "safe" without mentioning residual risk.
- Ignoring planning, warnings and relocation when asked about mitigation; structures are only part of the answer.
- Naming a strategy without saying who gains, who loses and over what time.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse** a hydrograph: break it into features, quote values, and explain each with a named process.
- **Evaluate** a strategy: use criteria such as effectiveness, cost, equity, environmental impact and sustainability, then make a judgement that depends on the place.
- **To what extent** questions need a clear position ("largely", "partly") supported by located evidence.
- **Suggest** asks for a reasoned proposal: for example, suggest why a lag time shortened after building work upstream.
