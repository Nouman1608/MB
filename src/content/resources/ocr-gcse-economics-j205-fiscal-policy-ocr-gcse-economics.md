## Why governments spend, and how they pay

Government spending has three broad purposes:

- **Providing services** the market would underprovide or that people believe everyone should have: health care, education, defence, policing, roads.
- **Supporting incomes** through benefits and state pensions, which also redistributes income.
- **Managing the economy**, for example by investing in infrastructure to raise growth.

Revenue comes mainly from taxes, plus borrowing when spending exceeds tax revenue.

| Direct taxes: on income and wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm earning it | Indirect taxes: on spending, collected by sellers and passed to the government |
|---|---|
| Income tax | VAT (value added tax) |
| National insurance contributions | Excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco |
| Corporation tax on company profits | |

## Deficits, surpluses and debt

- **Balanced budget**: revenue = spending.
- **Budget surplus**: revenue > spending.
- **Budget deficit**: spending > revenue, and the gap is borrowed.

Do not confuse the **deficit**, a gap in one year (a flow), with the **national debt**, the total owed from all past borrowing (a stock). Each deficit adds to the debt; a surplus can reduce it.

### Worked example 1: deficit, share of GDP and debt interest

Country F spends £1,130bn and raises £1,050bn in a year. Its GDP is £2,500bn and its debt at the start of the year is £2,400bn.

- Deficit = 1,130 − 1,050 = **£80bn**.
- As a share of GDP = 80 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **3.2%**.
- Debt at the end of the year ≈ 2,400 + 80 = **£2,480bn**.
- If the government pays 3.5% interest on new borrowing, the extra £80bn costs 80 × 0.035 = **£2.8bn a year**, money that cannot be spent on services.

Expressing the deficit as a share of GDP lets you compare countries of different sizes and the same country over time.

## Fiscal policy and the objectives

**Fiscal policy** is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.

| Expansionary fiscal policy | Contractionary fiscal policy |
|---|---|
| Raise spending and/or cut taxes | Cut spending and/or raise taxes |
| Raises total demand, output and jobs | Reduces total demand and inflationary pressure |
| Used in a downturn with high unemployment | Used when demand is too strong and inflation is high |
| Usually widens the deficit | Usually narrows the deficit |

**Conflicts between objectives.** An expansionary policy that cuts unemployment may push up inflation if the economy is near full capacity, and it widens the deficit. A contractionary policy that cuts inflation may raise unemployment. Fiscal policy can also target specific objectives: spending on training aims at structural unemployment, and progressive taxes aim at a fairer distribution.

## Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

The **average tax rate** is tax paid ÷ income × 100.

- **Progressive**: the average rate rises as income rises.
- **Proportional**: the average rate is the same at every income.
- **Regressive**: the average rate falls as income rises.

### Worked example 2: a progressive income tax

Country F charges 0% on the first £10,000, 20% on income from £10,000 to £45,000, and 40% above £45,000.

| | Ada: £20,000 | Ben: £75,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Taxed at 0% | £10,000 | £10,000 |
| Taxed at 20% | £10,000 → £2,000 | £35,000 → £7,000 |
| Taxed at 40% | none | £30,000 → £12,000 |
| Total tax | **£2,000** | **£19,000** |
| Average tax rate | **10.0%** | **25.3%** |

Ben's income is 3.75 times Ada's, but he pays 9.5 times as much tax. The tax narrows the gap in after-tax income.

### Worked example 3: why VAT is regressive

Over a year, Cara earns £15,000 and pays £1,500 in VAT on what she buys. Dev earns £80,000 and pays £5,000 in VAT.

- Cara: 1,500 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = **10.0%** of income.
- Dev: 5,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **6.25%** of income.

Dev pays more VAT in pounds, but a smaller share of his income, because higher earners save more and spend a smaller fraction of what they earn. So indirect taxes tend to be regressive.

## Taxes and spending in markets

An indirect tax raises firms' costs, so supply falls, price rises and quantity falls. How much of the tax consumers pay depends on price elasticity of demand: if demand is inelastic, as for fuel or cigarettes, most of the tax is passed on and sales fall only a little, so tax revenue is high. A subsidy does the reverse. Government spending also creates markets directly: a school-building programme raises demand for construction firms, their workers and their suppliers.

## Evaluating fiscal policy

**Benefits**
- Can be targeted at particular groups, regions or industries.
- Spending on infrastructure and education can raise long-run growth.
- Can support demand quickly when spending on benefits rises automatically in a downturn.

**Costs and limits**
- **Opportunity cost**: £10bn on one project is £10bn not spent elsewhere, or tax that could have been cut.
- **Borrowing costs**: deficits add to debt and future interest payments.
- **Time lags**: big projects take years to plan and build.
- **Incentives**: high tax rates may discourage work, investment or enterprise, and encourage avoidance.
- **Inflation risk**: expansion when the economy is near capacity raises prices more than output.

**Redistribution.** Progressive taxes and benefits reduce inequality and help low-income households, who spend more of any extra income. But very high top rates may discourage effort or lead skilled workers and firms to move, and generous benefits cost taxpayers. The effect depends on the size of the rates and how people respond.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the use of a cut in income tax to reduce unemployment.*

"A cut in income tax raises households' disposable income, so consumer spending rises. **[first link]** Firms see higher sales, so they increase output and need more workers, which reduces cyclical unemployment. **[chain to the objective]** However, some of the extra income may be saved or spent on imports, so the boost to domestic jobs is smaller. **[limit]** The cut also reduces tax revenue, so the deficit widens unless spending is cut, and there is an opportunity cost in services not funded. **[cost]** It will do little for structural unemployment, where the problem is skills, not demand. **[type matters]** The policy is therefore most effective when unemployment is cyclical and the government can afford to borrow, and least effective when unemployment is structural." **[judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Confusing deficit with debt.
- Calling national insurance an indirect tax. It is a direct tax on earnings.
- Saying a tax is progressive because rich people pay more in pounds. Look at the average rate.
- Forgetting the opportunity cost of spending.
- Assuming expansionary policy always works, whatever the type of unemployment.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out tax bands in a table; show deficit as a share of GDP when comparing.
- **Analyse**: build the chain from tax or spending change to demand, output, jobs or prices.
- **Evaluate**: name the objective, weigh benefits against opportunity cost and other limits, and say when the policy would work best.
