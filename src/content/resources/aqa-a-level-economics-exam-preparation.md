AQA A-Level Economics (7136) is assessed through three compulsory written papers, not tiered: Paper 1
(Markets and market failure, 2h, 80 marks, 33.3%), Paper 2 (National and international economy, 2h,
80 marks, 33.3%), and Paper 3 (Economic principles and issues, drawing on all content, 2h, 80 marks,
33.3%). These notes turn that structure into an exam-day plan, alongside the
[Microeconomics](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-microeconomics-revision-notes/) and
[Macroeconomic Performance](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-macroeconomic-performance-revision-notes/)
topic revision notes already on the site.

## Paper 1 and Paper 2 share the same two-section structure -- practise it as one skill

Both Paper 1 and Paper 2 split into Section A (a data-response question, choice of one of two
contexts, 40 marks) and Section B (an essay, choice of one of three, 40 marks). **Exam-preparation
priority**: because this structure is identical across both papers, practising data-response and
essay technique once and applying it to both Markets and market failure content and National and
international economy content is more efficient than treating each paper's technique as separate.

## Paper 3 is whole-course and includes both multiple choice and case-study analysis

Paper 3 draws on content from across the whole specification and splits into 30 marks of multiple
choice and 50 marks of case-study questions. **Exam-preparation priority**: leave dedicated
whole-course review time before Paper 3, and treat its multiple-choice section as a genuine test of
applied understanding, not a guessing exercise, since AQA's multiple-choice economics questions are
frequently built around applying a concept to a scenario.

## Data-response questions reward using the given data explicitly

Section A's data-response questions expect answers to engage directly with the specific extract or
data provided, not a generic answer that could apply to any economic scenario. **Exam-preparation
priority**: when practising data-response questions, deliberately quote or reference a specific figure,
trend or detail from the given extract within the answer itself, since examiners reward answers that
demonstrate genuine engagement with the provided material.

## Essay questions reward a reasoned judgement, weighing more than one side

Section B's essays require reaching a justified conclusion that weighs an argument against a
counterargument or limitation, not description of a single line of economic theory alone.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise a consistent essay structure -- state the economic theory,
apply it with a diagram or model where relevant, consider a counterargument or limiting factor, then
reach an explicit judgement -- across a range of essay titles, not just titles seen before.

## Worked practice scenario: a structured essay-planning answer

Question: "Evaluate the view that increasing the minimum wage will always increase unemployment."

```
Step 1 - state the theoretical starting point:
In a competitive labour market, a minimum wage set above the
equilibrium wage creates excess supply of labour (more workers
willing to work than firms demand at that wage), which classical
theory predicts increases unemployment among low-paid workers.

Step 2 - apply with a model/diagram reference:
This can be shown on a supply-and-demand diagram for labour, where the
minimum wage floor sits above the equilibrium wage, creating a gap
between quantity supplied and quantity demanded.

Step 3 - counterargument (the step "always" in the question invites
you to challenge):
However, if the labour market is monopsonistic (a single dominant
employer with wage-setting power), a minimum wage set appropriately
can actually increase both wages and employment by correcting the
employer's below-competitive wage-setting -- meaning the "always"
claim in the question does not hold in every market structure.

Step 4 - reach a justified conclusion:
Whether a minimum wage increases unemployment depends on the specific
market structure (competitive vs monopsonistic) and how far above
equilibrium the minimum wage is set -- the claim that it "always"
increases unemployment is not supported once monopsony power is
considered.
```

Notice the question's own wording ("always") is directly challenged in Step 3 -- examiners reward
essays that engage critically with absolute claims in a question title, rather than simply agreeing
with the premise throughout.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: practise the shared Section A/Section B structure across both Paper 1 and
  Paper 2 content; leave dedicated whole-course review time before Paper 3; practise referencing
  specific data-response extract details explicitly within answers; build a bank of the four-step
  essay structure (theory, application/diagram, counterargument, judgement) and rehearse it across
  different titles.
- **During Paper 1**: apply the shared data-response and essay technique to Markets and market
  failure content specifically.
- **During Paper 2**: apply the same technique to National and international economy content.
- **During Paper 3**: treat multiple-choice questions as genuine understanding checks, and expect
  case-study questions to draw on content from either Paper 1 or Paper 2's areas.
- **On every paper**: for essay questions, check your answer includes a counterargument and a final
  judgement engaging with the question's own wording, not just a one-sided explanation.

## Self-test

1. Why is it efficient to practise Section A/Section B technique once rather than separately for each
   paper?
2. What two question types make up Paper 3, and what fraction of its marks does each carry?
3. Why do data-response questions reward referencing specific details from the given extract?
4. In the worked scenario, why does the answer introduce monopsony in Step 3?

**Answers:** 1. Because Paper 1 and Paper 2 share an identical Section A (data-response)/Section B
(essay) structure, just applied to different content -- practising the technique once and applying it
to both papers' content is more efficient than treating each paper's technique as separate. 2.
Multiple choice (30 of 80 marks) and case-study questions (50 of 80 marks), drawing on content from
across the whole specification. 3. Because examiners reward answers that demonstrate genuine
engagement with the specific extract or data provided, rather than a generic answer that could apply
to any economic scenario. 4. Because the question's claim that a minimum wage "always" increases
unemployment only holds under a competitive labour market assumption -- introducing monopsony
directly challenges the word "always" in the question title, which is exactly the kind of critical
engagement a strong evaluative essay demonstrates.
