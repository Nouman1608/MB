## Section A

**1.** Which writer described natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts"? A: John Locke. B: Jeremy Bentham. C: Ronald Dworkin. D: Thomas Paine. **[1]**

**2.** In Hohfeld's analysis, what is the correlative of a liberty? A: a duty. B: a no-right. C: a liability. D: a disability. **[1]**

**3.** State what is meant by a residual liberty. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the three generations of rights described by Karel Vasak. **[3]**

**5.** State the central claim of natural rights theory. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the difference between a right and a liberty, using Hohfeld's analysis. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between the will theory and the interest theory of rights. **[4]**

**8.** Explain Dworkin's idea of rights as trumps and one criticism of it. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why the decision in Malone v Metropolitan Police Commissioner (1979) is used to criticise the protection of liberties in the United Kingdom before 1998. **[4]**

**10.** Explain the arguments for and against the universality of human rights. **[5]**

**11.** *A government proposes a new law allowing the police to torture terrorist suspects where this would save many lives. It argues that the benefit to the majority outweighs the harm to one person.* Consider how a utilitarian and a supporter of rights as trumps would respond to the proposal, and how the European Convention on Human Rights treats it. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss the view that human rights exist only because the law creates them. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B: Jeremy Bentham. **[1]**

**2.** B: a no-right. **[1]**

**3.** A freedom to do **anything that the law does not forbid** [1]; it exists only in the gaps left by law and can be removed by any new law [1].

**4.** **First generation**: civil and political rights [1]. **Second generation**: economic, social and cultural rights [1]. **Third generation**: collective rights such as development and a healthy environment [1].

**5.** People have rights **because they are human** [1], **independently of whether any law** or government recognises them [1].

**6.** In Hohfeld's analysis, a **claim-right** has a correlative **duty** on another person [1]; for example, a right to be paid imposes a duty to pay [1]. A **liberty** (privilege) means only that the holder has **no duty not to act**, and its correlative is a **no-right** [1]; no one is obliged to help the holder exercise it [1].

**7.** The **will theory**, associated with H. L. A. Hart, holds that a right gives the holder **control over another's duty**, with power to enforce or waive it [1]; it struggles to explain the rights of **children and people lacking capacity**, or unwaivable rights [1]. The **interest theory**, associated with Joseph Raz, holds that a person has a right when an aspect of their **well-being is sufficient reason** to impose a duty on others [1]; it may treat too many interests as rights [1].

**8.** Dworkin argued that rights **override collective goals and majority preferences**, so the state cannot remove them just because it would benefit the majority [1]. This explains why some rights cannot be balanced and why courts protect minorities [1]. A criticism is that it gives **unelected judges** too much power to decide which interests count as rights [1].

**9.** Before 1998 the UK protected **residual liberties**: people could do whatever was not forbidden [1]. In **Malone (1979)** police phone tapping was held lawful because **no law forbade it** [1]. Mr Malone had **no right to privacy** to assert against the state [1]. The European Court later found a **breach of Article 8 in Malone v UK (1984)**, showing that liberties gave weaker protection than rights [1].

**10.** For: rights rest on **shared human dignity**, as the UDHR 1948 states [1], and the **Vienna Declaration 1993** called them universal, indivisible, interdependent and interrelated [1]. Universality means a state cannot excuse abuses by pointing to its own laws [1]. Against: **cultural relativists** argue that rights reflect Western, individualist values [1]. The **margin of appreciation** is a limited recognition that states may apply rights differently [1].

**11.** A **utilitarian** such as Bentham judges a law by whether it **maximises overall happiness**, so might accept torture if it saved many lives [1], though a utilitarian might also weigh the long-term harm of such a law [1]. A supporter of **rights as trumps** (Dworkin) would reject the proposal: rights **override collective benefit** [1]. Under the Convention, **Article 3 is absolute** [1]: no justification or balancing is possible [1], and it **cannot be derogated from under Article 15** [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **Positivists** such as Bentham argue rights come only from real laws; abstract rights cannot be enforced [1].
- The **Human Rights Act 1998** supports this: Convention rights became enforceable in UK courts only when Parliament enacted it [1].
- **Natural rights** theorists such as **Locke** argue rights exist before and independently of the state [1].
- The **UDHR's "inherent dignity"** and the post-war reaction to lawful Nazi atrocities support a moral basis for rights [1].
- **Hart** accepted a **minimum content of natural law**, suggesting law and morality are connected in practice [1].
- **Dworkin's** rights as trumps show rights constrain law-making, not merely result from it [1].
- **Malone (1979)** shows what happens when protection depends only on what law happens to provide [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that law is needed to make rights enforceable, but their justification is moral [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using right and liberty as synonyms.
- Naming a theorist without explaining the argument.
- Forgetting to apply theory to the Convention or a case.
- Treating Article 3 as a qualified right in a utilitarian argument.
- Ending an essay without a judgement.
