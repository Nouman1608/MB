## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Lena, 34, has been caught taking small items such as pens and hair clips from shops. She has plenty of money, and keeps the items in a drawer without using them. She says she feels "wound up" before taking something and "a rush" afterwards.

**(a)** Explain why Lena's behaviour fits a diagnosis of kleptomania rather than ordinary theft. **[3]**

**(b)** Her psychologist uses the K-SAS before and after treatment. Explain **one** reason why her scores may not be accurate. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Grant et al. (2008) used a double-blind procedure. Explain why this was important. **[2]**

**7. (b)** Explain what the finding about family history of alcoholism suggests about treating gambling disorder. **[2]**

**7. (c)** Outline **one** weakness of pooling data from two different trials. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why the response criterion of a 35% reduction in symptoms lasting at least a month is useful in a drug trial. **[2]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to compare imaginal desensitisation with a waiting-list control for people with gambling disorder.

**(a)** Write a directional hypothesis for this study. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** ethical issue with using a waiting-list control and how to deal with it. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how the dopamine explanation relates to the determinism versus free will debate. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each for any two: repeated fire-setting; no obvious motive such as money, revenge, sabotage or a political statement; increasing tension or arousal before setting a fire; persistent fascination with fire or related things; pleasure, excitement or relief during and after.

**2.** Up to 3 marks. An intense positive feeling, such as a sense of power or excitement, became fixed to stealing during an earlier experience (1), creating a feeling-state (1). When the person later needs that feeling, for example when feeling worthless, the feeling-state is triggered and produces an urge to steal, which is why the behaviour is so specific (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks. Gambling activates dopamine in the brain's reward pathway, producing pleasure and motivation to repeat it (1); a person whose reward system responds strongly may develop urges they cannot control, as shown by some Parkinson's patients on dopamine-boosting drugs developing gambling problems (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. Many people gamble or are reinforced by wins without developing a disorder (1), so reinforcement alone cannot explain why only some people lose control; individual factors such as biology must also matter (1). Or: it cannot easily explain kleptomania when the stolen items have no value to the person.

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks, 3 for each therapy. Covert sensitisation: the client imagines the urge or act in vivid detail (1), then imagines an unpleasant consequence such as nausea or being arrested (1), so the act becomes associated with aversion and the urge weakens (1). Imaginal desensitisation: the client first learns relaxation (1), then imagines a series of trigger situations such as walking past a betting shop (1), and imagines leaving without acting on the urge while staying relaxed, so arousal linked to the urge is reduced (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation with idiographic versus nomothetic developed and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Idiographic*: both therapies use the client's own triggers and imagery, so they are tailored to the individual; case studies such as Glover (1985) give rich detail.
- *Nomothetic*: case studies cannot show the therapies work for most people; group comparisons are needed for general claims, and these are fewer than for drug treatments.
- *Ethics*: imagined aversion avoids the harm of real aversive stimuli.
- *Effectiveness*: they target learned associations and teach self-control, which may last after therapy ends, unlike drugs.
- *Limitations*: depend on the ability to imagine vividly and on motivation; may not address underlying causes such as a feeling-state.
- *Conclusion*: useful, ethical, individualised treatments whose evidence base would be strengthened by larger trials.

**6.** **(a)** Up to 3 marks. She does not need the items for use or money, since she can afford them and does not use them (1). She feels tension ("wound up") before stealing (1) and pleasure or relief ("a rush") afterwards, which matches the ICD-11 pattern (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Stealing is illegal and embarrassing (1), so she may under-report urges or thefts to present herself well, especially after treatment (social desirability bias) (1).

**7. (a)** Up to 2 marks. Neither participants nor researchers knew who received the drug or the placebo (1), so expectations could not bias participants' reports or researchers' ratings of improvement (1).

**7. (b)** Up to 2 marks. Gamblers with a family history of alcoholism may respond best to opiate antagonists (1), so clinicians could ask about family history to decide who should be offered the drug (1).

**7. (c)** Up to 2 marks. The trials used different drugs and lasted different lengths of time (16 and 18 weeks) (1), so differences between them add variability, making it harder to know whether results apply to each drug (1).

**8.** Up to 2 marks. It sets a clear, operationalised definition of success in advance (1), so researchers cannot choose a convenient measure after seeing results, and a lasting improvement is required rather than a brief fluctuation (1).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. For example: "Participants with gambling disorder who receive imaginal desensitisation will show a greater reduction in gambling-symptom scores after eight weeks than those on a waiting list." 1 for direction, 1 for operationalised variables.

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Withholding treatment from people with a harmful disorder could cause harm (1), for example further financial losses during the waiting period (1). The researcher should offer the treatment to the control group as soon as the study ends, and allow anyone whose problems worsen to withdraw and be treated immediately (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. The explanation suggests that behaviour is caused by dopamine activity in the reward pathway (1), which is a determinist view that the person has little control over the urges (1). This may reduce blame and stigma, but it may also suggest the person cannot change, which conflicts with psychological therapies that aim to restore self-control (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know that ICD-11 places gambling disorder elsewhere?**
It is a useful point for evaluation, but the syllabus groups gambling disorder with impulse control disorders, so answer on that basis.

**How much detail on Glover (1985) do I need?**
Enough to say it is a case study of a woman with kleptomania treated with covert sensitisation, and what that means for generalisation.
