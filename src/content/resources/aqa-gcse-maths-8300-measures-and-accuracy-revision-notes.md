These notes condense section 3.1.3, **Measures and accuracy** (N13 to N16), of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification, for teaching from September 2015 with exams from May/June 2017 onwards (version 1.0). Any of the three papers can test this; Paper 1 is non-calculator, Papers 2 and 3 allow a calculator.

## N13: Conversion factors

| Measure | Facts to know |
|---|---|
| Length | 10 mm = 1 cm; 100 cm = 1 m; 1000 m = 1 km |
| Mass | 1000 g = 1 kg; 1000 kg = 1 tonne |
| Capacity | 1000 ml = 1 litre; 1 cm³ = 1 ml; 1000 cm³ = 1 litre; 1 m³ = 1000 litres |
| Area | 1 cm² = 100 mm²; 1 m² = 10 000 cm²; 1 km² = 1 000 000 m² |
| Volume | 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³; 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³ |
| Time | 60 s = 1 min; 60 min = 1 h; 24 h = 1 day |

Imperial conversions (miles, pounds, pints and so on) are **given** in the question.

**Method: converting a unit**

```
1. Write the length conversion (e.g. 1 m = 100 cm).
2. Square it for area, cube it for volume.
3. Big unit -> small unit: multiply.  Small unit -> big unit: divide.
4. Check: the number of small units should be larger.
```

**Worked reminders.** 0.8 m³ = 0.8 × 1 000 000 = **800 000 cm³**. 6500 m² = 6500 ÷ 1 000 000 = **0.0065 km²**.

**Time.** Minutes ÷ 60 gives decimal hours: 2 h 48 min = 2 + 48/60 = **2.8 hours**. Decimal hours × 60 gives minutes: 0.75 h = 45 min.

**Compound measures** (speed, density, pressure, rates of pay, unit price) follow the same unit rules. See the [Ratio, proportion and rates of change revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-ratio-proportion-and-rates-of-change-revision-notes/) for the formulas.

## N14: Estimation

**Method: estimate a calculation**

```
1. Round every number to 1 significant figure.
2. Write the rounded calculation down (this earns the method mark).
3. Work it out mentally.  Dividing by 0.5 doubles; dividing by 0.2 multiplies by 5.
4. If asked, say whether the estimate is too big or too small, and why.
```

**Over or under?**

| You rounded... | Effect on the estimate |
|---|---|
| a number being multiplied or added **up** | too big |
| a number being multiplied or added **down** | too small |
| a divisor **up** | too small |
| a divisor **down** | too big |
| in both directions | cannot tell without more working |

Use your estimate to check a calculator answer. If the estimate is 10 and the display shows 103.7, a key or decimal point is wrong.

**Worked reminder.** 29 notebooks at £1.95 each: 30 × £2 = £60. Both numbers were rounded up and multiplied, so £60 is an **overestimate** (the true cost is £56.55).

## N15: Rounding

- **Decimal places**: count after the point. Keep trailing zeros: 3.996 to 2 d.p. is **4.00**.
- **Significant figures**: start at the first non-zero digit. 0.07049 to 2 s.f. is **0.070** (the final zero is significant and should be shown).
- **Keep place value**: 349 500 to 2 s.f. is **350 000**, not 35.
- **Context**: round up when everyone or everything must be included (buses, tins of paint); round down for "how many whole ones can you make or afford".
- **Money**: pounds to 2 d.p.
- **Never round mid-calculation.** Store full values; round once at the end.

## N15: Error intervals

**Method: rounding**

```
1. Find the unit of rounding (nearest 0.1, nearest 100, 2 s.f. -> what place?).
2. Halve it.
3. Lower limit = value − half unit  (≤)
   Upper limit = value + half unit  (<)
```

**Method: truncation**

```
Lower limit = the value itself     (≤)
Upper limit = value + one whole unit (<)
```

| Given | Error interval |
|---|---|
| p = 14.7 to 1 d.p. | 14.65 ≤ p < 14.75 |
| q = 5000 to the nearest 100 | 4950 ≤ q < 5050 |
| r = 9.2 truncated to 1 d.p. | 9.2 ≤ r < 9.3 |

## N16: Limits of accuracy

The two ends of the error interval are the **limits of accuracy**. To decide "can you be sure?", compare the relevant limit with the target, and quote it in your answer.

**(Higher) Upper and lower bounds in calculations**

| Operation | Maximum | Minimum |
|---|---|---|
| Add | UB + UB | LB + LB |
| Subtract | UB − **LB** | LB − **UB** |
| Multiply | UB × UB | LB × LB |
| Divide | UB ÷ **LB** | LB ÷ **UB** |

**(Higher) Worked reminder.** A rectangle is 6.5 m by 3.2 m, both to 1 d.p. Area bounds:

```
UB = 6.55 × 3.25 = 21.2875 m²
LB = 6.45 × 3.15 = 20.3175 m²
```

**(Higher) Method: answer to an appropriate degree of accuracy**

```
1. Find the UB and LB of the answer.
2. Round both to 1 s.f., then 2 s.f., then 3 s.f. ...
3. Stop at the most figures where they still agree.  That is your answer.
4. Give the reason: "both bounds round to ... to ... s.f."
```

For the rectangle: to 1 s.f. both bounds round to 20; to 2 s.f. they are 21 and 20. So the area is **20 m²** (1 s.f.).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Rounding vs truncation**: rounding goes half a unit either way; truncation goes a whole unit upwards only.
- **Error interval vs bounds**: an error interval is one measurement; bounds in a calculation (Higher) combine several.
- **d.p. vs s.f.**: 0.0503 is 4 d.p. but 3 s.f.
- **Length factor vs area/volume factor**: 100 for cm to m; 10 000 for cm² to m²; 1 000 000 for cm³ to m³.
- **Decimal time vs clock time**: 1.3 hours is 1 h 18 min, not 1 h 30 min.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 4.5 cm² to mm².
2. Convert 3750 cm³ to litres.
3. Write 3 hours 36 minutes in hours, as a decimal.
4. Round 0.30219 to 2 significant figures.
5. Round 64 970 to 2 significant figures.
6. Estimate 397 × 0.512 ÷ 19.6.
7. Write the error interval for y = 23.6, rounded to 1 decimal place.
8. w = 4.7, truncated to 1 decimal place. Write the error interval.
9. n = 2800 to the nearest 100. Write the error interval.
10. A rope is 12 m to the nearest metre. A hook can hold a rope up to 12.4 m long. Can you be sure the rope is short enough?
11. (Higher) a = 7.4 and b = 2.6, both to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of a − b.
12. (Higher) Using the same a and b, find the upper bound of a ÷ b to 3 s.f.

### Answers

1. 4.5 × 100 = **450 mm²**
2. 3750 ÷ 1000 = **3.75 litres**
3. 36 ÷ 60 = 0.6, so **3.6 hours**
4. **0.30**
5. **65 000**
6. 400 × 0.5 ÷ 20 = **10** (the exact value is 10.37...)
7. **23.55 ≤ y < 23.65**
8. **4.7 ≤ w < 4.8**
9. **2750 ≤ n < 2850**
10. 11.5 ≤ length < 12.5. The rope could be longer than 12.4 m (for example 12.45 m), so **no, you cannot be sure**.
11. 7.45 − 2.55 = **4.9**
12. 7.45 ÷ 2.55 = 2.9215... = **2.92**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Converting m² to cm² with × 100, or m³ to litres with × 1 000 000 instead of × 1000.
- Treating 0.4 hours as 40 minutes.
- Writing only the final estimate, with no rounded values shown.
- Dropping a significant trailing zero (writing 0.07 for 2 s.f.) or losing place value (writing 35 for 350 000).
- Rounding to the nearest whole number when the context needs rounding up (or down).
- Rounding part-way through and then rounding again at the end.
- Using ≤ at both ends of an error interval, or half a unit for a truncated value.
- Answering "can you be sure?" with yes/no but no limit quoted.
- (Higher) Using UB − UB or UB ÷ UB for a maximum.
- (Higher) Stating an answer to "appropriate accuracy" with no reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between rounding and truncation error intervals?**
A rounded value can be up to half a unit either side, while a truncated value can only be up to a whole unit above it. For example, 4.7 truncated to 1 decimal place gives 4.7 ≤ w < 4.8.

**Are imperial conversion factors given in the AQA GCSE Maths exam?**
Yes. Imperial conversions such as miles, pounds and pints are given in the question, but you must know the metric conversions yourself.

**How do you tell if an estimate is an overestimate?**
Look at the direction of each rounding. If both numbers in a multiplication were rounded up, the estimate is an overestimate; if roundings pull in different directions, you cannot be sure.

**Which content references cover measures and accuracy in AQA GCSE Maths 8300?**
Section 3.1.3 covers N13 to N16: standard units and conversions, estimation, rounding and error intervals, and limits of accuracy, with upper and lower bounds in calculations at Higher tier.
