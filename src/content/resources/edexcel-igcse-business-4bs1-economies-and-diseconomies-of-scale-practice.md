These questions cover section 5.1 Economies and diseconomies of scale (points 5.1.1 and 5.1.2) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [economies and diseconomies of scale study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-economies-and-diseconomies-of-scale/) and the [economies and diseconomies of scale revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-economies-and-diseconomies-of-scale-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define diseconomies of scale. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each of these is an internal economy of scale, an external economy of scale or a diseconomy of scale.

**(a)** A supermarket chain pays a lower price per tin because it orders in very large quantities. **[1]**
**(b)** A new motorway near a group of factories cuts delivery costs for all of them. **[1]**
**(c)** A firm that has grown to six sites finds managers at different sites doing the same work. **[1]**

**3.** Give two causes of diseconomies of scale. **[2]**

**4.** Penrith Pens has these total costs at different monthly outputs. **(Calculation)**

| Output (pens) | 1,000 | 3,000 | 6,000 | 9,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost | 8,000 | 18,000 | 30,000 | 49,500 |

**(a)** Calculate the average total cost at each output. **[4]**
**(b)** Identify the output at which average total cost is lowest. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one technical economy of scale a large car manufacturer could gain. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why fixed cost per unit falls as output rises. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one way a large business could reduce diseconomies of scale. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Marchetti Ices buys ice cream cones. It pays 0.12 per cone for orders under 20,000 a month and 0.09 per cone for orders of 20,000 or more. It uses 15,000 cones a month and plans to grow to 24,000.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly cost of cones at 15,000 and at 24,000. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the saving per cone. **[1]**
**(c)** Analyse one other internal economy of scale Marchetti Ices might gain as it grows. **[4]**

**9.** Tarnfield is a region with many textile businesses, specialist dye suppliers and a college that runs textile courses. Analyse how locating in Tarnfield could reduce average total cost for a new textile business. **[4]**

**10.** Orla's Organic Soaps has 20 employees and makes 8,000 bars a month at an average total cost of 2.40. Orla could move to a larger unit and make 20,000 bars a month at an average total cost of 1.90, using a bank loan of 60,000. She currently sells all 8,000 bars, and her market research suggests demand of about 14,000 bars a month.

**(a)** Calculate the total monthly cost at 8,000 bars and at 20,000 bars. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify whether Orla should expand to 20,000 bars a month. **[6]**

**11.** Grantley Hotels plc grew from 12 to 40 hotels in five years. Over that time its average total cost per room per night rose from 54 to 61, and guest complaints rose. Decisions on menus and room prices are made at head office, which now has five layers of management.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in average total cost per room per night. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Grantley Hotels should continue to grow. **[9]**

**12.** **(a)** Give one limit of growth other than diseconomies of scale. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a rise in average cost caused by higher world fuel prices is not a diseconomy of scale. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Rises in average total cost [1] that happen because a business has become too large, for example through poor communication or co-ordination [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Internal economy of scale [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** External economy of scale [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Diseconomy of scale [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: poor communication through many layers; difficulty co-ordinating and controlling many sites or departments; lower motivation among staff who feel distant from managers [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 8,000 ÷ 1,000 = **8.00** [1]; 18,000 ÷ 3,000 = **6.00** [1]; 30,000 ÷ 6,000 = **5.00** [1]; 49,500 ÷ 9,000 = **5.50** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** 6,000 pens [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Total cost rises at every step, yet average cost falls until 6,000. That is the difference between total and average cost that economies of scale depend on.

**5.** A large car maker can afford automated production lines and robots [1], which make each car faster and with less labour, so the cost per car falls [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Fixed costs, such as rent, stay the same whatever the output [1], so when output rises the same total is shared between more units [1], which means each unit carries a smaller share and average cost falls [1]. **[3]**

**7.** It could delegate decisions to managers at each site [1], so problems are dealt with quickly without passing through many layers [1], which reduces delays and mistakes and keeps average cost down [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 15,000 × 0.12 = **1,800** [1]; 24,000 × 0.09 = **2,160** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 0.12 − 0.09 = **0.03** per cone [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** For example, a marketing economy: as Marchetti sells more ice cream [1], the cost of promotion such as signs or a local radio advert is spread over more sales [1], so promotion cost per ice cream falls [1], which lowers average total cost and lets Marchetti keep prices competitive or raise profit [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A pool of skilled textile workers lives nearby, and the college trains new ones [1], so the business spends less on recruiting and training [1]. Specialist dye suppliers close by can deliver quickly and cheaply, cutting transport costs and the need to hold large stocks [1]. Because these savings come from outside the business, they are external economies that lower its average total cost from the start [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** 8,000 × 2.40 = **19,200** [1]; 20,000 × 1.90 = **38,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For expanding: average cost falls by 0.50 per bar, so each bar sold earns more profit [1], and Orla could cut prices to win customers from rivals [1]. Against expanding: research suggests demand of only about 14,000 bars, so 6,000 bars a month may not sell [1], and if she makes only 14,000 in the larger unit, fixed costs are spread over fewer bars, so average cost would be above 1.90 [1]. The 60,000 loan adds repayments and interest whatever is sold [1]. Judgement: for example, do not expand to 20,000 yet; first grow sales towards 14,000, then expand when demand is proven [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Economies of scale only help if the extra output is sold. The best answers use the gap between 20,000 and 14,000.

**11. (a)** (61 − 54) ÷ 54 × 100 [1] = **13.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Against further growth: average cost has risen by 13.0%, which is the sign of diseconomies of scale [1]. Five layers of management slow decisions, and head office setting menus and prices for 40 hotels cannot react to local conditions [1], so mistakes are repeated across many hotels [1]. Rising complaints suggest staff and service are suffering, which could damage the brand and future bookings [1]. For growth: a larger chain may still gain purchasing and marketing economies, such as bulk buying of linen and national advertising [1], and more hotels raise total revenue and market presence [1]. The diseconomies may be fixable: delegating menu and price decisions to hotel managers and removing management layers could bring costs back down [1]. Judgement: Grantley should pause growth until it has reorganised [1], because adding more hotels while communication problems persist is likely to push average cost and complaints higher still [1]. **[9]**

**12. (a)** Any one of: the market is too small; finance is not available; the owners want to keep control [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** A diseconomy of scale is a rise in average cost caused by the business being too large [1]. Higher world fuel prices raise costs for every business, whatever its size [1], so the rise is caused by an outside price change, not by the scale of the business [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying economies of scale lower total cost.
- Treating any cost rise as a diseconomy of scale.
- Confusing internal and external economies.
- Recommending growth without checking that demand exists for the extra output.
- Naming a type of economy without showing how it reduces cost per unit.
