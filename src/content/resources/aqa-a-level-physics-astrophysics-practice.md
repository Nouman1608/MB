These questions cover section 3.9 Astrophysics of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, version 1.4, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards), sub-sections 3.9.1 to 3.9.3. Astrophysics is one of the five options (sections 3.9 to 3.13); you study one, assessed in Paper 3 Section B. It is A-level only.

Review the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-astrophysics/) and [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-astrophysics-revision-notes/) first; the [course hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/physics/), [printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/physics/) and free [diagnostics](/diagnostics/) help you plan.

Data: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴; Wien constant 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K; 1 solar mass = 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg; 1 Mpc = 3.08 × 10²² m; 1 year = 3.15 × 10⁷ s; power of the Sun = 3.9 × 10²⁶ W.

## Questions

**1.** An astronomical refracting telescope has an objective of focal length 0.90 m and an eyepiece of focal length 25 mm. It is used in normal adjustment.

**(a)** Calculate the angular magnification. **[2]**
**(b)** State the separation of the lenses. **[1]**
**(c)** A planet subtends 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ rad at the unaided eye. Calculate the angle subtended by its final image. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Describe the path of light from a distant star through a Cassegrain telescope to the eyepiece. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a reflecting telescope does not suffer from chromatic aberration. **[2]**

**3.** A radio telescope has a dish of diameter 25 m and observes at a wavelength of 0.21 m.

**(a)** Calculate its minimum angular resolution. **[2]**
**(b)** Two radio sources are 5.0 × 10⁻³ rad apart. State whether the dish can resolve them. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the minimum angular resolution of a 0.20 m optical telescope at 550 nm. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the ratio of the collecting power of the dish to that of the optical telescope. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** Explain why radio telescopes can be ground-based but X-ray telescopes must be placed in space. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare a CCD with the eye as a detector in terms of quantum efficiency and convenience of use. **[2]**

**5.** Star A has apparent magnitude 0.5 and star B has apparent magnitude 5.5.

**(a)** State which star appears brighter. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the ratio of the intensity received from A to that from B. **[2]**
**(c)** Star B is 25 pc from Earth. Calculate its absolute magnitude. **[2]**

**6.** A star's black-body spectrum peaks at 290 nm. Its power output is 3.2 × 10²⁸ W.

**(a)** Calculate its surface temperature. **[2]**
**(b)** State its spectral class and intrinsic colour. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate its radius. **[3]**

**7.** **(a)** Explain why hydrogen Balmer absorption lines are strongest in A-class stars. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe the path on the HR diagram of a star like the Sun after it leaves the main sequence. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Give two reasons why exoplanets are difficult to detect directly. **[2]**
**(b)** A transit reduces the intensity of a star of radius 7.0 × 10⁸ m by 0.85%. Calculate the radius of the planet. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the radial velocity method. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Calculate the Schwarzschild radius of a supermassive black hole of 4.0 × 10⁶ solar masses. **[2]**
**(b)** A type 1a supernova reaches a peak apparent magnitude of 16.4. Its peak absolute magnitude is −19.3. Calculate its distance in parsecs. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how type 1a supernovae led to the proposal of dark energy. **[2]**
**(d)** A gamma ray burst releases 2.0 × 10⁴⁴ J. Compare this with the energy the Sun releases over a lifetime of 1.0 × 10¹⁰ years. **[2]**

**10.** A binary star system is viewed in the plane of its orbit. A line of rest wavelength 486.1 nm in one star's spectrum shifts by up to ±0.042 nm, with a period of 6.5 days.

**(a)** Explain why the line shifts periodically. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the star's orbital speed. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the radius of its circular orbit. **[2]**

**11.** A line of rest wavelength 393.4 nm is observed at 410.9 nm in a galaxy's spectrum. Take H = 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate z. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the recession speed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the distance to the galaxy in Mpc. **[2]**
**(d)** Estimate the age of the Universe in years, assuming H is constant. **[3]**
**(e)** State two other pieces of evidence for the Big Bang. **[2]**

**12.** A quasar has z = 0.16 and the intensity of its radiation at Earth is 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ W m⁻². Take H = 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

**(a)** State how quasars were first discovered. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the distance to the quasar in metres. **[2]**
**(c)** Estimate its power output. **[2]**
**(d)** Compare this with the power of the Sun. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** M = fₒ/fₑ = 0.90/0.025 [1] = **36** [1]
**(b)** fₒ + fₑ = **0.925 m** [1]
**(c)** β = Mα = 36 × 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ [1] = **7.9 × 10⁻³ rad** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert 25 mm to 0.025 m before dividing; a power-of-ten slip in (a) carries into (c), though the method in (c) can still earn credit.

**2. (a)** Light reflects off the parabolic concave primary mirror towards its focus [1]; a convex secondary mirror reflects it back [1]; through a hole in the primary to the eyepiece [1].
**(b)** Chromatic aberration comes from refraction, which differs with wavelength [1]; mirrors reflect all wavelengths at the same angle, so all colours focus at one point [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the mirror shapes; "a big mirror and a small mirror" is not enough.

**3. (a)** θ ≈ λ/D = 0.21/25 [1] = **8.4 × 10⁻³ rad** [1]
**(b)** **No**: 5.0 × 10⁻³ rad is smaller than 8.4 × 10⁻³ rad [1].
**(c)** θ ≈ 550 × 10⁻⁹/0.20 = **2.75 × 10⁻⁶ rad** [1]
**(d)** Collecting power ∝ D² [1]; (25/0.20)² = **1.6 × 10⁴** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), quote both numbers in your comparison; a bare "no" gives no reason for the judgement.

**4. (a)** The atmosphere is transparent to most radio wavelengths, so radio waves reach the ground [1]; X-rays are absorbed by the atmosphere, so the telescope must be above it [1].
**(b)** A CCD has a much higher quantum efficiency than the eye, detecting a larger fraction of incident photons [1]; it can integrate over long exposures and store a digital image for processing, unlike the eye [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs both detectors in each point; describing only the CCD is incomplete.

**5. (a)** **Star A** (lower magnitude) [1]
**(b)** Ratio = 2.51^(5.5 − 0.5) = 2.51⁵ [1] = **99.6 (about 100)** [1]
**(c)** 5.5 − M = 5 log(25/10) [1]; M = 5.5 − 1.99 = **3.5** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Keep d in parsecs inside the logarithm; converting to metres first gives a nonsense magnitude.

**6. (a)** T = 2.9 × 10⁻³/290 × 10⁻⁹ [1] = **1.0 × 10⁴ K** [1]
**(b)** Class **A**, **blue-white** [1]
**(c)** A = P/σT⁴ = 3.2 × 10²⁸/(5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (1.0 × 10⁴)⁴) [1]; r = √(A/4π) [1] = **2.1 × 10⁹ m** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Raise T to the fourth power and remember A = 4πr²; stopping at A gives an area, not a radius.

**7. (a)** Balmer lines need hydrogen atoms with electrons in the n = 2 state [1]; in cooler stars few atoms are excited to n = 2 [1]; in the hottest stars most hydrogen is ionised, so A stars have the most atoms in n = 2 [1].
**(b)** It expands and cools, moving up and to the right to become a red giant [1]; it then moves left and down to become a white dwarf [1].
*Examiner insight:* Both temperature limits are needed in (a); explaining only why cool stars have weak lines leaves the argument half done.

**8. (a)** The planet is very faint compared with its star [1]; the angular separation is too small to resolve [1].
**(b)** Fractional dip = (r_p/r_s)², so r_p = 7.0 × 10⁸ × √0.0085 [1] = **6.5 × 10⁷ m** [1]
**(c)** The star and planet orbit their common centre of mass [1]; the star's spectral lines show a periodic Doppler shift with the planet's orbital period [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert 0.85% to 0.0085 before taking the square root; using 0.85 gives a planet almost as large as the star.

**9. (a)** Rs = 2GM/c² = 2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.96 × 10³⁶/(3.00 × 10⁸)² [1] = **1.2 × 10¹⁰ m** [1]
**(b)** m − M = 16.4 − (−19.3) = 35.7 [1]; d = 10 × 10^(35.7/5) [1] = **1.4 × 10⁸ pc** [1]
**(c)** All type 1a supernovae reach about the same peak absolute magnitude, so they act as standard candles [1]; distant ones appeared fainter than expected, suggesting accelerating expansion, attributed to dark energy [1].
**(d)** Sun: 3.9 × 10²⁶ × 1.0 × 10¹⁰ × 3.15 × 10⁷ = 1.2 × 10⁴⁴ J [1]; the burst releases about **1.6 times** the Sun's lifetime output [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the double negative matters: m − M is 16.4 + 19.3, and a sign slip changes d by many orders of magnitude.

**10. (a)** The star moves alternately towards and away from Earth as it orbits [1].
**(b)** v = cΔλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.042/486.1 [1] = **2.6 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** T = 6.5 × 86 400 = 5.62 × 10⁵ s; r = vT/2π [1] = **2.3 × 10⁹ m** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert days to seconds before using r = vT/2π; units must be consistent for the final answer.

**11. (a)** z = (410.9 − 393.4)/393.4 = **0.0445** [1]
**(b)** v = zc = **1.33 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** d = v/H = 13 300 km s⁻¹/68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ [1] = **196 Mpc** [1]
**(d)** H = 68 × 10³/3.08 × 10²² = 2.21 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ [1]; 1/H = 4.53 × 10¹⁷ s [1] = **1.4 × 10¹⁰ years** [1]
**(e)** Cosmological microwave background radiation [1]; relative abundance of hydrogen and helium [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c), match units: v in km s⁻¹ with H in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ gives Mpc directly.

**12. (a)** As bright radio sources [1].
**(b)** v = 0.16 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 4.8 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹; d = 48 000/68 = 706 Mpc [1] = **2.2 × 10²⁵ m** [1]
**(c)** P = 4πd²I = 4π × (2.17 × 10²⁵)² × 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ [1] = **3.0 × 10³⁹ W** [1]
**(d)** About **7.6 × 10¹²** times the Sun's power [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert d from Mpc to metres before using I = P/4πd², or the power comes out wrong by a huge factor.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting M = fₑ/fₒ or omitting fₑ from the lens separation.
- Comparing an angle in degrees with λ/D in radians.
- Calling the higher-magnitude star brighter.
- Putting d in metres into m − M = 5 log(d/10).
- Forgetting the fourth power of T, or not square-rooting to get r.
- Using the percentage dip without converting it to a fraction.
- Leaving H in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ when computing 1/H in seconds.
