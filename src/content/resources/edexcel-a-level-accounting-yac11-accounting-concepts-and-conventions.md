This guide goes deeper into outcomes **1.1.8** to **1.1.10** of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11) specification, Issue 2, September 2018: "the concepts of going concern, prudence, accruals and consistency"; "the concepts and conventions of historic cost, materiality, money measurement, realisation and business entity"; and "the use of International Accounting Standards (IAS)". This is Unit 1, International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Each concept is defined with a short example in the [role, purpose and concepts study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-the-role-and-purpose-of-accounting-and-accounting-conce/), and the topic overview is in the [principles and double entry bookkeeping study guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-accounting-principles-and-double-entry-bookkeeping/). This page builds the skills examiners reward beyond definitions: linking each concept to the treatments you meet across Unit 1, correcting a draft profit for several breaches at once, explaining where concepts conflict, and connecting them to specific standards. Use the [concepts revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-accounting-concepts-and-conventions-revision-notes/) for recall and the [concepts practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-accounting-concepts-and-conventions-practice/) for exam-style work.

## The nine in one table

| Concept or convention | One-line rule | Typical breach |
|---|---|---|
| Going concern | Prepare statements assuming the business will continue for the foreseeable future | Valuing assets at cost less depreciation when closure is planned |
| Prudence | Do not overstate assets or profit, or understate liabilities or expenses | Valuing inventory above its net realisable value |
| Accruals | Match income and expenses to the period they relate to | Treating rent received in advance as this year's income |
| Consistency | Use the same policies from period to period | Changing depreciation method each year to improve profit |
| Historic cost | Record assets at the price paid | Revaluing inventory to selling price |
| Materiality | Strict treatment only where an item could affect users' decisions | Capitalising a $40 calculator |
| Money measurement | Record only items that can be measured reliably in money | Adding a value for staff loyalty |
| Realisation | Recognise revenue when goods or services are provided and the right to payment arises | Recording goods on sale or return as sales |
| Business entity | Record only the business's transactions | Paying the owner's private bills as business expenses |

## Concepts behind treatments across Unit 1

Questions often ask which concept "underlies" or "justifies" a treatment from another part of the specification.

| Treatment | Main concept | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Charging depreciation | Accruals | Spreads cost over the years that benefit |
| Showing assets at cost less depreciation | Going concern | Assumes the assets will be used, not sold off |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts | Prudence | Anticipates a likely loss |
| Inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | Prudence | Losses recognised now; gains wait until sale |
| Accrued expenses and prepayments | Accruals | Expenses matched to the period of benefit |
| Drawings recorded against capital | Business entity | The owner's withdrawals are not business expenses |
| Expensing small tools that last for years | Materiality | Tracking them costs more than the information is worth |
| Keeping the same depreciation method | Consistency | Profits remain comparable over time |
| Capital and revenue expenditure split | Accruals | Long-term benefits spread; short-term costs charged now |

## Worked example: correcting a draft profit

Ravel Interiors has a draft profit for the year of $38,400. The following items have not been treated correctly.

1. The owner's private car insurance of $1,100 was paid from the business bank account and charged as an expense.
2. Goods with a selling price of $3,000 (cost $2,000) were sent to a customer on sale or return and recorded as sales. The customer has not yet accepted them. They are not in closing inventory.
3. Rent of $900 received from a subtenant for next year has been included in this year's income.
4. A calculator costing $40 was recorded as a non-current asset. No depreciation was charged on it.
5. Closing inventory includes damaged goods at cost of $1,500. They can be sold for $700 after repairs costing $100.

| Item | Concept | Effect on profit |
|---|---|---|
| Draft profit | | 38,400 |
| 1 Private insurance | Business entity | +1,100 |
| 2 Sale or return: remove sale 3,000, add inventory at cost 2,000 | Realisation (inventory at cost: prudence) | −1,000 |
| 3 Rent received in advance | Accruals | −900 |
| 4 Calculator expensed | Materiality | −40 |
| 5 Inventory to NRV: 700 − 100 = 600, a fall of 900 | Prudence | −900 |
| **Corrected profit** | | **36,660** |

Notes on the method:

- Item 1 also changes capital: the $1,100 is drawings, so the correction increases profit and increases drawings by the same amount; capital at the year end is unchanged overall.
- Item 2 needs two adjustments. Revenue and trade receivables fall by $3,000; inventory rises by $2,000.
- Item 3 becomes a current liability (other payables) of $900.
- Item 5 uses net realisable value, the selling price less costs to complete and sell.

Show each adjustment with its sign and name the concept beside it; the concept names often carry marks of their own.

## When concepts conflict

Concepts sometimes point in different directions. Explaining the tension is good analysis.

- **Prudence and accruals.** Accruals would spread the cost of developing a new product over the years it earns revenue; prudence warns against carrying a cost forward as an asset when future revenue is uncertain. Where doubt is serious, prudence usually wins and the cost is expensed.
- **Prudence and consistency.** A business that has always used straight line depreciation may find that reducing balance better reflects an asset losing value fast. Consistency argues against changing; a fairer presentation argues for it. A change is acceptable if it gives a more appropriate view and its effect is disclosed.
- **Historic cost and relevance.** Land bought decades ago is shown at its original cost, which is objective but may be far below today's value, so the statement of financial position becomes less useful to a lender.
- **Prudence and neutrality.** Prudence does not allow deliberate understatement. Creating excessive allowances to reduce profit this year would let profit be released later, so it misleads users as much as overstatement does.
- **Materiality and completeness.** Expensing small items keeps the records simple but means some long-lasting items are not shown as assets. Judgement depends on the size of the business.

## 1.1.10 International Accounting Standards

IAS set out how transactions are recognised, measured and presented, so that statements prepared by different businesses and in different countries can be compared and trusted. Several link directly to the concepts in 1.1.8 and 1.1.9:

| Standard | What it covers | Concepts it puts into practice |
|---|---|---|
| IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements | Required statements and their content | Going concern, accruals basis, materiality, consistency of presentation |
| IAS 2 Inventories | Measuring inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | Prudence, historic cost |
| IAS 8 Accounting Policies, Changes in Accounting Estimates and Errors | Selecting and changing policies, correcting errors | Consistency |
| IAS 16 Property, Plant and Equipment | Cost of non-current assets and their depreciation | Historic cost, accruals |

The specification also states that examiners use IAS terminology, such as "trade receivables" rather than "debtors", and that candidates should use these terms.

**Benefits of IAS** to users: comparability between businesses and countries; greater confidence that statements follow recognised rules; less room for choosing treatments to flatter results.

**Limitations**: standards still allow choices and estimates (for example, the depreciation method and useful life), so comparability is not complete; small businesses may find compliance costly; standards change, which affects comparisons over time.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a concept correctly but not applying it to the item in the question.
- Naming accruals when the issue is when revenue is earned (realisation), or the reverse.
- Treating prudence as permission to understate profit.
- Correcting only one side of an item, such as removing a sale on sale or return but not adding the goods to inventory.
- Valuing damaged inventory at selling price without deducting costs to complete and sell.
- Treating consistency as forbidding any change; a justified, disclosed change is allowed.
- Writing "IAS 2 says…" for a rule it does not contain; quote standards only where you are sure.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify** the concept: one name per item, matched to the scenario.
- **Explain** a treatment: concept + what it requires + how it applies to this item.
- **Calculate** a corrected profit: start from the draft figure, list each adjustment with + or −, and total.
- **Evaluate** a proposed change of policy: weigh comparability (consistency) against a fairer view, consider the effect on profit and users, and conclude.
