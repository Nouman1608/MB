This guide covers **section 5.4 Supply-side policy** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus asks for the "meaning of supply-side policy, in terms of its effect on LRAS curves", its "objectives: increasing productivity and productive capacity", its "tools ... for example training, infrastructure development, support for technological improvement", and "AD/AS analysis" of its impact on equilibrium national income, real output, the price level and employment.

The broader [monetary policy and supply-side policy study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-monetary-policy-and-supply-side-policy/) sets out the basics. This page goes further: how to measure productivity and unit labour cost, why supply-side policy affects both AD and AS, and why its effect on actual output depends on how much spare capacity exists. All countries and figures are invented.

## 5.4.1 Meaning: a policy aimed at LRAS

**Supply-side policy** is government action to increase the economy's productive capacity by raising the quantity, quality or efficiency of the factors of production. Its defining effect is a rightward shift of the **long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** curve. Because LRAS shows the output the economy can produce when resources are fully employed, a shift in LRAS is the AD/AS version of an outward shift of the production possibility curve.

The syllabus allows two drawings of LRAS:

- **Vertical LRAS.** The line sits at full-capacity output. Supply-side policy moves the whole line to the right.
- **Three-section LRAS** (highly elastic, upward sloping, vertical). Supply-side policy moves the upward-sloping and vertical sections to the right, so the economy reaches full capacity at a higher output.

Keep two kinds of supply shift apart. A change in **costs**, such as a rise in oil prices, shifts short-run aggregate supply (SRAS). A change in **capacity** shifts LRAS. Many supply-side measures do both: a training scheme that raises output per worker lowers unit costs (SRAS shifts right) and raises the maximum the economy can produce (LRAS shifts right).

## 5.4.2 Objectives: productivity and productive capacity

**Productivity** is output per unit of input. Labour productivity is usually measured as output per worker or output per hour worked. **Productive capacity** is the maximum output the economy can produce with its resources and technology. Higher productivity is one way to raise capacity; a larger or healthier labour force, more capital and better technology are others.

Productivity matters for prices because of **unit labour cost**, the wage cost of producing one unit of output:

unit labour cost = wage per hour ÷ output per hour

**Worked example 1: productivity and unit labour cost.** A factory in Country M employs 400 workers for 40 hours a week, which is 16,000 hours. It produces 12,000 units a week, and the wage is 15 per hour.

- Productivity = 12,000 ÷ 16,000 = 0.75 units per hour.
- Unit labour cost = 15 ÷ 0.75 = 20 per unit.

After a government-funded training scheme, the same hours produce 13,200 units.

- Productivity = 13,200 ÷ 16,000 = 0.825 units per hour, a rise of 10%.
- Unit labour cost = 15 ÷ 0.825 = 18.18 per unit, a fall of 9.1%.

Firms can produce more at a lower cost per unit, so SRAS shifts right. Because each worker can now produce more, the economy's capacity rises and LRAS shifts right too. If wages rise in line with productivity, unit labour cost stays at 20, but real incomes rise without adding to inflation.

## 5.4.3 Tools: what each one improves

The syllabus names three tools "for example", so other measures that raise productivity or capacity are also valid if you explain the link.

| Tool | What it improves | Example measures | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training and education | Quality of labour (human capital) | Apprenticeships, vocational courses, retraining for workers whose jobs have disappeared | Benefits take years; workers may not use the skills, or may emigrate |
| Infrastructure development | Capital available to all firms; lower transport and energy costs | Roads, ports, railways, power stations, broadband networks | Very high cost; long building time; risk of choosing the wrong projects |
| Support for technological improvement | Efficiency and quality of production | Grants for research, tax allowances for firms that invest in new technology, funding for universities | Results are uncertain; firms may be paid for research they would have done anyway |

Strong answers say **which factor of production** a tool improves and **how** that raises output per unit of input, then link to LRAS. A list of tools with no mechanism earns little.

## 5.4.4 AD/AS analysis: three points that lift an answer

### Point 1: potential output rises before actual output

A rightward shift of LRAS raises what the economy **can** produce. Actual output rises only if aggregate demand is high enough to buy it.

**Worked example 2: capacity and spare capacity.** Country N's full-capacity output is 800 billion and actual real output is 760 billion, so spare capacity is 40 billion. A supply-side programme raises full capacity by 5% to 840 billion.

- If AD does not change, actual output stays at 760 billion and spare capacity grows to 80 billion.
- If AD later grows so that the economy wants to buy 830 billion, output can rise to that level while still below capacity, so the price level rises far less than it would have done with capacity stuck at 800 billion.

The lesson: supply-side policy creates room for **non-inflationary growth**, but it does not by itself fill spare capacity in a recession. On the three-section LRAS, an economy in the highly elastic section gains little immediate output from a supply-side shift.

### Point 2: many supply-side measures shift AD first

Building a railway or paying for training is government spending, a component of AD. So the first effect is often a rightward shift of AD, which raises output and employment but may also raise the price level. The LRAS effect arrives later, when the railway opens or the trained workers enter jobs. An answer that shows both stages, and their timing, shows strong analysis.

### Point 3: the price level can fall while output rises

When SRAS and LRAS shift right and AD is unchanged, the new equilibrium has **higher real output and a lower price level**. This is the key contrast with expansionary fiscal or monetary policy, which raises output and the price level together. Employment usually rises with output, but it may rise less than output if the gain comes from each worker producing more.

## Worked example 3: an annotated model answer

*Question:* Analyse how government investment in a national broadband network could affect real output and the price level.

> Building the network is government spending, so AD shifts right in the short run, raising real output and employment, with some upward pressure on the price level if the economy is near capacity. **[Stage one: the demand-side effect.]** Once the network operates, firms can sell online to more customers and use faster communication, so each worker and machine produces more. **[Mechanism: productivity.]** Lower costs per unit shift SRAS right, and the higher capacity shifts LRAS right. **[Link to the AS curves.]** With AD and both AS curves to the right, real output rises; the effect on the price level depends on the relative size of the shifts, but over time the extra capacity reduces inflationary pressure. **[Conclusion with a condition.]**

## Common mistakes

- Shifting only AD for a supply-side policy, or only SRAS when capacity has risen.
- Writing that supply-side policy works quickly. Most measures take years.
- Confusing a rise in output with a rise in productivity. More workers raise output; productivity is output per worker or per hour.
- Calculating unit labour cost as output ÷ wage instead of wage ÷ output per hour.
- Claiming that supply-side policy always raises employment. If productivity rises faster than demand, firms need fewer workers.
- Treating the syllabus examples as a closed list.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Explain:** tool, factor of production improved, productivity or capacity, LRAS shift.
- **Analyse:** draw AD/AS with labelled axes, LRAS1 and LRAS2 (and SRAS if relevant), both equilibria, and comment on output, the price level and employment.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** weigh time lags, cost and opportunity cost, the uncertainty of results, and whether demand is strong enough to use new capacity. Finish with a judgement that depends on conditions.

The distinction between market-based and interventionist supply-side policy is A Level content (section 10.3), so at AS focus on the named tools and their effect on LRAS.
