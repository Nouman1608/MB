## Core definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Externality | A side effect of a transaction that reaches an outsider who neither pays nor is paid |
| MSC | Private marginal cost, MPC, with the extra cost to others added |
| MSB | Private marginal benefit, MPB, with the extra benefit to others added |
| Socially optimum output | Where MSB = MSC |
| Allocative efficiency | Producing where MSB = MSC, so community surplus is maximised |
| Welfare loss | Loss of community surplus when output is not at the social optimum |
| Merit good | Under-consumed, often because of a positive externality or undervalued benefit |
| Demerit good | Over-consumed, often because of a negative externality or undervalued harm |
| Common pool resource | Rivalrous but non-excludable |
| Tragedy of the commons | Each user's rational overuse destroys the shared resource |

## The four diagrams in one table

Axes: price or cost (vertical), quantity (horizontal). Always draw the private curves, the social curve, both outputs and the triangle.

| Case | What differs from the private curves | Market versus optimum | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative externality of production | MSC above MPC | Overproduction | Factory pollution |
| Negative externality of consumption | MSB below MPB | Overconsumption | Traffic in a city |
| Positive externality of production | MSC below MPC | Underproduction | Orchard feeding neighbouring bees |
| Positive externality of consumption | MSB above MPB | Underconsumption | Education |

Method in steps for any diagram:

1. Draw demand (MPB) and supply (MPC) and label the market output.
2. Add the single social curve that differs (MSC or MSB).
3. Mark the social optimum where MSB = MSC and label it.
4. Shade the welfare loss triangle between the two outputs.
5. Add the policy curve if the question asks for one.

## Worked reminder: negative externality and a tax

Fictional market in the country Veltria: MPB P = 40 − Q, MPC P = 4 + Q, marginal external cost 6 crowns (VC).

- Market: 40 − Q = 4 + Q, so Q = 18, P = 22 VC.
- MSC = 10 + Q. Optimum: 40 − Q = 10 + Q, so Q = 15, P = 25 VC.
- Pigouvian tax = 6 VC per unit, which shifts MPC up to MSC.
- Consumers pay 25 VC; producers keep 25 − 6 = 19 VC; revenue = 6 × 15 = 90 VC.
- **HL only:** at Q = 18, MSC = 28 and MSB = 22. Gap 6, base 3. Welfare loss = ½ × 3 × 6 = 9 VC.

The tax equals the marginal external cost. The tax revenue is a rectangle: tax per unit times the optimum output.

## Common pool resources

- **Characteristics:** rivalrous and non-excludable.
- **Why overuse happens:** each user gets the whole gain from an extra unit, and the damage to the stock is shared.
- **Outcome:** the tragedy of the commons, and unsustainable production, a negative externality.
- **Rule of thumb for fisheries and grazing:** open access adds users while the *average* return covers the cost; the social optimum stops when the *extra* return falls below cost.

Fictional check: a pasture where the extra milk from the fourth cow is 18 and from the fifth is 14, with a cost of 16 per cow. The best herd stops at four cows, since the fifth adds 14, less than 16.

| Management approach | How it works | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Tax or fee | Charges users for the damage | Hard to set at the right level |
| Regulation or quota | Caps catch or use | Needs monitoring and enforcement |
| Tradable permits | Cap plus trading of rights | Initial allocation can be unfair |
| Collective self-governance | Community writes and polices rules | May fail in large groups |
| International agreement | States share targets | Free riding, weak enforcement |

## Policy table

| Policy | Targets | Effect on the diagram | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect (Pigouvian) tax | Negative externality | MPC shifts up towards MSC | Hard to measure the external cost |
| Carbon tax | Emissions of a polluting industry | MPC shifts up by the tax | Industry may relocate; price rises hit households |
| Subsidy | Positive externality | MPC shifts down towards MSC | Costs the government; can be wasted |
| Legislation and regulation | Pollution, unsafe practice | Vertical line at the permitted quantity; limits or bans the activity | Enforcement cost; no incentive to do better |
| Education and awareness | Demerit and merit goods | Demand shifts left (demerit) or right (merit) | Slow and uncertain effect |
| Tradable permits | Total pollution | Fixes the total; price set by trading | Cap set wrongly; hard to monitor |
| International agreements | Global issues | Shared targets across states | Monitoring, enforcement and free riding |
| Government provision | Merit goods | State supplies directly | Opportunity cost; may be inefficient |

## Permits: worked reminder

Two firms each emit 60 units and the cap is 80, so total cuts of 40 are needed. Firm A cuts a unit for 3 and firm B for 8 (fictional crowns).

- No trading: each cuts 20. Cost = 20 × 3 + 20 × 8 = 220.
- With trading: A cuts 40 for 120 and sells 20 permits to B. Cost = 120.
- Saving = 100, because the low-cost firm does the cutting.
- The permit price settles between 3 and 8.

## Evaluating policies

Use three headings, as the guide does.

- **Measurement:** the external cost or benefit has to be estimated, so the tax or subsidy may be wrong.
- **Effectiveness:** depends on elasticity, enforcement, substitutes and time.
- **Stakeholders:** consumers, producers, workers, taxpayers, governments and the affected third parties each gain or lose differently.

For international cooperation add: sustainability problems are global; countries have different interests; monitoring and enforcement are hard.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Merit good versus positive externality:** a merit good is defined by under-consumption through undervaluing the benefit. A positive externality is a benefit to others. A good can be both.
- **Common pool resource versus public good:** a common pool resource is rivalrous. A public good is non-rivalrous. Both are non-excludable. Public goods are covered in a separate sub-topic.
- **Tax versus permits:** a tax fixes the price per unit of pollution; permits fix the quantity.
- **MPC versus MSC:** MPC is what the producer pays; MSC includes the cost to others.

## Quick self-test

Use this fictional market for questions 7 to 10: MPB P = 40 − Q, MPC P = 4 + Q, marginal external cost 6.

1. Define a negative externality of consumption.
2. State the condition for allocative efficiency.
3. Which curve lies below MPB when consumption causes harm to others?
4. Name the two characteristics of a common pool resource.
5. Give one reason a carbon tax may be hard to set at the right level.
6. State one advantage of tradable permits over regulation.
7. Find the market output.
8. Find the socially optimum output.
9. State the tax per unit that achieves the optimum.
10. **HL only:** calculate the welfare loss.

### Answers

1. A cost imposed on third parties by the consumption of a good, for which they are not compensated.
2. MSB = MSC.
3. The MSB curve.
4. Rivalrous and non-excludable.
5. The cost of the damage from carbon is uncertain and hard to value in money.
6. The total is capped, and the cheapest cuts are made first, so the cap is met at lower total cost.
7. 40 − Q = 4 + Q, so Q = 18 (price 22).
8. MSC = 10 + Q; 40 − Q = 10 + Q gives Q = 15 (price 25).
9. 6 per unit, equal to the marginal external cost.
10. Gap at Q = 18 is 28 − 22 = 6; base is 3; ½ × 3 × 6 = 9.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving the social curve unlabelled or drawing it through the market equilibrium.
- Not naming both outputs on the quantity axis.
- Shading too much: the welfare loss is only the triangle between the two outputs.
- Moving the wrong curve for a subsidy or tax. Taxes and subsidies on suppliers shift supply.
- Describing the tragedy of the commons without saying why individual users overuse.
- Giving a policy without saying what it does to a curve or to the price.
- Evaluation that lists strengths and limits but never weighs them for the situation given.
- Skipping the working in a calculation. Show every step.
- Mixing up common pool resources and public goods.
