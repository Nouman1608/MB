whole section is marked **(HT only)**, so it is **Higher tier only**: Foundation tier students are not
assessed on it. It is examined on Paper 2. Two parts are also marked **physics only**: calculations for
events such as collisions (4.5.7.2) and changes in momentum (4.5.7.3).

| Spec point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|

| 4.5.7.3 Changes in momentum (physics only) | Apply F = m Δv / Δt (equation sheet); explain safety features (air bags, seat belts, gymnasium crash mats, cycle helmets, cushioned playground surfaces) using rate of change of momentum; relate force, mass, velocity and acceleration |

## 4.5.7.1 Momentum

```
momentum = mass × velocity
p = m v       p in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s),  m in kg,  v in m/s     (recall)
```

Momentum is a **vector**, because velocity is a vector. It has the same direction as the velocity. In a
calculation, choose one direction as positive; momentum in the opposite direction is then **negative**.

```
a 0.40 kg ball moving right at 5.0 m/s:   p = 0.40 × 5.0    = +2.0 kg m/s
the same ball moving left at 5.0 m/s:     p = 0.40 × (−5.0) = −2.0 kg m/s
```

The two balls have the same speed and the same size of momentum, but their momenta are different, because
the directions are different.

## 4.5.7.2 Conservation of momentum

**In a closed system, the total momentum before an event is equal to the total momentum after the event.**

A **closed system** is one in which no external resultant force acts on the objects; for trolleys on a track,
that means friction is small enough to ignore. Events include collisions, objects joining together, and
explosions or objects pushing apart.

### Method in steps

1. Choose the positive direction and write it down.
2. Write total momentum before = sum of m v for every object, with signs.
3. Write total momentum after in the same way, with the unknown as a letter.
4. Set them equal and solve. Check the sign of the answer for the direction.

**Worked example 1: objects that stick together.** Trolley A (0.50 kg) moves right at 4.0 m/s. Trolley B
(0.30 kg) moves left at 2.0 m/s. They collide and stick together.

```
take right as positive
before:  0.50 × 4.0 + 0.30 × (−2.0) = 2.0 − 0.60 = 1.4 kg m/s
after:   (0.50 + 0.30) × v = 0.80 v
0.80 v = 1.4   →   v = 1.75 m/s, to the right (positive)
```

**Worked example 2: objects that separate after a collision.** A 2.0 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m/s hits a
stationary 1.0 kg trolley. Afterwards the 2.0 kg trolley moves on at 1.0 m/s in the same direction. Find the
velocity of the 1.0 kg trolley.

```
before:  2.0 × 3.0 + 1.0 × 0 = 6.0 kg m/s
after:   2.0 × 1.0 + 1.0 × v = 2.0 + v
2.0 + v = 6.0   →   v = 4.0 m/s, in the original direction
```

**Worked example 3: pushing apart from rest.** Two ice skaters stand still, then push each other away. The
60 kg skater moves off at 1.5 m/s. Find the velocity of the 45 kg skater.

```
before: total momentum = 0 (both at rest)
after:  60 × 1.5 + 45 × v = 0
45 v = −90   →   v = −2.0 m/s  (2.0 m/s in the opposite direction)
```

The total momentum stays zero, so the two momenta are equal in size and opposite in direction. The lighter
skater moves faster.

### Explaining an event in words

To **describe and explain** an example without numbers, say what the total momentum is before, state that it
is conserved, and deduce what must happen after. For example, a person jumps from a stationary rowing boat
onto a jetty. Before the jump the total momentum is zero. Afterwards the person has momentum towards the
jetty, so the boat must have an equal momentum in the opposite direction: it moves away from the jetty.

### Investigating collisions

Trolleys on a level track can be used to test conservation. Two light gates measure each trolley's velocity
before and after the collision (from the length of a card on the trolley divided by the time it blocks the
gate). Measure the masses on a balance, calculate the momentum of each trolley before and after, and compare
the totals. Small differences come from friction, which means the system is not perfectly closed.

## 4.5.7.3 Changes in momentum (physics only)

When a force acts on an object that is moving, or able to move, its momentum changes. Combining two
equations you already know:

```
F = m a   and   a = (v − u) / t
F = m (v − u) / t   →   F = m Δv / Δt      (equation sheet)
```

**m Δv is the change in momentum**, so **force equals the rate of change of momentum**. A given change in
momentum needs a large force if it happens quickly, and only a small force if it happens slowly.

**Worked example 4: a change of direction.** A tennis ball of mass 0.058 kg reaches a racket at 20 m/s and
leaves in the opposite direction at 30 m/s. It is in contact with the racket for 0.0050 s.

```
take the direction the ball leaves in as positive
Δv = 30 − (−20) = 50 m/s
change in momentum = 0.058 × 50 = 2.9 kg m/s
F = 2.9 / 0.0050 = 580 N
```

Forgetting the reversal of direction (using 30 − 20 = 10 m/s) gives a force five times too small.

**Worked example 5: increasing the stopping time.** A 1200 kg car travelling at 15 m/s crashes and stops.

```
change in momentum = 1200 × 15 = 18 000 kg m/s
stopping in 0.030 s:  F = 18 000 / 0.030 = 600 000 N
stopping in 0.15 s:   F = 18 000 / 0.15  = 120 000 N
```

The change in momentum is the same either way. Making the stop last five times as long makes the force five
times smaller.

### Safety features

Each feature works in the same way: it **increases the time** over which the person's momentum changes, so
the **rate of change of momentum** is smaller and the **force** on the person is smaller.

| Feature | How it increases the time |
|---|---|
| Air bag | Inflates and squashes as the head and chest move into it, so they slow down over a longer time |
| Seat belt | Stretches slightly, so the body slows down over a longer time than hitting the dashboard |
| Gymnasium crash mat | Squashes as a gymnast lands, extending the time to stop |
| Cycle helmet | Its foam crushes on impact, so the head stops over a longer time |
| Cushioned playground surface | Compresses when a child falls, so the child stops over a longer time |

### Annotated model answer

**Question (4 marks).** Explain how a cycle helmet reduces the risk of a head injury in a fall.

> When the head hits the ground, its momentum falls to zero **[the change in momentum]**. The foam in the
> helmet crushes, so the head takes longer to stop **[time increased]**. This means the rate of change of
> momentum is smaller **[link to F = mΔv/Δt]**, so the force on the head is smaller and the injury is less
> severe **[force reduced, consequence]**.

"It absorbs the impact" or "it cushions the head" earns nothing on its own: the marks come from the chain
time → rate of change of momentum → force.

## Common mistakes

- Treating momentum as a scalar and ignoring opposite directions.
- Forgetting that objects that stick together share one velocity and a combined mass.
- Writing Δv = final − initial without signs when the object bounces back.
- Saying safety features "reduce the change in momentum". The change is the same; the **time** is longer.
- Saying momentum is conserved when an external force (such as friction or braking) acts.
- Mixing up kg m/s with N.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: state your positive direction, write the before and after totals, then solve.
- **Explain** a safety feature: always give the three-step chain, naming the feature's action.
- **Describe and explain** an example of conservation: state the total before, apply conservation, and give
  the consequence after.
