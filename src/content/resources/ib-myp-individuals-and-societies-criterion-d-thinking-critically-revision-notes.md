## What the guide states

- Students use critical thinking skills to develop and apply their understanding of individuals and societies and the process of investigation.
- Year 5 strands: (i) discuss concepts, issues, models, visual representation and theories; (ii) synthesize information to make valid, well-supported arguments; (iii) analyse and evaluate a range of sources and data in terms of origin and purpose, examining value and limitations; (iv) interpret different perspectives and their implications.
- Maximum 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.
- In the on-screen examination, the Thinking critically task is worth 36 marks (20 for Criterion D, 8 for Criterion C, 8 for Criterion A). It uses structured questions that end in an extended piece of writing.

## Top-band wording (Year 5, 7-8)

| Strand | Descriptor | Plain words |
|---|---|---|
| i | completes a detailed discussion of concepts, issues, models, visual representation and theories | balanced, detailed, uses evidence |
| ii | synthesises information to make valid, well-supported arguments | combines sources into a reasoned claim |
| iii | effectively analyses and evaluates a range of sources/data in terms of origin and purpose, consistently recognising value and limitations | several sources, each judged fairly |
| iv | thoroughly interprets a range of different perspectives and their implications | who thinks what, why and what follows |

Band climb for strand iii: describes a limited number (1-2); analyses and/or evaluates, some value and limitations (3-4); effectively analyses and evaluates a range, usually recognising value and limitations (5-6); consistently recognises (7-8).

## Command terms

| Term | Guide meaning |
|---|---|
| Analyse | break down to bring out the essential elements or structure |
| Discuss | considered, balanced review including a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses; conclusions supported by evidence |
| Evaluate | appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations |
| Interpret | use knowledge to recognise trends and draw conclusions from information |
| Synthesize | combine different ideas to create new understanding |
| Justify | give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer |
| Explain | detailed account including reasons or causes |

## OPVL: the source checklist

| Letter | Ask | Example prompts |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | who, when, where, what type? | primary or secondary, date, author's role |
| Purpose | why, for whom? | inform, persuade, defend, record, entertain |
| Value | what is useful for this question? | firsthand, detailed, expert, balanced, evidence given |
| Limitations | what is missing or misleading? | one viewpoint, no evidence, written later, interest in the outcome |

Rule: value and limitations must be linked to **the question asked**. A source can be valuable for one question and weak for another.

### Sentence frames

- "This source was written by ... in ..., so it is ... (primary or secondary)."
- "Its purpose was to ..., which suggests that ..."
- "It is valuable because ..."
- "However, it is limited because ..., so it cannot show ..."

## Bias: what to say

Do not stop at "it is biased". Say **what** the bias is, **why** it exists and **how** it affects the evidence. Then say what the source is still useful for. A biased source, such as a company's advert, can be an excellent source for the company's point of view.

## Perspectives

1. **Identify** the perspective (who, and what is their view).
2. **Explain** why they hold it (position, interests, values, experience).
3. **State the implication**: what follows for people or events if their view is accepted.

Example: a council wants a new road. The owner of a shop in the area supports it because more traffic means more customers. Residents oppose it because of noise and air pollution. Implication: if the road is built, shop income may rise but residents' health and quality of life may suffer, so the council must weigh economic against social and environmental effects.

## Building an argument

**Claim, evidence, reasoning, counter-view, conclusion** (CERCC).

- **Claim**: a clear position that answers the question.
- **Evidence**: facts and quotations from at least two sources.
- **Reasoning**: why the evidence supports the claim.
- **Counter-view**: the best argument against, and a response.
- **Conclusion**: a judgment that follows, qualified where the evidence is limited.

Synthesis means combining ideas from different sources into something new, not summarising source A and then source B. Use linking words such as "both suggest", "in contrast", "taken together".

## Discussing concepts, models and issues

1. Define the idea or model.
2. Give arguments in support, with evidence.
3. Give arguments against or limits, with evidence.
4. Weigh up and conclude.

Models simplify reality. State what the model explains well and what it leaves out.

## Visual and data sources

- **Map**: location, scale, what is shown, what is missing.
- **Graph or table**: quote figures, describe the trend, give a reason, state limits (for example years not covered, no reasons given).
- **Cartoon or poster**: message, symbols, audience, origin and purpose.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the source instead of evaluating it.
- "It is biased" with no reasons.
- Rejecting a source for bias rather than using it for its own perspective.
- One source used when the question asks for a range.
- Summarising instead of synthesising.
- One-sided discussion.
- A conclusion with no judgment, or one that ignores evidence.

## Quick self-test

1. What do the letters O, P, V and L stand for?
2. A source was written 60 years after the event by someone who was not there. Name one limitation and one possible value.
3. Give the difference between "analyse" and "evaluate".
4. Why is "it is biased" not enough?
5. Write a linking phrase that shows synthesis.

### Answers

1. Origin, purpose, value, limitations.
2. Limitation: it is not firsthand and may be affected by later knowledge or the writer's views. Value: the writer may have had time to compare many sources and see the long-term results.
3. Analyse breaks something into parts and relationships. Evaluate weighs strengths and limitations to make an appraisal.
4. It does not say what the bias is, why it exists or how it affects the evidence, and a biased source can still be useful for showing a particular point of view.
5. For example: "Taken together, Sources A and B suggest that ..." or "Both sources agree that ..., but they differ on ...".

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use OPVL?**
The guide asks for analysis of origin and purpose and for value and limitations. OPVL is a classroom device for covering all four.

**Can I mention my own opinion?**
Yes, if it is supported by evidence and reasoning. The descriptors ask for valid, well-supported arguments, not opinions on their own.

**How do I show a range of sources?**
Use at least two or three different types or views, and compare them directly.
