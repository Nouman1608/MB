This study guide covers **the HL essay** in IB Diploma Programme Language A: Literature. It is
aligned to the IB Diploma Programme subject brief *Language A: literature* and draws on all three
areas of exploration: Readers, writers and texts; Time and space; and Intertextuality: connecting
texts. It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL 2021). The essay is
**HL only**: SL students do not write it.

## What the subject brief says

The brief gives these facts about the HL essay, and no more:

- It is a **written coursework component**.
- It is a **1,200–1,500 word essay on one work studied**.
- It is worth **20% of the final grade at HL**.
- It sits with Paper 1 and Paper 2 under **external** assessment. The individual oral is the internal
  component.

At HL, Paper 2 drops from 35% to 25% and the oral from 30% to 20%; the essay takes up that 20%. Your
teacher will share the assessment criteria, the rules on drafts and feedback, and your school's
deadlines. Ask early what counts towards the word count.

The brief's assessment objectives describe what the essay has to show. You must **know, understand
and interpret** a work and "contexts in which texts are written and/or received"; **analyse and
evaluate** "ways in which the use of language creates meaning"; and **communicate** "ideas in clear,
logical and persuasive ways".

## Choosing the work

You write on one literary work your class studied. The examples on this page use Kate Chopin's short
story "The Story of an Hour" (1894), which is out of copyright and about a thousand words long, so
you can check every quotation. Louise Mallard, who has "a heart trouble", is told her husband has
died in a railway accident. Alone in her room she finds herself whispering "free, free, free!" He
walks in alive, and she dies. Treat it as a model only.

Choose a work where:

1. **Something puzzles you.** A line you cannot settle, an ending you disagree with.
2. **The evidence clusters.** You can find six or more precise moments that bear on one question.
3. **You can analyse the craft.** You can say *how* the writer's choices create the effect.
4. **The question fits.** 1,500 words is short. A narrow question on a rich work beats a broad one.

## From topic to line of inquiry

The question your whole essay answers is usually called a **line of inquiry**. A topic names an area.
A line of inquiry asks something arguable about it and points at the writer's methods.

| Level | Example on Chopin |
|---|---|
| Topic | Freedom in "The Story of an Hour" |
| Better | How does Louise feel about her husband's death? |
| Line of inquiry | How does Chopin turn Louise's "heart trouble" from a medical diagnosis into a judgement on marriage, and how far does the final sentence invite us to reject the doctors' reading? |

The third version names a feature (the heart as image and diagnosis), a claim to test (it becomes a
judgement on marriage) and a tension (the doctors' verdict against the reader's).

### Three ways in, one work

Each area of exploration suggests a different kind of question. Because the essay is on one work,
"connecting texts" here means how the work uses conventions, allusions or other texts, not a
comparison. Comparison is Paper 2's job.

- **Readers, writers and texts.** *How does Chopin's narration move in and out of Louise's thoughts,
  and how does that shape our judgement of her "monstrous joy"?*
- **Time and space.** *How might a reader in 1894, when a married woman had limited legal and
  economic independence, and a reader today judge Louise's wish to "live for herself" differently?*
- **Intertextuality: connecting texts.** *How do allusions such as "a goddess of Victory" and "a
  very elixir of life" give an hour in a bedroom the scale of myth, and what happens to that scale
  in the final line?*

### Worked example: narrowing a question

**Start:** "I want to write about marriage in the story."

**Step 1: collect evidence before the question.** Re-read and log every moment that involves the
heart or the body:

- She is "afflicted with a heart trouble", so the news is broken "as gently as possible".
- She sinks into the chair, "pressed down by a physical exhaustion".
- As the word "free" arrives, "Her pulses beat fast, and the coursing blood warmed and relaxed every
  inch of her body."
- The doctors say she "died of heart disease — of the joy that kills."

**Step 2: find the pattern.** The heart is weak when she is a wife and strong when she imagines
living alone. The only people who "read" her heart at the end are the doctors, who get it wrong.

**Step 3: ask the question the pattern raises.** *Why does the diagnosis in the last line sound so
confident, when the reader knows more than the doctors do?*

**Step 4: write a working thesis.** Chopin makes Louise's heart the story's real narrator: it
weakens under marriage and quickens at the thought of "years to come that would belong to her
absolutely". The doctors' final verdict, "the joy that kills", is dramatic irony. The reader
knows it was the loss of that joy, not the joy itself, that killed her.

That thesis gives three body sections: the heart as a sign of confinement; the body's awakening by
the window; and the final diagnosis as a misreading the reader is asked to correct.

## Testing the line of inquiry

Before you draft, put the question through three tests.

- **Could someone disagree?** "Chopin shows Louise is unhappy" is a fact. "The final line asks us to
  side against the doctors" can be argued both ways, so it is worth an essay.
- **Is there enough evidence?** Count your logged moments. Fewer than six usually means the question
  is too narrow or on the wrong work.
- **Does it fit?** If each body section needs its own background, the question is too wide.

If a question fails, change the question, not the evidence.

## Research and evidence

### The work comes first

Your main evidence is the work itself. Keep an **evidence log** as you re-read:

| Quotation | Feature | Effect | Supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| "facing the open window" | setting, symbol | the window opens onto a world she has not been allowed to enter | section 2 |
| "free, free, free!" | repetition, exclamation | the word grows from a whisper into a chant; she is learning to say it | section 2 |
| "And yet she had loved him — sometimes. Often she had not." | short sentences, self-correction | honesty arrives in stages, each sentence taking back part of the last | section 1 |
| "of the joy that kills" | dramatic irony | the doctors' certainty is wrong, and the reader knows why | section 3 |

Add a location for each quotation from the edition you used, so you can reference it later.

### Critical perspectives

The brief says the course asks you to "consider their own interpretations as well as the critical
perspectives of others". In the essay, read critics **after** your own reading. Use them to test
your thesis: does a critic see the ending as tragedy, irony or both? Do you agree, and why? If a
critic's idea shapes a point, credit it even when you do not quote it. The brief does not say
whether secondary sources are required, so check with your teacher.

Record full details of every source the moment you use it: author, title, publisher or website,
date and page.

## Drafting

Plan to a word budget before you write. A worked budget for a 1,400-word target:

| Part | Words |
|---|---|
| Introduction | 150 |
| Three body sections × 370 | 1,110 |
| Conclusion | 140 |
| **Total** | **1,400** |

That leaves 100 words of room under the 1,500 maximum.

Then draft and revise in separate passes:

1. **Skeleton.** Thesis plus a one-sentence claim for each body section.
2. **Body first.** Write the sections; write the introduction last, once you know what you argued.
3. **Argument pass.** Does each paragraph answer the line of inquiry? Cut any that does not.
4. **Evidence pass.** Check every quotation against the text, word for word.
5. **Precision pass.** Replace "shows" with verbs that say what the language does: withholds,
   undercuts, delays, echoes.
6. **Length pass.** If you are over, cut summary and repeated points before you cut analysis.

Use the feedback your teacher gives within your school's process. The final version must be your
own work.

### Model body paragraph (original text written for Marlbridge)

> Chopin lets Louise's honesty arrive in stages, and the syntax records each retreat. "And yet she
> had loved him — sometimes." The dash holds the confession back for a moment, and "sometimes"
> shrinks the love it seemed to affirm. The next sentence, "Often she had not", is shorter and
> plainer, as if the truth needs fewer words once it is admitted. Then comes an exclamation, not a
> question: "What did it matter!" The narration has moved inside Louise's mind, so the reader hears
> her conclusion before the narrator can judge it. This matters for the ending. Because we have
> watched her reach this freedom one clause at a time, the doctors' tidy phrase "the joy that kills"
> sounds like a verdict written by people who never heard these sentences.

The claim is about craft, each quotation is embedded and analysed, and the ending links back to the
thesis.

## Referencing

- **Quote exactly.** Keep the spelling, punctuation and capitals of your edition. Show a cut with an
  ellipsis (…) and any word you add with square brackets.
- **Embed quotations** in your own grammar: *she whispers "free, free, free!" as if learning a new
  word*. Avoid a quotation standing alone as a sentence.
- **Keep them short.** Three to eight words that carry the feature you analyse usually do more than
  a long passage.
- **Give a location** in the style your school uses, such as MLA.
- **List sources** at the end: the work itself and every secondary source you used.
- **Credit ideas as well as words.** A paraphrased critic still needs a citation.

## Common errors

- **A theme, not a question.** "Freedom in the story" gives you nothing to prove.
- **Retelling the plot.** Louise's story takes a few sentences to summarise; the reader of your
  essay knows the work. Spend the words on analysis.
- **A technique with no effect.** "Chopin uses repetition" earns little until you say what repeating
  "free" does.
- **Context as decoration.** A paragraph on the 1890s helps only if it changes how a line is read.
- **Mis-quoting.** Chopin writes "no one to live for her", not "no one to live for". Changing one word
  changes the meaning you analyse.
- **Going over 1,500 words.** Plan to a budget, and cut summary first.
