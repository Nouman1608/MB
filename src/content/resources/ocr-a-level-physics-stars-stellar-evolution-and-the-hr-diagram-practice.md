## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by each term.

**(a)** planetary satellite **[1]**
**(b)** comet **[1]**
**(c)** galaxy **[1]**

**2.** Explain how an interstellar cloud of gas and dust turns into a stable star. **[4]**

**3.** A main sequence star radiates 6.0 × 10²⁶ W, all supplied by fusion. Calculate the mass converted to energy each second. **[3]**

**4.** Describe what happens to a star like the Sun from the time hydrogen in its core is used up until it becomes a white dwarf. **[5]**

**5.** A white dwarf has a mass of 1.1 solar masses and a radius of 6.0 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Explain how electron degeneracy pressure supports a white dwarf. **[2]**
**(b)** State the Chandrasekhar limit. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the mean density of this white dwarf. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how a massive star ends its life in a supernova. **[6]**

**7.** A neutron star has mass 2.2 × 10³⁰ kg and radius 11 km.

**(a)** Calculate its mean density. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the gravitational field strength at its surface. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the escape velocity from its surface and state whether it is a black hole. **[3]**

**8.**
**(a)** Describe two characteristics of a neutron star. **[2]**
**(b)** State what is meant by the event horizon of a black hole. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that the event horizon radius is 2GM/c², then find it for a black hole of 12 solar masses. **[3]**

**9.** Four stars have these luminosities (in solar units) and surface temperatures.

| Star | Luminosity | Temperature / K |
|---|---|---|
| W | 2 × 10⁻³ | 9 000 |
| X | 8 × 10² | 4 500 |
| Y | 5 × 10⁴ | 25 000 |
| Z | 1 × 10⁵ | 3 000 |

**(a)** Name the region of the HR diagram for each star. **[4]**
**(b)** Find how many times more luminous Z is than X, and suggest why Z is more luminous although it is cooler. **[2]**

**10.** Compare a white dwarf and a neutron star in terms of what supports them, their size, their density and how each forms. **[7]**

**11.**
**(a)** State where a star like the Sun lies on the HR diagram during its main sequence life, as a red giant and as a white dwarf. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the star is more luminous as a red giant than on the main sequence even though its surface is cooler. **[2]**
**(c)** State one difference between a white dwarf and a main sequence star of the same surface temperature. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A smaller body that orbits a planet (a moon is a natural example) [1]
**(b)** A small icy body in an elongated orbit around a star, developing a tail of gas and dust near it [1]
**(c)** A very large group of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity [1]
*Examiner insight:* One precise idea per mark gains credit; a comet described only as "a rock" does not distinguish it.

**2.** Gravity pulls the cloud into clumps, which collapse [1]; gravitational potential energy becomes thermal energy, heating the protostar [1]; the core becomes hot enough for hydrogen nuclei to overcome repulsion and fuse into helium [1]; outward gas pressure and radiation pressure balance gravity, so the star is stable [1]
*Examiner insight:* Answers on stability gain credit by naming both pressures and saying they balance gravity, not just that fusion occurs.

**3.** E = mc² per second, so m = P t / c² [1]; m = 6.0 × 10²⁶ × 1 / (3.00 × 10⁸)² [1]; **6.7 × 10⁹ kg** each second [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the unit kg per second or the time of 1 s; a bare number leaves the working unclear.

**4.** Core hydrogen runs out, so the core contracts and heats [1]; hydrogen fuses in a shell around the core and the outer layers expand and cool, giving a red giant [1]; the outer layers are ejected as a planetary nebula [1]; the core remains as a white dwarf [1]; no fusion occurs in it and it cools slowly [1]
*Examiner insight:* A named sequence with no physical reason for each change gains little; link each stage to the pressure balance.

**5. (a)** Electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state (Pauli exclusion principle) [1]; squeezing them forces them to higher energies, giving an outward pressure independent of temperature [1]
**(b)** The maximum mass of a stable white dwarf, about 1.4 solar masses [1]
**(c)** M = 1.1 × 1.99 × 10³⁰ = 2.19 × 10³⁰ kg [1]; V = (4/3)π(6.0 × 10⁶)³ = 9.05 × 10²⁰ m³ [1]; ρ = M/V = **2.4 × 10⁹ kg m⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A limit given without its unit (solar masses or kg) is incomplete; cubing the radius before multiplying avoids a common slip.

**6.** The star becomes a red supergiant [1]; heavier nuclei fuse in layers up to iron [1]; fusing iron does not release energy, so the core loses its outward pressure [1]; the core mass is too large for electron degeneracy pressure to support it, so it collapses rapidly [1]; the outer layers are thrown off in a supernova [1]; the remnant is a neutron star or a black hole, depending on its mass [1]
*Examiner insight:* The step explaining why collapse occurs is the one most often missing; a list of stage names alone does not explain.

**7. (a)** V = (4/3)π(1.1 × 10⁴)³ = 5.58 × 10¹² m³ [1]; ρ = 2.2 × 10³⁰ / 5.58 × 10¹² [1]; **3.9 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³** [1]
**(b)** g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.2 × 10³⁰ / (1.1 × 10⁴)² [1]; **1.2 × 10¹² N kg⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** v = √(2GM/r) = √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.2 × 10³⁰ / 1.1 × 10⁴) = **1.6 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹** [1]; this is 0.54 of c [1]; it is below c, so light can escape and the object is not a black hole [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert 11 km to metres before substituting, and finish by comparing your answer with c so the conclusion is justified.

**8. (a)** Any two from: radius about 10 to 15 km; almost entirely neutrons; density about 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ like a nucleus. One mark each [1] [1]
**(b)** The boundary around a black hole [1]; within it the escape velocity exceeds c, so nothing, including light, can escape [1]
**(c)** Set v = c in v² = 2GM/r, so r = 2GM/c² [1]; M = 12 × 1.99 × 10³⁰ = 2.39 × 10³¹ kg and r = 2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.39 × 10³¹ / (3.00 × 10⁸)² [1]; **3.5 × 10⁴ m** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In "show that" questions, start from the stated condition and arrive at the given result; do not begin with the formula already written.

**9. (a)** W: white dwarf (dim, fairly hot) [1]; X: red giant [1]; Y: main sequence (hot and luminous) [1]; Z: super red giant [1]
**(b)** 1 × 10⁵ / 8 × 10² = **125 times** [1]; Z has a much larger surface area (it is a bigger star) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Place a star using both coordinates; naming only the luminosity does not separate a giant from a hot main sequence star.

**10.** A white dwarf is supported by electron degeneracy pressure [1]; a neutron star by pressure between neutrons [1]; white dwarf radius is about that of Earth, 10⁶ m [1]; neutron star radius about 10 to 15 km [1]; densities of the order of 10⁹ kg m⁻³ and 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ respectively [1]; a white dwarf is the core left after a low-mass star ejects a planetary nebula [1]; a neutron star is the remnant of a massive star's core after a supernova [1]
*Examiner insight:* Compare like with like (support with support, size with size); two separate descriptions without comparison rarely earn full credit.

**11. (a)** Main sequence: a diagonal band from upper left to lower right [1]; red giant: upper right [1]; white dwarf: lower left [1]
**(b)** The surface is cooler, so each square metre emits less power [1]; the surface area is vastly greater, so the total power is greater [1]
**(c)** The white dwarf has much lower luminosity because it is much smaller [1]
*Examiner insight:* Full explanations of luminosity use both temperature and surface area, not only one.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not converting km to m before calculating density, field strength or escape velocity.
- Giving the Chandrasekhar limit with no unit or a unit of kg alone with the wrong value.
- Saying a white dwarf is held up by fusion.
- Describing the planetary nebula as made of planets.
- Omitting the reason the core collapses in a massive star.
- Reading the temperature axis the wrong way round.
- Using only one of gas pressure and radiation pressure.
- Failing to compare an escape velocity with c.
