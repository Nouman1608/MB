These notes condense section 12.2, Acid–base titrations, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). Both outcomes are required for every candidate. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [acid–base titrations study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-acid-base-titrations/); then try the [acid–base titrations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-acid-base-titrations-practice/).

## The two outcomes in one line each

1. **Describe** an acid–base titration using a burette, a volumetric pipette and a suitable indicator.
2. **Describe** how to identify the end-point using an indicator: the first permanent colour change.

## The method in eight steps

1. Rinse the volumetric pipette with the alkali; fill to the line with a pipette filler.
2. Run the 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask.
3. Add 2–3 drops of indicator.
4. Rinse the burette with acid; fill through a funnel; remove the funnel; fill the tip.
5. Read the burette at eye level, bottom of the meniscus.
6. Add acid while swirling over a white tile; dropwise near the end-point.
7. Stop at the first permanent colour change; read the burette.
8. Rough titration first, then repeat until results are concordant (within 0.10 cm³).

## Why each technique point matters

| Technique | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|
| Rinse pipette and burette with the solution they will hold | water left inside dilutes the solution |
| Flask rinsed with distilled water only | (no problem: water does not change the moles of alkali added) |
| Remove the funnel | drips from the funnel change the reading |
| Fill the tip below the tap | an air bubble leaving the tip is recorded as liquid added |
| Eye level, bottom of meniscus | parallax error; reading too high or too low |
| White tile | colour change harder to see |
| Swirl | acid and alkali do not mix, so the colour change appears early |
| Dropwise near the end-point | end-point overshot |
| Few drops of indicator | colour change blurred |

## Indicators

| Indicator | Acid | Alkali | End-point (acid added to alkali) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methyl orange | red | yellow | yellow → orange |
| Thymolphthalein | colourless | blue | blue → colourless |
| Universal indicator | not used | not used | gradual change, no sharp end-point |

Memory hook: **"Methyl orange ends orange."**

## Processing results

```
titre = final reading − initial reading
ignore the rough titre
choose concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³)
mean = sum of concordant titres ÷ number used
```

Burette readings: two decimal places, ending in 0 or 5, unit cm³.

## Titration in salt preparation

To make a soluble salt from an acid and an alkali: titrate with indicator to find the volume, then repeat with the **same volumes but no indicator**, so the salt is not contaminated. Evaporate and crystallise. Details are in [Acids, Bases and Salts](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Titration | adding one solution from a burette to a measured volume of another until they react exactly |
| End-point | the point at which the indicator changes colour permanently, showing exact neutralisation |
| Titre | the volume of solution added from the burette to reach the end-point |
| Rough titration | a fast first run to find the approximate end-point |
| Concordant results | titres that agree closely, usually within 0.10 cm³ |
| Volumetric pipette | apparatus that delivers one fixed volume precisely |
| Burette | graduated tube with a tap, for adding and measuring variable volumes |
| Meniscus | the curved liquid surface; read at the bottom |

## Exam traps

- "Until neutral" scores nothing: **name the colours**.
- Methyl orange is **yellow** in alkali, not orange; orange is the end-point colour.
- Do not average the rough titre or a result that is not concordant.
- A conical flask, not a beaker, so it can be swirled.
- Rinse with the **solution**, except the conical flask, which is rinsed with distilled water.
- Two decimal places for every burette reading: 0.00, not 0.
- A measuring cylinder is **not** a substitute for the pipette in a titration.

## Self-test

1. Name the apparatus used to measure 25.0 cm³ of alkali into the flask.
2. Why is a pipette filler used?
3. State the colour change at the end-point when acid is added from a burette to alkali containing thymolphthalein.
4. Why is universal indicator not used in titrations?
5. Why is the burette rinsed with the acid before it is filled?
6. Why does it not matter if the conical flask is wet with distilled water?
7. Why is a white tile placed under the flask?
8. A burette reads 1.20 cm³ at the start and 24.65 cm³ at the end. Calculate the titre.
9. Titres: rough 25.4 cm³; then 24.60, 24.95, 24.70 cm³. Which are concordant, and what is the mean?
10. How do you know you have overshot the end-point using methyl orange?
11. Why is the funnel removed before taking the initial reading?
12. When a titration is used to make a salt, why is it repeated without indicator?

## Answers

1. Volumetric pipette.
2. To fill the pipette safely, without sucking solution into the mouth.
3. Blue to colourless.
4. It changes colour gradually over a range of pH, so there is no sharp end-point.
5. So that water left in the burette does not dilute the acid.
6. Water does not change the amount (moles) of alkali delivered by the pipette.
7. To make the colour change easier to see.
8. Titre = 24.65 − 1.20 = 23.45 cm³.
9. 24.60 and 24.70 cm³ are concordant; mean = (24.60 + 24.70) ÷ 2 = 24.65 cm³. The rough titre and 24.95 cm³ are left out.
10. The solution turns red instead of orange.
11. Liquid dripping from the funnel would change the reading, and the funnel can block a clear view of the scale.
12. So the salt is not contaminated by the indicator.
