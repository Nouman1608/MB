## Item 1: the circular flow, built in stages

**Stage 1: households and firms.** Households sell the services of their factors of production to firms, which pay factor incomes (wages, rent, interest and profit). Households spend that income on firms' output. So **output = income = expenditure**, and national income can be measured in any of the three ways.

**Stage 2: add the financial sector.** Households save part of their income (a withdrawal). Banks lend to firms, which invest in capital goods (an injection).

**Stage 3: add government.** Taxes on incomes and spending are withdrawn; government expenditure on public services and investment is injected.

**Stage 4: add the rest of the world.** Spending on imports leaves the domestic flow; spending by foreigners on exports enters it.

Each stage adds a withdrawal and its paired injection: S with I, T with G, M with X. Different people decide each side of a pair, so the two need not be equal.

### Income and wealth

**Income** is a flow: an amount received per period, such as wages per month. **Wealth** is a stock: the value of assets held at a point in time, such as property, shares and savings.

They feed each other in both directions:

- Saving part of income adds to wealth.
- Wealth earns income: interest on savings, rent from property, dividends on shares.
- Wealth can be run down to pay for spending when income falls.

**Worked example 1.** A household earns 40 000 a year in wages and saves 15% of it. It also owns shares worth 60 000, paying a 3% dividend that it spends.

```
dividend income = 3% × 60 000 = 1 800
total income    = 40 000 + 1 800 = 41 800 a year
saving          = 15% × 40 000 = 6 000, added to wealth
```

A rise in share prices would increase wealth with no change in income, which is why the two must be kept apart.

## Item 2: injections and withdrawals

| | Injections (J) | Withdrawals (W) |
|---|---|---|
| Components | Investment (I), government expenditure (G), exports (X) | Savings (S), taxation (T), imports (M) |
| Effect on the flow | Add spending on domestic output | Remove income from spending on domestic output |
| Main influence | Decisions largely independent of current domestic income: business expectations, government policy, foreign incomes | Rise with domestic income: higher income means more saved, taxed and spent on imports |

This difference is what makes the flow settle. Suppose injections exceed withdrawals (a **net injection**). Spending on domestic output exceeds the income leaving the flow, so firms raise output and income rises. As income rises, withdrawals rise too, until they match injections again. A **net withdrawal** works the other way: income falls until withdrawals have fallen to equal injections.

### Equilibrium income when withdrawals are a fixed share of income

**Worked example 2.** In Country H, I = 120, G = 200 and X = 130 (billions). Withdrawals are always 30% of national income Y.

```
J = 120 + 200 + 130 = 450
equilibrium where W = J:  0.3Y = 450  →  Y = 1500
```

Government spending then rises by 30, so J = 480.

```
0.3Y = 480  →  Y = 1600
change in Y = 100 = 30 ÷ 0.3
```

The rise in income is 100 ÷ 30 = 3.33 times the injection. That ratio is the multiplier, 1/MPW.

## Item 3: equilibrium real national output

**Equilibrium real national output** is the level at which AD equals AS: planned spending equals planned production, so firms have no reason to change output. It is the AD/AS version of the W = J condition above.

Changes in equilibrium come from shifts in AD, AS, or both.

| Shift | Real output | Price level |
|---|---|---|
| AD right only | Rises | Rises (unless there is spare capacity on a horizontal AS) |
| SRAS left only | Falls | Rises |
| LRAS right only | Rises | Falls |
| AD right and SRAS right | Rises | Uncertain: depends on the size of each shift |
| AD right and SRAS left | Uncertain | Rises |

When two curves shift together, state which effect is certain and which depends on the relative sizes.

## Item 4: the multiplier

### The marginal propensities

| Propensity | Definition | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| MPC | Share of a change in income spent on consumption | ΔC ÷ ΔY |
| MPS | Share of a change in income saved | ΔS ÷ ΔY |
| MPT | Share of a change in income paid in tax | ΔT ÷ ΔY |
| MPM | Share of a change in income spent on imports | ΔM ÷ ΔY |

MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM. The higher any withdrawal propensity, the more income leaks out in each round and the **smaller** the multiplier. A higher MPC (on domestic output) gives a **larger** multiplier.

The two formulas agree when the MPC measures the share of extra income spent on domestic output: the rest is withdrawn, so MPC = 1 − MPW and 1/(1 − MPC) = 1/MPW.

### Where the leakages go

**Worked example 3.** An injection of 100 (millions) enters an economy where MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.25 and MPM = 0.15, so MPW = 0.5 and the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2.

| Round | Income | Saved | Taxed | Imports | Spent on domestic output |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 10 | 25 | 15 | 50 |
| 2 | 50 | 5 | 12.5 | 7.5 | 25 |
| 3 | 25 | 2.5 | 6.25 | 3.75 | 12.5 |
| All rounds | 200 | 20 | 50 | 30 | 100 |

Total income rises by 200, twice the injection. Total withdrawals are 20 + 50 + 30 = 100, exactly equal to the injection: the process stops when the new withdrawals match the new injection.

### Significance for AD and economic activity

- A given change in G, I or X shifts AD by more than the initial change.
- It works downwards too: a fall in exports of 20 with a multiplier of 2 cuts income by 40.
- Open economies with a high MPM, or economies with high tax rates, have smaller multipliers, so domestic policy has less effect on domestic output.
- The rise in **real** output depends on spare capacity: near full capacity, the larger AD shift raises the price level more than output.
- The process takes time, and the propensities are estimates that can change.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (8 marks).** Examine the significance of a high marginal propensity to import for the effect of a rise in government expenditure on national income.

**Answer.** A rise in government expenditure is an injection that becomes income for households and firms. *(Knowledge.)* In each round, part of that income is spent on imports and leaves the domestic circular flow. A high MPM raises the MPW, so the multiplier, 1/MPW, is small. *(Analysis: chain from MPM to MPW to multiplier.)* For example, if MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2 and MPM = 0.3, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.67, compared with 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5 if MPM were 0.1. So the AD curve shifts right by less and national income rises by less. *(Application with figures.)*

However, the extra imports raise trading partners' incomes, which may increase their demand for this country's exports. *(Evaluation.)* The effect on real output also depends on spare capacity: if the economy is near full capacity, even a small multiplier mainly raises prices. *(Evaluation.)*

## Common mistakes

- Counting saving as an injection or exports as a withdrawal.
- Confusing income (a flow per period) with wealth (a stock at a moment).
- Using 1/MPC instead of 1/(1 − MPC).
- Saying the multiplier always raises real output, ignoring capacity and the price level.
- Treating J = W as "the government balances its budget"; only total injections and total withdrawals need match.

## Exam technique

The specification's command word tariffs are define (2), calculate (2 or 4), draw (4), explain (4), analyse (6), examine (8), discuss (14), and evaluate or to what extent (20).

- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute, and give units. The specification advises showing working.
- **Explain and analyse:** link the injection or withdrawal to spending, income and output in a chain.
- **Examine, discuss and evaluate:** weigh the multiplier's size, time lags, spare capacity and leakages abroad.
