Condensed notes for sub-topic 3.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680). The full explanations, the strategy evaluation table and an annotated answer are in the [water-related diseases study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-0680-water-related-diseases/).

## The big contrast

- **Malaria**: parasite (*Plasmodium*) carried by a **vector**, the female *Anopheles* mosquito. Water is where the vector breeds.
- **Cholera**: **bacterium** carried in **water or food contaminated with faeces**. Water is the route of infection itself.

Every control question starts by asking yourself which of these two routes you are breaking.

## Malaria transmission (3.3.1): three steps

1. Vector: female *Anopheles* mosquito (females bite because they need blood to produce eggs).
2. Infected mosquito bites a human → *Plasmodium* parasite passes into the blood.
3. Non-infected mosquito feeds on an infected human → picks up the parasite → can infect the next person.

Breeding: eggs laid on still or slow-moving water (ponds, puddles, drains, uncovered containers, irrigation channels); larvae develop in the water.

## Malaria control (3.3.2)

**Personal protection** (stops the bite or the infection)

- Nets, ideally insecticide-treated, used while sleeping.
- Insect repellent on skin or clothing.
- Vaccination, given to young children in some countries where malaria is common; partial protection.
- Antimalarial drugs to prevent infection or treat it.

**Vector control** (reduces mosquito numbers)

- Cover or drain breeding areas.
- Spray large areas with insecticides.
- Sterilise male mosquitoes and release them; matings produce no offspring.
- Biological control, such as fish that eat larvae.

## Cholera control (3.3.3)

- Handwashing with soap.
- Adequate sanitation and sewage treatment.
- Potable water supply: boiling and chlorination.
- Vaccination (oral vaccine), often used in outbreaks.

## Benefits and limitations (3.3.4): one of each to recall

| Strategy | + | – |
|---|---|---|
| Treated nets | Cheap; protect at night | Only in bed; tear; insecticide resistance |
| Repellent | Protects outdoors | Must be reapplied; ongoing cost |
| Malaria vaccine | Fewer severe cases in children | Partial; several doses; supply needed |
| Antimalarials | Cure; fewer parasites in community | Cost; side effects; drug resistance |
| Drain or cover sites | No chemicals; removes source | Impractical over large wet areas; habitat loss |
| Insecticide spraying | Fast, large scale | Harms non-target species; resistance |
| Sterile males | Species-specific | Costly; slow |
| Biological control | Long-lasting; no chemicals | Can harm native species |
| Handwashing | Very cheap | Needs soap, water, behaviour change |
| Sanitation and sewage treatment | Permanent solution | Very expensive; slow to build |
| Boiling | Simple | Needs fuel; deforestation, smoke |
| Chlorination | Large volumes; protects pipes | Chemicals, staff, monitoring |
| Cholera vaccine | Rapid protection in outbreaks | Not lifelong; water still unsafe |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vector | An organism that carries a disease-causing organism from one host to another |
| Parasite | An organism that lives in or on a host and harms it |
| *Plasmodium* | The malaria parasite |
| *Anopheles* | The genus of mosquito whose females spread malaria |
| Transmission | The passing of a disease from one host to another |
| Potable water | Water safe to drink |
| Chlorination | Adding chlorine to water to kill bacteria |
| Sanitation | Safe, hygienic disposal of human waste |
| Biological control | Using a natural predator or disease to reduce a pest population |
| Resistance | When a parasite or insect is no longer killed by a drug or insecticide that used to work |

## How each strategy breaks the chain

- No bite: nets, repellent.
- Fewer vectors: draining, spraying, sterile males, biological control.
- Fewer parasites in people: drugs, vaccination, which also means fewer mosquitoes pick up the parasite.
- No faeces in water: sanitation, sewage treatment.
- Bacteria killed before drinking: boiling, chlorination.
- Fewer bacteria on hands or food: handwashing.
- People protected even if exposed: cholera vaccination.

## Exam traps

- The mosquito is the **vector**, not the cause. The cause of malaria is the parasite.
- Write **female *Anopheles***, not just "mosquito".
- Cholera has **no vector**: never suggest nets, repellent or spraying for it.
- Boiling is a cholera strategy, not a malaria one.
- Resistance applies to both insecticides (in mosquitoes) and antimalarial drugs (in parasites): say which.
- "Discuss" needs both sides and a judgement linked to the scenario, such as a poor rural area with many ponds.

## Self-test

1. Name the parasite that causes malaria.
2. Name the vector of malaria and state which sex transmits it.
3. Why do only females bite?
4. How does a non-infected mosquito become infected?
5. Where do mosquitoes lay their eggs?
6. State **two** personal protection methods against malaria.
7. Explain how releasing sterilised male mosquitoes reduces malaria.
8. Give an example of biological control of mosquitoes.
9. How is cholera spread?
10. State **two** ways of making water potable.
11. Give one limitation of boiling water.
12. Give one benefit and one limitation of spraying insecticides over large areas.

**Answers**

1. *Plasmodium*.
2. The *Anopheles* mosquito; the female.
3. They need a blood meal to produce eggs.
4. By feeding on the blood of an infected human, taking in the parasite.
5. On still or slow-moving water, such as ponds, puddles and uncovered containers.
6. Any two: insecticide-treated nets, insect repellent, vaccination, antimalarial drugs.
7. Females that mate with sterile males produce no offspring, so the mosquito population falls and fewer bites pass on the parasite.
8. Fish that eat mosquito larvae in ponds or water tanks.
9. By drinking water or eating food contaminated with faeces containing the bacteria.
10. Boiling and chlorination.
11. It needs fuel, which costs money and, if wood is burned, contributes to deforestation and smoke.
12. Benefit: kills large numbers of mosquitoes quickly. Limitation: harms non-target species, or mosquitoes become resistant.
