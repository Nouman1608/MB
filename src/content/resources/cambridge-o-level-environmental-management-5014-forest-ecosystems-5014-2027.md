Sub-topic 5.2 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) is a tightly listed piece of content. It names exactly six causes of deforestation, exactly seven impacts, one definition of sustainable forest management, and seven reasons why that management is needed. Because the lists are closed ("limited to" in the syllabus), the examiner can ask about any item by name, and you can be sure nothing outside the lists will be required. This guide goes through each item in more depth than the topic overview, shows how the items link into chains of cause and effect, and models the kind of answer that earns full marks.

For the whole of Topic 5, see the [ecosystems, biodiversity and fieldwork study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study-guide/).

## What 5.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | Command | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 5.2.1 | Describe and explain | Causes of deforestation: logging and timber extraction; subsistence and commercial farming; roads and settlements; rock, ore and mineral extraction; hydro-electric power stations; climate change |
| 5.2.2 | Describe and explain | Impacts: habitat loss; loss of biodiversity; soil erosion and desertification; silting and flooding; global warming; changes to rainfall patterns; genetic depletion |
| 5.2.3 | Define | Sustainable management of forests |
| 5.2.4 | Describe and explain | The need for sustainable management: climate regulation; role in the water cycle; flood control; prevention of soil erosion; genetic resource; food, medicine and raw materials; recreation, ecotourism and education |

Notice that 5.2.4 is about **why** forests need managing, not **how**. Methods such as sustainable forestry, national parks and ecotourism are listed as conservation strategies in sub-topic 5.3, so a 5.2.4 question rewards reasons, not a list of methods.

## The causes of deforestation (5.2.1)

Each cause has a *reason* (why people clear the trees) and a *mechanism* (how the forest is lost). Explaining both turns a one-mark statement into a two-mark explanation.

- **Logging and timber extraction.** Hardwoods have high market value for furniture, flooring and construction, and softwoods supply pulp for paper. Even where only valuable trees are felled, heavy machinery and skid trails damage the trees around them, and the gaps let in light and drying winds.
- **Subsistence and commercial farming.** Subsistence farmers clear small plots to grow food for their families; when soil fertility falls after a few years they may move and clear again. Commercial farming clears much larger areas for cattle ranching or for plantations of a single crop sold for profit. Commercial clearance removes forest permanently, because the land is kept in production.
- **Roads and settlements.** Roads built for logging, mining or development make remote forest accessible. Settlers follow the roads, clearing land for homes and farms, so a single road can lead to clearance far wider than the road itself.
- **Rock, ore and mineral extraction.** Open-pit mines and quarries remove the vegetation and the soil above the deposit. Waste rock is dumped on surrounding land, and the mine needs access roads, housing and power lines.
- **Hydro-electric power stations.** A dam floods the valley behind it to create a reservoir, drowning the forest that grew there. Rotting submerged vegetation releases greenhouse gases, and the scheme also needs roads and transmission lines.
- **Climate change.** Higher temperatures and longer droughts stress trees, making them more likely to die or to burn in wildfires. This cause is different from the others because it is indirect: people do not clear the trees on purpose.

## The impacts of deforestation (5.2.2)

The seven impacts are easier to remember, and to explain, if you group them.

**Living things.** *Habitat loss* removes the food, shelter and breeding sites that species depend on. *Loss of biodiversity* follows, because species that cannot move or adapt decline or become locally extinct. *Genetic depletion* is the loss of genetic variety within a species: when a population shrinks or is split into small isolated groups, fewer different genes survive, so the species is less able to adapt to disease or environmental change.

**Soil.** Without a canopy to intercept rain and roots to bind the soil, rain splashes directly onto bare ground and surface run-off washes the topsoil away: *soil erosion*. In drier regions, exposed soil loses its organic matter and moisture and the land can become desert-like: *desertification*.

**Water.** Eroded soil is carried into rivers, where it settles on the river bed: *silting*. A silted channel holds less water, and more rain reaches the river quickly as run-off, so *flooding* becomes more frequent downstream.

**Atmosphere and climate.** Trees store carbon. When they are burned or left to decay, carbon dioxide is released, and the forest can no longer absorb it by photosynthesis, adding to *global warming*. Forests also return large amounts of water vapour to the air by transpiration; when they are removed, less vapour enters the atmosphere and *rainfall patterns change*, often becoming lower or less reliable in the region.

### Linking causes to impacts

The highest marks go to answers that connect a cause to a chain of impacts. For example, a hydro-electric scheme floods a valley (cause) → habitat loss for species in the valley → biodiversity loss and isolated populations on surviving hilltops → genetic depletion. Each arrow is a separate creditable point.

## Defining sustainable management (5.2.3)

Learn the syllabus definition word for word: sustainable management of forests means **balancing the needs of the environment, wildlife and humans while conserving forests for future generations**. A definition question usually carries one or two marks, and examiners look for both halves: the balance of three needs, and the future-generations idea. "Using forests without damaging them" misses both.

## Why forests need sustainable management (5.2.4)

| Reason | What to explain |
|---|---|
| Climate regulation | Trees are carbon sinks (they absorb carbon dioxide by photosynthesis) and carbon stores (they hold carbon in wood, roots and soil) |
| Role in the water cycle | Interception by leaves slows rain; roots take up water; transpiration returns vapour to the air; surface run-off is reduced |
| Flood control | Slower, smaller flows of water reach rivers, lowering flood peaks |
| Prevention of soil erosion | Roots bind soil; leaf litter and the canopy protect the surface from rain splash |
| Genetic resource | Wild species and varieties carry genes that may be useful for crop breeding or medicines in future |
| Food, medicine and raw materials | Fruit, nuts, game, medicinal plants, timber, fuelwood, rubber and fibres |
| Recreation, ecotourism and education | Visitors bring income and jobs; forests are places to learn about nature |

The first four reasons are environmental services; the last three are resources and opportunities that people use. A balanced answer shows that keeping the forest standing has value for local people as well as for the planet.

## Worked example 1: forest-cover data

*The figures are invented for practice.* A region had 4 800 km² of forest in 2010 and 4 140 km² in 2020.

- Area lost = 4 800 - 4 140 = **660 km²**.
- Percentage loss = 660 / 4 800 x 100 = **13.75%**, which is **13.8%** to three significant figures.
- Mean annual loss = 660 / 10 = **66 km² per year**.

If the same mean annual loss continued, the forest would fall to 4 140 - (66 x 10) = **3 480 km²** by 2030. Say that this is an estimate which assumes a constant rate; a "predict" question rewards that caution.

## Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how deforestation on hillsides can increase flooding in a town downstream. **[4]**

**Answer:** Removing trees means less rain is intercepted by leaves, so more rain reaches the ground quickly *(1: interception)*. With fewer roots taking up water and less transpiration, the soil becomes saturated sooner and more water flows over the surface as run-off *(1: run-off)*. The run-off erodes the bare soil and carries it into the river, where it is deposited and silts up the channel *(1: silting)*. The channel holds less water and receives more water more quickly, so it overflows its banks in the town *(1: link to flooding)*.

Why it scores four: each sentence gives one process and links it to the next, using the syllabus terms. A list of four impacts with no links would score lower, because the command word is "explain".

## Common mistakes

- Answering a 5.2.4 "need for sustainable management" question with methods such as replanting. Give reasons why management is needed.
- Writing "erosion" without saying what erodes it and why the trees mattered (rain splash, run-off, roots binding soil).
- Treating genetic depletion as the same as biodiversity loss. Biodiversity loss is fewer species; genetic depletion is less variety within a species.
- Saying deforestation "causes global warming" with no mechanism. Mention released carbon dioxide and the loss of a carbon sink.
- Forgetting that climate change is listed as a cause as well as being linked to an impact.

## Exam technique

**Describe** questions need the features or the stages: name the cause and what physically happens to the forest. **Explain** questions need a reason with "because" or "so", and they reward chains. **Define** questions need the syllabus wording. **Suggest** questions give you an unfamiliar place, such as a map of a new road or reservoir: apply the listed causes and impacts to the features you can see, and quote figures with units if a table or graph is provided. **Discuss** questions about forest management need both sides, then a conclusion: protecting forests keeps their services, but people who depend on clearing them need other sources of income.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three needs that sustainable forest management balances. *The environment, wildlife and humans.*
2. Why can a new road cause more deforestation than the land it is built on? *It opens the forest to settlers, loggers and farmers, who clear land along it.*
3. State the difference between a carbon sink and a carbon store. *A sink absorbs carbon dioxide; a store holds carbon over time.*
4. Explain how deforestation can change local rainfall. *Less transpiration means less water vapour enters the air, so less rain may fall.*
5. Forest falls from 2 500 km² to 2 150 km². Calculate the percentage loss. *350 / 2 500 x 100 = 14%.*
