This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.3 Making marketing decisions: segmentation, targeting, positioning** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **the process and value of segmentation, targeting and positioning**, with segmentation methods including demographic, geographic, income and behavioural segmentation
- **influences on choosing a target market and positioning**, where targeting may include niche and mass marketing and positioning should include market mapping.

## The process in outline

Marketers often follow a sequence sometimes called STP:

1. **Segmentation**: divide the market into groups of customers who share needs or characteristics.
2. **Targeting**: choose which segment or segments to serve.
3. **Positioning**: decide how the product should be seen in the minds of the target customers compared with rivals, and design the marketing mix to deliver that image.

The reasoning is that customers differ, and a business with limited resources cannot serve everyone equally well. By choosing carefully, it can match the marketing mix to a group whose needs it understands.

## Step 1: Segmentation

A **market segment** is a group of customers within a market who share one or more characteristics that affect how they buy. **Market segmentation** is the process of identifying those groups.

### Methods of segmentation

| Method | Based on | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | Age, gender, family size, occupation, education | Toys for under-fives; magazines for retired readers |
| Geographic | Where customers live: region, urban or rural, climate | Waterproof clothing for wet regions; local newspapers |
| Income | Spending power, often grouped into income bands | Premium and budget supermarkets |
| Behavioural | How customers behave: usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought, occasion of purchase | Frequent-flyer schemes; gifts bought for special occasions |

Some textbooks add psychographic segmentation (lifestyle, attitudes, values). The specification names the four above, so be ready to apply them, and you may mention others where the case makes them relevant.

Businesses often combine methods. A bank may target "young adults on middle incomes who bank mainly on their phones", which uses age, income and behaviour together.

### What makes a good segment

For segmentation to be worth doing, a segment should be:

- **identifiable and measurable**: you can describe it and estimate its size
- **large enough to be profitable**
- **reachable** through available media and distribution
- **responsive**: it reacts differently from other segments to the marketing mix.

### Value of segmentation

- Marketing spend is **focused** on likely buyers, which improves efficiency.
- Products can be **designed closely** to customers' needs.
- It can reveal **gaps** in the market that rivals have missed.
- Different prices can be charged to segments willing to pay more.
- It helps loyalty, since customers feel the product is designed for them.

**Costs and risks:** research is costly; serving several segments may need several products or campaigns; a segment may be too small; and customers' behaviour can change so segments need reviewing. Over-narrow segmentation can also lead a business to ignore customers outside the segment.

## Step 2: Targeting

**Targeting** is the choice of which segment or segments to serve. There are two main approaches.

| | Mass marketing | Niche marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Sell a standard product to a large part of the market with one marketing mix | Focus on a small, specialised segment with a tailored mix |
| Advantages | Large sales volume; economies of scale; lower unit costs | Less competition; customers may accept higher prices; strong loyalty; lower marketing cost for a small firm |
| Disadvantages | High marketing spend; heavy competition; product may not suit anyone exactly | Limited sales and growth; dependence on one segment; a larger rival may enter |
| Typical user | Large firm with big production capacity | Small firm or a specialist brand |

A firm may also run **differentiated marketing**, with different mixes for several segments, as a car maker does with city cars, family cars and luxury cars.

### Influences on choosing a target market

- **Size and growth of the segment**: is it big enough, and growing?
- **Profit potential**: how much will customers spend, and how costly is it to serve them?
- **Competition**: is the segment already crowded, or are customers badly served?
- **The business's strengths and resources**: capacity, skills, finance and brand image.
- **Marketing objectives**: a volume or share objective points to mass marketing; a profit or loyalty objective may favour a niche.
- **Ethical issues**: for example, whether marketing to children or vulnerable groups is responsible.
- **Technology**: digital data and social media make it cheaper to find and reach small segments, which has helped niche businesses.

## Step 3: Positioning

**Positioning** is how customers see a product or brand compared with competing ones. A business chooses a position (for example "the cheapest", "the most reliable" or "the most stylish") and uses the marketing mix to build and maintain it. **Repositioning** changes this image, for example when a brand tries to move upmarket.

### Market mapping

A **market map** (or perceptual map) is a diagram that places products on two axes that matter to customers, such as price and quality. It helps a business to:

- see where competitors sit
- find a **gap** where customers' needs are not met
- check that its own position matches its target market
- decide whether to reposition.

*Example.* A map of fitness-tracker brands with price (low to high) on one axis and range of features (basic to advanced) on the other:

| Brand | Price | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Pulsar | Low | Basic |
| Kestrel | Medium | Medium |
| Stridewell | High | Advanced |
| Arrowhead | High | Basic |

Nobody offers *low price with advanced features*. That gap may be an opportunity, or it may show that such a product cannot be made at a profit. The map shows where a gap is, not whether it is worth filling. Further research is needed.

Limits of market maps: the choice of axes is a judgement; they are a snapshot; and they show how the business sees the market, which may differ from how customers see it.

## Worked example: choosing a target segment

Fenwick Fitness plans to launch a wearable activity tracker. Research on three segments gives:

| Segment | Size (people) | Expected buyers | Average spend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 120,000 | 10% | £60 |
| Young professionals | 80,000 | 15% | £150 |
| Retired people | 50,000 | 8% | £120 |

**Revenue potential** = size × expected buyers × average spend.

- Students: 120,000 × 0.10 × £60 = **£720,000**
- Young professionals: 80,000 × 0.15 × £150 = **£1,800,000**
- Retired people: 50,000 × 0.08 × £120 = **£480,000**

Total potential across the three segments = **£3,000,000**. Young professionals offer 60% of it (1.8 ÷ 3.0 × 100).

**Analysis.** Young professionals are the best segment on revenue, even though students are the largest group, because they buy more often and spend more. Fenwick might target young professionals with a mid-price tracker with advanced features, positioned as "the smart choice for busy people". Before deciding, it should check costs, since a segment with high revenue potential may also be expensive to reach and have strong competitors. Retired people might be a later niche with a simpler product. The numbers are estimates from research, so they carry the uncertainty discussed in the [markets and customers guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-understanding-markets-and-customers/).

## Linking to the marketing mix

Positioning is delivered through the mix. A premium position needs high-quality product design, a higher price, selective distribution and an upmarket promotional style. If these elements send mixed messages, such as a "luxury" brand sold in discount shops with constant price cuts, customers become confused and the position weakens. Section 3.3.4 covers the mix.

## Technique for exam questions

- **Identify** the segmentation method in a case by looking at what the customers are grouped by (age is demographic, location is geographic, income band is income, buying habits are behavioural).
- **Analyse** by linking the target market to the mix, for example "targeting teenagers means promotion through social media".
- **Evaluate** niche versus mass by considering the firm's size, resources and objectives, and make a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing segmentation (dividing the market) with targeting (choosing) and positioning (the image).
- Calling any small business "niche" without a specific segment.
- Describing a market map without saying what the gap means.
- Assuming a gap on a map is always an opportunity.
- Recommending a mass approach for a small firm with limited resources, without comment on cost.
