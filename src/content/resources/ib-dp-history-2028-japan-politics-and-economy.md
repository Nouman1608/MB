This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. Politics and economy in Japan (1912 to 2020) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers the effects of the First World War, the liberal 1920s, the rise of militarism and empire, defeat in 1945, post-war recovery and the problems of Japan after 1989.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-japan-politics-and-economy-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-japan-politics-and-economy-practice/).

## Impact of the First World War on Japan

**Military and economic gains.** Japan joined the Allies in 1914 under the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, took the German base at Qingdao in China, and seized German islands in the Pacific. Its navy escorted Allied shipping. The war was an economic boom: exports of textiles, ships and munitions soared, and Japan turned from debtor to creditor.

**Peace conferences.** At the Paris Peace Conference (1919) Japan was one of the "Big Five" and received the former German rights in Shandong and a League of Nations mandate over the German Pacific islands north of the equator. Its proposal for a racial equality clause was rejected, which left a lasting sense of grievance. Japan became a permanent member of the League Council.

**Washington Naval Conference (1921 to 1922).** The powers agreed on limits for battleships (5:5:3 for Britain, the USA and Japan). The Four-Power Treaty replaced the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and Japan agreed to return Shandong to China. Many in the navy felt the ratio was unfair. Japan's influence in the Pacific nevertheless stayed large.

## Domestic achievements and challenges in the 1920s

**Liberal reforms under Taishō (1912 to 1926).** Party politics grew. Hara Takashi, the first commoner to lead a party government, became prime minister in 1918. Universal male suffrage was introduced in 1925, raising the electorate from about three million to about twelve million. The same year, the Peace Preservation Law gave the state power to suppress radical groups. Labour unions and women's groups grew, but women could not vote. The Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 devastated Tokyo and led to the massacre of Koreans by vigilantes.

**The Shōwa era (from 1926).** After Hirohito became emperor in 1926, politics saw two main parties but also corruption, links with the *zaibatsu* (large business groups such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi), and military resentment of cuts to the army budget.

**Ultra-nationalism.** Societies such as the **Black Dragon Society** (founded in 1901, with a record of promoting expansion on the Asian mainland) and thinkers such as Kita Ikki called for a "Shōwa Restoration" that would remove party politicians and business elites and put the emperor in direct control. In the army, the **Imperial Way Faction** wanted a coup and direct action, while the rival Control Faction favoured planned, disciplined preparation for total war.

**The Great Depression.** Japan returned to the gold standard in January 1930, just as world trade collapsed. Exports of silk fell, farm incomes dropped sharply, and there was hunger in some rural areas. The Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo broke with gold in December 1931 and used deficit spending and a cheaper yen, which brought a quick recovery. But party governments were blamed for the crisis.

## Growth of Japanese imperialism

**Rising militarism.** Violence undermined civilian government: Prime Minister Hamaguchi was shot in 1930, businessmen were assassinated in 1932, and on 15 May 1932 naval officers killed Prime Minister Inukai, which ended the era of party-led cabinets. On 26 February 1936, junior officers of the Imperial Way Faction tried a coup in Tokyo and killed leading statesmen. The emperor ordered it put down, but the army gained political power from the fear it caused.

**Manchuria and China.** In September 1931 officers of the Kwantung Army staged an explosion near Mukden and seized Manchuria, creating the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932. After the League's Lytton Report condemned it, Japan left the League in 1933. In July 1937 a clash at the Marco Polo Bridge began the Second Sino-Japanese War. Japan's forces captured Nanjing in December 1937. The war could not be won, but the army would not withdraw.

**Empire and alliances.** In 1940 Japan announced the **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere**, an Asian bloc led by Japan, which in practice meant Japanese control of the resources of Southeast Asia. In September 1940 it signed the **Tripartite Pact** with Germany and Italy, and in 1940 and 1941 it occupied French Indochina.

## Reasons for Japanese defeat in the Second World War

**The embargo and Pearl Harbor.** The USA reacted to Japanese expansion by restricting exports of scrap metal and fuel from 1940, freezing Japanese assets in July 1941 and halting oil exports in August 1941. Japan imported most of its oil, so the army and navy saw a choice between a humiliating retreat from China and a war to seize the oil of the Dutch East Indies. The government decided to attack the American fleet at Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, hoping to win a short war and negotiate a settlement.

**Military factors.** Early victories gave Japan a huge empire, but the Battle of Midway (June 1942) destroyed four of its carriers. The United States had far greater industrial capacity, and American submarines cut Japanese shipping and oil supplies. US forces advanced by "island hopping" and from 1944 bombed Japanese cities, including the fire raid on Tokyo of March 1945. The Soviet Union declared war in August 1945, and the atomic bombs fell on Hiroshima and Nagasaki on 6 and 9 August.

**Political factors.** Decision-making was split between the army and navy and between factions, and there was no overall strategy. General Tōjō Hideki's government fell in 1944 after the loss of Saipan. Leaders could not agree to surrender until the emperor intervened on 15 August 1945.

**The home front.** The government controlled the economy under the National Mobilisation Law (1938), and by 1944 rationing, food shortages, forced labour and the conscription of students and women had cut living standards. Propaganda, neighbourhood associations and secret police enforced loyalty.

## Recovery and growth in post-war Japan

**The US occupation (1945 to 1952).** General Douglas MacArthur led the Allied occupation, which was in practice American. It demobilised the army, held war crimes trials in Tokyo, purged militarists, and kept the emperor as a symbol. The **1947 constitution** made the Diet sovereign, gave women the vote, guaranteed rights, and in **Article 9** renounced war. Land reform gave most tenants ownership of their plots, unions were legalised, and the education system was reformed. The plan to break up the *zaibatsu* was only partly carried out.

**The Reverse Course (1947 to 1952).** As the Cold War grew and China moved towards communism, the USA decided that a stable, anti-communist Japan mattered more than reform. It eased the purge of wartime leaders, curbed radical unions, and imposed financial discipline (the Dodge Line of 1949). The Korean War (1950 to 1953) brought large American orders. The San Francisco Peace Treaty and the US–Japan Security Treaty took effect in 1952.

**The "economic miracle".** Between the mid-1950s and early 1970s growth averaged around 10 per cent a year. Reasons include:

- the Liberal Democratic Party's long rule from 1955, which gave stability;
- the **Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI)**, which guided investment and technology and protected key industries;
- high savings, good education, a skilled workforce and company loyalty (with lifetime employment in large firms);
- low defence spending, because of Article 9 and the US security guarantee;
- access to the US market and cheap oil.

Prime Minister Ikeda's Income Doubling Plan (1960) set the tone. The Tokyo Olympics and the first bullet train (1964) marked the new Japan, and by 1968 it had the world's second-largest economy.

**Globalisation (1960s to 1980s).** Japan joined the OECD in 1964 and exported cars, electronics and steel. Oil shocks in 1973 and 1979 forced restructuring toward efficient, energy-saving industry. Trade disputes with the USA led to export restraints, and the Plaza Accord of 1985 pushed up the value of the yen and fed a speculative boom in land and shares.

## Economic, political and social challenges (1989 to 2020)

**The "Lost Decade".** The bubble burst in the early 1990s. Share and land prices collapsed, banks held bad loans, and the banking crisis of 1997 to 1998 deepened stagnation. Prices fell (deflation), and growth stayed low into the 2000s, which is why many speak of the "lost decades".

**Political instability.** The LDP lost power in 1993 for the first time since 1955, and governments changed often. Koizumi Junichirō (2001 to 2006) pursued reforms, such as privatising the postal system. The Democratic Party won in 2009 but lost in 2012 after the earthquake, tsunami and Fukushima nuclear disaster of 2011. Abe Shinzō (2012 to 2020) pursued "Abenomics": easy money, public spending and reform.

**Regional foreign relations.** Disputes over wartime history (the comfort women issue, visits to the Yasukuni Shrine), territorial claims (the Senkaku or Diaoyu islands, Takeshima or Dokdo, the Northern Territories) and North Korea strained relations. China overtook Japan as the second-largest economy around 2010. Japan remained close to the USA, and in 2014 to 2015 reinterpreted its constitution to allow limited collective self-defence.

**Society.** A demanding work culture, with long hours and deaths from overwork (*karōshi*), coexisted with a falling birth rate (about 1.4 children per woman) and the world's oldest population. The population began to fall after 2008, putting pressure on pensions, health care and the labour market.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Chalmers Johnson (*MITI and the Japanese Miracle*) saw Japan as a "developmental state" in which bureaucrats guided growth. Others, such as Ezra Vogel, praised Japan's methods, while critics argued that MITI's role was exaggerated and that private firms drove growth.
- John Dower (*Embracing Defeat*) stressed the ways Japanese society shaped the occupation.
- Historians debate whether 1930s Japan was "fascist" or a distinct form of military-bureaucratic authoritarianism.
- Debate over the decision for war in 1941: a trap forced by the US embargo, or a gamble taken by leaders who could have withdrawn.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent was MITI responsible for Japan's economic miracle?"** Evaluate MITI's role in guiding investment, technology and trade; set against private enterprise, savings, education, US aid and the Cold War, and a stable LDP. Use Johnson's thesis and its critics, and reach a judgement that MITI was one important part of a wider set of causes.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the 1930s as inevitable; many Japanese opposed militarism.
- Blaming only the army without noting the roles of business, the press and public opinion.
- Missing the Reverse Course in a question on the occupation.
- Calling the Lost Decade a complete failure; Japan remained wealthy and stable.
