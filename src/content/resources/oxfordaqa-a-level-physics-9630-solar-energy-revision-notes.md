## Equations to know

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| I = P/4πr² | Intensity at distance r from a source of power P |
| I₂/I₁ = (r₁/r₂)² | Comparing intensities at two distances |
| Power on a panel = I × A | Input power (panel facing the Sun) |
| P = IV | Electrical power from a cell at one point on its curve |
| R = V/I at maximum power | Load resistance that draws maximum power |
| Efficiency = electrical output / light input | Performance of a cell or panel |

## Condensed recall

- **Intensity:** power per unit area at right angles to the radiation, W m⁻².
- About 1.4 × 10³ W m⁻² above the atmosphere; roughly 1 × 10³ W m⁻² at the surface on a clear day with the Sun high.
- Surface intensity is reduced by absorption and scattering in the atmosphere, a low Sun (longer path, beam spread over more ground), cloud, and is zero at night.
- The inverse square law assumes a point source radiating equally in all directions and no absorption between.
- **V-I curve:** nearly constant current up to the knee, then a steep fall to zero at the open-circuit voltage.
- Short-circuit current ∝ light intensity; open-circuit voltage changes little.
- Power is zero at short circuit and open circuit; maximum near the knee.
- **Series cells:** voltages add, current shared; one shaded cell limits the string.
- **Parallel strings:** currents add, voltage that of one string.
- **Required practical 10:** small lamp as point source, LDR on a metre rule in a darkened room; measure to the filament; subtract background; R/d² constant (or lg R against lg d gradient 2) shows I ∝ 1/d², assuming conductance ∝ intensity.

## Reading a V-I curve

| Region of the curve | What is happening | Power |
|---|---|---|
| V = 0 (short circuit) | Largest current, set by the light intensity | Zero |
| Flat part | Current almost constant as V rises | Rising |
| Knee | Current starts to fall | Maximum |
| Steep part | Current falls rapidly | Falling |
| I = 0 (open circuit) | Largest voltage | Zero |

## Designing an array in four steps

1. Cells per string = required voltage ÷ voltage of one cell at maximum power (round up).
2. Strings in parallel = required current ÷ current of one cell (round up).
3. Total cells = cells per string × number of strings.
4. Check: total power = number of cells × power of one cell.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intensity | Power per unit area perpendicular to the direction of the radiation |
| Inverse square law | Intensity proportional to 1/r² for a point source |
| Short-circuit current | Current from the cell when V = 0 |
| Open-circuit voltage | Terminal voltage when no current flows |
| Maximum power point | Point on the V-I curve where IV is largest |
| String | Cells connected in series |
| Array | Strings of cells connected in parallel to give the required voltage and current |
| LDR | Light-dependent resistor; resistance falls as light intensity rises |

## Exam traps

- Earth's radius used instead of the Sun–Earth distance.
- Ratio of distances not squared.
- Maximum power taken at the short-circuit current or open-circuit voltage.
- Series and parallel rules swapped.
- Conclusion about I ∝ 1/d² stated without the LDR assumption.
- Distance measured to the bulb casing rather than the filament.

## Quick self-test

1. Define intensity and give its unit.
2. Give two reasons why intensity at the Earth's surface is less than above the atmosphere.
3. The distance from a point source is doubled. What happens to the intensity?
4. The Sun's power is 3.85 × 10²⁶ W. Find the intensity at 1.08 × 10¹¹ m.
5. At what distance from the Sun is the intensity 100 W m⁻²?
6. At which two points on a solar cell's V-I curve is the power zero?
7. A cell's maximum power point is at 0.50 V and 2.0 A. Find the maximum power and the load resistance.
8. Twenty cells, each 0.50 V and 3.0 A at maximum power, are connected in series. Find the voltage and current.
9. Five such strings are connected in parallel. Find the voltage, current and power.
10. What happens to the short-circuit current if the light intensity halves?
11. In Required practical 10, why is distance measured to the filament?
12. What assumption is needed to conclude I ∝ 1/d² from LDR readings?

### Answers

1. Power per unit area at right angles to the radiation; W m⁻².
2. Any two: absorption and scattering by the atmosphere; a low Sun gives a longer path and spreads the beam over more ground; cloud.
3. It falls to a quarter.
4. I = 3.85 × 10²⁶/(4π × (1.08 × 10¹¹)²) = 2.6 × 10³ W m⁻².
5. r = √(P/4πI) = √(3.85 × 10²⁶/(4π × 100)) = 5.5 × 10¹¹ m.
6. At short circuit (V = 0) and at open circuit (I = 0).
7. P = 1.0 W; R = 0.50/2.0 = 0.25 Ω.
8. 10 V and 3.0 A.
9. 10 V, 15 A, 150 W.
10. It halves, since it is proportional to the intensity.
11. The filament is the source; measuring to the casing adds a constant error to every distance.
12. That the LDR's conductance (1/R) is proportional to the intensity of light falling on it.
