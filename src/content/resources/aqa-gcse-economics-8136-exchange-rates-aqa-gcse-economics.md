Section 3.2.4.2 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has two content points: "How exchange rates are determined" and "The effects of changes in the exchange rate on consumers and producers". The additional guidance says students should understand "how exchange rates are determined through the interaction of demand and supply". This guide works through the market for pounds as you would draw it, takes five common causes of change and says which curve moves, and then follows a single firm that both buys and sells abroad, to show why the overall effect of a change is rarely one-sided.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Rates and prices below are invented. | The effects of changes in the exchange rate on producers | Explain the effects on exporters, firms that import materials, and firms competing with imports |

## Reading an exchange rate

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If £1 = $1.25, then $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80. Both statements describe the same rate. When the pound **appreciates**, each pound buys more foreign currency (for example $1.25 rising to $1.40). When it **depreciates**, each pound buys less (for example $1.25 falling to $1.10).

## The market for pounds

The pound floats: its value is set in the foreign exchange market, where currencies are bought and sold. Think of it like any other market.

- The **price** on the vertical axis is the exchange rate, such as dollars per pound.
- The **quantity** on the horizontal axis is the quantity of pounds traded.
- The **demand curve** for pounds slopes downwards: a cheaper pound makes UK goods, holidays and assets cheaper for foreigners, so they want more pounds.
- The **supply curve** of pounds slopes upwards: a dearer pound makes foreign goods cheaper for UK residents, so they sell more pounds to buy foreign currency.
- The **equilibrium** exchange rate is where the two curves cross.

**Who demands pounds?** Anyone abroad who must pay in pounds: buyers of UK exports, foreign tourists in the UK, foreign firms investing in the UK, foreign savers depositing money in UK banks, and people who expect the pound to rise.

**Who supplies pounds?** UK residents who must pay in foreign currency: buyers of imports, UK tourists abroad, UK firms investing overseas and UK savers moving money abroad.

## Five causes of change: which curve moves?

| Event | Curve that shifts | Direction | Effect on the pound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foreign demand for UK whisky and cars rises | Demand for pounds | Right | Appreciates |
| UK households take more holidays abroad | Supply of pounds | Right | Depreciates |
| UK interest rates rise relative to other countries | Demand for pounds (foreign savers want UK deposits) | Right | Appreciates |
| UK inflation is higher than in other countries | Demand left (UK exports less competitive) and supply right (more imports bought) | Both | Depreciates |
| Investors expect the pound to rise and buy now | Demand for pounds | Right | Appreciates |

Two rules help: **anything that makes foreigners want UK things raises demand for pounds**; **anything that makes UK residents want foreign things raises the supply of pounds**.

## Effects on consumers

| | Pound appreciates | Pound depreciates |
|---|---|---|
| Imported goods | Cheaper in pounds | Dearer in pounds |
| Holidays abroad | Cheaper | Dearer |
| General price level | Downward pressure on inflation, as imports and imported materials cost less | Upward pressure on inflation |

## Effects on producers: one firm, both sides

### Worked example: a UK furniture maker (invented)

The firm buys timber from the USA for $26,000 a month and sells chairs to US buyers at £500 each. The pound falls from £1 = $1.30 to £1 = $1.17, a depreciation of 0.13 ÷ 1.30 × 100 = **10%**.

**Its costs.** Timber cost in pounds: before, 26,000 ÷ 1.30 = **£20,000**; after, 26,000 ÷ 1.17 = **£22,222** (to the nearest pound). Monthly costs rise by about £2,222.

**Its exports.** The £500 chair costs a US buyer 500 × 1.30 = **$650** before and 500 × 1.17 = **$585** after. US buyers find it cheaper, so sales may rise.

**Overall.** The firm gains if the extra export sales earn more than the £2,222 rise in timber costs. That depends on:

- **how responsive US buyers are to price.** If their demand is price elastic, a $65 fall may bring many extra orders;
- **how much of its costs are imported.** A firm using mostly UK materials gains more from a depreciation;
- **how long the change lasts.** Orders and supply contracts are often agreed months ahead, so effects arrive slowly.

### Summary for producers

| Type of firm | Pound appreciates | Pound depreciates |
|---|---|---|
| Exporter | Goods dearer abroad; sales or profit margins fall | Goods cheaper abroad; sales may rise |
| Firm importing materials | Costs fall | Costs rise |
| Firm competing with imports at home | Foreign rivals become cheaper | Foreign rivals become dearer, so it may gain sales |
| UK tourist industry | Fewer foreign visitors, as the UK is dearer for them | More foreign visitors |

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how a rise in the number of UK residents holidaying abroad could affect the value of the pound. **[3]**

*Model answer:* UK tourists need foreign currency, such as euros, to pay for hotels and meals abroad [identifies the transaction]. They sell pounds to buy that currency, so the supply of pounds on the foreign exchange market increases [names the curve and the shift]. With more pounds supplied and no change in demand, the price of the pound falls, so the pound depreciates [completes the chain].

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the demand curve when the event is about UK residents buying foreign things, which shifts supply.
- Multiplying when converting from a foreign currency into pounds; divide by the rate instead.
- Stating that a depreciation is good for all firms; firms that import materials face higher costs.
- Ignoring the size of the response: the effect on export sales depends on how sensitive buyers are to price.
- Calling an appreciation "good for the economy" without saying for whom.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: pounds to foreign currency, multiply; foreign currency to pounds, divide. Write the rate you use.
- **Explain** questions on determination: name who is buying or selling pounds, say which curve shifts and which way, then give the effect on the rate.
- **Evaluate** questions on effects: separate the effects on different groups, and judge using the firm's mix of imported costs and exports in the case.
