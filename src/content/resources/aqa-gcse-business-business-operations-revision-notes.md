## Introduction: interdependence and functional plans

Business operations are what a business does to produce goods and provide services. Operations, human resources, marketing and finance are interdependent: a decision in one area changes what the others can do.

An objective is split into functional plans, each linked to the others. Take the objective "raise sales by 15% in a year" for a joinery firm:

- Operations: plan extra production capacity and secure timber supply.
- Human resources: recruit and train two more craftspeople.
- Marketing: promote the range to more customers.
- Finance: budget for the wages and materials, and forecast the cash needed.

If marketing wins orders that operations cannot make, customers are let down. That is the link to show in an answer.

## 3.3.1 Production processes

| | Job production | Flow production |
|---|---|---|
| Output | One item at a time, made to order | Large volumes of identical items, made continuously |
| Suits | Bespoke or one-off products (a made-to-measure staircase) | Standardised, high-demand products (tinned soup) |
| Strength | Flexible and personal | Low cost per unit at high output |
| Weakness | Slow, skilled labour, high unit cost | Costly machinery, inflexible, large demand needed |

Worked reminder (unit cost). Sundrake Joinery has fixed costs of £18,000 a month and variable costs of £6 per unit. Unit cost = (fixed costs + variable costs) / output. At 3,000 units: (18,000 + 18,000) / 3,000 = £12. At 6,000 units: (18,000 + 36,000) / 6,000 = £9. Spreading fixed costs over more output is why flow production lowers unit cost.

**Lean production** means producing with as little waste as possible: waste of time, materials, space, effort and money. Techniques include removing unnecessary steps, keeping stock low, fixing faults at the point they occur, and involving workers in suggesting improvements.

**Just in time (JIT)** is a lean technique: materials arrive just before they are needed and finished goods go out quickly, so little stock is held. It links to section 3.3.2 below.

## 3.3.2 The role of procurement

**Procurement** is obtaining the goods and services a business needs from suppliers. **Logistics** is moving and storing those goods (transport, warehousing, delivery). Both affect efficiency and lower unit costs, but cutting cost must be balanced against the quality of service.

### Managing stock: JIT and JIC

| | JIT | JIC |
|---|---|---|
| Stock held | Minimal | Buffer stock kept |
| Benefit | Lower holding costs, less waste, less cash tied up | Spare stock meets sudden demand |
| Cost | More frequent deliveries; smaller orders lose purchasing economies of scale | Cost of holding buffer stock; risk of damage or going out of date |
| Risk | A late delivery stops production | Money tied up in unused stock |

Worked reminder. Trewin Bakery buys £6,000 of flour a month. JIC: one monthly order with a 5% discount costs £5,700, plus £250 to store it = £5,950. JIT: four weekly orders of £1,500 each at full price, plus £40 per delivery = £6,000 + £160 = £6,160. JIC is £210 cheaper here, though the answer changes if storage costs rise or demand becomes hard to predict. You are not asked to draw or interpret stock control charts.

To evaluate JIT for a given business, ask: is demand predictable, are suppliers reliable and close, does the product go off quickly? JIT suits a business with all three.

### Choosing a supplier

Analyse three factors in the context of the business:

- Price: lower prices cut costs but may signal lower quality.
- Quality: poor materials cause faulty products, waste and complaints.
- Reliability: late or incomplete deliveries disrupt production, especially under JIT.

Worked reminder. Supplier A charges £2.40 a unit with 4% faulty; Supplier B charges £2.55 with 1% faulty. Cost per usable unit if faulty units are wasted: A = 2.40 / 0.96 = £2.50; B = 2.55 / 0.99 = £2.58 (to the nearest penny). A is still cheaper, but B may be chosen if a faulty unit would damage the brand.

### Supply chain management

A supply chain is the series of businesses and activities that take a product from raw materials to the final customer. Effective management means:

- working with suppliers so key processes run efficiently and cost effectively;
- getting goods and services at the best price and value;
- cutting waste and unnecessary costs, giving a streamlined process and fast production times.

## 3.3.3 The concept of quality

Customers expect goods that work, last and match their description, and services that are reliable, friendly and on time. Quality means meeting those expectations.

Identifying and measuring quality problems: inspection and testing, customer complaints and returns, reviews and feedback, and measures such as the defect rate.

Defect rate = faulty items / items produced × 100. If 45 of 1,500 items are faulty: 45 / 1,500 × 100 = 3%.

Consequences of quality issues: returns and refunds, lost sales, damaged reputation, and product recalls.

**Total quality management (TQM)** makes quality the responsibility of every employee in every department, aiming to get things right first time rather than checking at the end. Advantages: fewer defects and less waste, higher customer satisfaction, a better reputation.

| Costs of maintaining quality | Benefits of maintaining quality |
|---|---|
| Inspection costs | Additional sales |
| Staff training | Better image and reputation |
| Time and effort in the provision of services | Ability to charge a higher price |
| | Fewer product recalls (a recall is costly) |

As a business grows by **outsourcing** (paying another business to carry out an activity) or **franchising** (a franchisee runs outlets under the brand), it has less direct control, so quality can become inconsistent. Standards, training and checks must be agreed with the other party.

## 3.3.4 Good customer services

Understand the sales process: the customer finds out about the product, is helped to choose, buys, and is supported afterwards.

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Product knowledge | Staff can answer questions and advise on the best choice |
| Customer engagement | Greeting, listening, a positive experience |
| Post-sales services | User training, help lines, servicing |

Benefits: greater customer satisfaction, customer loyalty, increased spend, profitability.

Dangers of poor service: dissatisfied customers, a poor reputation through word of mouth, and a reduction in revenue.

Worked reminder. A café has 2,000 customers a month spending £9 each. Poor service loses 5%: 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 customers, so 100 × £9 = £900 lost a month, or £10,800 a year.

ICT has changed service: websites give information and support at any hour; e-commerce lets customers order online; social media allows quick two-way contact and public replies to complaints, which also means poor service becomes visible quickly.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the production method that makes one item to order.
2. A firm has fixed costs of £20,000 and variable costs of £5 a unit. Calculate the unit cost at 4,000 units.
3. State two features of lean production.
4. Give one cost of JIT and one benefit of JIC.
5. Name the three supplier factors in the specification.
6. Define supply chain.
7. Calculate the defect rate when 18 of 600 items are faulty.
8. State one way TQM can reduce costs.
9. Why might franchising put quality at risk?
10. Give two post-sales services.
11. State two dangers of poor customer service.
12. Explain one way social media changes customer service.

### Answers

1. Job production.
2. (20,000 + 5 × 4,000) / 4,000 = 40,000 / 4,000 = £10.
3. Any two: little waste; low stock; fixing faults at source; removing unnecessary steps.
4. JIT: more frequent deliveries and lost bulk-buying discounts. JIC: cost of holding buffer stock.
5. Price, quality, reliability.
6. The series of businesses and activities that take a product from raw materials to the customer.
7. 18 / 600 × 100 = 3%.
8. Fewer defects mean less waste and fewer costly recalls.
9. The head office has less direct oversight of each outlet, so standards may vary.
10. Any two: user training, help lines, servicing.
11. Any two: dissatisfied customers, poor word-of-mouth reputation, reduced revenue.
12. Customers can contact the business quickly and publicly, so a complaint can be answered fast, but a poor reply is seen by many people.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a technique without applying it to the business in the case study.
- Writing "JIT is cheaper" with no mention of delivery costs or the risk of late supplies.
- Mixing up production methods (job, flow) with stock methods (JIT, JIC).
- Giving unit cost as only the variable cost, forgetting fixed costs per unit.
- Naming a quality cost or benefit but not saying why it matters to that business.
- Treating the cheapest supplier as automatically best, ignoring quality and reliability.
- Saying "good customer service is polite" without a specific method such as product knowledge or post-sales support.
- Giving an evaluation with no judgement: weigh both sides, then decide and justify.
