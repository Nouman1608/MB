These questions cover OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205), Version 2.0 (June 2026), sections 4.1 and 4.2: why countries trade, free trade agreements including the EU, and the balance of payments on current account. All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

**1.** Explain the meaning of a deficit on the current account. **[2]**

**2.** Country C's current account for one year, in CU billion: goods exports 84, goods imports 96; services exports 51, services imports 38; primary income received 30, paid 22; secondary income received 6, paid 14. Calculate the balance on current account and say whether the result is a surplus or a deficit. **[4]**

**3.** Explain two reasons why a country might import goods. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what a free trade agreement is and one benefit it could bring to a producer. **[3]**

**5.** Country D's exports were CU 80 billion in year 1 and CU 92 billion in year 2. Imports were CU 90 billion and CU 99 billion.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in exports. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the balance in each year and state what happened to the deficit. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one way in which the European Union differs from a basic free trade agreement. **[4]**

**7.** An imported bicycle sells for CU 220, including a 10% tariff. A trade agreement removes the tariff.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in the price, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why domestic bicycle makers might be worse off. **[2]**

**8.** Country G's exports and imports of goods and services (CU billion):

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exports | 200 | 210 | 225 | 228 | 240 | 236 |
| Imports | 230 | 245 | 250 | 236 | 238 | 252 |

**(a)** Calculate the balance in year 5 and in year 6. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse the change in exports and imports between year 1 and year 6. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse how rapid growth in household spending could lead to a current account deficit. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the importance of the current account to the UK economy. **[8]**

**11.** "A high level of consumer spending is the main cause of a current account deficit." Evaluate this statement. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Debits (money flowing out) exceed credits (money flowing in) [1] across trade, income and transfers, so the balance is negative [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that mentions only goods describes the balance of trade, so state that income and transfers are included too.

**2.** Goods 84 - 96 = -12 [1]. Services 51 - 38 = +13 [1]. Primary 30 - 22 = +8 and secondary 6 - 14 = -8 [1]. Total -12 + 13 + 8 - 8 = **+1, a surplus of CU 1 billion** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each part separately, because working that can be followed is easier to reward; a wrong total with visible working is easier to credit than a bare answer, and the words "surplus" and the unit belong in the final line.

**3.** Lacks resources or the climate to produce a good [1], so importing is the only way to obtain it [1]. Overseas producers may be cheaper [1], so consumers and firms pay less than for home-made goods [1]. (Other valid reasons: variety, inputs for firms.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a developed consequence; two bare labels such as "price" and "choice" are usually thin for four marks.

**4.** A deal between countries to remove or reduce barriers such as tariffs on trade between them [1]. Producers gain access to a bigger market without tariffs [1], so they can sell more and earn more revenue [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that barriers are reduced between members, not abolished everywhere, and name the kind of barrier such as a tariff or quota, so that the definition is precise.

**5. (a)** (92 - 80) = 12 [1]; 12 / 80 x 100 = **15%** [1].
**(b)** Year 1: 80 - 90 = -10; year 2: 92 - 99 = -7 [1]. The deficit narrowed from CU 10 billion to CU 7 billion [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the new figure is a common slip: the starting value is the base, so check which year you are dividing by before you write the percentage.

**6.** The EU has a customs union, with a common tariff on imports from outside [1], whereas members of a basic agreement set their own tariffs on outsiders [1]. The EU also has a single market [1] where goods, services, capital and people move with few barriers, so integration is deeper [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both sides stated; describing the EU alone leaves the "difference" unproved.

**7. (a)** Pre-tariff price = 220 / 1.1 = 200 [1], so the fall is 20 [1]; 20 / 220 x 100 = **9.1%** [1].
**(b)** Cheaper imports compete directly with home-made bicycles [1], so makers may sell less or cut jobs [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 200 as the base gives 10%, which answers a different question; the price before the change, 220, is the starting value.

**8. (a)** Year 5: 240 - 238 = **+2** [1]; year 6: 236 - 252 = **-16** [1].
**(b)** Exports rose from 200 to 236, a rise of 18% [1]. Imports rose from 230 to 252, a rise of 9.6% [1]. Exports grew faster [1], so the deficit narrowed from 30 to 16, though it moved back into deficit in year 6 after a surplus in year 5 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis tends to improve when figures from the table are quoted and a change is calculated rather than described in words alone.

**9.** Faster spending raises demand for goods [1], including imports [1]. Imports (debits) may grow faster than exports [1], so debits exceed credits and the account moves towards deficit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Chains that link each step with "so" or "which means" show analysis; a list of effects shows less.

**10.** For: the UK trades heavily, so exports support jobs and income [1]. A persistent deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets [1], adding to what is owed abroad [1], and may put pressure on the currency [1]. Against: a deficit can reflect imports of machinery that raise future output [1], and size matters, since a small share of GDP is easier to finance [1]. Other objectives, such as unemployment or inflation, may be more pressing in some years [1]. Judgement: the current account matters most when the deficit is large, lasting and due to weak competitiveness, so it is important but not always the first priority [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned final judgement that refers back to size, cause or duration tends to separate stronger answers from lists of points.

**11.** For: higher spending raises imports [1], and if firms cannot supply it at home, a deficit follows [1]. Against: a strong exchange rate makes exports dearer and imports cheaper [1], low productivity makes exports uncompetitive [1], and weak growth abroad cuts demand for exports [1]. Spending on imported machinery may be investment, not consumption [1]. Judgement: spending is often a major factor, but the main cause depends on the economy; where exports are uncompetitive, competitiveness matters more [1]. Support for the judgement with evidence, for example the size of each cause in the data [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Take a position on the word "main" and justify it; answers that list causes without ranking them leave the question unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the balance of trade when the current account is asked for.
- Leaving out the sign, the word "surplus" or "deficit", or the unit.
- Using the new value as the base in a percentage change.
- Listing reasons without developing each with a consequence.
- Describing the EU as only a free trade area.
- Writing "deficits are bad" with no mention of size, cause or time.
- Quoting a table without calculating anything when asked to analyse.
- Finishing an evaluation without a judgement.
- Mixing up credits and debits, so that a deficit is reported as a surplus.
- Treating a one-year change as proof of a lasting trend.
