These questions cover section **3.10.2 Physics of the ear** of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), part of the A-level only Medical physics option. Learn the content first in the [physics of the ear study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-physics-of-the-ear/) and the [physics of the ear revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-physics-of-the-ear-revision-notes/).

Data: threshold of hearing I₀ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻². Areas and ratios for the ear are illustrative values for practice.

## Section A

**1.** State the function of the Eustachian tube. **[1]**

**2.** Name the structure in the inner ear that vibrates with greatest amplitude at a different position for each frequency. **[1]**

**3.** Calculate the intensity level of a sound of intensity 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ W m⁻². **[2]**

**4.** The intensity level of a sound rises from 62 dB to 74 dB. Calculate the factor by which its intensity has increased. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why intensity level is measured on a logarithmic scale. **[2]**

**6.** State the difference between the dB and dBA scales. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A sound wave makes the eardrum vibrate.

**(a)** Describe how the vibration is transmitted to the inner ear and converted into nerve signals. **[3]**
**(b)** The eardrum has area 0.58 cm² and the oval window has area 0.032 cm². The force on the eardrum is 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ N and the ossicles multiply the force by 1.3. Calculate the pressure on the eardrum, the pressure on the oval window and the pressure gain. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of acoustic impedance, why this pressure gain is needed. **[2]**

**8.** A small loudspeaker acts as a point source emitting 0.20 W of sound power.

**(a)** Calculate the intensity level 5.0 m from the loudspeaker. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the distance at which the intensity level is 70 dB. **[3]**
**(c)** State two assumptions made in your calculations. **[2]**

**9.** In a workshop, one machine alone produces 82 dB at a worker's position and a second machine alone produces 86 dB at the same position.

**(a)** Calculate the intensity level when both machines run. **[3]**
**(b)** The workshop limit is 85 dB. Calculate the factor by which the total intensity must be reduced. **[2]**

**10.** This question is about equal loudness curves.

**(a)** Describe how an equal loudness curve is produced. **[3]**
**(b)** A 40 Hz tone and a 1 kHz tone both have an intensity level of 50 dB. State which sounds louder and explain why. **[2]**
**(c)** State how the shape of the equal loudness curves changes as loudness increases. **[1]**

**11.** Two people have their threshold of hearing measured. Person X has worked for many years near loud machinery. Person Y is elderly and has not been exposed to loud noise.

**(a)** Describe how the threshold curve of each person differs from that of a young adult with normal hearing. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how person Y's hearing loss affects what they hear in conversation. **[1]**

**12.** After a noise injury, a person's threshold of hearing at 4 kHz rises by 25 dB.

**(a)** Calculate the factor by which the intensity of a 4 kHz tone must now be increased for them to just hear it. **[2]**
**(b)** A sound level meter set to the dB scale reads 65 dB for a low-frequency rumble. Explain why this reading may overstate how loud the rumble seems. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** It connects the middle ear to the throat so that the air pressure is equal on both sides of the eardrum [1]

**2.** The basilar membrane (in the cochlea) [1]

**3.** Level = 10 log(2.5 × 10⁻⁹/1.0 × 10⁻¹²) [1]; = **34 dB** [1]

**4.** Increase = 12 dB, so the intensity ratio = 10^(12/10) [1]; = **15.8** (about 16) [1]

**5.** Perceived loudness is roughly proportional to the logarithm of intensity [1]; the audible range covers about 12 orders of magnitude of intensity, which a log scale compresses into manageable numbers [1]

**6.** The dB scale treats all frequencies equally, measuring intensity relative to I₀ [1]; the dBA scale weights frequencies according to the ear's response, so it matches perceived loudness [1]

**7. (a)** The ossicles (hammer, anvil, stirrup) transmit the vibration as a lever to the oval window [1]; pressure waves pass through the fluid in the cochlea and make the basilar membrane vibrate most at a position that depends on frequency [1]; hair cells at that position generate nerve impulses carried by the auditory nerve [1]
**(b)** Eardrum: p = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴/0.58 × 10⁻⁴ = **3.4 Pa** [1]; oval window: F = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ N, p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴/3.2 × 10⁻⁶ = **81 Pa** [1]; pressure gain = 81/3.4 = **24** [1]
**(c)** The cochlear fluid has a much larger acoustic impedance than air [1]; without the pressure gain, almost all the sound energy would be reflected at the boundary instead of entering the inner ear [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert cm² to m² (× 10⁻⁴) before dividing; the gain is a ratio, so a unit slip that is the same in both areas cancels, but the pressures will be wrong.

**8. (a)** I = P/4πr² = 0.20/(4π × 5.0²) [1]; = 6.37 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻² [1]; level = 10 log(6.37 × 10⁸) = **88 dB** [1]
**(b)** I = 10⁻¹² × 10⁷ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ W m⁻² [1]; r = √(P/4πI) = √(0.20/(4π × 1.0 × 10⁻⁵)) [1]; = **40 m** [1]
**(c)** Sound is emitted equally in all directions [1]; no sound energy is absorbed or reflected between the source and the listener [1]

**9. (a)** I₁ = 10⁻¹² × 10^8.2 = 1.58 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻²; I₂ = 10⁻¹² × 10^8.6 = 3.98 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻² [1]; total = 5.57 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻² [1]; level = 10 log(5.57 × 10⁸) = **87.5 dB** [1]
**(b)** Limit intensity = 10⁻¹² × 10^8.5 = 3.16 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻² [1]; factor = 5.57/3.16 = **1.8** [1]
*Examiner insight:* An answer of 168 dB for (a) shows the levels were added; the combined level is always only a little above the louder source.

**10. (a)** A listener with normal hearing hears a 1 kHz reference tone at a fixed intensity level [1]; a test tone at another frequency is adjusted until the listener judges it equally loud, and its level is recorded [1]; this is repeated across the frequency range and the points joined; results are averaged over many listeners [1]
**(b)** The 1 kHz tone sounds louder [1]; the ear is much less sensitive at 40 Hz, so the equal loudness curves are much higher there and 50 dB at 40 Hz lies on a lower-loudness curve [1]
**(c)** The curves become flatter (more even across frequency) [1]

**11. (a)** Both curves are higher than normal where hearing is lost [1]; person X's curve is raised over a band of frequencies, giving a notch, typically near 4 kHz [1]; person Y's curve is raised mainly at high frequencies, increasingly with frequency, and the upper limit of hearing is lower [1]
**(b)** High-pitched sounds such as some consonants are hard to hear, so speech is less clear [1]

**12. (a)** Factor = 10^(25/10) [1]; = **316** (about 320) [1]
**(b)** The dB scale gives equal weight to all frequencies [1]; the ear is less sensitive at low frequencies, so the rumble is perceived as quieter than a mid-frequency sound at 65 dB, and its dBA reading would be lower [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding decibel levels instead of intensities.
- Writing I = I₀ × 10 × L instead of I = I₀ × 10^(L/10).
- Leaving areas in cm² or mm² when finding a pressure.
- Describing equal loudness curves without naming the 1 kHz reference.
- Drawing or describing hearing loss as a lowered threshold.
