This guide goes deeper on **3.1 The nature of marketing**, the first subtopic of AS Level topic 3 (Marketing) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus divides it into seven parts:

- **3.1.1** The role of marketing and its relationship with other business activities: marketing objectives, and the link between marketing objectives and corporate objectives.
- **3.1.2** Demand and supply: the factors influencing demand for and supply of a business's products, and interactions between demand, supply and price.
- **3.1.3** Markets: consumer and industrial markets; local, national and international markets; product orientation and customer (market) orientation; measuring market share and market growth, and the implications of changes in them.
- **3.1.4** Consumer and industrial marketing: classification of products; how marketing differs for B2C and B2B products.
- **3.1.5** Mass marketing and niche marketing: features, advantages and disadvantages.
- **3.1.6** Market segmentation: geographic, demographic and psychographic methods, with advantages and disadvantages.
- **3.1.7** Customer relationship marketing (CRM): aims, costs and benefits.

## Marketing's relationship with other functions

Marketing objectives must fit the corporate objective **and** be achievable by the other departments.

| Marketing decision | Effect on another function |
|---|---|
| Launch a new product range | Operations must have capacity; finance must fund development |
| Target a premium segment | Operations must meet higher quality standards |
| Increase sales by 20% | HR may need to recruit; finance must fund extra inventory |
| Cut prices to win market share | Finance must accept lower margins; operations must cut unit costs |

A marketing objective that ignores these links is unlikely to succeed. In an answer, show the chain: corporate objective → marketing objective → what the other functions must do.

## Demand and supply: movements and shifts

Students often confuse two different changes:

- A **change in price** causes a movement *along* the demand curve: quantity demanded changes, but the curve does not shift.
- A **change in any other factor** (income, the price of a substitute or complement, tastes, advertising, population) **shifts** the whole demand curve.

The same applies to supply: price causes a movement along the curve; costs, technology, the number of producers or external events such as weather shift it.

**Combined shifts.** When demand and supply change at the same time, the effect on either price or quantity may be uncertain.

| Change | Effect on price | Effect on quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand rises, supply rises | Uncertain | Rises |
| Demand rises, supply falls | Rises | Uncertain |
| Demand falls, supply rises | Falls | Uncertain |
| Demand falls, supply falls | Uncertain | Falls |

**Applied example.** A cold winter raises demand for wood-burning stoves, while a shortage of steel cuts supply. Both push the price up, so the price is very likely to rise. Quantity depends on which change is larger. A stove maker that can secure steel could benefit from the higher price.

## Market share and growth: the harder questions

Two traps catch candidates.

**1. Share and sales can move in opposite directions.** A firm's sales can rise while its share falls, if the market grows faster.

**2. A change in share is measured in percentage points, not per cent.**

**Worked example.** Calder Lane Pet Foods:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Firm's sales ($m) | 12.0 | 13.2 |
| Market sales ($m) | 80.0 | 96.0 |

- Year 1 share = 12.0 ÷ 80.0 × 100 = 15.0%.
- Year 2 share = 13.2 ÷ 96.0 × 100 = 13.75%.
- Share fell by 1.25 **percentage points**, which is a fall of 1.25 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = 8.3% of its original share.
- Firm's sales growth = 1.2 ÷ 12.0 × 100 = 10%; market growth = 16.0 ÷ 80.0 × 100 = 20%.

**Implication:** the firm grew, but rivals grew faster. It is losing ground in a growing market, which could weaken its bargaining power with retailers and its economies of scale.

**Target calculation.** To restore a 15% share in Year 3 if the market grows another 10% (to $105.6m), sales would need to be 0.15 × 105.6 = $15.84m, a 20% rise on Year 2. This tests whether a marketing objective is realistic.

## Product orientation or market orientation?

Market orientation is usually safer, but not always better.

- **Market orientation suits** markets where tastes change and competitors are close, such as clothing or food.
- **Product orientation can suit** businesses whose products are technically new, where customers cannot describe what they want until they see it, or markets where reliability and engineering quality decide purchases.

Most businesses blend the two: they develop products from their technical strengths but test them with customers before launch. An evaluation should say which emphasis suits the business in the case and why.

## Classifying products and B2B marketing

A common classification splits **consumer products** into convenience products (bought often with little thought, such as milk), shopping products (compared before buying, such as a laptop) and speciality products (sought out for brand or uniqueness, such as a designer watch); and **industrial products** into items such as raw materials, components, equipment and business services. The classification matters because it changes the marketing:

- Convenience products need wide distribution and brand reminders.
- Shopping products need comparison information and competitive pricing.
- Speciality products need exclusive outlets and strong brand image.
- Industrial products need technical specification, reliability and service contracts.

In B2B, buying decisions often involve several people (a buyer, an engineer and a finance manager), so the seller must satisfy each one. Orders are larger, and losing one customer can hurt badly, which is why long-term relationships matter more than advertising.

## Mass or niche: a numerical comparison

**Sedgely Bikes** compares two options:

| | Mass-market commuter bike | Niche racing bike |
|---|---|---|
| Expected units a year | 20 000 | 1 500 |
| Price | $300 | $2 400 |
| Variable cost per bike | $210 | $1 300 |
| Contribution per bike | $90 | $1 100 |
| Total contribution | $1 800 000 | $1 650 000 |

The mass option earns slightly more total contribution, but needs far more production capacity, distribution and promotion, and faces heavier price competition. The niche option earns a higher margin with fewer sales and may build a stronger brand, but depends on one small group of customers. The judgement depends on the firm's capacity, finance and existing brand.

## Segmentation: choosing a method

The best method depends on what drives buying in the market:

- **Geographic** suits products affected by climate, culture or local regulations.
- **Demographic** suits products whose use depends on age, income or family size, such as toys or pensions.
- **Psychographic** suits products bought to express values or lifestyle, such as organic food or adventure travel.

Many firms combine methods: a gym chain might target young professionals (demographic) who value fitness and convenience (psychographic) in city centres (geographic).

## CRM: methods and evaluation

Common CRM methods include loyalty schemes, personalised emails based on past purchases, account managers for key B2B customers, after-sales follow-up and responsive complaint handling.

**Evaluation points:**

- Keeping an existing customer is usually cheaper than winning a new one, so CRM can raise profit if retention improves.
- CRM depends on accurate data; poor data leads to irrelevant messages that annoy customers.
- Customers may join several loyalty schemes, so loyalty may be weaker than it looks.
- Holding personal data brings legal duties and reputational risk if it is misused.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a price change as a shift in demand.
- Stating that market share rose because sales rose.
- Mixing percentage points with percentages.
- Assuming niche marketing always means higher profit.
- Describing segmentation methods without matching them to the product.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate**: show the formula, the working and the unit (%, $ or percentage points).
- **Explain**: give the factor and its effect on demand, price or the business.
- **Analyse**: link a market change to the business's sales, share and objectives.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: weigh options such as mass against niche, and decide using the case data.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend a segmentation method or CRM scheme for the named business, with reasons from the case.
