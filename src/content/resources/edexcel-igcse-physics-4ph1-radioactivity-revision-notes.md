Condensed notes for sub-topic **7(b) Radioactivity** (statements 7.2 to
7.16) of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Physics (4PH1). All core
content. Full explanations and worked examples are in the
[Radioactivity study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-4ph1-radioactivity/).

## The nucleus

- Proton: charge +1, in nucleus. Neutron: charge 0, in nucleus. Electron: charge −1, around nucleus.
- **Mass (nucleon) number A** = protons + neutrons (top number).
- **Atomic (proton) number Z** = protons (bottom number).
- Neutrons = A − Z.
- **Isotopes:** same Z, different number of neutrons.

## Four radiations

| | α | β⁻ | γ | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Helium nucleus (2p + 2n) | Fast electron from nucleus | EM wave | Neutron |
| A changes by | −4 | 0 | 0 | −1 |
| Z changes by | −2 | +1 | 0 | 0 |

- **Ionising:** α strongest, γ weakest.
- **Penetrating:** α least (paper / few cm air), β middle (few mm aluminium), γ most (thick lead or concrete reduces it).
- Strong ionisers lose energy fast → short range.
- Decay is **random** and comes from **unstable nuclei**.
- β⁻: a neutron turns into a proton + electron; the electron leaves.

## Nuclear equations

- Top numbers balance (mass). Bottom numbers balance (charge).
- α as ⁴₂He; β⁻ as ⁰₋₁e; γ as ⁰₀γ; neutron as ¹₀n.
- Example: ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁴₂He.

## Penetration practical (7.6)

- Measure background first.
- Fixed source-to-tube distance.
- Add paper, then aluminium, then lead; record count rate each time.
- Subtract background; the absorber that stops it identifies the radiation.
- Simulations are allowed by the specification.

## Detection and background

- Detectors: **photographic film**, **Geiger-Müller tube**.
- **Earth:** rocks, soil, building materials, radon gas, food and drink.
- **Space:** cosmic rays.
- Always subtract background count rate.

## Half-life

- Activity (Bq) decreases over time.
- **Half-life:** time for activity (or number of unstable nuclei) to halve.
- Different for different isotopes.
- After n half-lives, the fraction left is 1/2ⁿ: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16...
- **Graph method:** read time to halve from two starting points; average.

## Uses (7.14)

| Use | Radiation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Smoke detector | α | Ionises air; stopped by smoke; cannot escape casing |
| Paper or foil thickness | β | Partly absorbed, so count depends on thickness |
| Pipe leak tracer | γ | Passes through soil; short half-life |
| Sterilising equipment and food | γ | Penetrates packaging; kills microbes |
| Medical tracer | γ | Detected outside the body; short half-life |
| Radiotherapy | γ | Kills cancer cells in a targeted beam |

## Safety

- **Irradiation:** exposure from an external source; stops when removed. Object does **not** become radioactive.
- **Contamination:** radioactive material on or in something; continues until removed.
- Dangers: **mutations**, **damage to cells and tissue**.
- Waste: sealed containers, shielding, secure storage (often deep underground), monitoring; long half-lives mean long storage.
- Reduce dose: less time, more distance, shielding.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Isotope | Atom of the same element with a different number of neutrons |
| Ionising radiation | Radiation that knocks electrons out of atoms, forming ions |
| Activity | Number of decays per second, in becquerels |
| Half-life | Time for activity or number of unstable nuclei to fall by half |
| Background radiation | Radiation always present from natural and artificial sources |
| Contamination | Unwanted radioactive material on or inside an object |
| Irradiation | Exposure to radiation from an external source |
| Mutation | Change in DNA caused, for example, by ionising radiation |

## Exam traps

- β⁻ comes from the **nucleus**, not the electron shells.
- Balancing only A, not Z.
- Gamma changes neither A nor Z.
- Not subtracting background before calculating half-life.
- Saying sterilised food becomes radioactive.
- "Alpha is safe": only outside the body.

## Self-test

1. How many neutrons are in ²³⁸₉₂U?
2. What are isotopes?
3. Why does alpha radiation have the shortest range?
4. What happens inside the nucleus during β⁻ decay?
5. Complete: ⁴⁰₁₉K → ? + ⁰₋₁e.
6. State the change in A and Z for neutron emission.
7. Name two sources of background radiation from Earth and one from space.
8. A source's activity falls from 1200 Bq to 150 Bq in 24 min. Find the half-life.
9. Why is a short half-life chosen for a medical tracer?
10. Why does gamma, not alpha, sterilise packaged equipment?
11. Distinguish contamination from irradiation.
12. Give two ways of reducing the risks of storing radioactive waste.

## Answers

1. 238 − 92 = 146.
2. Atoms of the same element (same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons.
3. It is strongly ionising, so it loses its energy in a short distance.
4. A neutron changes into a proton, and a high-speed electron is emitted.
5. ⁴⁰₂₀Ca (calcium-40).
6. A decreases by 1; Z is unchanged.
7. Earth: any two of rocks/soil, building materials, radon gas, food and drink. Space: cosmic rays.
8. 1200 → 600 → 300 → 150 is 3 half-lives; half-life = 24 ÷ 3 = 8 min.
9. Its activity falls quickly, so the patient's dose is limited, while it still lasts long enough to be detected.
10. Gamma penetrates the packaging; alpha would be stopped by it.
11. Contamination: radioactive material is transferred onto or into the object and keeps emitting. Irradiation: exposure from outside; it stops when the source is removed and leaves nothing radioactive behind.
12. Any two: seal in containers; shield with concrete, lead or water; store securely deep underground; monitor the site.
