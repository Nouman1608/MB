These questions cover subtopic 24.2 (Standard electrode potentials, standard cell potentials
and the Nernst equation) of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027
series. Use F = 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹ and these E⦵ values:

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg | −2.38 |
| Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe | −0.44 |
| Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni | −0.25 |
| Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn²⁺ | +0.15 |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu | +0.34 |
| I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻ | +0.54 |
| O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂O₂ | +0.68 |
| Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ | +0.77 |
| Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻ | +1.07 |
| Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ ⇌ 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O | +1.33 |
| Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻ | +1.36 |
| MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O | +1.52 |

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *standard cell potential*. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the standard hydrogen electrode, including the conditions used. **[3]**

**3.** Using the table, which species is the strongest reducing agent?

A: Fe²⁺

B: I⁻

C: Mg

D: Cu

**[1]**

**4.** What is E⦵<sub>cell</sub> for a cell made from Ni²⁺/Ni and Cu²⁺/Cu half-cells?

A: +0.09 V

B: +0.59 V

C: +0.34 V

D: −0.59 V

**[1]**

**5.** Explain why the electrode in a Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ half-cell must be platinum, and state the
concentrations of the ions used to measure E⦵. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A cell is made from an Fe²⁺/Fe half-cell and a Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell under standard
conditions.

**(a)** Calculate E⦵<sub>cell</sub>. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the positive electrode and state the direction of electron flow in the external
circuit. **[2]**

**(c)** Write the overall equation for the cell reaction. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate ΔG⦵ for the cell reaction in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**

**7.** Use E⦵ values to answer the following.

**(a)** Show that bromine can oxidise Fe²⁺ ions under standard conditions. **[1]**

**(b)** Deduce whether Sn⁴⁺ ions can oxidise Fe²⁺ ions. **[1]**

**(c)** Write the equation for the feasible reaction between tin and iron species in their
different oxidation states. **[1]**

**8.** Acidified manganate(VII) ions react with hydrogen peroxide, releasing oxygen.

**(a)** Calculate E⦵<sub>cell</sub> for the reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Construct the balanced ionic equation, showing how you combined the half-equations.
**[3]**

**9.** A Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell is set up with [Ni²⁺] = 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ at 298 K.

**(a)** Use the Nernst equation to calculate the electrode potential. **[2]**

**(b)** This half-cell is connected to a standard Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell. Calculate the cell
potential and compare it with the standard value. **[2]**

**10.** A Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell has an electrode potential of +0.31 V at 298 K. Calculate the
concentration of Cu²⁺ ions. **[3]**

**11.** A cell is made from standard Fe²⁺/Fe and Cu²⁺/Cu half-cells. Water is added to the
copper half-cell, lowering [Cu²⁺]. Predict, with a reason, the effect on the cell potential.
**[2]**

**12.** E⦵<sub>cell</sub> for the reaction of acidified dichromate(VI) with chloride ions is
−0.03 V, yet warm dichromate(VI) with concentrated hydrochloric acid releases some chlorine.
Explain why. **[2]**

**13.** Consider Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ and Fe³⁺.

**(a)** List them in order of decreasing strength as oxidising agents. **[1]**

**(b)** State which halide ions Fe³⁺ can oxidise under standard conditions. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The potential difference between two half-cells [1] measured under standard conditions
(298 K, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ ions, 101 kPa gases) when no current flows [1].

**2.** Hydrogen gas at 101 kPa bubbled over a platinum electrode [1]; the platinum dips into a
solution of H⁺(aq) at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ [1]; temperature 298 K, with E⦵ defined as 0.00 V [1].

**3.** **C** [1]. Mg²⁺/Mg has the most negative E⦵, so Mg is most easily oxidised.

**4.** **B** [1]. 0.34 − (−0.25) = +0.59 V.

**5.** Neither ion is a metal that can act as the electrode, so an inert conductor is needed to
transfer electrons [1]. Both Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺ at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ [1].

**6. (a)** 0.34 − (−0.44) = **+0.78 V** [1].

**(b)** Copper is the positive electrode [1]; electrons flow through the wire from the iron
electrode to the copper electrode [1].

**(c)** Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) [1].

**(d)** ΔG⦵ = −nE⦵F = −2 × 0.78 × 96 500 [1] = −150 540 J mol⁻¹ = **−151 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].

**7. (a)** E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 1.07 − 0.77 = +0.30 V, positive, so feasible [1].

**(b)** E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 0.15 − 0.77 = −0.62 V, so Sn⁴⁺ cannot oxidise Fe²⁺ [1].

**(c)** 2Fe³⁺ + Sn²⁺ → 2Fe²⁺ + Sn⁴⁺ [1].

**8. (a)** 1.52 − 0.68 = **+0.84 V** [1].

**(b)** Reverse the peroxide half-equation: H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ [1]. Multiply the MnO₄⁻
half-equation by 2 and the peroxide one by 5, so 10 electrons are transferred [1]. Add and
cancel H⁺: 2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ + 5H₂O₂ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂ + 8H₂O [1].

**9. (a)** E = −0.25 + (0.059 ÷ 2) log(0.0100) [1] = −0.25 − 0.059 = **−0.309 V** [1].

**(b)** E<sub>cell</sub> = 0.34 − (−0.309) = **+0.649 V** [1]; larger than the standard value of
+0.59 V, because the nickel half-cell has become more negative [1].

**10.** 0.31 = 0.34 + (0.059 ÷ 2) log[Cu²⁺] [1]; log[Cu²⁺] = −0.03 ÷ 0.0295 = −1.02 [1];
[Cu²⁺] = **0.096 mol dm⁻³** [1].

**11.** The cell potential decreases [1]. Lowering [Cu²⁺] shifts Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu to the left,
making the copper electrode potential less positive, so the difference between the two
half-cells is smaller [1].

**12.** The value is only slightly negative, so it can be reversed by non-standard conditions
[1]. A high [Cl⁻] (and high [H⁺]) makes chloride easier to oxidise and the dichromate potential
more positive, giving a positive E<sub>cell</sub>; warming increases the rate [1].

**13. (a)** Cl₂ > Br₂ > Fe³⁺ > I₂ [1].

**(b)** Iodide only [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "relative to the standard hydrogen electrode" or a standard condition out of a
  definition.
- Multiplying E⦵ values when a half-equation is multiplied to balance electrons.
- Saying electrons flow through the salt bridge.
- Using a metal electrode instead of platinum for gas/ion or ion/ion half-cells.
- Including a solid metal in the Nernst concentration term.
- Forgetting to convert ΔG from J mol⁻¹ to kJ mol⁻¹, or using the wrong n.
