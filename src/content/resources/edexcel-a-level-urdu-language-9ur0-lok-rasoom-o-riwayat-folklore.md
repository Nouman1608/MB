This guide covers **sub-theme 2.1, folklore and tradition (لوک رسوم و روایت)**, of Theme 2 in Pearson Edexcel A Level Urdu (9UR0). The specification names three aspects:

- **لوک موسیقی**: folk music
- **مغرب کا اُبھرتا ہوا اثر**: the emerging influence of the West
- **قومی اور مذہبی تہوار**: national and religious festivals

Theme 2 is set in the Urdu-speaking world: Pakistan and the areas of India where Urdu is an official language. The [folklore revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-folklore-and-tradition-revision-notes/) cover folk tales, wedding customs and preservation; this page concentrates on the three named aspects, which those notes touch only briefly. For the whole theme see the [Theme 2 study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-theme-2-study-guide/), and practise with the [folklore practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-urdu-language-9ur0-lok-rasoom-o-riwayat-folklore-practice/).

The sub-theme is not a research subject, so it appears in translation passages, reading texts and listening material rather than in Paper 1 Section C.

## Aspect 1: folk music (لوک موسیقی)

Folk music is music of the people, usually rooted in a region, a language and a way of life. To write about it well you need three kinds of knowledge: forms, performers and functions.

**Forms.** Learn a few names and what they are:

- **کافی** (kafi): a form of Sufi poetry sung in Punjabi and Sindhi, associated with poets such as Bulleh Shah and Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and with singers such as Abida Parveen.
- **ماہیا** and **ٹپے**: short Punjabi folk songs, often of love and separation, sung at gatherings.
- **جگنی** (jugni): a Punjabi folk form made famous by Alam Lohar, who accompanied himself on the **چمٹا** (a pair of metal tongs with jingles).
- **دھمال**: the ecstatic devotional dance and drumming at Sufi shrines, most famously at the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan.

**Instruments.** **ڈھول** (drum), **الغوزہ** (double flute), **اکتارا** (one-stringed instrument), **رباب** (a plucked instrument important in Pashto music), **سارنگی** (bowed instrument). Naming one or two shows precise knowledge.

**Functions.** Folk music accompanies harvests, weddings, shrines and festivals; it carries regional languages and memory; and it gives voice to ordinary people's joys and griefs. Its survival depends on transmission: traditionally from master to pupil (**استاد شاگرد کی روایت**) and within hereditary musician families.

**The debate.** Is folk music dying or changing? Arguments for decline: fewer live performances, loss of patrons, young people preferring pop. Arguments for renewal: recordings, television and online platforms bring folk songs to new and international audiences, and fusion projects rearrange them. A strong answer asks whether a rearranged folk song is still folk music.

## Aspect 2: the emerging influence of the West (مغرب کا اُبھرتا ہوا اثر)

**اُبھرتا ہوا** means "rising, emerging": the aspect is about a process, not a finished state. Look at its influence in four areas:

1. **Music**: guitars, drums and pop forms. Vital Signs' *Dil Dil Pakistan* (1987) is often remembered as an early landmark of Pakistani pop. Fusion mixes western instruments with folk and Sufi material.
2. **Celebrations**: birthday parties with cakes, New Year's Eve, and debates over Valentine's Day, which some celebrate and others oppose as foreign to local values.
3. **Dress and food**: western clothes alongside shalwar kameez; fast-food chains alongside traditional dishes; wedding fashions that combine both.
4. **Language**: English words and phrases mixed into everyday Urdu (**اردو میں انگریزی الفاظ کی آمیزش**), especially among urban young people and in advertising.

**Three positions to be able to argue.**

- **Threat**: western influence weakens local language, values and traditions, and turns culture into imitation (**نقالی**).
- **Exchange**: cultures have always borrowed; Urdu itself is a language of mixing, and borrowing can enrich.
- **Selection**: the question is not whether influence arrives but how a society chooses: keeping what it values, adopting what is useful, and adapting rather than copying.

The third position usually produces the most sophisticated writing, because it lets you weigh both sides and reach a reasoned conclusion.

## Aspect 3: national and religious festivals (قومی اور مذہبی تہوار)

Learn the occasions accurately and be able to describe how they are marked.

**National days in Pakistan:**

| Date | Occasion | How it is marked |
|---|---|---|
| 23 March | یومِ پاکستان, Pakistan Day | marks the Lahore Resolution of 1940; parade and ceremonies |
| 14 August | یومِ آزادی, Independence Day | flags, national songs, decorated streets, fireworks |
| 6 September | یومِ دفاع, Defence Day | remembrance of the 1965 war |
| 9 November | یومِ اقبال, Iqbal Day | the birth anniversary of Allama Iqbal |
| 25 December | یومِ قائد, Quaid-e-Azam Day | the birth anniversary of Muhammad Ali Jinnah |

**Religious festivals:** **عید الفطر** at the end of Ramadan, with Eid prayers, new clothes, **عیدی** (gifts of money to children) and visits; **عید الاضحیٰ**, with the sacrifice of animals and sharing of meat; **عید میلاد النبی** on 12 Rabi-ul-Awwal, with gatherings and illuminations; and **محرم**, a time of mourning for many, especially on **عاشورہ**. Pakistan's religious minorities celebrate Christmas, Easter, Diwali, Holi and the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, which brings Sikh pilgrims from abroad to Nankana Sahib.

**Seasonal and regional festivals:** **بسنت**, the spring kite festival associated with Lahore, has been restricted in Punjab because of deaths and injuries caused by sharp kite strings, a useful example of a tradition in conflict with public safety.

If you are studying an Urdu-speaking area of India rather than Pakistan, the religious festivals are largely shared, but the national days differ; keep to one context in any answer.

**Analytical questions to prepare.** What do festivals do for a society (unity, identity, charity, memory)? How are they changing (commercialisation, social media, cost)? Do national days unite people across regions and religions, or are they marked mainly by the state?

## Worked example: translating a festival passage into English

Original passage, written for this guide:

> چاند رات کو بازاروں میں ایسی رونق ہوتی ہے کہ تل دھرنے کی جگہ نہیں ملتی۔ لڑکیاں ہاتھوں پر مہندی لگواتی ہیں اور چوڑیاں خریدتی ہیں۔ اگلی صبح نمازِ عید کے بعد بچوں کو عیدی ملتی ہے، جس کا وہ پورا سال انتظار کرتے ہیں۔

**Model translation:** On the night before Eid the markets are so lively and crowded that there is not an inch of space to spare. Girls have henna applied to their hands and buy bangles. The next morning, after the Eid prayers, children receive their Eid money, which they wait for all year.

**Points to notice:**

- **چاند رات** literally "moon night" is the eve of Eid when the new moon is sighted; translate the meaning, not the words.
- **تل دھرنے کی جگہ نہیں ملتی** is an idiom ("no room to place a sesame seed"): render it as an English idiom of crowding.
- **مہندی لگواتی ہیں** is causative: the girls have henna applied, they do not apply it themselves.
- **جس کا ... انتظار کرتے ہیں**: the relative clause refers back to عیدی; keep it attached to the right noun.

## Worked example: evaluating a view on Western influence

A Paper 3 recording might include a speaker who says that young people celebrate foreign days more than their own festivals. A balanced evaluation sentence:

> اگرچہ یہ درست ہے کہ شہروں میں کچھ نوجوان غیر ملکی تہوار جوش و خروش سے مناتے ہیں، لیکن یہ کہنا مبالغہ ہوگا کہ وہ اپنی عید یا یومِ آزادی کو بھول گئے ہیں، کیونکہ ان مواقع پر بھی گلیوں اور گھروں میں وہی رونق نظر آتی ہے۔

(Although it is true that some young people in cities celebrate foreign festivals enthusiastically, it would be an exaggeration to say they have forgotten their own Eid or Independence Day, because the same liveliness is seen in streets and homes on those occasions too.)

The frame **اگرچہ ... لیکن ... کیونکہ** concedes, counters and justifies in one sentence: concise evidence of complex language.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing folklore (2.1) with popular culture (2.2); folk music is rooted in tradition, while popular culture is spread mainly by industries and media.
- Getting dates wrong: 23 March is Pakistan Day, 14 August Independence Day.
- Describing western influence as entirely good or entirely bad.
- Translating idioms such as **تل دھرنے کی جگہ نہ ہونا** word for word.
- Writing **تہوار** with feminine agreement; it is masculine (**تہوار منایا جاتا ہے**).

## Exam technique

- In reading questions, festivals are often described with emotive vocabulary (**رونق**, **جوش و خروش**, **چہل پہل**); learn these to recognise tone quickly.
- In Paper 3 evaluation, use the three positions above (threat, exchange, selection) to structure your judgement.
- When asked **وضاحت کیجیے**, give the reason as well as the fact: not only that Basant is restricted, but why.
