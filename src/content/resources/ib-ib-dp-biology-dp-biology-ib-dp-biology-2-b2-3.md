## B2.3.1 Same genome, different cells

After fertilization the zygote divides repeatedly, producing unspecialized cells. These then **differentiate**: each develops a particular structure and function. Every cell keeps the full genome, so differentiation is a matter of **gene expression**. Different cells switch on different genes, make different proteins and so develop differently.

What tells a cell which genes to express? In the early embryo, signalling chemicals are unevenly distributed, forming **concentration gradients**. A cell's position in the gradient determines the signal it receives, and so which genes it activates. Cells at one end of an embryo therefore develop differently from cells at the other.

## B2.3.2 to B2.3.4 Stem cells and potency

A stem cell has two properties: it can **divide repeatedly** to make more stem cells (self-renewal), and it can **differentiate** along one or more pathways.

**Niches (B2.3.3).** In adults, stem cells sit in particular microenvironments, called niches, that keep them as stem cells or trigger division and differentiation when needed. The guide's examples are **bone marrow**, whose stem cells produce blood cells, and **hair follicles**, whose stem cells regenerate the follicle and grow hair.

**Potency (B2.3.4).** Potency describes how many cell types a stem cell can become, and it narrows as development proceeds.

| Type | Range of cell types | Example source |
|---|---|---|
| Totipotent | Any cell type, including extra-embryonic tissues such as the placenta; can form a whole organism | Zygote and cells of the very early embryo |
| Pluripotent | Any cell type of the body, but not a whole organism | Cells of the embryo a few days after fertilization |
| Multipotent | A limited range of related cell types | Adult stem cells, such as those in bone marrow |

### Worked example 1: classifying potency

1. A cell from a two-cell human embryo is separated and develops into a complete embryo. It is **totipotent**: it produced every tissue, including those outside the embryo.
2. A cell from a later embryo can become nerve, muscle or gut cells in culture, but cannot form placenta. It is **pluripotent**.
3. A bone marrow cell produces red cells, platelets and several white cell types, but not skin or neurons. It is **multipotent**: many cell types, but all blood cells.

The deciding question in each case: can it make everything, everything in the body, or only one family of cells?

## B2.3.5 Cell size matches function

The guide asks you to compare sizes of specialized human cells. Approximate values:

| Cell | Approximate size | Link to function |
|---|---|---|
| Egg | About 0.1 mm across | Large store of cytoplasm and nutrients for the early embryo |
| Sperm | Head a few µm; total length about 50 µm | Small and streamlined for swimming |
| Red blood cell | About 7–8 µm across | Small enough to squeeze through capillaries |
| White blood cells | Mostly about 10–15 µm | Large enough to engulf bacteria (phagocytes) |
| Motor neuron | Cell body tens of µm; axon up to about 1 m | Long axon carries impulses over large distances |
| Striated muscle fibre | Can be several centimetres long | Long fibres shorten along their length to move bones |

## B2.3.6 Why most cells are small

A cell exchanges materials across its surface, but its need for exchange depends on its volume. As a cell grows, volume increases faster than surface area, so the **surface area to volume ratio** falls.

For a sphere of radius r:

- surface area = 4πr²
- volume = (4/3)πr³
- surface area ÷ volume = 3 ÷ r

### Worked example 2: spheres of different size

| Radius / µm | Surface area ÷ volume / µm⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| 2 | 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 |
| 5 | 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6 |
| 10 | 3 ÷ 10 = 0.3 |

Doubling the radius halves the ratio. A large cell has too little surface for its volume: it cannot take in oxygen and nutrients, or remove waste and heat, fast enough. Exchange also takes longer because materials must diffuse further to reach the centre.

## B2.3.7 Raising the ratio without shrinking

Cells that exchange a lot use three strategies: **flattening**, **microvilli** and **invagination** (infolding of the membrane). The guide's examples are red blood cells (flattened, biconcave discs) and proximal convoluted tubule cells in the kidney (microvilli on the side facing the filtrate, and infoldings on the opposite side).

### Worked example 3: the effect of flattening

A spherical cell of radius 3 µm has volume (4/3)π × 3³ = 113 µm³ and surface area 4π × 3² = 113 µm², a ratio of **1.0 µm⁻¹**.

The same volume is reshaped into a flat disc 1 µm thick.

1. Disc volume = πR² × 1 = 113, so R² = 36 and R = 6.0 µm.
2. Disc surface area = 2πR² + 2πR × thickness = 226 + 38 = 264 µm².
3. Ratio = 264 ÷ 113 = **2.3 µm⁻¹**.

The same volume now has 2.3 times as much surface, so exchange is faster. Every part of the cytoplasm is also close to the surface.

## B2.3.8 to B2.3.10 (HL only) Cells built for one job

**Pneumocytes (B2.3.8).**

| | Type I | Type II |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Extremely thin and flat | Rounded, cuboidal |
| Proportion of alveolar surface | Most of it | Small |
| Function | Gas exchange: short diffusion distance between air and blood | Secrete surfactant, which reduces surface tension and stops alveoli collapsing |
| Key feature | Very thin cytoplasm | Many lamellar bodies (surfactant stores) |

**Muscle (B2.3.9).** Cardiac muscle cells are branched, joined end to end, and usually have one or two nuclei. Striated muscle fibres are long, unbranched and multinucleate, formed during development by fusion of many cells. Both contain myofibrils for contraction. A striated fibre's single membrane around many nuclei makes it an atypical cell, so it is used to test the definition of a cell.

**Gametes (B2.3.10).**

| Sperm | Egg |
|---|---|
| Small, little cytoplasm, so light | Large, much cytoplasm with nutrient reserves |
| Flagellum for swimming | Not motile |
| Many mitochondria in the midpiece supply ATP for the flagellum | Many mitochondria for the early embryo |
| Acrosome holds enzymes to penetrate the egg's outer layers | Outer coat changes after fertilization so no further sperm enter |

## Common mistakes

- Saying differentiated cells lose genes; they express different genes.
- Mixing up totipotent and pluripotent: only totipotent cells can make extra-embryonic tissue.
- Saying larger cells have a larger surface area to volume ratio.
- Describing microvilli as increasing volume.
- Swapping the roles of type I and type II pneumocytes.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Distinguish**: the three potency types by the range of cells formed.
- **Calculate**: show formulae and units for surface area to volume.
- **Explain**: link a feature, such as flattening, to faster exchange.
- **Compare and contrast**: sperm and egg, or type I and type II pneumocytes, feature by feature.
- **Discuss**: whether a striated muscle fibre is a single cell.
