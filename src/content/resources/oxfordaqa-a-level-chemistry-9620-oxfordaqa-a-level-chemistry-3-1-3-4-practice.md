These questions cover **3.1.3.4 Bonding and physical properties** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Melting points are approximate. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [bonding and physical properties study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-4/) or the [bonding and physical properties revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-4-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State the type of crystal structure in each substance.

**(a)** magnesium **[1]**

**(b)** graphite **[1]**

**(c)** ice **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by a delocalised electron, and name the two crystals from the six named in the specification that contain delocalised electrons. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity but an aqueous solution of sodium chloride does. **[2]**

**4.** On gentle heating, solid iodine turns directly into a purple vapour. State the particles present in the vapour and name what is overcome during this change. **[2]**

**5.** A beaker of crushed ice is heated steadily. Its temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted. Explain why. **[2]**

**6.** The enthalpy change for melting ice is +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the energy needed to melt 45.0 g of ice at 0 °C. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon.

**(a)** Explain why both have very high melting points. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why graphite is soft enough to leave a mark on paper. **[2]**

**8.** Diagrams of structures must show a stated number of particles.

**(a)** Draw a labelled diagram of the structure of magnesium showing six metal ions. **[3]**

**(b)** Draw a diagram of part of the diamond structure showing five carbon atoms, and state the bond angle. **[2]**

**9.** Four substances have these properties.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts as solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium bromide | 734 | No | Yes |
| Silicon dioxide | 1710 | No | No |
| Tin | 232 | Yes | Yes |
| Sulfur (S₈) | about 115 | No | No |

**(a)** Give the type of crystal structure of each substance. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why tin conducts as a solid but potassium bromide conducts only when molten. **[2]**

**(c)** Each S₈ molecule contains strong S–S covalent bonds. Explain why sulfur still has a low melting point. **[2]**

**10.** A sample of 36.0 g of ice at −10 °C is heated at a constant rate until it is steam at 110 °C. For water, melting needs +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹ and boiling needs +40.7 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the graph of temperature against time. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy needed for melting and for boiling the sample. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain, in terms of hydrogen bonds, why boiling needs much more energy than melting. **[2]**

**11.** Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and magnesium oxide, Mg²⁺O²⁻, melts at about 2850 °C.

**(a)** Explain why magnesium oxide has the higher melting point. **[3]**

**(b)** Magnesium conducts electricity as a solid but magnesium oxide does not. Explain this difference. **[2]**

**12.** This question is about ice.

**(a)** Draw a diagram to show one water molecule hydrogen bonded to two others. Include partial charges and the relevant lone pairs. **[3]**

**(b)** State how many hydrogen bonds each water molecule forms in ice. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why ice melts at a much lower temperature than sodium chloride. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. (a)** Metallic [1]

**(b)** Macromolecular (giant covalent) [1]

**(c)** Molecular [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* "Covalent" alone for graphite is not enough; the answer must show it is a giant structure.

**2.** An electron that is not attached to a particular atom or bond and can move through the structure [1]; graphite and magnesium [1] **[2]**

**3.** In the solid the ions are fixed in position in the lattice [1]; in solution the ions are free to move and carry charge [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Any mention of electrons moving in sodium chloride solution loses the second mark.

**4.** I₂ molecules [1]; van der Waals (induced dipole–dipole) forces between the molecules [1] **[2]**

**5.** The energy supplied is used to overcome the hydrogen bonds between water molecules [1], not to increase the kinetic energy of the molecules, so the temperature does not rise [1] **[2]**

**6.** n = 45.0 ÷ 18.0 = 2.50 mol [1]; energy = 2.50 × 6.01 = **15.0 kJ** [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Both are macromolecular, with every atom joined by covalent bonds [1]; many strong covalent bonds must be broken, needing a lot of energy [1]

**(b)** In graphite each carbon forms three covalent bonds [1], leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can move along the layers [1]; in diamond all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in localised covalent bonds, so no electrons are free to move [1]

**(c)** Graphite is made of layers held together only by weak forces [1]; the layers slide over each other easily, so layers rub off onto the paper [1] **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* In (b), keep conductivity and softness separate; layers sliding earns nothing for conductivity.

**8. (a)** Six ions in a regular arrangement, each labelled Mg²⁺ (or 2+) [1]; delocalised electrons drawn between the ions, labelled [1]; twelve electrons shown (two per ion), or a labelled "sea" of delocalised electrons [1]

**(b)** One central carbon bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement [1]; bond angle 109.5° [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Ions labelled as atoms, or a metal drawn with no electrons, lose the first two marks in (a).

**9. (a)** Potassium bromide: ionic [1]; silicon dioxide: macromolecular (giant covalent) [1]; tin: metallic [1]; sulfur: molecular [1]

**(b)** Tin has delocalised electrons that move in the solid [1]; in potassium bromide the ions are fixed in the solid but free to move when molten [1]

**(c)** Melting overcomes only the weak van der Waals forces between S₈ molecules [1]; the S–S covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken [1] **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Silicon dioxide does not conduct when molten, which rules out ionic; say so if a question asks you to justify the classification.

**10. (a)** Temperature rises, then a horizontal section at 0 °C (melting) [1]; rises again, then a longer horizontal section at 100 °C (boiling), then rises [1]

**(b)** n = 36.0 ÷ 18.0 = 2.00 mol [1]; melting: 2.00 × 6.01 = **12.0 kJ** and boiling: 2.00 × 40.7 = **81.4 kJ** [1]

**(c)** On melting only some hydrogen bonds are overcome; the molecules stay close together [1]; on boiling the molecules separate completely, so all the remaining hydrogen bonds must be overcome [1] **[6]**

**11. (a)** Both are giant ionic lattices [1]; Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have higher charges than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (and are smaller) [1]; so the electrostatic attraction between ions is stronger and more energy is needed to overcome it [1]

**(b)** Magnesium has delocalised electrons that can move through the solid [1]; in solid magnesium oxide the ions are fixed and there are no delocalised electrons [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* "Stronger bonds" without the reason (larger ionic charges) scores only the first mark in (a).

**12. (a)** Three water molecules with correct bent shape [1]; δ+ on the hydrogen atoms and δ− on the oxygen atoms [1]; dashed hydrogen bonds from a δ+ hydrogen to a lone pair on an oxygen of the neighbouring molecule [1]

**(b)** Four [1]

**(c)** Melting ice overcomes only hydrogen bonds between molecules [1], which are much weaker than the electrostatic attractions between ions that must be overcome in sodium chloride [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In (a), a hydrogen bond drawn between two hydrogen atoms, or with no lone pair shown, loses the third mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts.
- Giving electrons as the charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds.
- Using sliding layers to explain graphite's conductivity.
- Drawing the wrong number of particles, or leaving particles unlabelled.
- Answering "stronger bonds" without naming the bond or force and why it is stronger.
