## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain why exposure and vulnerability to climate risk differ, using factors such as location, wealth, social differences and risk perception.
- Compare two or more societies with contrasting vulnerability, in detail.
- Evaluate government-led adaptation and mitigation, including global efforts, offsetting and trading, and technology.
- Evaluate civil society and corporate strategies, through a case study of one country's non-governmental stakeholders.

## Classifying adaptation

Grouping adaptation measures lets you evaluate them rather than list them.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Hard (structural) | Engineered works that resist the hazard | Sea walls, storm-surge barriers, raised roads |
| Soft (non-structural) | Changes to behaviour, land use, knowledge or institutions | Early-warning systems, zoning that keeps homes off floodplains, crop insurance |
| Ecosystem-based | Using natural systems to reduce risk | Mangrove planting, wetland restoration, reforesting slopes |
| Planned | Organised in advance by governments or organisations | National adaptation plans |
| Autonomous | Made by households or firms on their own initiative | Farmers changing planting dates or crops |

Hard measures give visible protection but are costly, need maintenance and can encourage building in risky places. Soft and ecosystem-based measures are usually cheaper and more flexible but slower to show results. A well-evaluated answer explains which type suits which place, rather than declaring one type best.

## Why trading emissions lowers costs

Carbon trading is easy to describe and harder to explain. The key idea is that firms differ in how cheaply they can cut emissions.

### Worked example: two firms under a cap

Figures are invented. Each firm emits 100 tonnes. The government sets a cap requiring total emissions to fall by 40 tonnes. Firm A can cut emissions at 20 dollars a tonne; Firm B's cuts cost 60 dollars a tonne.

```
Option 1: each firm must cut 20 tonnes
  Firm A: 20 x 20 = 400 dollars
  Firm B: 20 x 60 = 1,200 dollars
  Total cost: 1,600 dollars

Option 2: permits can be traded
  Firm A cuts 40 tonnes: 40 x 20 = 800 dollars
  Firm A sells its 20 spare permits to Firm B at 40 dollars each
    Firm A receives 20 x 40 = 800 dollars, so its net cost is 0
    Firm B pays 800 dollars instead of cutting at 1,200 dollars
  Total cost of the cuts: 800 dollars
```

The same 40 tonnes are cut, but the total cost halves (from 1,600 to 800 dollars), because cuts happen where they are cheapest. Both firms are better off. The evaluation that follows: trading only works if the cap is tight; too many permits make the price fall and remove the incentive, as happened in the early years of the European Union's scheme. It also lets high emitters keep polluting locally, which matters where pollution harms nearby communities.

## Power over decision-making

The guide stresses that the sources of emissions may be far from the places most affected. This creates a mismatch of power: those with the most influence over emissions are often least exposed, while those most exposed have the least influence.

- **Consensus rules.** Decisions under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change are made by consensus, so any large group of states can slow agreement.
- **Coalitions of the vulnerable.** The Alliance of Small Island States, formed in 1990, negotiates as a bloc so that low-lying island nations carry more weight than they would alone. They pressed for the 1.5°C goal included in the Paris Agreement.
- **Economic weight.** Large emitters decide whether global targets are met in practice, and changes of national government can reverse their commitments.
- **Finance.** Whoever funds adaptation and technology shapes which projects happen and where.

When you evaluate a response, ask who made the decision, who pays and who benefits. These questions expose the geography of power behind each response.

## Risk perception and vulnerability within a place

Vulnerability is not only about wealth between countries. Within one city, people face different risks because of where they live, what they own and how they read warnings.

Hurricane Katrina struck New Orleans in August 2005. Levee failures flooded much of the city. Many residents without cars, disproportionately poorer and Black households, could not follow the evacuation order, and some low-lying neighbourhoods such as the Lower Ninth Ward were devastated. Older residents and people with disabilities were also at greater risk. Some residents had stayed through earlier hurricanes and judged the danger lower than it was: **risk perception** shaped their decisions as well as their means.

Pairing a within-country contrast like this with an international one, such as low-lying Bangladesh and the Netherlands, shows that the guide's factors operate at more than one scale.

## Building a non-governmental case study

The guide asks for one country's response focused on non-governmental stakeholders. Organise it under four headings so you can reuse it for different questions:

1. **Stakeholders.** Name the NGOs, community groups, businesses, investors, scientists or faith groups involved.
2. **Methods.** Litigation, protest, lobbying, investment choices, voluntary targets, local projects.
3. **Outcomes.** What changed, with dates, and what did not.
4. **Limits.** Scale, enforcement, opposition, and whether actions reduce emissions or only change reputations.

Corporate action fits the same frame. Many companies have joined voluntary commitments to buy 100 per cent renewable electricity or to set emission targets checked by outside bodies. Their value depends on whether targets cover the firm's whole supply chain, and whether claims are verified; otherwise they risk greenwashing.

## Geo-engineering: the governance question

Solar radiation management could, in theory, cool the planet quickly and cheaply compared with cutting emissions. That is exactly why it is controversial. A single country or company might attempt it alone, its effects on regional rainfall could create losers in other countries, and it would not stop ocean acidification. The deeper point for an exam answer is that geo-engineering moves the climate question from "who cuts emissions" to "who controls the thermostat", which is a question of global power.

## Common mistakes

- Treating adaptation and mitigation as alternatives, when most places need both.
- Explaining carbon trading as "buying the right to pollute" without explaining why it lowers the total cost of cuts.
- Using a government policy as the case study when the question asks about civil society or corporations.
- Comparing vulnerability only between countries, never within one.
- Evaluating technology as a complete solution without asking who controls it and who bears its risks.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** needs criteria. Useful ones are effectiveness, cost, fairness, timescale and who decides.
- **Discuss** needs at least two perspectives, such as those of a small island state and a large emitter, before a conclusion.
- **To what extent** questions on responses need a clear position: for example, that global agreements are necessary but insufficient without national and non-governmental action.
- **Compare** two societies point by point, using the same factors for each, rather than writing two separate descriptions.
