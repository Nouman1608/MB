These notes condense the [study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-structures-processes-issues/) for section 3.2, Social structures, social processes and social issues.

They follow the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards. Section 3.2 is a set of skills you use in every topic: comparing theories in context, responding to extracts, judging methods and challenging everyday views. The specification does not tie it to one paper. Paper 1 (families and education) and Paper 2 (crime and deviance, and social stratification) both assess "relevant areas of social theory and methodology". Test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-structures-processes-issues-practice/), and track progress on the [AQA GCSE Sociology hub](/boards/aqa/gcse/sociology/) and [checklist](/checklists/aqa/gcse/sociology/).

## What section 3.2 asks of you

1. **Theories.** Critically evaluate and compare and contrast theories, with the key features of each *in the context of a specific topic*.
2. **Extracts.** Read and respond to extracts showing different views; analyse and evaluate how sociologists interpreted the issues.
3. **Methods.** Understand relevant methods and methodological issues in each topic: official statistics, qualitative and quantitative approaches, mixed methods.
4. **Issues.** Explore and debate contemporary social issues; challenge everyday understandings from a sociological perspective.

Section 3 adds: synthesise evidence from different sources, analyse written, visual and numerical information, and draw connections between topic areas.

## Key terms

All terms below appear in Appendix A of the specification except "social structure", "social process" and "agency", which are working definitions from this unit's title and the aims in section 4.1, and "official statistics" (Appendix A lists "official crime statistics").

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social structure | Lasting, organised pattern of institutions and relationships (families, schools, class, criminal justice) |
| Social process | The way structures act on people over time (socialisation, social control, labelling) |
| Agency | People's ability to act and make choices within structures |
| Theoretical perspective | A general approach to explaining society, such as functionalism or Marxism |
| Value consensus | Agreement across society on shared values (a functionalist idea) |
| Patriarchy | Male domination of society and its institutions |
| Social construct | Something whose meaning is created by society rather than fixed by nature |
| Official statistics | Numerical data collected by government bodies |
| Dark figure of crime | Crime that is unreported or unrecorded |
| Qualitative data | Data in words or meanings, from methods such as unstructured interviews |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data, from methods such as questionnaires with closed questions |
| Mixed methods research | Combining qualitative and quantitative methods in one study |
| Triangulation | Using more than one method or source to check findings |
| Validity | How far data give a true picture of what is studied |
| Reliability | How far the same results would be obtained if the research were repeated |
| Selective use of data | Choosing only the figures that support an argument |

## Perspectives at a glance

| Perspective | One-line key feature | Strong in | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | Institutions meet society's needs; value consensus | Explaining order and shared values | Can idealise middle-class life (Parsons) and assume consensus (Merton) |
| Marxism | Class conflict over the means of production | Explaining persistent class inequality | Willis shows people do not simply accept ruling-class ideas |
| Feminism | Patriarchy shapes institutions; men benefit | Revealing inequality in the home and at work | Heidensohn criticised for generalising to all women |
| Interactionism | Meanings and labels are created in interaction | Explaining self-fulfilling prophecies | Can neglect where the power to label comes from |

Remember which perspectives the specification names. Interactionism appears for processes within schools (3.4.4) and for crime and deviance in 3.5.1 to 3.5.3. Data on crime (3.5.4), the other education points, families and stratification name functionalist, feminist and Marxist.

## Named studies at a glance (methods focus)

| Who | Perspective | Method and sample (as printed) | Key finding or idea | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Willmott and Young (1973) | Functionalist | Survey; nearly 2,000 interviewed in Greater London and surrounding areas | Symmetrical family; stratified diffusion | Feminists saw little evidence of symmetry |
| Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980) | (class-based inequality) | Over 8,000 males born 1913–1952 | Service-class boys about 11 times as likely to attend university as working-class boys | Excluded females |
| Townsend (1979) | (relative deprivation) | Questionnaires; over 2,000 households, 6,000+ individuals | Over 22% poor in 1968–69 vs just over 6% on the state standard | Index may confuse choice with poverty |
| Ball (1981) | Often read as interactionist (teacher expectations) | Three years' participant observation in one school | Banding and mixed ability shape pupils' experience | One school; hard to generalise |
| Willis (1977) | Marxist | Observation, participant observation, group discussions, informal interviews, diaries | Counter school culture leads to manual work | Small group, 1970s labour market |
| Carlen (1988) | Feminist | In-depth unstructured interviews; 39 women aged 15–46 | Crime when the class deal and gender deal fail | Small sample |
| Devine (1992) | (affluent worker debate) | Interviews with Vauxhall workers in Luton and their wives, late 1980s | No clear evidence of privatised instrumentalism | Returns to one town only |
| Becker (1963) | Interactionist | (theory) | Labels, master status, deviant career | Neglects source of power |
| Murray (1984) | New Right | (argument) | Underclass shaped by benefit dependency | Criticised for a poor evidence base |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Compare vs contrast.** Compare = similarities *and* differences; contrast = differences only. A "compare and contrast" answer needs both.
- **Between vs within perspectives.** Zaretsky (Marxist) vs Delphy and Leonard (feminist) disagree on who benefits from the family. Willis vs Bowles and Gintis (both Marxist) disagree on how successfully school socialises.
- **Mixed methods vs triangulation.** Mixed methods combines qualitative and quantitative data. Triangulation uses more than one method or source to cross-check.
- **Recorded vs reported vs actual crime.** Reported is what victims tell the police; recorded is what police log; actual includes the dark figure.
- **Validity vs reliability.** Qualitative work tends to be stronger on validity; quantitative work on reliability.
- **Everyday vs sociological understanding.** Everyday views stress individual nature or choice; sociology looks for structures and processes.
- **Structure vs agency.** Structural theories (functionalism, Marxism) vs interactionism, which stresses meaning and reaction to labels.

## Extract routine: five questions

1. Who wrote it, and when?
2. Which perspective? (Look for "functions", "capitalism", "patriarchy", "labels".)
3. What is the main claim, in one sentence?
4. What evidence or method lies behind it?
5. What are its limits: change since, who was left out, what rivals say?

Appendix B gives ready-made limits: Oakley predates civil partnerships; the Rapoports predate open acceptance of gay and lesbian households; Halsey excluded females.

## Mini essay plan

*Typical question: "Discuss how useful official statistics are for sociologists studying social issues."*

1. **Define** official statistics and why they appeal: cheap, large-scale, show trends.
2. **Crime:** dark figure; victim surveys and self-report studies; Becker's labelling view of figures. Marxist point about under-recorded white collar crime.
3. **Poverty:** Townsend's three measures give just over 6%, a little over 9% and over 22%, so the definition makes the figure.
4. **Families:** divorce figures miss separations and empty shell marriages.
5. **Counter-argument:** without statistics, the trends in divorce or crime could not be seen at all, and qualitative studies such as Carlen's 39 interviews cannot show how common a pattern is.
6. **Judgement:** statistics are useful for showing *what* and *how much*, less for *why*; mixed methods combine their strengths with qualitative depth.

## Connections across topics

- **Labelling:** Ball (teacher expectations) and Becker (master status) both show self-fulfilling prophecies.
- **Social control:** Heidensohn links control in the home to women's lower crime.
- **Class:** Halsey, Albert Cohen (status frustration), Carlen (class deal), Marx.
- **Patriarchy:** Delphy and Leonard, Heidensohn, Walby's six structures.

## Quick self-test

1. What does section 3.2 mean by the "key features" of a theory "in context"?
2. Name the perspective of Zaretsky and of Delphy and Leonard.
3. On what main point do Zaretsky and Delphy and Leonard disagree?
4. Give one way Willis differs from Bowles and Gintis.
5. Define mixed methods research.
6. Define triangulation.
7. Give Townsend's three poverty estimates for 1968–69.
8. Which group did Halsey's sample leave out?
9. Which method did Carlen use, and with how many women?
10. Give one everyday understanding of crime that sociologists challenge, and the challenge.
11. Name two concepts that connect education and crime.

### Answers

1. What the theory says about the specific topic in the question (for example, Parsons on primary socialisation in families), not a general summary.
2. Zaretsky: Marxist. Delphy and Leonard: feminist.
3. Who benefits: Zaretsky says capitalism; Delphy and Leonard say men.
4. Willis rejects a direct link between economy and school and finds pupils resisting, while Bowles and Gintis stress correspondence and obedience.
5. Combining qualitative and quantitative methods in one study.
6. Using more than one method or source to check findings.
7. Just over 6% (state standard), a little over 9% (relative income), more than 22% (relative deprivation).
8. Females.
9. In-depth unstructured interviews with 39 women.
10. "Crime statistics show who the criminals are"; figures reflect reporting, recording and labelling, and miss the dark figure.
11. Labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy (also social control).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a general summary of a perspective instead of its key features in the topic named.
- Contrasting two theories without any similarity when the question says "compare".
- Quoting an extract back without naming its perspective or judging it.
- Calling Albert Cohen a Marxist; the specification presents his work as functionalist.
- Saying official statistics are simply "wrong", rather than explaining which part is missing and why.
- Mixing up mixed methods and triangulation.
- Using personal opinion to "challenge an everyday view" instead of a named study or concept.
- Giving Townsend's or Halsey's figures without saying what they show.
- Ending an extended answer without a judgement that follows from the evidence.
