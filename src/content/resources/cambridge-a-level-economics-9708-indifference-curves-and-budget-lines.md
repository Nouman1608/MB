This guide covers **section 7.2 Indifference curves and budget lines** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus lists four statements: the "meaning of an indifference curve and a budget line", the "causes of a shift in the budget line", "income, substitution and price effects for normal, inferior and Giffen goods", and the "limitations of the model of indifference curves".

The broader [utility, indifference curves and budget lines study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-utility-indifference-curves-and-budget-lines/) works through a price fall for each type of good. This page goes further: it explains why each property of an indifference curve must hold, sets out how to construct the effects diagram step by step, and works through a price **rise**, the case students find hardest to draw. All consumers and figures are invented.

## 7.2.1 Indifference curves: four properties and why they hold

An **indifference curve** joins all the combinations of two goods that give a consumer the same level of satisfaction. A set of curves for one consumer is an **indifference map**.

| Property | Reason |
|---|---|
| Slopes downward | If the consumer has more of one good, they must have less of the other to stay equally satisfied, assuming more of a good is preferred to less |
| Convex to the origin | The marginal rate of substitution (MRS) diminishes: the more of X a consumer has, the less Y they will give up for one more X |
| Higher curves are preferred | A curve further from the origin contains bundles with more of both goods, or more of one and no less of the other |
| Curves never cross | If two curves crossed, the shared point would give two different levels of satisfaction at once, which contradicts the definition |

The **MRS** of X for Y is the amount of Y the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of X while staying on the same curve. It is the slope of the indifference curve, ignoring the sign.

## 7.2.1 Budget lines: the equation behind the line

A **budget line** shows all combinations of two goods a consumer can just afford with a given income and given prices:

price of X × quantity of X + price of Y × quantity of Y = income

**Worked example 1.** Income is 200, the price of X is 4 and the price of Y is 2.

- If all income goes on X: 200 ÷ 4 = 50 units. This is the horizontal intercept.
- If all income goes on Y: 200 ÷ 2 = 100 units. This is the vertical intercept.
- Slope = price of X ÷ price of Y = 4 ÷ 2 = 2, ignoring the sign. One more unit of X costs 2 units of Y.

**The optimum.** The consumer reaches the highest affordable indifference curve where the budget line is tangent to it. There, the MRS equals the price ratio. At any point where the budget line cuts an indifference curve, the consumer's willingness to swap goods differs from the market rate of exchange, so moving along the line reaches a higher curve.

## 7.2.2 Causes of a shift in the budget line

Starting from Worked example 1:

| Change | Effect on the line | New intercepts (X, Y) |
|---|---|---|
| Income rises to 240 | Parallel shift outward; slope unchanged | 60, 120 |
| Income falls to 160 | Parallel shift inward | 40, 80 |
| Price of X rises to 5 | Pivots inward around the Y intercept; slope rises to 2.5 | 40, 100 |
| Price of Y falls to 1 | Pivots outward around the X intercept; slope rises to 4 | 50, 200 |
| Both prices rise by 25% (to 5 and 2.5) | Parallel shift inward, exactly as if income fell to 160 | 40, 80 |
| Income and both prices all rise by 25% | No change: real income and relative prices are the same | 50, 100 |

The last two rows give two useful rules: an equal percentage change in both prices acts like a change in income, and an equal percentage change in income and all prices leaves the budget line where it was.

## 7.2.3 Income, substitution and price effects: a price rise

When the price of a good changes, the **price effect** is the total change in the quantity demanded. It splits into:

- the **substitution effect**: the change caused only by the change in relative prices, with the consumer kept on the original indifference curve (real income held constant). It always moves quantity in the opposite direction to the price change.
- the **income effect**: the change caused by the change in real income. A price rise lowers real income.

### Constructing the diagram

1. Draw the original budget line BL1, tangent to IC1 at point A.
2. The price of X rises, so the budget line pivots inward on the Y axis to BL2, tangent to a lower curve IC2 at point C.
3. Draw a dashed line BLc **parallel to BL2** (new relative prices) but **tangent to IC1** (original satisfaction). Mark the tangency B.
4. A to B is the substitution effect. B to C is the income effect. A to C is the price effect.

### Worked example 2: a normal good

A consumer has income 200, and the price of Y is 2. The price of X rises from 4 to 5. The consumer's preferences are such that they always spend half their income on each good (an invented assumption that gives a smooth indifference map).

| Point | Quantity of X | Quantity of Y | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (before) | 25 | 50 | 100 ÷ 4 = 25 and 100 ÷ 2 = 50 |
| C (after) | 20 | 50 | 100 ÷ 5 = 20 |
| B (compensated) | 22.36 | 55.90 | On IC1 at the new price ratio; it would cost 5 × 22.36 + 2 × 55.90 = about 223.6 |

- Substitution effect on X: 25 → 22.36 = **−2.64**.
- Income effect on X: 22.36 → 20 = **−2.36**.
- Price effect: 25 → 20 = **−5**.

Both effects reduce the quantity of X, which is what makes X a normal good. The compensated point shows the consumer would need about 23.6 more income (about 223.6 rather than 200) to stay as satisfied after the price rise, which measures the fall in real income.

### Normal, inferior and Giffen goods after a price rise

| Good | Substitution effect | Income effect (real income falls) | Price effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | Quantity falls | Quantity falls | Quantity falls |
| Inferior | Quantity falls | Quantity rises, by less than the substitution effect | Quantity falls |
| Giffen | Quantity falls | Quantity rises, by more than the substitution effect | Quantity rises |

**Annotated model answer.** *Question:* Explain why a Giffen good may have an upward-sloping demand curve.

> A Giffen good is a strongly inferior good that takes a large share of a low-income consumer's budget. **[Conditions.]** When its price rises, the substitution effect reduces the quantity demanded, because other goods are now relatively cheaper. **[Substitution effect.]** But the price rise cuts real income sharply, and because the good is inferior, lower real income increases demand for it as the consumer can no longer afford better alternatives. **[Income effect, with direction.]** If the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, quantity demanded rises when price rises, so the demand curve slopes upward. **[Comparison of sizes.]**

## 7.2.4 Limitations of the model

- It shows only two goods, while real consumers choose among many.
- Indifference curves cannot be observed, so the model is hard to test.
- It assumes rational, well-informed consumers with stable and consistent preferences.
- The budget line assumes fixed prices, no bulk discounts, and no saving or borrowing.
- Goods are treated as divisible into any quantity.
- Giffen goods are possible in theory, but clear examples are rare.

Evaluate by noting that, unlike marginal utility theory, the model does not need utility to be measured, only ranked, which is a real advantage.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the compensated line parallel to the original budget line instead of the new one.
- Writing the slope of the budget line as price of Y ÷ price of X.
- Treating every inferior good as a Giffen good.
- Forgetting that a price change pivots the line, while an income change shifts it in parallel.
- Saying the substitution effect can be positive for a price rise.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Explain:** state the direction of each effect and why.
- **Analyse:** draw and label BL1, BL2, BLc, IC1, IC2 and points A, B and C, and name each movement.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** weigh the model's insight against its assumptions; in the unstructured Paper 4 essays, link the limitations to the question set.
