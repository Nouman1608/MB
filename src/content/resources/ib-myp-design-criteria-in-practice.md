This guide works through what genuinely top-band evidence looks like
for each of IB Middle Years Programme Design's four assessment
criteria, applied to a real design project. The [Design
Cycle revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-design-design-cycle-revision-notes/) and [how MYP Design is
assessed](/resources/ib-myp-design-assessment-revision-notes/) set out
the design cycle's four stages and the four criteria that map onto
them -- this guide goes further, since a project can move through all
four design-cycle stages without producing top-band evidence for the
corresponding criterion at each one.

## Where this fits in the subject

MYP subjects are graded against four equally weighted criteria, each
on an 8-point scale, assessed through an ePortfolio rather than a
timed written paper. This means the depth of documentation at each
design-cycle stage is not optional supporting detail -- it is the
actual evidence a moderator sees and marks. A finished prototype
photograph alone, without the accompanying inquiry, specification,
planning and testing documentation described below, gives a moderator
no basis to award marks against Criteria A, B or D at all, regardless
of how well the prototype itself performs.

## Criterion A: Inquiring and analysing

Top-band evidence identifies a genuine problem needing a solution,
analyses the need for that solution with reference to a real target
audience or client, and researches existing similar products or
solutions before writing a design brief. A lower-band response to
"design a solution for reducing food waste in a school canteen" might
jump straight to a brief without establishing why food waste happens
at that specific canteen. Top-band Criterion A evidence first
investigates the actual problem -- surveying students and staff,
observing canteen practices, researching existing food-waste-reduction
solutions used elsewhere -- and only then writes a design brief
grounded in that specific, evidenced analysis of the target audience's
actual need.

## Criterion B: Developing ideas

Top-band evidence writes a detailed, testable design specification
that genuinely drives development of a solution, generates and
evaluates a range of feasible design ideas against that specification,
and presents the chosen solution with justified reasoning for why it
was selected over the alternatives. Top-band Criterion B evidence does
not present only one idea -- it generates several genuinely different
possible solutions, evaluates each against the specification's
testable criteria (cost, materials, target-audience appeal), and
explains specifically why the chosen design best meets the
specification compared with the rejected alternatives, rather than
simply asserting that the final choice is best.

## Criterion C: Creating the solution

Top-band evidence follows a detailed, logically sequenced plan to
create a prototype sufficient for testing and evaluation, including
how time, resources and any risks were managed during creation, with
changes to the original plan justified and recorded as they occur. A
top-band Criterion C log records not just the final prototype but the
sequence followed to build it, and explicitly notes any point where
the original plan had to change -- a material substitution due to
availability, for example -- explaining why the change was made and
how it affected the following steps. A project that produces only a
finished prototype with no record of the process followed to reach it
cannot demonstrate top-band Creating the solution evidence, regardless
of how good the final prototype looks.

## Criterion D: Evaluating

Top-band evidence designs specific, measurable tests against the
original specification, carries them out, and objectively evaluates
the solution's success against each specification point individually
-- including identifying specific areas for improvement and explaining
the solution's real impact on its intended client or target audience,
rather than a general statement that the project "worked well." Rather
than concluding "the prototype canteen food-waste solution was
successful," top-band Criterion D evidence tests the solution against
each specific point in the original specification (did it reduce
measured food waste by a target amount over a trial period?), reports
the actual test results honestly even where they fall short of the
specification, and identifies specific, actionable improvements based
on that evidence.

## How to approach it

Because each criterion maps directly onto one design-cycle stage --
inquiring and analysing (the problem stage), developing ideas (the
specification stage), creating the solution (the build stage),
evaluating (the test stage) -- moving through all four stages in
sequence gives a natural structure for generating evidence against all
four criteria, provided each stage is documented with the depth
described above rather than treated as a box to tick before moving to
the next. Document as you work rather than reconstructing the process
afterward from memory, since a moderator can generally tell the
difference between contemporaneous process evidence and a tidied-up
account written after the prototype was already finished. For every
stage, ask explicitly which criterion this piece of documentation is
meant to evidence, and check it against the specific expectations
described above for that criterion, rather than assuming thorough work
automatically translates into strong evidence on the page.

## Common mistakes

Treating a criterion as evidenced simply because the corresponding design-cycle stage was completed --
completing the "creating" stage of the design cycle is not the same as producing top-band Criterion C
evidence, which specifically requires a documented, justified process log, not just a finished object.
Presenting only one design idea under Criterion B, which makes it structurally impossible to
demonstrate the comparative evaluation top-band evidence requires. Concluding an evaluation with a
general, unsupported statement of success rather than testing the solution against each specific point
in the original specification. Writing up the inquiry, specification or process documentation only
after the practical work is finished, rather than as the work happens, which tends to produce vaguer,
less specific evidence than genuine contemporaneous documentation.
