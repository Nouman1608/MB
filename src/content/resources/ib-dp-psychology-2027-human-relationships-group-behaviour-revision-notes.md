These notes condense the **group behaviour** strand of the human relationships context (SL and HL, first assessment 2027). The guide lists six areas of study. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour/) for detail, then try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour-practice/). The sub-headings of the guide are not used to form examination questions, so expect the content terms and the concepts.

## The six areas at a glance

| Area | Learning objective (summarised) | Key idea | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acculturation | Strategies and their effect on behaviour and mental health | Berry: integration, assimilation, separation, marginalisation | Berry et al. (2006) on adaptation of immigrant youth |
| Conformity | Role in understanding group behaviour | Normative and informational influence | Asch line study; Sherif autokinetic study |
| Cultural dimensions | Role of one or more dimensions | Individualism-collectivism, power distance and others | Hofstede; Bond and Smith (1996) |
| Social identity theory | Apply to explain and change behaviour | Categorise, identify, compare; in-group favouritism | Tajfel et al. (1971); Sherif (1954) |
| Compliance techniques | Apply one or more to change behaviour | Foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face, low-ball | Freedman and Fraser (1966); Cialdini et al. (1975) |
| Social learning | Apply to change behaviour | Attention, retention, reproduction, motivation | Bandura (1961) Bobo doll |

## Key definitions

- **Group behaviour:** how people act as members of a group and under its influence.
- **Acculturation:** change that follows sustained contact between cultures.
- **Acculturative stress:** stress caused by the demands of adapting to a new culture.
- **Enculturation:** learning the norms and values of your own culture from childhood.
- **Conformity:** changing behaviour or belief to match a group.
- **Normative influence:** conforming to be accepted.
- **Informational influence:** conforming because you believe the group is right.
- **Compliance:** agreeing to a direct request.
- **Cultural dimension:** a measurable way in which cultures differ in values.
- **Social identity:** the part of self-concept that comes from group membership.
- **In-group favouritism:** preferring members of your own group.
- **Superordinate goal:** a shared goal that needs the cooperation of two groups.
- **Vicarious reinforcement:** learning from seeing a model rewarded or punished.

## Acculturation

- **Berry's two questions:** keep my culture? have contact with the new culture? Yes and yes is integration. No and yes is assimilation. Yes and no is separation. No and no is marginalisation.
- **Effects:** integration is generally linked to better adaptation; marginalisation to the poorest. Acculturative stress comes from language, discrimination and lost support.
- **Evaluate:** strategies are not always chosen freely; people use different strategies in different settings; evidence is correlational and self-reported, so the direction of cause is unclear.
- **Concepts:** change, perspective, responsibility (avoid stigma).

## Conformity

- **Asch:** a clear majority gave the wrong line answer. About a third of critical responses conformed, about three-quarters of participants conformed at least once, and an ally reduced conformity.
- **Sherif:** a group norm formed in an ambiguous judgement task, which is informational influence.
- **Evaluate:** controlled, repeated, but artificial and low stakes; American student samples; deception. Bond and Smith (1996) found more conformity in collectivist cultures.
- **Concepts:** causality, bias, measurement, responsibility.

## Cultural dimensions

- Use a dimension to make a prediction, then limit it. For instance, collectivism predicts stronger in-group loyalty and more conformity.
- **Evaluate:** based on one company, so it may not generalise; averages hide individual variation; an etic approach may miss local meaning.
- **Concepts:** perspective, measurement, bias.

## Social identity theory

- **Three processes:** categorisation, identification, comparison.
- **Minimal groups (Tajfel et al., 1971):** arbitrary groups were enough for in-group favouritism.
- **Reducing conflict (Sherif, 1954):** shared goals reduced hostility between competing groups.
- **Applying it:** mixed teams, joint goals, a broader shared identity.
- **Evaluate:** artificial, possible demand characteristics, little about individual differences; ethical issues in the camp study.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, bias.

## Compliance techniques

| Technique | Mechanism | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| Foot-in-the-door | Small request first, large later; self-perception | Field studies with high realism, but effect size varies |
| Door-in-the-face | Refused large request, then smaller one; reciprocity | May depend on the same person making both requests |
| Low-ball | Commitment made before the true cost is revealed | Raises concerns about manipulation |

- **Applying to change group behaviour:** a pledge first, then a rota for recycling.
- **Concepts:** change, responsibility (manipulation, informed consent).

## Social learning

- **Bandura's four processes:** attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
- **Bobo doll (1961):** children who saw a violent model imitated it more.
- **Applying it:** respected role models for positive group norms; make the reward visible.
- **Evaluate:** controlled but artificial; children as participants; underplays biology and choice.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, responsibility.

## A model Section A answer

**Question:** Explain social identity theory, with one example. (4 marks, written for practice.)

**Strong answer:** Social identity theory proposes that part of our self-concept comes from the groups we belong to. People categorise themselves and others, identify with their group and compare it with others, and to protect self-esteem they favour the in-group. For example, supporters of one football team may rate their own fans as friendlier and blame the other side for trouble, even though both groups behave similarly.

The answer gets the full range because the knowledge is accurate and the example is relevant and explained.

## A model Section B approach

An unseen scenario might describe a new employee who copies everyone's habits and then agrees with decisions he privately doubts. Steps:
1. Name the content terms: social learning, normative influence.
2. Apply each term to a specific detail in the scenario, not in general.
3. Add a short evaluation or implication, such as how a manager could encourage dissent.

## Concept links to prepare

- **Change:** which strategies (shared goals, role models, small requests) work and for how long.
- **Causality:** laboratory evidence versus complex real groups.
- **Bias:** in-group bias; cultural bias in samples.
- **Measurement:** whether line tasks or point allocation truly measure social behaviour.
- **Perspective:** etic and emic approaches to culture.
- **Responsibility:** manipulation, deception, stigma for immigrant groups.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the strategies of Berry with no consequences for mental health.
- Confusing acculturation (adapting to a new culture) with enculturation (learning your own).
- Calling compliance "conformity". Compliance needs a request; conformity needs only group pressure.
- Treating a country average as a fact about every person.
- Using one concept only in a Section C answer.

## Self-test

1. What is the difference between normative and informational influence?
2. Name Berry's four acculturation strategies.
3. Give one strength and one limitation of Asch's line study.
4. How does Sherif's camp study support social identity theory's approach to reducing conflict?
5. Explain how the foot-in-the-door technique could raise attendance at a school club.
6. Name Bandura's four conditions for social learning.
7. Why is it risky to use cultural dimensions to predict one person's behaviour?

**Answers:** 1. Normative influence is conforming to be liked or accepted; informational influence is conforming because you think the group is right. 2. Integration, assimilation, separation, marginalisation. 3. Strength: controlled, repeated findings; limitation: artificial task with low stakes and a narrow sample. 4. Shared goals that needed both groups changed the way the groups categorised each other and reduced hostility. 5. Ask students to agree to a small step first, such as signing up for a taster session, so they see themselves as people who attend and are more likely to agree to a regular commitment. 6. Attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. 7. Dimensions describe national averages; individuals vary, so predicting one person risks stereotyping.
