All data are invented. Revise first with the [scarcity, choice and opportunity cost study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-scarcity-choice-and-opportunity-cost/) and the [scarcity, choice and opportunity cost revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-scarcity-choice-and-opportunity-cost-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** What is the fundamental economic problem? **[1]**

A: Prices rise faster than incomes

B: Resources are limited while wants are unlimited

C: Some goods are in shortage at the current price

D: Governments cannot raise enough tax revenue

**2.** A farmer can use a field to grow maize, which she values most, then beans, then cotton. She grows maize. What is the opportunity cost? **[1]**

A: The beans and the cotton

B: The beans only

C: The cotton only

D: Nothing, because she chose her preferred crop

**3.** Which **one** of the following is a decision about "how to produce"? **[1]**

A: A government decides to provide free school meals to low-income families

B: A car maker decides to build electric rather than petrol cars

C: A clothing firm replaces hand stitching with automated sewing machines

D: A city decides to build more housing rather than offices

**4.** Distinguish between scarcity and a shortage. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a firm, as well as an individual, must make choices. **[2]**

**6.** A company paid 40,000 cu for specialist software three years ago. It cannot be resold. The company could use its staff's time to make product X, earning 15,000 cu, or product Y, earning 12,000 cu. Identify the opportunity cost of making product X, and explain why the 40,000 cu is not part of it. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A delivery firm decides how many extra routes to run each day. The extra revenue from each additional route is shown below. Each extra route costs 55 cu in fuel and wages.

| Extra route | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extra revenue (cu) | 90 | 72 | 58 | 41 |

**(a)** State how many extra routes the firm should run, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total gain to the firm from the routes you chose in (a). **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why this is described as a decision made at the margin. **[2]**

**8.** A government has a budget of 120 million cu. It can either build a new university campus or fund a three-year programme to train 40,000 unemployed workers. Each costs 120 million cu.

**(a)** If it chooses the campus, state the opportunity cost. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how the time period over which benefits arise could affect the government's choice. **[4]**

**9.** A workshop can produce 200 chairs using either method A (60 days of labour and basic tools hired for 300 cu) or method B (25 days of labour and a machine hired for 1,500 cu). Labour costs 30 cu a day.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of each method. **[2]**

**(b)** State which method uses fewer resources, and explain how a rise in the daily wage to 50 cu would affect the choice. **[3]**

**10.** Explain what is meant by the "for whom" question, and explain **two** different ways it could be answered. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why free goods have no opportunity cost but goods provided free of charge by the government do. **[4]**

**12.** Assess whether opportunity cost is a useful concept for a government deciding how to spend its budget. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Options A, C and D are particular problems; only B is the fundamental problem that applies to every economy. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Only the next best alternative, beans, counts. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. It changes the method of production. A is "for whom"; B and D are "what". **[1]**

**4.** Scarcity is the permanent situation in which wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them [1]. A shortage is a market situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price, which can be removed if the price rises [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A firm has limited capital, labour and finance [1], so it must choose which products to make and how to make them, and each choice means giving up another use of those resources [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The opportunity cost is product Y, worth 12,000 cu, the next best use of the staff's time [1]. The 40,000 cu was spent in the past [1] and cannot be recovered whatever the firm chooses now, so it is not an alternative given up [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Three routes [1]. The first three each add more revenue (90, 72, 58) than their cost (55), but the fourth adds only 41, less than 55 [1].

**(b)** Gain = (90 − 55) + (72 − 55) + (58 − 55) [1] = 35 + 17 + 3 = **55 cu** [1].

**(c)** The firm compares the extra revenue and the extra cost of each additional route [1], rather than deciding on the total number all at once; it keeps adding routes while the extra benefit is at least the extra cost [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** The training programme for 40,000 workers [1].

**(b)** The training programme gives benefits quickly, as trained workers find jobs within a few years [1], reducing unemployment and benefit spending soon [1]. The campus takes time to build and its graduates enter work only after several years [1], but it may raise skills and output for decades, so a government with a long time horizon may prefer it [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Method A: 60 × 30 + 300 = **2,100 cu** [1]. Method B: 25 × 30 + 1,500 = **2,250 cu** [1].

**(b)** Method A is cheaper, so it uses fewer resources for the same output [1]. At 50 cu a day, A costs 60 × 50 + 300 = 3,300 cu and B costs 25 × 50 + 1,500 = 2,750 cu [1], so the capital-intensive method B becomes cheaper: the answer to "how" depends on relative factor prices [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The "for whom" question is about how the output of an economy is distributed among its people [1]. One way: through the market, where goods go to those willing and able to pay, so distribution follows income [1]. Another way: through the government, which can share goods according to need, for example by rationing or by providing services free to all [1]. These lead to different outcomes: the market may leave low-income households without essentials, while state allocation may be fairer but less responsive to individual preferences [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A free good, such as air in an unpolluted place, is not scarce, so using it means no resources are diverted from other uses [1], so it has no opportunity cost [1]. Goods provided free of charge by the government, such as school places, use scarce resources such as teachers and buildings [1], which could have been used for other purposes and are paid for through taxes, so they have an opportunity cost [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 8 marks across knowledge, analysis and evaluation.
- Definition: the next best alternative forgone [1].
- Useful: it makes the government compare projects rather than judge each in isolation [1].
- Useful: it reminds voters and officials that "free" public services use scarce resources [1].
- Useful: applied at the margin, it helps decide whether one more unit of spending on a service is worth more than its alternative [1].
- Limitation: the next best alternative may be hard to identify among many options [1].
- Limitation: benefits such as health or culture are hard to value in money, so comparisons are uncertain [1].
- Limitation: benefits arise over different time periods, and political pressures may override the analysis [1].
- Judgement: useful as a way of thinking about choices, even if it cannot give precise answers, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "next best" out of the definition.
- Adding all the rejected alternatives together.
- Counting money already spent that cannot be recovered.
- Treating scarcity and shortage as the same.
- Ignoring the time dimension when assessing a choice.
- Ending an assess question without a judgement.
