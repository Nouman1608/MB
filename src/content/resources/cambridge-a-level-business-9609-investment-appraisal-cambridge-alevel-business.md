## 10.3.1 Why investment appraisal is needed

Capital investment has four features that make careful appraisal worthwhile:

- **Large sums**: a mistake can threaten the survival of the business.
- **Long time scale**: costs come now, benefits come over many years, so forecasts are uncertain.
- **Hard to reverse**: specialised equipment or buildings may sell for far less than they cost.
- **Limited funds**: choosing one project means giving up others, an opportunity cost.

Appraisal gives a consistent way to compare projects, test them against targets, and show managers and lenders the basis for a decision.

## 10.3.2 What payback and ARR measure

**Payback** answers: *how quickly do we get our money back?* Build a cumulative cash flow; in the year it passes the cost, find the fraction of the year needed, assuming inflows are even through the year. Convert the fraction to months by multiplying by 12.

**ARR** answers: *what average annual return does the project earn on the money tied up in it?* The syllabus formula is ARR = (average profit / average investment) × 100. Using the standard approach:

- Total profit = total net cash inflows (including any residual value) − initial cost.
- Average profit = total profit ÷ number of years.
- Average investment = (initial cost + residual value) ÷ 2.

Subtracting the initial cost and adding back the residual value means depreciation has been allowed for, so do not deduct it again.

## 10.3.3 What NPV measures

**NPV** answers: *how much value, in today's money, does the project add after allowing for the time value of money?* A dollar received in Year 4 is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar could earn interest and because later money is less certain.

- Present value = net cash flow × discount factor for that year.
- NPV = total present value of inflows − initial cost (the cost is paid now, so it is not discounted).
- A residual value received at the end is discounted with the final year's factor.

**Choosing the discount rate.** Businesses often use their cost of borrowing, sometimes raised to allow for risk. A higher rate makes later cash flows count for less.

## Worked example: Westerby Hotel

Westerby has $200,000 to invest and two options:

- **Project S**: solar panels. Net savings of $48,000 a year for 5 years; residual value $40,000.
- **Project R**: restaurant refit. Net inflows of $90,000, $70,000, $50,000, $30,000 and $20,000; no residual value.

**Payback**

- S: 200,000 ÷ 48,000 = 4.17 years, about **4 years 2 months**.
- R: cumulative 90,000 then 160,000; still needed 40,000; 40,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.8 of Year 3, so **2.8 years**, about 2 years 10 months.

**ARR**

| | S | R |
|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 240,000 + 40,000 = 280,000 | 260,000 |
| Total profit | 80,000 | 60,000 |
| Average profit (÷ 5) | 16,000 | 12,000 |
| Average investment | (200,000 + 40,000) ÷ 2 = 120,000 | 200,000 ÷ 2 = 100,000 |
| ARR | **13.3%** | **12.0%** |

**NPV at 8%** (factors 0.926, 0.857, 0.794, 0.735, 0.681)

| Year | S cash | S present value | R cash | R present value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 48,000 | 44,448 | 90,000 | 83,340 |
| 2 | 48,000 | 41,136 | 70,000 | 59,990 |
| 3 | 48,000 | 38,112 | 50,000 | 39,700 |
| 4 | 48,000 | 35,280 | 30,000 | 22,050 |
| 5 | 48,000 + 40,000 | 32,688 + 27,240 | 20,000 | 13,620 |
| Total | | 218,904 | | 218,700 |
| **NPV** | | **18,904** | | **18,700** |

**NPV at 12%** (factors 0.893, 0.797, 0.712, 0.636, 0.567): S has total present value 173,040 + 22,680 = 195,720, so NPV = **−4,280**. R has total present value 202,180, so NPV = **2,180**.

## Interpreting the results

| Measure | Favours | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | R | Most of R's cash arrives early |
| ARR | S | S earns more profit in total, helped by its residual value |
| NPV at 8% | S, narrowly | S's larger total just outweighs R's earlier timing |
| NPV at 12% | R | A higher rate shrinks S's later cash and residual value more |

Three lessons follow:

1. **The methods answer different questions**, so they can rank projects differently. Payback rewards speed; ARR rewards total return; NPV rewards value after timing.
2. **The discount rate matters.** At 8% the projects are almost equal; at 12% S destroys value. If Westerby borrows at 8% with little risk, S is acceptable; if its finance is dear or the savings are uncertain, R is safer.
3. **Small forecast errors can reverse a decision.** If R's inflows were 10% lower, its present value at 8% would be 218,700 × 0.9 = 196,830, an NPV of −3,170. Both projects sit close to zero, so neither is a clear winner on the numbers.

## 10.3.4 Qualitative factors and the decision

Because the numbers are close, qualitative factors decide. For Westerby:

- **Objectives and image.** Solar panels support an environmental message that may attract guests and corporate bookings.
- **Risk.** Solar savings depend on energy prices, which may rise or fall; restaurant inflows depend on tastes and competition, and a refit needs refreshing within a few years.
- **Staff and customers.** A refit closes the restaurant for weeks, losing revenue and disrupting staff; panels cause little disruption.
- **Finance.** If funds are short, R's faster payback lowers risk to cash flow.
- **Reliability of forecasts.** Steady energy savings may be easier to forecast than restaurant demand.

A well-argued recommendation might be: choose S if Westerby can borrow at around 8% and values its environmental image; choose R if cash is tight or its cost of finance is higher.

## Comparison of methods

| Method | Question it answers | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | How soon is the cost recovered? | Ignores everything after payback and the time value of money |
| ARR | What average return is earned? | Ignores timing; a dollar in Year 5 counts the same as Year 1 |
| NPV | What value is added in today's money? | Depends on the chosen discount rate; harder to explain |

All three rely on forecast cash flows, which are least reliable for distant years.

*Recommend which project Westerby Hotel should choose.*

> "On NPV at 8%, S adds $18,904 and R $18,700, so the difference is too small to decide on. ARR slightly favours S at 13.3%, but R pays back in 2.8 years compared with over four. At 12%, S has a negative NPV, so S is acceptable only if Westerby's cost of finance is low. If finance costs more or cash is tight, R is the safer choice."

It uses all three methods, notes the closeness of the figures, brings in the rate, and makes a conditional recommendation.

## Common mistakes

- Discounting the initial cost.
- Forgetting to include the residual value in ARR or NPV.
- Dividing ARR by the initial cost rather than average investment.
- Treating a positive NPV as proof that a project should go ahead.
- Recommending on one method without noting what the others show.

## Exam technique

**Calculate**: set out cumulative flows and discount tables clearly. **Interpret**: say what each result means against a target. **Evaluate** or **recommend**: combine methods, test the effect of forecast errors or a different discount rate, add qualitative factors, then decide with a condition.
