Section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification includes "The limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis when assessing business performance", and adds that "limitations will include those relating to both financial and non-financial factors". The [limitations and computers guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-limitations-of-financial-statements-and-the-use-of-comp/) introduces the general limits: past data, the snapshot date, estimates and the need for comparison. This guide goes a level deeper. It shows the specific weakness of each ratio in the specification, works through four distortions with figures, and models an evaluate answer. All figures are invented and in dollars.

Recap with the [limitations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-revision-notes/), then practise with the [limitations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/). The ratio formulas themselves are in the [accounting ratios guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-accounting-ratios-and-financial-measures/); OxfordAQA publishes its own "Formulae for accounting ratios" guide, which your exam follows.

## Each ratio has its own weak spot

A general statement such as "ratios use past figures" earns little. Linking a limitation to the particular ratio in the question earns much more.

| Ratio | A specific limitation |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin % | Hides the mix of products: selling more low-margin goods lowers the overall margin even if every product's margin is unchanged |
| Markup % | Depends on how closing inventory is valued; an inventory valuation error changes cost of sales and so the markup |
| Rate of inventory turnover | Average inventory from only two dates (opening and closing) may not represent the level held during the year |
| Profit in relation to revenue % | Affected by how the owner is paid: a sole trader's profit is before any reward for the owner's work |
| Return on capital employed % | Capital employed uses net book values, so older, heavily depreciated assets make the ratio look higher |
| Current ratio | Treats all current assets as equally available, though inventory may be slow to sell |
| Liquid capital ratio | One year-end figure; seasonal businesses can look weak or strong depending on the date |
| Trade receivable days | Should use credit revenue; if total revenue (including cash sales) is used, days look shorter |
| Trade payable days | Should use credit purchases; one large late invoice at the year end can distort it |

## Distortion 1: using total revenue for receivable days

Lumen Lighting had revenue of 365,000, of which 146,000 was cash sales. Trade receivables at the year end were 30,000.

- Using total revenue: 30,000 ÷ 365,000 × 365 = **30 days**.
- Using credit revenue of 219,000: 30,000 ÷ 219,000 × 365 = **50 days**.

Credit customers actually take about 50 days to pay. If the business offers 30 days' credit, the first figure hides a problem with credit control. When a question gives credit sales separately, use them.

## Distortion 2: the age of assets and ROCE

Two courier businesses each earn profit from operations of 36,000 a year.

| | Swift Parcels | Metro Dispatch |
|---|---|---|
| Capital employed | 120,000 | 240,000 |
| Return on capital employed | 30% | 15% |

Swift Parcels looks twice as efficient. But its vans were bought years ago and are heavily depreciated, so its capital employed is low. Metro Dispatch has just bought new vans. Swift will soon need to replace its fleet at today's prices, which will raise its capital employed and depreciation and lower its ROCE. The ratio compares historic cost figures that were recorded at different times.

## Distortion 3: inflation

Over four years Ardley Books' revenue rose from 200,000 to 220,000, an increase of 10%. Over the same period, assume book prices rose by 15% (an invented figure for illustration).

At the earlier prices, the later revenue is worth 220,000 ÷ 1.15 = about 191,300. In real terms, sales volume has fallen by about 4.3%. The statements, which use money measurement and historic cost, show growth that did not happen. Comparisons over several years are most affected.

## Distortion 4: comparing a sole trader with a company

A sole trader and a limited company each have revenue of 150,000. The sole trader shows profit of 30,000. The company pays its director, who does the same work as the sole trader, a salary of 24,000, and shows profit of 6,000.

| | Sole trader | Company |
|---|---|---|
| Profit in relation to revenue | 20% | 4% |

The difference comes from how the owner is rewarded. The sole trader's reward is taken as drawings, which are not an expense. Deduct a notional 24,000 for the owner's work and the sole trader's profit is also 6,000. Comparisons between different types of organisation must allow for this.

## Non-financial factors: what the statements leave out

Financial statements record only items that can be measured in money (the money measurement concept). Many things that decide future performance are missing. Choose the factors that matter to the stakeholder in the question.

| Stakeholder | Non-financial questions the statements cannot answer |
|---|---|
| Lender | Is the owner experienced? Is the market growing? Will the main customer stay? |
| Supplier | Does the business have a reputation for paying on time? Is it loyal to suppliers? |
| Employee | Is morale good? Is the business planning job cuts or expansion? |
| Owner or investor | Are there new competitors? Is there a new product or technology on the way? |
| Local community | Does the business pollute? Is it a responsible employer? |

Other factors include changes in the law, the state of the economy and the quality of management. None of these appears in a ratio, yet each can change profit sharply next year.

## Annotated answer: an evaluate question

*A bank is considering a loan to Ardley Books. It has calculated ratios for the last two years. Evaluate the usefulness of ratio analysis to the bank in making its decision.*

1. **Usefulness, applied.** "Ratios let the bank compare Ardley's liquidity and profitability across two years quickly. A current ratio and liquid capital ratio show whether Ardley can meet short-term debts, which matters because the bank needs interest paid on time." *Shows the benefit for this user.*
2. **Financial limitation, applied.** "Revenue rose by 10%, but with book prices up by 15% sales volume actually fell, so the trend in revenue-based figures overstates Ardley's performance." *Uses the case data, not a general statement.*
3. **Second financial limitation.** "The ratios are based on the year-end statement of financial position. A bookshop may hold high inventory before a busy season, so the liquidity ratios could look different a month later." *Links the snapshot limit to this kind of business.*
4. **Non-financial limitation.** "The ratios say nothing about competition from online sellers or about the owner's experience, both of which affect whether Ardley can repay." *Relevant to the lender.*
5. **Judgement.** "Ratios are a useful starting point, but the bank should also look at cash flow forecasts, the trend in volume, and non-financial information before lending." *Balanced and reasoned.*

## Common mistakes

- Writing "ratios may be calculated wrongly". The limitation is in what the figures can show, not in arithmetic.
- Listing limitations without naming the ratio or the business they affect.
- Using total revenue for receivable days when credit sales are given.
- Forgetting that inflation affects comparisons over several years more than within one year.
- Calling staff morale or reputation a financial factor.
- Giving only limitations when the question asks you to evaluate usefulness.

## Exam technique

- **Explain a limitation**: name it, show how it affects a specific ratio, and say what the user might wrongly conclude.
- **Evaluate**: weigh usefulness against limitations for the stakeholder named, then give a judgement and what else they should look at.
- Use figures from the question whenever you can; one applied point beats three generic ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can an old business show a higher ROCE than a new one?**
Its assets are shown at historic cost less many years of depreciation, so capital employed is low and the same profit gives a higher percentage.

**Is the money measurement concept a limitation?**
Yes. Only items with a money value are recorded, so factors such as staff skill, reputation and customer loyalty never appear in the statements.
