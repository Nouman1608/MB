Key question 2.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How do we learn our identity?* The
sociological answer is that identity is learned, not inherited: we become who we are through
socialisation into a culture. In Paper 1 this content is tested in Question 2, which you can choose
instead of Question 3. Strong answers do three things: they use the syllabus concepts precisely, they
explain *how* each agency socialises (the process, not just the name), and they judge how effective
agencies are and which theory explains socialisation best.

The [O Level guide to how we learn our identity](/resources/o-level-sociology-learning-and-influences-on-identity/)
gives an overview of 2.1 and 2.3 together. This guide goes further into the evidence, the processes
and the theoretical debate.

## What the syllabus covers

- **2.1.1** (a) Society as a product of social construction: culture, norms, values, customs, roles,
  status (achieved/ascribed), stereotypes; (b) how these concepts influence individual behaviour and
  social identity.
- **2.1.2** The social construction of our identity: the nature–nurture debate, the relativity of
  culture, socialisation, norms and values.
- **2.1.3** Primary and secondary socialisation: the key agencies (family, education, peer group,
  media, religion, workplace) and the effectiveness of each.
- **2.1.4** Processes used by agencies, for example canalisation, hidden curriculum, media
  representations, peer pressure, religious teachings, role modelling and workplace training.
- **2.1.5** The debate between perspectives: consensus and conflict; functionalist ideas (inadequate
  socialisation, social cohesion/value consensus); Marxist ideas (socialisation into capitalist
  ideology, reproduction of social class); feminist ideas (gender role socialisation into
  masculinity/femininity).

## How the building blocks fit together

The 2.1.1 concepts form a chain. **Values** are general beliefs about what is good or important, such
as respect for elders. **Norms** turn values into specific rules for behaviour, such as giving up a
seat to an older person. **Customs** are norms that have lasted for generations and become tradition.
**Roles** bundle norms together into the behaviour expected of a position: a teacher, a daughter, a
team captain. **Status** is the social position itself and the respect attached to it, either
**ascribed** (fixed at birth, such as sex or birth order) or **achieved** (earned, such as becoming a
nurse). **Stereotypes** are fixed and oversimplified beliefs about whole groups. Together these make
up **culture**, the whole way of life of a group.

The exam asks how these concepts influence behaviour and identity (2.1.1b). The mechanism is
expectation: people know what is expected of their roles and statuses, are judged against those
expectations by others, and come to see themselves in those terms. A girl repeatedly told that she is
"the responsible one" may come to define herself that way; a stereotype that boys are poor readers
may lead some boys to stop trying.

## Nature or nurture?

The **nature** side argues that behaviour is largely inborn, shaped by genes and biology. The
**nurture** side argues that it is mainly learned through socialisation. Sociologists emphasise
nurture, using two kinds of evidence.

**Cases of extreme isolation.** "Genie", discovered in Los Angeles in 1970 at the age of 13 after being
kept almost entirely isolated by her father, could barely speak. Despite years of later teaching she
never fully acquired the grammar of a first language. Such cases suggest that language and social
behaviour depend on contact with others at the right stage of life. They must be used carefully: they
are rare, often involve abuse, and the child may have had other difficulties from birth.

**The relativity of culture.** If behaviour were fixed by biology, it would be similar everywhere. In
fact what counts as polite, modest, attractive or adult varies between societies and over time.
Margaret Mead's *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935) described three groups in
New Guinea: among the Arapesh both men and women were expected to be gentle and cooperative, among the
Mundugumor both were expected to be aggressive, and among the Tchambuli women were the more dominant
and practical sex. Mead concluded that masculine and feminine temperaments are largely cultural. Later
researchers questioned some of her findings, so present the study as influential evidence rather than
proof.

A balanced answer accepts that biology sets some limits (for example, physical development), but that
how a society interprets and responds to those differences is cultural.

## Agencies and processes: how socialisation actually works

**Primary socialisation** takes place in early childhood, mainly in the family, and teaches language,
basic norms and an early sense of self. **Secondary socialisation** continues throughout life through
other agencies. The syllabus asks for both the *process* each agency uses and its *effectiveness*.

| Agency | Process | Why it is effective | Why its effect is limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Family** | Role modelling; canalisation | Earliest and most emotionally intense influence | Influence weakens in adolescence; families differ widely |
| **Education** | Hidden curriculum; formal teaching | Compulsory, daily, for many years | Pupils may reject school values, forming anti-school groups |
| **Peer group** | Peer pressure; acceptance and rejection | Strongest in teenage years, when status among friends matters most | Groups change; individuals belong to several peer groups at once |
| **Media** | Media representations; role models | Constant exposure across all ages | Audiences interpret and resist messages |
| **Religion** | Religious teachings; rituals | Gives moral rules backed by belief and community | Influence varies with how religious a society or family is |
| **Workplace** | Workplace training; induction; dress codes | Shapes adult routines and occupational identity | Starts late; many people work informally or not at all |

Ann Oakley described how families shape gender identity through **manipulation** (encouraging or
discouraging behaviour according to sex, for example fussing over a girl's appearance) and
**canalisation** (channelling children towards "appropriate" toys and activities, such as dolls for
girls and construction sets for boys). She also identified **verbal appellations** ("good girl",
"brave boy") and **different activities** given to boys and girls. These are useful named processes
for the family's role in gender socialisation.

Angela McRobbie's study of the teenage magazine *Jackie* (1978) is an example of **media
representations**: she argued that the magazine taught girls that romance and finding a boyfriend
were their central concerns.

## The theoretical debate

**Consensus: functionalism.** Functionalists see socialisation as the way society passes on shared
values from one generation to the next, creating **value consensus** and **social cohesion**. Talcott
Parsons argued that the family's basic functions are the primary socialisation of children and the
stabilisation of adult personalities. Problems such as crime are explained by **inadequate
socialisation**: when families or schools fail to teach shared norms, individuals do not conform.
*Criticism:* this assumes everyone shares the same values and ignores whose values are taught.

**Conflict: Marxism.** Marxists argue that agencies socialise people into **capitalist ideology**:
beliefs that make inequality seem natural and fair, such as the idea that success depends only on
effort. Louis Althusser called education an "ideological state apparatus" because it spreads ruling
class ideas. Socialisation also **reproduces social class**: working-class children are prepared for
working-class jobs, middle-class children for middle-class ones. *Criticism:* people are not passive;
many recognise and challenge inequality.

**Conflict: feminism.** Feminists argue that **gender role socialisation** teaches **masculinity** and
**femininity** as if they were natural, preparing girls for caring and domestic roles and boys for
dominance and paid work. Oakley's processes and McRobbie's study of *Jackie* provide evidence.
*Criticism:* gender roles have changed considerably, and feminists may underestimate girls' and
women's ability to reject these messages.

## Worked example: a "discuss" answer (practice)

*Discuss how the family socialises children into gender roles.*

> The family is the main agency of primary socialisation, so it shapes gender identity before other
> agencies have any influence. Oakley identified canalisation: parents channel children towards
> gendered toys and activities, for example buying a girl a toy kitchen and a boy a football, so that
> children learn which interests are "normal" for their sex. Through manipulation, parents praise or
> discourage behaviour according to sex, such as comforting a crying girl but telling a boy to be
> brave. Role modelling matters too: if a mother does most of the cooking and a father controls the
> money, children may copy these patterns. Feminists see this as the start of patriarchal
> inequality. However, many families now challenge traditional roles, and children are also influenced
> by school, peers and media, so the family is one influence among several.

**Why it works:** it names processes, gives a concrete example of each, links to a theory, and ends
with an evaluative point.

## Common mistakes

- Listing agencies without the process each one uses.
- Naming the family as an agency of secondary socialisation.
- Using cases of isolated children as proof without noting their limitations.
- Describing a theory without saying what it argues socialisation is *for*.
- Confusing norms (specific rules) with values (general beliefs).

## Exam technique

- **Define** (parts a and b): one precise sentence, ideally with an example.
- **Explain** (parts c and d): three developed points, each with a process and an example.
- **Discuss** (part e): explain in depth with evidence; a brief counter-point helps.
- **Evaluate** (part f): weigh two or more perspectives or agencies and reach a judgement.
