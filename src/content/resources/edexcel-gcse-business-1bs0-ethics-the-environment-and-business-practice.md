## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term pressure group. **[2]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an environmental consideration for a business? **[1]**

A: Paying staff more than it must

B: Reducing packaging waste

C: Making advertising claims honest

D: Paying suppliers on time

**3.** Which **two** of the following are methods a pressure group might use? **[2]**

A: A boycott

B: A tariff

C: A petition

D: A franchise

E: A merger

**4.** Penny Lane Laundry washes 12,000 loads a year. It switches to an eco-friendly detergent that costs £0.25 more per load. Last year its profit was £45,000.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost of the detergent per year. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in profit, assuming nothing else changes. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** Greywell Toys was criticised by a campaign group for using plastic packaging. It now packs its toys in card boxes and its television adverts show children returning old toys for recycling. State **one** way Greywell Toys has changed its promotion. **[1]**

**6.** Give **two** possible costs to a business of becoming more sustainable. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** way that acting ethically could increase a business's profit. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Quayside Burgers runs food stalls at festivals. A pressure group has posted videos online showing litter from its packaging after events. Outline **one** way this campaign could affect the place element of Quayside's marketing mix. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **one** possible trade-off between the environment and profit for a business. **[3]**

**10.** Oakridge Chocolates sells 50,000 bars a year at £2.50 each. Each bar costs £1.30 to make. The owner decides to buy cocoa only from growers who are paid a fair price, which adds £0.12 to the cost of each bar.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in total contribution if price and sales stay the same. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse the possible impact of this decision on Oakridge Chocolates. **[6]**

**11.** After the change in question 10, each Oakridge bar costs £1.42 to make. The owner must choose a price.

Option 1: keep the price at £2.50 and promote the fair-price cocoa; sales are forecast to rise to 56,000 bars.

Option 2: raise the price to £2.65 to cover the extra cost; sales are forecast to fall to 47,000 bars.

Justify which option Oakridge Chocolates should choose. **[9]**

**12.** Lindale Haulage runs 20 diesel lorries and makes a profit of £400,000 a year. It is considering replacing 5 of them with electric lorries. Each electric lorry would cost £40,000 a year more to finance than a diesel lorry but would save £22,000 a year in fuel. Its largest customer, a supermarket group, brings £150,000 of Lindale's profit and has said it will favour hauliers that cut their emissions over the next three years.

Evaluate whether Lindale Haulage should switch 5 lorries to electric. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An organised group of people who campaign [1] to change the behaviour of businesses or governments, for example through petitions or boycotts [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* A, C and D are ethical considerations about people. Environmental considerations concern the natural world.

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** £0.25 × 12,000 = **£3,000** [1]

**(b)** £3,000 ÷ £45,000 × 100 [1] = **6.7%** [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* The divisor is the original profit, £45,000. Dividing by the new profit gives a different and wrong answer.

**5.** Its adverts now show children returning old toys for recycling [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* The card boxes are a product change. The question asks about promotion, so choose the advert.

**6.** Any two from: dearer materials or packaging [1]; investment in new equipment such as electric vehicles or solar panels; redesigning products; time and money spent measuring and reporting environmental impact [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Customers who care about fair treatment may choose the business over its rivals [1], which increases its sales [1]. Some may also be willing to pay a higher price, so the extra revenue can be greater than the extra cost of acting ethically, raising profit [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Another valid chain starts with staff: better treatment lowers staff turnover, which cuts recruitment and training costs.

**8.** Festival organisers may not want to be linked with litter, so they could refuse Quayside a pitch at future events [1], which would cut the number of places it can sell and reduce its sales until it changes its packaging [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Place means where and how customers can buy. Tie the point to festivals, which is where Quayside sells.

**9.** Using recycled or biodegradable materials is better for the environment [1] but these materials often cost more than standard ones [1], so unless the business can raise its price or sell more, its profit per unit falls [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** £0.12 × 50,000 [1] = **£6,000** [1]

**(b)** The extra £6,000 cuts contribution from £60,000 to £54,000 if nothing else changes [1], so profit falls unless Oakridge raises its price or sells more [1]. However, the fair-price cocoa gives Oakridge an ethical message it can promote [1], which may attract customers who care how cocoa growers are treated and help it stand out from cheaper brands [1]. Over time this could build loyalty and allow a higher price, which would reduce or remove the trade-off [1]. The risk is that price-sensitive customers will not notice or value the change, so Oakridge bears the cost without extra sales [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers gain from using the figures in the case. Showing both sides of the trade-off is what this item is about.

**11.** Option 1 contribution = (£2.50 − £1.42) × 56,000 = £1.08 × 56,000 [1] = **£60,480** [1]. Option 2 contribution = (£2.65 − £1.42) × 47,000 = £1.23 × 47,000 [1] = **£57,810** [1]. Option 1 earns £2,670 more [1] and is higher than the £60,000 earned before the change, so the ethical choice pays for itself if the forecast is right [1]. Option 1 also grows market presence, with 9,000 more bars sold than Option 2 [1]. However, Option 1 relies on promotion persuading 6,000 extra customers; if sales stayed at 50,000, contribution would be only £54,000 [1]. **Judgement:** choose Option 1, because it gives the higher contribution and uses the ethical change as a selling point, but Oakridge should check sales after a few months and be ready to raise its price if the extra customers do not appear [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* A Justify answer needs both options calculated and a reason the rejected option is weaker. Testing what happens if the forecast is wrong shows judgement.

**12.** Net extra cost per lorry = £40,000 − £22,000 = £18,000 [1]. For 5 lorries = £90,000 a year [1], which is 22.5% of current profit (£90,000 ÷ £400,000 × 100) [1]. Against switching: profit would fall to £310,000 if nothing else changed, a large trade-off for a haulage firm [1]. For switching: the supermarket brings £150,000 of profit, more than the £90,000 extra cost, so losing it would be worse than paying for the electric lorries [1]. Switching also cuts emissions, supporting sustainability and Lindale's reputation with other customers [1]. Fuel prices could rise, which would increase the saving from electric lorries [1]. However, the supermarket has only said it will "favour" low-emission hauliers, so Lindale might keep the contract without switching all 5 [1]. **Conclusion:** Lindale should switch, perhaps starting with fewer lorries, because the risk of losing £150,000 profit outweighs the £90,000 cost; the decision depends on how firmly the supermarket enforces its emissions target [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Evaluate rewards a conclusion that follows from the weighing. Comparing the £90,000 cost with the £150,000 at risk gives the judgement a clear basis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling every responsible choice "environmental" when it is about people, which is ethical.
- Using the new profit, not the original, as the divisor in a percentage change.
- Answering a marketing mix question without naming the element.
- Giving only the cost of acting ethically or sustainably, with no possible benefit.
- Reaching a conclusion that does not follow from the figures calculated.
