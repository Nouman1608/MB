## 1.2.1 Eukaryotic organelles: structure and function

For each organelle learn its appearance, its number of membranes and its function.

### Membranes and the nucleus

- **Cell surface membrane.** A phospholipid bilayer with proteins. In a transmission electron micrograph it shows as a thin dark-light-dark line. It is partially permeable and controls what enters and leaves the cell. Its detailed structure belongs to [Topic 4, Cell membranes and transport](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-cell-membranes-and-transport/).
- **Nucleus.** The largest organelle in most cells. It contains the DNA as chromatin. It is bounded by the **nuclear envelope**, which is two membranes with **nuclear pores** through it. The outer membrane is continuous with the rough endoplasmic reticulum.
- **Nucleolus.** A dense, darker region inside the nucleus with no membrane of its own. It makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosome subunits.

### The internal membrane system

- **Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER).** A network of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) with ribosomes on the outer surface. Proteins made on these ribosomes enter the cisternae and are transported through the cell.
- **Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER).** Membrane tubules and sacs with no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including steroids.
- **Golgi body** (Golgi apparatus or Golgi complex). A stack of curved, flattened sacs, with vesicles at the edges. Vesicles from the RER fuse with it. It modifies proteins (for example, adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), packages them into vesicles and makes lysosomes.
- **Lysosomes.** Small spherical vesicles surrounded by a single membrane. They contain hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes, kept apart from the rest of the cytoplasm. They break down worn-out organelles and material taken into the cell.

### Energy organelles

- **Mitochondria.** Surrounded by an envelope of two membranes. The inner membrane is folded into **cristae**, and the fluid inside is the **matrix**. They are the site of aerobic respiration, which makes ATP. The matrix contains **small circular DNA** and 70S ribosomes.
- **Chloroplasts** (plant cells that photosynthesise). Also surrounded by a double-membrane envelope. Inside, the stroma contains stacks of thylakoid membranes called grana, and often starch grains. They are the site of photosynthesis. Like mitochondria, they contain **small circular DNA** and 70S ribosomes.

### Ribosomes

Ribosomes are not surrounded by a membrane. Each is made of two subunits of RNA and protein, and they are the site of protein synthesis. The S number measures how fast a particle sediments in a centrifuge, so a larger S means a larger particle.

- **80S** ribosomes are in the cytoplasm, free or attached to the RER.
- **70S** ribosomes are in mitochondria and chloroplasts.

### Microtubules, centrioles, cilia and microvilli

- **Microtubules.** Long, hollow tubes made of the protein tubulin. They help form the cytoskeleton, which supports the cell and moves vesicles and organelles. They also form the spindle in cell division.
- **Centrioles.** A pair of short hollow cylinders, usually at right angles to each other, near the nucleus in animal cells. Each cylinder is a ring of nine groups of three (triplets of) microtubules. They are involved in organising the spindle. Plant cells of flowering plants have no centrioles.
- **Cilia.** Hair-like extensions of the cell surface, covered by the cell surface membrane. Inside is a ring of nine pairs of microtubules around two central microtubules (the 9 + 2 arrangement). Each cilium grows from a basal body, which has the same structure as a centriole. Cilia beat to move fluid or mucus over a surface, for example in the airways.
- **Microvilli.** Small finger-like folds of the cell surface membrane, much smaller than cilia and with no 9 + 2 core. They increase the surface area for absorption, for example on cells lining the small intestine.

### Plant cell structures

- **Cell wall.** Made mainly of cellulose, outside the cell surface membrane. It is freely permeable. It gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when it takes in water.
- **Plasmodesmata.** Narrow channels through the walls of neighbouring cells, lined by cell surface membrane. They connect the cytoplasm of one cell with the next, so substances can pass between cells.
- **Large permanent vacuole and tonoplast.** A large central space filled with cell sap. It is bounded by a partially permeable membrane called the **tonoplast**. Water taken into the vacuole pushes the cytoplasm against the wall and keeps the cell turgid.

Decide first what kind of image you have.

- **Light micrograph (photomicrograph).** Resolution is limited to about 200 nm. You can see the nucleus, cell wall, chloroplasts and large vacuole. Mitochondria may appear only as tiny dots. Ribosomes, ER and Golgi detail are not visible.
- **Transmission electron micrograph (TEM).** A thin section, in black and white. Internal membranes, cristae, ribosomes and nuclear pores are visible.
- **Scanning electron micrograph (SEM).** Shows surfaces in three dimensions, such as cilia or microvilli on the outside of a cell.

Recognition clues:

| You see | It is |
|---|---|
| Large round body, double membrane with gaps, dark patch inside | Nucleus with pores and nucleolus |
| Oval body, double membrane, inner one folded into shelves | Mitochondrion |
| Larger body, double membrane, internal stacks of membranes | Chloroplast (grana) |
| Parallel flattened sacs covered in dots | RER |
| Curved stack of sacs with vesicles budding off, no dots | Golgi body |
| Small round vesicle with uniform dark contents | Lysosome |
| Hollow cylinders in a pair, near the nucleus | Centrioles |

### Worked example 1: identifying a cell from a micrograph

**A TEM of a cell shows: a thick layer outside the cell surface membrane; no nucleus; scattered small dark dots in the cytoplasm; no structure with a double membrane. A 2 µm scale bar measures 25 mm and the cell measures 45 mm long. Identify the type of cell and calculate its length and the magnification.**

Step 1: Use the scale bar. 25 mm represents 2 µm, so 45 mm represents (45 ÷ 25) × 2 = **3.6 µm**.

Step 2: Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Convert the bar to the same unit: 25 mm = 25 000 µm. 25 000 ÷ 2 = **× 12 500**.

Step 3: Interpret. No nucleus and no double-membrane organelles means a prokaryote. A wall outside the membrane and a length within 1–5 µm fit a typical bacterium. The dark dots are 70S ribosomes.

**Answer:** a bacterium (prokaryotic cell), 3.6 µm long, magnification × 12 500.

## 1.2.3 Comparing plant and animal cells

Both are eukaryotic, so both have a nucleus, RER, SER, Golgi body, mitochondria, 80S ribosomes, lysosomes, microtubules and a cell surface membrane.

| Feature | Typical plant cell | Typical animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Present, cellulose | Absent |
| Plasmodesmata | Present | Absent |
| Chloroplasts | In photosynthesising cells | Absent |
| Vacuole | Large, permanent, with tonoplast | Small and temporary, if present |
| Centrioles | Absent (flowering plants) | Present |
| Cilia, microvilli | Absent | Present in some cells |
| Shape | Regular, fixed by wall | Variable |

### Worked example 2: a "compare" answer

**Compare the structure of a palisade mesophyll cell with a liver cell. [4]**

Give matched pairs and at least one similarity:

1. Both have a nucleus, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes (similarity).
2. The palisade cell has a cellulose cell wall; the liver cell has none.
3. The palisade cell has chloroplasts; the liver cell has none.
4. The palisade cell has a large permanent vacuole with a tonoplast; the liver cell does not.
5. The liver cell has centrioles; the palisade cell does not.

Each point names both cells. "Plant cells have a wall" on its own does not compare.

## 1.2.4 ATP from respiration

You only need to **state** this: cells use **ATP made in respiration** as the immediate energy source for energy-requiring processes. Examples include protein synthesis, active transport, cell division, muscle contraction and the movement of vesicles. This is why cells with a high energy demand, such as secretory cells, have many mitochondria. The detail of how ATP is made comes later in [Topic 12, Energy and respiration](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-energy-and-respiration/).

## 1.2.5 The typical bacterium

The syllabus lists six features of a typical prokaryotic cell:

- **unicellular**: each bacterium is one cell
- generally **1–5 µm** in diameter, much smaller than most eukaryotic cells
- a **cell wall made of peptidoglycan**, not cellulose
- **circular DNA** in the cytoplasm, not enclosed in a nucleus
- **70S ribosomes** only
- **no organelles surrounded by double membranes**: no nucleus, mitochondria or chloroplasts

## 1.2.6 Bacterium versus plant and animal cells

| Feature | Typical bacterium | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleus with envelope | No | Yes | Yes |
| Main DNA | Circular, in cytoplasm | Linear, in nucleus | Linear, in nucleus |
| Small circular DNA | It is the main DNA | In mitochondria and chloroplasts | In mitochondria |
| Ribosomes | 70S only | 80S in cytoplasm, 70S in organelles | 80S in cytoplasm, 70S in mitochondria |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan | Cellulose | None |
| Double-membrane organelles | None | Nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts | Nucleus, mitochondria |

Notice that 70S ribosomes and circular DNA are not unique to bacteria. They are also found inside mitochondria and chloroplasts. A question that asks which feature "only prokaryotes" have needs care.

## 1.2.7 Viruses

All viruses are **non-cellular**. A virus has:

- a **nucleic acid core**, either DNA or RNA
- a **capsid** made of protein around it
- in some viruses, an outer **envelope** made of phospholipids.

A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no cell surface membrane of its own. This is why section 1.2 asks whether the cell is the basic unit of life. Viruses are needed later in [Topic 10, Infectious diseases](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-infectious-diseases/).

## Common errors

- Calling the nuclear envelope a single membrane. It is two membranes.
- Saying the nucleolus is an organelle with a membrane. It has none.
- Writing that only bacteria have 70S ribosomes. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have them too.
- Mixing up cilia and microvilli. Only cilia have the 9 + 2 microtubule core and beat.
- Stating that plant cells have no mitochondria because they have chloroplasts. They have both.
- Writing "the vacuole membrane" instead of "tonoplast" when the name is asked for.
- Saying a virus has a cell wall or a cell membrane. The envelope is phospholipid, but the virus is not a cell.
- Labelling a lysosome as a ribosome. Ribosomes are much smaller and have no membrane.
