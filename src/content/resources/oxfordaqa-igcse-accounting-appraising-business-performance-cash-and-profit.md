This guide is aligned to the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification, version 1.2, for exams from May/June 2026 onwards. It covers the part of specification section 3.5 (topic 5, Interpretation, analysis and communication of financial information) on appraising business performance with financial statements and ratios, and on the difference between cash and profit. The specification says content 3 to 5 is assessed in Paper 2. All amounts on this page are in dollars.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification point | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Calculation and interpretation of financial measures and ratios | Work out the nine measures listed below, then say what each result tells you |
| Appraising business performance using financial statements and ratios | Judge profitability and liquidity, using the income statement and statement of financial position |
| The difference between cash and profits | Explain why a business can make a profit yet run short of cash, and the reverse |
| The effect of transactions on profitability and liquidity | Say how a given transaction changes profit, cash and the liquidity ratios |

The specification lists these measures: gross profit margin %, markup %, rate of inventory turnover, profit in relation to revenue %, return on capital employed %, current ratio, liquid capital ratio, trade receivable days and trade payable days. It says the formulae are in OxfordAQA's guide "Formulae for accounting ratios" and that you are expected to know the formulae and use the results to inform judgements. The forms below are the standard ones. Check them against that guide before the exam, because it decides the exact version.

## The measures and what they show

Profitability measures

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin % | gross profit / revenue x 100 | Gross profit earned from each 100 of revenue |
| Markup % | gross profit / cost of sales x 100 | Gross profit added on top of cost |
| Profit in relation to revenue % | profit for the year / revenue x 100 | How much of each 100 of revenue is left after all expenses |
| Return on capital employed % | profit from operations / capital employed x 100 | Profit earned from the money invested in the business |
| Rate of inventory turnover | cost of sales / average inventory (times) | How often inventory is sold and replaced in a year |

ROCE uses profit from operations (before loan interest), as OxfordAQA's own 9215 mark scheme does (Paper 2, May/June 2026). Where there is no loan interest, as in the examples on this page, profit from operations and profit for the year are the same figure.

Capital employed is non-current assets plus current assets minus current liabilities. It also equals capital plus non-current liabilities. Average inventory is (opening inventory + closing inventory) / 2. If only closing inventory is given, use that and say so.

Liquidity and efficiency measures

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | current assets : current liabilities | Ability to meet short-term debts |
| Liquid capital ratio | (current assets - inventory) : current liabilities | The same test, without inventory, which may be slow to turn into cash |
| Trade receivable days | trade receivables / credit sales x 365 | Average days customers take to pay |
| Trade payable days | trade payables / credit purchases x 365 | Average days the business takes to pay suppliers |

Give margins and ROCE as percentages, turnover as "times", days as a number of days, and the two liquidity ratios as "x : 1" or a decimal. Always show the formula line first.

## Worked example 1: two years at Kesterwick Outdoor Supplies

Kesterwick Outdoor Supplies is a sole trader. The figures for the years ended 31 December are below. All purchases and 75% to 80% of sales are on credit, and the credit sales and credit purchases are given.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 480,000 | 560,000 |
| Cost of sales | 300,000 | 364,000 |
| Expenses | 120,000 | 140,000 |
| Opening inventory | 34,000 | 38,000 |
| Closing inventory | 38,000 | 56,000 |
| Credit sales | 360,000 | 448,000 |
| Credit purchases | 304,000 | 382,000 |
| Trade receivables | 38,000 | 72,000 |
| Trade payables | 38,000 | 70,000 |
| Bank (overdraft in 2026) | 12,000 | (9,000) |
| Non-current assets (net) | 250,000 | 262,000 |

Step 1: profit. Gross profit is 180,000 in 2025 and 196,000 in 2026. Profit for the year is 60,000 and 56,000; with no loan interest, these are also the profits from operations used for ROCE.

Step 2: current assets and liabilities. In 2025, current assets are 38,000 + 38,000 + 12,000 = 88,000 and current liabilities are 38,000. In 2026, current assets are 56,000 + 72,000 = 128,000 and current liabilities are 70,000 + 9,000 overdraft = 79,000. Capital employed is 250,000 + 88,000 - 38,000 = 300,000 in 2025 and 262,000 + 128,000 - 79,000 = 311,000 in 2026.

Step 3: the ratios.

| Measure | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 37.5% | 35.0% |
| Markup | 60.0% | 53.8% |
| Profit in relation to revenue | 12.5% | 10.0% |
| Return on capital employed | 20.0% | 18.0% |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 8.33 times | 7.74 times |
| Current ratio | 2.32 : 1 | 1.62 : 1 |
| Liquid capital ratio | 1.32 : 1 | 0.91 : 1 |
| Trade receivable days | 38.5 days | 58.7 days |
| Trade payable days | 45.6 days | 66.9 days |

The turnover uses average inventory of 36,000 and 47,000. Receivable days use credit sales, for example 72,000 / 448,000 x 365 = 58.7.

## How to write an appraisal

A list of ratios earns little. Appraisal means using the figures to reach a judgement. Work in this order.

1. State the result with its direction, for example "gross profit margin fell from 37.5% to 35.0%".
2. Compare it with something: the previous year, another business, or a target. A single ratio on its own means little.
3. Give a reason that fits the facts in the question, for example "cost of sales rose faster than revenue, so either suppliers charged more or prices were cut".
4. Say whether this is good or bad for the business and who it matters to.
5. Finish with a judgement or a recommendation.

Appraisal of Kesterwick, profitability. Revenue rose by 16.7% but profit fell by 6.7%, from 60,000 to 56,000. Gross profit margin fell by 2.5 percentage points, so the extra sales were earned at a lower margin, possibly through discounts. Expenses also rose faster than the gross profit gain, so profit in relation to revenue fell from 12.5% to 10.0%. ROCE fell from 20.0% to 18.0%, so each 100 invested earned less. Profitability weakened even though the business grew.

Appraisal of Kesterwick, liquidity. The current ratio fell from 2.32 : 1 to 1.62 : 1 and the liquid capital ratio from 1.32 : 1 to 0.91 : 1. A liquid ratio below 1 means that, without selling inventory, the business cannot cover its current liabilities. Receivable days rose from 38.5 to 58.7 while payable days rose from 45.6 to 66.9. Customers are paying slower, and Kesterwick is paying its own suppliers slower to cope, which risks supplier goodwill. Inventory turnover also slowed, so more cash is tied up in inventory. A sensible recommendation is to chase customers harder, offer a discount for early payment and reduce inventory levels.

## Cash and profit are different

Profit is revenue minus expenses for a period. It follows the accruals approach, so a sale is counted when it is made, not when the money arrives. Cash is the money actually received and paid, including the bank balance. Liquidity is the ability to pay debts as they fall due, which depends on cash and on assets that turn into cash quickly.

| Item | Effect on profit | Effect on cash |
|---|---|---|
| Sale on credit | Increases profit now | No cash until the customer pays |
| Depreciation | Reduces profit | No cash leaves; it is not a payment |
| Buying a non-current asset | No effect at purchase (depreciation later) | Cash falls at once |
| Drawings by the owner | None (not an expense) | Cash falls |
| Loan received | None (a liability) | Cash rises |
| Repaying a loan | None | Cash falls |
| Extra capital paid in | None | Cash rises |
| Accrued expense | Reduces profit | No cash paid yet |
| Prepaid expense | Not charged this year | Cash already paid |
| Buying inventory | No effect until sold | Cash falls, or payables rise if on credit |

So a profitable business can have no cash, for example when profit is tied up in receivables and inventory or spent on non-current assets and drawings. A business making a loss can have cash if it has just taken a loan or sold a non-current asset.

## Worked example 2: effect of transactions on liquidity

Dalloway Print Studio starts with inventory 20,000, trade receivables 25,000, bank 15,000 and trade payables 30,000. Current assets are 60,000, so the current ratio is 2.00 : 1 and the liquid capital ratio is (60,000 - 20,000) / 30,000 = 1.33 : 1. Treat each transaction separately from this starting point.

| Transaction | Effect on profit | Cash | Current ratio | Liquid ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buys a machine for 9,000 from the bank | None | -9,000 | 1.70 : 1 | 1.03 : 1 |
| A customer pays 10,000 owed | None | +10,000 | 2.00 : 1 | 1.33 : 1 |
| Buys 12,000 of inventory on credit | None | None | 1.71 : 1 | 0.95 : 1 |
| Charges 5,000 depreciation | -5,000 | None | 2.00 : 1 | 1.33 : 1 |
| Sells goods on credit for 16,000, cost 10,000 | +6,000 | None | 2.20 : 1 | 1.87 : 1 |
| Owner takes 4,000 drawings from the bank | None | -4,000 | 1.87 : 1 | 1.20 : 1 |
| Pays suppliers 6,000 from the bank | None | -6,000 | 2.25 : 1 | 1.42 : 1 |

Two lessons. First, the last row shows the current ratio rising while cash falls, because paying a liability shrinks both sides when the ratio is above 1. A better ratio does not mean more cash. Second, depreciation changes profit and the statement of financial position, but not cash or the current ratio, because non-current assets are not current assets.

## Worked example 3: why profit and cash moved apart

Return to Kesterwick in 2026. Profit was 56,000, yet the bank went from 12,000 in credit to a 9,000 overdraft, a fall of 21,000. Expenses include 14,000 depreciation, non-current assets cost 26,000 to buy (250,000 + 26,000 - 14,000 = 262,000) and drawings were 45,000.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Profit for the year | 56,000 |
| Add back depreciation (not a cash payment) | 14,000 |
| Less increase in inventory (56,000 - 38,000) | (18,000) |
| Less increase in trade receivables (72,000 - 38,000) | (34,000) |
| Add increase in trade payables (70,000 - 38,000) | 32,000 |
| Cash from trading | 50,000 |
| Less non-current assets bought | (26,000) |
| Less drawings | (45,000) |
| Fall in bank balance | (21,000) |

This table is an explanation tool, not a statement the specification asks you to prepare. The point is that profit of 56,000 was absorbed by growth in receivables and inventory, new equipment and drawings.

## Common errors

- Writing the ratio with no formula or no unit, so a correct method earns less.
- Using revenue instead of credit sales for receivable days, or cost of sales for payable days when the question gives credit purchases.
- Dividing markup by revenue instead of cost of sales.
- Treating drawings or depreciation as if they change cash and profit in the same way.
- Saying a rising current ratio always means better liquidity.
- Describing the ratio without a reason or a judgement.
- Comparing two ratios from different years when the basis changed, such as average inventory in one year and closing inventory in the other.
