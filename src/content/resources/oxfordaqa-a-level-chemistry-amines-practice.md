This set tests section 3.3.11, Amines, of the OxfordAQA 9620 specification (International AS and A-level Chemistry, Version 5.3), used for exams from the May/June 2020 series onwards, across subsections 3.3.11.1 to 3.3.11.3 (preparation, base properties, nucleophilic properties). As International A2 material, it is assessed at **International A-level only**: Unit 4 examines 3.3.7 to 3.3.16, while Unit 5 (synoptic) may test anything. Question 3 also refers to nitration (section 3.3.10.2), and question 11 uses substitution by cyanide ions (section 3.3.3).

Before starting, work through the [amines study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amines/) and the [revision notes on amines](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amines-revision-notes/). Planning tools: the OxfordAQA chemistry [hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/chemistry/), a [checklist you can print](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/chemistry/) and our [diagnostics](/diagnostics/).

**Ar values to use:** C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0 and Cl = 35.5.

## Questions

**1.** State whether each of these amines is primary, secondary or tertiary.
(i) (CH₃)₂CHCH₂NHCH₃ (ii) C₆H₅N(CH₃)₂ (iii) CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂NH₂ **[3]**

**2.** Pentanedinitrile, NC–CH₂CH₂CH₂–CN, is fully reduced to pentane-1,5-diamine. Name a reagent that can carry out this reduction, then write its equation with [H] as the reducing agent. **[3]**

**3.** 1-Chloro-3-nitrobenzene, ClC₆H₄NO₂, is made by nitration and then reduced to 3-chlorophenylamine, ClC₆H₄NH₂, using tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reduction, using [H]. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why sodium hydroxide is added once the reduction is complete. **[2]**
**(c)** 11.03 g of 1-chloro-3-nitrobenzene gave 6.43 g of 3-chlorophenylamine. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**4.** Benzylamine, C₆H₅CH₂NH₂, and phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂, both contain a benzene ring.

**(a)** Explain why benzylamine is the stronger base. **[4]**
**(b)** State where ammonia fits in base strength relative to these two amines. **[1]**

**5.** 3-Methylbutan-1-amine, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂NH₂, dissolves in water.

**(a)** Give the equation for its reaction with water and explain the term weak base for this amine. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the equation for its complete reaction with sulfuric acid. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid that reacts exactly with 0.435 g of 3-methylbutan-1-amine. **[3]**

**6.** Methylamine reacts with 2-bromopropane to give the secondary amine N-methylpropan-2-amine, (CH₃)₂CHNHCH₃.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism. Describe each curly arrow and give the structure of the intermediate. **[4]**
**(b)** Give the structural formula of the tertiary amine formed if the product reacts with more 2-bromopropane. **[1]**

**7.** Phenylamine is heated with a large excess of iodomethane.

**(a)** Give the formulae of the three nitrogen-containing products formed in turn. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the third product does not react with more iodomethane. **[1]**
**(c)** State how the conditions should change to make the first product the main one. **[1]**

**8.** N,N-Dimethyldodecylamine, CH₃(CH₂)₁₁N(CH₃)₂, reacts with chloromethane.

**(a)** Give the formula of the organic product. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the product can act as a cationic surfactant. **[2]**
**(c)** State one use of quaternary ammonium salts of this type. **[1]**

**9.** Benzoyl chloride, C₆H₅COCl, reacts with excess propan-1-amine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism, saying where each curly arrow starts and ends and showing the intermediate. **[4]**
**(b)** Give the name of the amide formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Write an equation for the reaction of cyclohexanecarbonyl chloride, C₆H₁₁COCl, with excess ammonia. **[2]**

**10.** 3-Chlorophenylamine, ClC₆H₄NH₂, reacts with ethanoic anhydride in a 1 : 1 ratio.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** 5.10 g of 3-chlorophenylamine gave 5.42 g of the amide, C₈H₈ClNO. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**11.** A chemist needs cyclopentylmethanamine, C₅H₉CH₂NH₂, and has bromocyclopentane, C₅H₉Br.

**(a)** Give a two-step route. For each step give the reagent and conditions, name the intermediate, and write an equation for step 2 using [H]. **[4]**
**(b)** A colleague suggests heating bromocyclopentane with excess ammonia instead. Give two reasons why this would not give a pure sample of the target. **[2]**
**(c)** A 0.500 g sample of the product, impure, was dissolved in water and titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. The impurities do not react with acid. The titre was 48.60 cm³.

(i) Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
(ii) Calculate the percentage by mass of cyclopentylmethanamine in the sample. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) **secondary** [1]; (ii) **tertiary** [1]; (iii) **primary** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In (iii) only one carbon is bonded to N, even though that carbon carries three others.

**2.** H₂ with a nickel catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether [1]; NC(CH₂)₃CN + 8[H] → **H₂N(CH₂)₅NH₂**: correct diamine [1]; 8[H] [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two nitrile groups need twice the hydrogen; an equation balanced for one group only is unbalanced, so count H atoms on both sides before moving on.

**3. (a)** ClC₆H₄NO₂ + 6[H] → ClC₆H₄NH₂ + 2H₂O [1]
**(b)** In the acid, the amine is protonated and is present as its salt, ClC₆H₄NH₃⁺ [1]; NaOH removes H⁺ from the –NH₃⁺ group and releases the free amine [1].
**(c)** n(ClC₆H₄NO₂) = 11.03 ÷ 157.5 = 0.0700 mol [1]; theoretical mass = 0.0700 × 127.5 = 8.93 g [1]; yield = 6.43 ÷ 8.93 × 100 = **72.0%** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both Mr values; a percentage worked from masses alone (6.43 ÷ 11.03) skips the mole step and is not a valid method.

**4. (a)** Both bases accept H⁺ using the lone pair on N [1]. In benzylamine, N is bonded to a CH₂ group, not the ring, so its lone pair is not delocalised and the alkyl carbon releases electrons onto N [1]. In phenylamine, N is bonded directly to the ring and its lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring, reducing electron density on N [1]. So the lone pair is more available in benzylamine, which accepts H⁺ more readily [1].
**(b)** **Benzylamine > ammonia > phenylamine** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The key fact is that the CH₂ group separates N from the ring; an answer that treats both as aromatic amines cannot explain the difference.

**5. (a)** (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ [1]; only a small proportion is protonated / the equilibrium lies to the left [1].
**(b)** 2(CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂SO₄ → ((CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂NH₃)₂SO₄ [1]
**(c)** n(amine) = 0.435 ÷ 87.0 = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(H₂SO₄) = 5.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; V = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0500 = 0.0500 dm³ = **50.0 cm³** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Sulfuric acid has two protons to give, so it reacts with two amine molecules; using 1 : 1 doubles the volume to 100 cm³.

**6. (a)** Methylamine's N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon carrying Br [1]; the C–Br bonding pair moves onto Br, so bromide is expelled [1]; intermediate (CH₃)₂CH–N⁺H₂CH₃, with the positive charge on N [1]; a second methylamine uses its lone pair to remove H⁺ from N⁺, and the N–H electrons return to N [1].
**(b)** **((CH₃)₂CH)₂NCH₃** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Begin the first arrow at the lone pair itself, not at the N symbol or a hydrogen, so the electron pair donor is clear.

**7. (a)** C₆H₅NHCH₃ [1]; C₆H₅N(CH₃)₂ [1]; **C₆H₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ I⁻** [1]
**(b)** In C₆H₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ the nitrogen has no lone pair left, so it cannot act as a nucleophile [1].
**(c)** Use a large excess of phenylamine (not of iodomethane) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the quaternary product as a salt with its iodide ion; a neutral formula with five bonds to N is not a sensible structure.

**8. (a)** **CH₃(CH₂)₁₁N⁺(CH₃)₃ Cl⁻** [1]
**(b)** The N⁺ head is positively charged and interacts with water [1]; the long hydrocarbon tail has no charge and dissolves in grease or oil, so the ion collects at the oil–water boundary [1].
**(c)** In fabric softeners or hair conditioners [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Cationic" refers to the charge on the head group, so name the positive N⁺; describing only the chain gives half the explanation.

**9. (a)** Arrow from the lone pair on N of propan-1-amine to the δ+ carbonyl carbon, and arrow from the C=O bond to O [1]; tetrahedral intermediate with O⁻ and N⁺H₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ on the same carbon [1]; the O⁻ lone pair re-forms C=O while the C–Cl bonding pair moves onto Cl, expelling chloride [1]; chloride or another propan-1-amine molecule takes H⁺ off N⁺ [1].
**(b)** **N-propylbenzamide** [1]
**(c)** C₆H₁₁COCl + 2NH₃ → **C₆H₁₁CONH₂** [1] + NH₄Cl, with 2NH₃ on the left [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A direct one-step swap of Cl for N describes substitution, not addition–elimination; the O⁻ intermediate must appear.

**10. (a)** (CH₃CO)₂O + ClC₆H₄NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₆H₄Cl [1] + CH₃COOH [1]
**(b)** n(amine) = 5.10 ÷ 127.5 = 0.0400 mol [1]; Mr(C₈H₈ClNO) = 169.5, so theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 169.5 = 6.78 g [1]; yield = 5.42 ÷ 6.78 × 100 = **79.9%** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The by-product here is ethanoic acid; writing HCl, as for an acyl chloride, is wrong chemistry for an anhydride.

**11. (a)** Step 1: KCN in ethanol and water, heat under reflux [1]; intermediate **cyclopentanecarbonitrile**, C₅H₉CN [1]. Step 2: H₂ with a nickel catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether [1]; C₅H₉CN + 4[H] → C₅H₉CH₂NH₂ [1].
**(b)** Ammonia puts N straight onto the ring carbon, giving cyclopentylamine, C₅H₉NH₂, which has one carbon fewer than the target [1]; the amine formed can react further with bromocyclopentane, giving a mixture of secondary and tertiary amines and a quaternary salt [1].
**(c)** (i) C₅H₉CH₂NH₂ + HCl → C₅H₉CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1]
(ii) n(HCl) = 0.100 × 48.60 ÷ 1000 = 4.86 × 10⁻³ mol = n(amine) [1]; mass = 4.86 × 10⁻³ × 99.0 = 0.481 g [1]; percentage = 0.481 ÷ 0.500 × 100 = **96.2%** [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "it gives a mixture" is only one of the two reasons; the wrong carbon count is a separate point and needs its own sentence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying an amine by the carbon next to N rather than by the carbons on N.
- Treating benzylamine-type compounds as aromatic amines.
- Unbalanced [H] equations, especially for molecules with two nitrile or nitro groups.
- Arrows that start at an atom rather than at its lone pair or a bond.
- No + charge on N in the intermediate, or no final H⁺ removal.
- An addition–elimination mechanism drawn as one step with no O⁻.
- HCl given as a by-product of an acid anhydride reaction.
- Dropping the N- from N-substituted amide names.
