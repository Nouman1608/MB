## Source evaluation — the skill the paper is built on

Assess every source using **NOP**:

- **Nature** — what kind of source is it? A diary, a speech, a cartoon, a photograph?
- **Origin** — who produced it, and when?
- **Purpose** — *why* was it produced? To inform, persuade, justify, record?

**A biased source is not a useless source.** Students routinely write "this source is biased, so it is not useful" — which inverts the skill. A propaganda poster is highly useful **as evidence of what its producers wanted people to believe**, even though it is a poor guide to what actually happened.

**Usefulness always depends on the question asked.** The same source may be unreliable for establishing facts and extremely valuable for revealing attitudes. Saying which is what earns the mark.

**For "how far do the sources agree" questions:** find genuine points of agreement and disagreement, explain each with reference to NOP, then reach a judgement about the extent.

## Causation

Examiners reward **weighing**, not listing.

The structure that scores:

1. Explain how each factor contributed — not just that it did.
2. **Link factors** where they reinforce each other.
3. Judge which mattered most, and **state the criterion** for the judgement.

**Distinguish the trigger from the underlying causes.** The immediate event starts things, but only because the conditions made escalation possible. "The spark is not the cause" applies across the depth studies.

## Recurring themes across the depth studies

**Treaties and their consequences** — the Treaty of Versailles was **harsh enough to embitter Germany but not harsh enough to keep her weak**. That single assessment answers most Versailles evaluation questions.

**Why international organisations fail** — the League of Nations failed for **structural** reasons, not one incidental one: the USA never joined, it had no army, decisions required unanimity, key powers were excluded, sanctions were weak, and Britain and France pursued their own interests. Naming several structural causes beats naming one.

**Dictatorship and control** — the mechanisms recur across topics: propaganda, censorship, a secret police, youth organisations, control of education, elimination of opposition, and economic policy used to buy consent. Being able to apply that framework to any regime is more useful than memorising one.

**Appeasement** — argue both sides. *For:* Britain was militarily unprepared, public opinion feared war, Versailles was widely seen as unjust, and the USSR seemed the greater threat. *Against:* it emboldened Hitler, sacrificed Czechoslovakia, and let Germany grow stronger relatively.

**Cold War origins** — ideological opposition, wartime suspicion, the atomic bomb, and disagreements at Yalta and Potsdam over Poland and Germany.

## Writing the answers

- Use **precise** evidence: names, dates, figures, treaties. "Several wars" is weak; "three wars between 1864 and 1871" is strong.
- Every paragraph should make a point, support it, and **link back to the question**.
- Conclusions must **judge**, not summarise — and say what the judgement rests on.
- Answer the question set, not the one you revised for.

## Exam traps

- Dismissing a source as useless because it is biased.
- Describing a source instead of evaluating it.
- Listing causes without explaining or weighing them.
- Narrating events rather than arguing a case.
- Vague evidence with no dates or names.
- A conclusion that restates rather than judges.

## Self-test

1. What does NOP stand for, and why does purpose matter most?
2. Why is a biased source still useful?
3. What structure earns marks in a causation question?
4. Summarise the central problem with the Treaty of Versailles.
5. Give four structural reasons the League of Nations failed.

**Answers:** 1. Nature, Origin, Purpose; purpose matters most because knowing *why* a source was created reveals what it is likely to exaggerate, omit or distort. 2. It is reliable evidence of the attitudes, intentions and beliefs of the person or group who produced it, even if unreliable about events. 3. Explain how each factor contributed, link factors together, then judge which mattered most and state the criterion for that judgement. 4. It was harsh enough to embitter Germany but not harsh enough to prevent her recovering strength. 5. The USA never joined; it had no army; decisions required unanimity; key powers were excluded; sanctions were ineffective; Britain and France pursued self-interest — any four.
