These questions cover 3.5.2 Social control, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 2: formal and informal methods of social control, unwritten rules and sanctions, interactionist, functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives, and Heidensohn on female conformity. All people and places in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [social control study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-control/) and the [social control revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-social-control-revision-notes/). For questions across Paper 2, try the [crime and stratification practice questions](/resources/gcse-sociology-crime-stratification-practice/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is an example of informal social control?

A. A magistrate imposing a fine
B. A police officer issuing a caution
C. Friends ignoring someone who lied to them
D. A head teacher excluding a pupil

**[1]**

**2.** Which theory did Heidensohn use to explain female conformity?

A. Anomie theory
B. Control theory
C. Labelling theory
D. Status frustration theory

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "sanctions". **[2]**

**4.** Identify one formal and one informal agent of social control. **[2]**

**5.** Describe one example of an unwritten rule and explain how it is enforced. **[3]**

**6.** Describe one way in which, according to Heidensohn, women are controlled at work. **[2]**

**7.** Identify and explain one way in which daughters are more closely controlled than sons, according to Heidensohn. **[3]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Read the item.

> Kayla, 15, lives in Northfold (a fictional town). Her parents insist she is home by 8pm, while her older brother can stay out until 11pm. When Kayla went to a party without asking, her parents took away her phone, and girls at school spread rumours about her. Her brother was praised by friends for "getting away with" a similar party.

**(a)** Identify one negative informal sanction in the item. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the item and Heidensohn's ideas, explain why girls may commit fewer crimes than boys. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the difference between how functionalists and Marxists view formal social control. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how an interactionist would view the work of agents of formal social control. **[3]**

**11.** Explain one similarity and one difference between Heidensohn and Carlen. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that informal social control is more effective than formal social control. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that social control mainly benefits society as a whole. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Friends ignoring someone who lied to them [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The other three options are all applied by official agents using written rules.

**2.** B. Control theory [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Anomie belongs to Merton and status frustration to Albert Cohen.

**3.** Rewards or punishments [1] used to encourage people to conform to norms or laws and to discourage deviance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include both rewards and punishments; many answers give only punishments.

**4.** Formal: for example, the police, courts or prison service [1]. Informal: for example, the family, peer group or religion [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "The government" is acceptable as formal; "society" is too vague for either.

**5.** For example, people are expected to queue in a shop rather than push to the front [1]. The rule is not written down but is widely known [1]. Someone who pushes in may face negative informal sanctions, such as tutting, comments or being sent to the back [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the enforcement: an informal sanction.

**6.** Women are controlled by male-dominated hierarchies or workers' organisations [1], or by sexual harassment that intimidates them and limits their behaviour [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Either point can be the first mark; the second needs development of how it controls women.

**7.** Daughters have more limits on when they may leave the home [1], so they spend more time in the house under parental supervision [1], which gives them fewer opportunities to commit crime than sons [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Heidensohn also notes daughters are expected to do more domestic tasks; this is credited as the identified point if explained.

**8. (a)** Any one: her phone was taken away; girls spread rumours about her [1].
**(b)** Heidensohn argues daughters are more closely controlled than sons [1], shown by Kayla's 8pm curfew compared with her brother's 11pm [1]. Kayla's reputation was damaged by rumours, showing the fear of losing reputation that controls girls' behaviour [1]. Her brother was praised for similar behaviour, so boys face weaker control and more opportunity to offend, which helps explain girls' lower crime rate [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare Kayla with her brother; the contrast is the evidence of patriarchal control.

**9.** Functionalists see formal social control as maintaining value consensus and social order [1], so the police and courts protect everyone and benefit society as a whole [1]. Marxists see formal social control as serving the ruling class [1], for example laws protecting private property and the working class being policed more heavily [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The difference is whose interests are served; make that contrast explicit.

**10.** Interactionists focus on how agents such as the police use discretion in face-to-face situations [1]. Some groups, such as young working-class males, may be stopped or labelled more readily than others [1]. Labelling can then lead to a master status and further deviance, so control can create deviance rather than only preventing it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking to Becker's labelling theory shows you can connect 3.5.1 and 3.5.2.

**11.** Similarity: both are feminists who use control theory [1], seeing women's behaviour as shaped by the controls and rewards of a patriarchal society [1]. Difference: Heidensohn explains why most women conform, because patriarchal control is so effective [1], whereas Carlen explains why some working-class women offend, because the rewards of the class and gender deals fail [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Learn this contrast; it appears in different forms across Paper 2.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Informal control uses unwritten rules and everyday relationships; formal control uses written rules and official agents [1].
- Most people conform because they have internalised norms through socialisation [1].
- Informal sanctions such as disapproval or exclusion from friends work constantly, not just when someone is caught [1].
- Functionalists see socialisation into shared values as the main basis of social order [1].
- Heidensohn shows informal control in the home and in public is very effective at limiting women's behaviour [1].
- Informal control is cheap and present in every setting, unlike police patrols [1].
- Against: formal control deals with serious crimes where informal control has failed [1].
- Formal sanctions such as prison can remove offenders and deter others [1].
- Informal control is weaker in large, anonymous cities, where people may not know their neighbours [1].
- Marxists would argue formal control matters because it protects the property of the ruling class [1].
- Interactionists argue formal control can label people and create further deviance, so it may be counterproductive [1].
- Judgement: informal control explains everyday conformity for most people, while formal control is a backstop for when it fails, so informal control is more effective in most situations [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Define both kinds of control early, then compare them directly rather than describing each in turn.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Functionalists argue control maintains value consensus and order, benefiting everyone [1].
- Sanctions reinforce shared norms and boundaries of acceptable behaviour [1].
- Formal control protects all citizens from crimes such as violence and theft [1].
- Against: Marxists argue control mainly benefits the ruling class [1].
- Laws protect private property, which most benefits those who own the most [1].
- Informal control through media and education encourages acceptance of inequality [1].
- Feminists argue control mainly benefits men by keeping women in subordinate roles [1].
- Heidensohn: women are controlled at home, in public and at work [1].
- Daughters' closer supervision shows control is applied unequally by gender [1].
- Interactionists argue agents of control label some groups more readily, so control is applied unequally [1].
- Evaluation: Heidensohn generalises to all women and her claims are not always backed by strong research evidence [1].
- Judgement: control does provide order that benefits most people, but it is applied unequally by class and gender, so it does not benefit everyone equally [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "Society as a whole" is the functionalist phrase; build your argument around testing it against Marxist, feminist and interactionist views.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving an agent without a sanction, or a sanction without an agent.
- Forgetting positive sanctions.
- Writing that informal control is "less serious" rather than explaining unwritten rules.
- Confusing Heidensohn with Carlen.
- Leaving out the interactionist perspective.
- Not using item details, such as the curfew comparison in Question 8.
