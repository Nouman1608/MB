## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "revenue expenditure". **[2]**

**2.** State whether each item is capital expenditure or revenue expenditure.

**(a)** Carriage paid on a new machine **[1]**

**(b)** Repairs to the roof of the existing warehouse **[1]**

**(c)** Fitting a security system to newly bought premises **[1]**

**(d)** Servicing the delivery van **[1]**

**3.** State whether each item is a capital receipt or a revenue receipt.

**(a)** Proceeds from selling an old office computer **[1]**

**(b)** Rent received from subletting part of the shop **[1]**

**(c)** Additional capital paid in by the owner **[1]**

**(d)** Discount received from a supplier **[1]**

**4.** A furniture shop buys sofas to sell to customers. Explain why this is revenue expenditure even though a sofa lasts for many years. **[2]**

**5.** The owner of a business brings her own car, valued at $7,000, into the business for business use. Prepare the journal entry to record this. A narrative is not required. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why treating capital expenditure as revenue expenditure understates profit for the year. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Liam's draft profit for the year ended 30 June 2028 was $28,400. He depreciates equipment at 20% per year on the cost of equipment held at the year end. The draft net book value of equipment was $14,800. These items were then discovered:

1. A new printer costing $1,500 was debited to office expenses.

2. Repairs to equipment of $800 were debited to the equipment account and depreciation was charged on them.

3. A loan of $6,000 from a finance company was credited to revenue.

4. Commission received of $350 was credited to Liam's capital account.

5. A delivery charge of $120 on the new printer was debited to carriage inwards.

**(a)** For each item, state the effect of the error on the profit for the year and on the net book value of equipment. Give the direction and the amount. **[10]**

**(b)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the corrected net book value of equipment. **[2]**

**8.** Sana bought premises for $120,000 by bank transfer on 1 April 2028. She also paid, by bank transfer, legal fees of $3,400 on the purchase, $6,800 for building a new loading bay, and $900 for replacing broken windows.

**(a)** Calculate the total capital expenditure. **[2]**

**(b)** Prepare journal entries to record the legal fees and the window replacement. Narratives are not required. **[4]**

**(c)** Premises are not depreciated. Explain the effect on profit if the loading bay had been debited to repairs. **[2]**

**9.** During the year Fiona's business received the following amounts:

| Receipt | $ |
|---|---|
| Sales | 84,000 |
| Loan from bank | 10,000 |
| Sale of old van (net book value $3,100) | 3,500 |
| Rent received | 2,400 |
| Additional capital from Fiona | 5,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the total of revenue receipts. **[2]**

**(b)** State the amount that will appear in the statement of profit or loss for the sale of the van, and describe it. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the total cash received is not the same as the income shown in the statement of profit or loss. **[2]**

**10.** Hassan has spent $4,000 on major repairs to his delivery van. He wants to debit the amount to the motor vehicles account so that his profit looks higher when he applies for a bank loan. Motor vehicles are depreciated at 25% per year on cost.

**(a)** Calculate the effect on Hassan's profit for the year if the repairs are capitalised and depreciated. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why the repairs should be treated as revenue expenditure. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Spending on the day-to-day running of the business [1], including maintaining non-current assets in their existing condition, whose benefit is used up within the period [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Capital [1]

**(b)** Revenue [1]

**(c)** Capital [1]

**(d)** Revenue [1] **[4]**

**3.** **(a)** Capital receipt [1]

**(b)** Revenue receipt [1]

**(c)** Capital receipt [1]

**(d)** Revenue receipt [1] **[4]**

**4.** The sofas are bought to be resold as part of normal trading, so they are purchases (inventory) [1]. They are not kept for use in the business, so they are not non-current assets [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Dr Motor vehicles $7,000 [1], Cr Capital $7,000 [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The full cost is charged as an expense in one year [1], although the asset benefits several years, so only a depreciation charge should have been made this year; expenses are too high and profit too low [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)**

| Item | Profit for the year | Net book value of equipment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Understated by $1,500 − $300 = $1,200 [1] | Understated by $1,200 [1] |
| 2 | Overstated by $800 − $160 = $640 [1] | Overstated by $640 [1] |
| 3 | Overstated by $6,000 [1] | No effect [1] |
| 4 | Understated by $350 [1] | No effect [1] |
| 5 | Understated by $120 − $24 = $96 [1] | Understated by $96 [1] |

**(b)** $28,400 + $1,200 + $350 + $96 [1] − $640 − $6,000 [1] = **$23,406** [1].

**(c)** $14,800 + $1,200 + $96 − $640 [1] = **$15,456** [1]. **[15]**

**8.** **(a)** $120,000 + $3,400 + $6,800 [1] = **$130,200** [1].

**(b)**

```
                                     Debit $   Credit $
Premises                              3,400
  Bank                                            3,400
Repairs (premises repairs)              900
  Bank                                              900
```

Legal fees: Dr Premises [1], Cr Bank $3,400 [1]. Windows: Dr Repairs [1], Cr Bank $900 [1].

**(c)** Expenses would include $6,800 that should be capitalised [1], so profit for the year would be understated by $6,800, and premises would also be understated by $6,800 [1]. **[8]**

**9.** **(a)** $84,000 + $2,400 [1] = **$86,400** [1].

**(b)** A profit on disposal of $400 ($3,500 − $3,100) shown as other income [1].

**(c)** Cash received includes capital receipts (the loan, the additional capital and the van proceeds) [1], which are not income; of the van proceeds, only the $400 profit on disposal is income [1]. **[5]**

**10.** **(a)** Depreciation charged would be 25% × $4,000 = $1,000, so expenses fall by $4,000 − $1,000 [1] and profit is overstated by **$3,000** [1].

**(b)** Repairs keep the van in its existing condition and give no benefit beyond the period, so matching requires them to be charged against this year's revenue [1]; capitalising them would overstate both profit and the value of the van [1]. The bank relies on the statements to decide whether Hassan can repay a loan [1], so overstated figures would mislead it and could lead to a loan he cannot afford [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating delivery or installation of a new asset as an expense.
- Giving the effect without the direction (overstated or understated).
- Forgetting the depreciation adjustment when an asset item is expensed or a repair is capitalised.
- Treating the whole sale proceeds of a non-current asset as income.
- Saying a loan affects assets rather than liabilities.
