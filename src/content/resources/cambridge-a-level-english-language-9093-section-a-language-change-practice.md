## Section A

**2.** Define amelioration and pejoration, giving **one** example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between derivation and inflection, with an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what the theory of lexical gaps and substratum theory each propose about the causes of language change. **[4]**

**5.** Explain the difference between the tree model and the wave model of language change. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) **Blending** (video + blog) [1]. (b) **Acronym** (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation) [1]. (c) **Backformation** [1]. (d) **Compounding** [1]. (e) **Conversion**, or semantic change of an existing word into a new verb sense [1].

**2.** **Amelioration**: a word's meaning becomes more positive over time [1], e.g. "nice", once meaning foolish, or "knight", once meaning servant [1]. **Pejoration**: a word's meaning becomes more negative over time [1], e.g. "villain", once a farm labourer, or "silly", once meaning blessed [1].

**3.** **Derivation** creates a new word, often of a different class, by adding an affix [1], e.g. "kind" to "kindness" [1]. **Inflection** adds a grammatical ending that marks features such as tense, number or person without creating a new word [1], e.g. "walk" to "walked", or the older "-eth" in "hath" [1].

**4.** **Lexical gaps**: new words are created or borrowed when a concept or object has no existing name [1], which explains much vocabulary for new inventions and practices [1]. **Substratum theory**: when a population shifts from one language to another, features of its earlier language persist in the new one [1], affecting pronunciation, grammar or vocabulary of the variety that results [1].

**5.** The **tree model** presents languages as descending from common ancestors and splitting into branches [1], like a family tree, so relationships are vertical inheritance [1]. The **wave model** presents changes spreading outward from a centre to neighbouring varieties [1], so it explains features shared across branches by contact and geography rather than descent [1].

**6.** Any four, one mark for identifying and one for explaining each. **"vnto", "vs"**: the letter v used at the start of a word where modern spelling has u, showing that u and v were not yet fixed as separate vowel and consonant letters [1] [1]. **Final -e** in "Towne", "fruite", "sweete": extra letters reflecting older spelling habits, later dropped as spelling was standardised [1] [1]. **Capitalised common nouns** ("Towne", "Merchant", "Market", "Silkes"): a graphological convention of the period that later faded [1] [1]. **Variant spellings** "courtesie", "Oringe", "speake": inconsistent with modern forms, showing there was no dictionary standard yet [1] [1]. Alternatively, "shewed" for "showed", an older spelling that later lost out.

**7.** Any three, two marks each: **"did greet vs"** uses unstressed auxiliary "do" in a positive statement, common then but now used only for emphasis [1] [1]; **"a tongue I knew not"** negates without "do", where Contemporary English would say "I did not know" [1] [1]; **"where was sold Silkes..."** places the verb before the subject and lacks agreement with a plural subject, a word order now unusual [1] [1]. Alternatively, "thereof" as a compound adverb now largely confined to legal English.

**8.** Trade and colonial contact brought new goods and ideas, and English **borrowed** words from the languages of trading partners to name them [1] [1]; "orange" itself reached English through other European languages from older Asian sources [1]. The **theory of lexical gaps** explains this: there was no English word for the new goods, so borrowed words filled the gap [1].

**9.** Description: "hath" **dominates in 1550 but declines steadily**, while "has" **rises**, the two **crossing at around 1650**, and "hath" becomes **rare by 1800** [1] [1]. Interpretation: the third-person "-eth" inflection was **replaced by "-s"** in written English over this period [1], probably through **standardisation in print and the spread of a form already used in speech**, consistent with cultural transmission between generations of writers [1]. Limitation, any one, explained: the data come from **printed books only**, so they show formal written usage rather than speech, where "has" may have spread earlier [1] [1]. Alternatively, older scans can misread characters, distorting counts.

**10.** In 1600-1700 "nice" collocates with **"point", "distinction", "scruple"**, suggesting a meaning of **precise, subtle or fastidious** [1] [1]. In 1950-2000 it collocates with **intensifiers "very", "really" and everyday nouns "day", "people"**, showing a general meaning of **pleasant or agreeable** [1] [1]. The process is **amelioration** (with broadening into a general term of approval) [1].

**11.** Credit a balanced discussion: **prescriptive view explained** — change seen as decline from a correct standard, often linked to eighteenth-century grammars and dictionaries [1] [1]; **descriptive view explained** — change is natural and inevitable in a living language and should be recorded without judgement [1] [1]; **first example used as evidence**, e.g. loss of "thou" or "-eth", now accepted without complaint, showing yesterday's change becomes today's standard [1] [1]; **second example used as evidence**, e.g. conversion of "text" to a verb, which fills a functional need [1] [1]; **a reasoned conclusion** that changes reflect users' needs, though standard forms keep a social value in formal contexts [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling archaic spellings without explaining what they show about standardisation.
- Describing an n-gram trend without dates or comparative language.
- Treating the collocates in a word table as a list instead of as evidence of meaning.
- Naming a theory without linking it to a specific change.
- Presenting the prescriptive view as fact rather than as an attitude.
