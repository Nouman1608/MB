This guide goes deeper on one subtopic of Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **1.3 Size of business**, part of AS Level topic 1, Business and its environment. A Level candidates need it too, because Papers 3 and 4 assume all AS content.

The syllabus splits 1.3 into three parts:

- **1.3.1 Measurements of business size**: the appropriateness of different methods of measuring the size of a business.
- **1.3.2 Significance of small businesses**: the advantages and disadvantages of being small; the strengths and weaknesses of family businesses; the importance of small businesses and their role in the economy; their role as part of the industrial structure in some industries.
- **1.3.3 Business growth**: why and how a business might grow internally (organic growth); horizontal, vertical (backward and forward) and conglomerate growth through merger and takeover, including friendly mergers and hostile takeovers; the impact on stakeholders; why a merger or takeover may or may not achieve its objectives; the importance of joint ventures and strategic alliances.

Use it with the [Size of business revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-size-of-business-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [Size of business practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-size-of-business-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/). The [combined guide to business structure and size](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-business-structure-and-size-of-business/) defines each growth type; this page focuses on judgement and calculation. All businesses and figures below are invented, and sums of money are in dollars.

## 1.3.1 Judging measures of size

The syllabus asks for **appropriateness**, so the skill is matching the measure to the purpose of the comparison. Compare three businesses.

| Measure | Corvan Software | Elsmere Hotels plc | Pinto Bakery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employees | 45 | 620 | 12 |
| Sales revenue | $18m | $31m | $0.9m |
| Capital employed | $6m | $140m | $0.4m |
| Market size | National software market, $600m | Regional hotel market, $258m | Town bread market, $3m |
| Market share | 18 ÷ 600 = 3% | 31 ÷ 258 ≈ 12% | 0.9 ÷ 3 = 30% |
| Revenue per employee | $400 000 | $50 000 | $75 000 |

What the table shows:

- **By employees**, Elsmere is far larger than Corvan. But Corvan earns eight times as much revenue per employee, so employee numbers understate the size of knowledge-based and capital-intensive firms and overstate labour-intensive ones such as hotels.
- **By revenue**, Corvan is more than half Elsmere's size, but revenue says nothing about profit or the assets used.
- **By capital employed**, Elsmere is enormous because hotels need buildings. Capital employed suits comparisons of asset-heavy firms but depends on how assets are valued.
- **By market share**, Pinto Bakery looks the most powerful, with 30%. This shows the weakness of market share: it depends on how the market is defined. Pinto dominates a small local market but is tiny in absolute terms.
- For a **public limited company**, **market capitalisation** (share price × number of shares) is another option. If Elsmere has 50 million shares at $3.20, its market capitalisation is **$160m**, but this moves with share prices daily and is unavailable for private companies.

**Exam judgement:** use more than one measure; choose employees for questions about management or employment, revenue or market share for questions about competitive position, and capital employed for questions about investment.

## 1.3.2 The significance of small businesses

### Being small: two sides

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Close personal contact with customers and staff | Limited access to finance; lenders see higher risk |
| Quick decisions and flexibility | Few economies of scale, so higher average costs |
| Can serve niche markets large firms ignore | Owner overload and lack of specialist managers |
| Lower overheads | Vulnerable to the loss of one large customer or key person |

### Family businesses

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Shared commitment and trust; values passed down | Recruitment to senior roles may favour family over talent |
| Long-term outlook, less pressure for short-term profit | Family disputes can spill into business decisions |
| Strong reputation tied to the family name | Succession problems when the founder retires |
| Flexibility from informal communication | Resistance to outside ideas and finance that would dilute control |

A strong answer links the strength or weakness to the case. For example, if the founder is about to retire and the children work elsewhere, succession is the critical weakness; if the firm competes on craftsmanship and trust, the family name may be its main asset.

### Importance in the economy

- **Employment:** collectively, small firms employ a large share of the workforce, often in areas large firms avoid.
- **Innovation:** new firms bring new products and methods, and some grow into large businesses.
- **Competition:** they keep pressure on larger firms' prices and service.
- **Supply chains:** many supply parts or services to large firms.
- **Enterprise:** they offer a route into business for entrepreneurs.

### Role in industrial structure

In some industries small businesses are the normal form. Where demand is local or personalised (hairdressing, plumbing, restaurants) or where economies of scale are small, large firms gain little advantage. In other industries small firms survive alongside giants by **specialising**: a small engineering firm may make one precision component for a large car maker, and the large firm may prefer to buy it rather than make it. So "small" does not mean "weak": the role a small firm plays in its industry matters more than its size alone.

## 1.3.3 Business growth

### Organic growth: why and how

**Why:** economies of scale, higher market share and profit, security against takeover, and satisfying owners' ambitions. **How:** new outlets, new products, new markets, increased capacity, more marketing.

*Worked example.* Pinto Bakery retains $60 000 of profit a year and wants a second shop costing $150 000. Funding it from retained profit takes $150 000 ÷ $60 000 = **2.5 years**. Organic growth keeps the family in control and avoids interest, but is slow: a rival could open first. A loan would speed growth but add interest costs and risk.

### External growth: types and motives

| Type | Typical motive |
|---|---|
| Horizontal | Larger market share; economies of scale; remove a competitor |
| Vertical backward | Control over supply, quality and input costs |
| Vertical forward | Control over distribution and access to customers |
| Conglomerate diversification | Spread risk across unrelated markets |

A **friendly merger** is agreed by both boards. A **hostile takeover** is opposed by the target's directors; the bidder offers shareholders a price high enough to persuade them to sell.

### Will the takeover achieve its objectives? A calculation

Fenwick Foods plc offers $15m for Arlow Sauces Ltd, which is valued at $12m. The **premium** is $15m - $12m = **$3m**, or 3 ÷ 12 × 100 = **25%** above the valuation. Fenwick expects cost savings ("synergies") of $1.2m a year from combining production and distribution.

```
Years to recover the premium = 3.0 / 1.2 = 2.5 years
If only half the savings arrive: 3.0 / 0.6 = 5 years
```

The deal looks attractive only if the savings materialise. Reasons a takeover may **not** achieve its objectives:

- **Overpaying:** competition between bidders pushes up the premium.
- **Culture clash:** different management styles reduce motivation.
- **Loss of key staff:** skilled employees or managers leave.
- **Diseconomies of scale:** the larger business becomes harder to coordinate.
- **Customer reaction:** customers loyal to the acquired brand switch.

Reasons it **may** succeed: genuine cost savings, complementary skills or products, rapid access to new markets, and careful integration planning.

### Impact on stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Target's shareholders | Gain from the premium |
| Bidder's shareholders | Lose if the premium is too high; gain if synergies arrive |
| Employees | Job losses from cutting duplicated roles; new opportunities in a larger firm |
| Customers | Wider range or lower prices; or less choice and higher prices if competition falls |
| Suppliers | A bigger, more powerful buyer may push prices down |
| Local community | Job losses if a site closes; investment if a site expands |

### Joint ventures and strategic alliances

A **joint venture** creates a separate business owned by two or more firms; a **strategic alliance** is an agreement to cooperate while staying separate. Both give access to markets, technology or expertise at lower cost and risk than a takeover, and can be ended more easily. They are important for entering foreign markets where local knowledge or local partners are needed. Limitations: disagreements over control and profit sharing, and the risk that a partner learns enough to become a competitor.

## Common mistakes

- Using a single measure of size as if it were definitive.
- Treating market share as a measure of absolute size.
- Saying all small firms are weak, ignoring niches and their role in supply chains.
- Describing a takeover type without explaining the motive.
- Assuming a merger achieves its objectives because it was planned.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate**: show workings and units, such as market share or years to recover a premium.
- **Explain**: reason plus effect, applied to the case business.
- **Analyse**: a chain, such as horizontal takeover → larger market share → bulk-buying economies → lower average cost.
- **Assess**, **Evaluate** or **To what extent**: weigh both sides and state what the judgement depends on.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend one growth method for the case business and support it with evidence.
