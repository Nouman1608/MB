These questions cover the "Maps in association with photographs" part of section 3.3.1.1, Cartographic skills, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3, including "photographs: use and interpret ground, aerial and satellite photographs" and "draw sketches from photographs". For all the skills in the unit, see the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for revision and
> practice on this content. They are **not** reproduced past-paper questions,
> and they do **not** replicate the exam's exact structure, question count or
> mark tariffs. Real papers print the photographs and maps; here they are
> described in words, and all places are fictional.

Related: [photographs and sketches study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-photographs-sketches/) and [photographs and sketches revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-photographs-sketches-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a label and an annotation. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** differences between a vertical aerial photograph and an oblique aerial photograph. **[2]**

**3.** Name **three** features that a sketch map should include. **[3]**

## Section B

**Photograph A** (a ground photograph of the fictional Lenn Valley): in the foreground, a wide, flat, grassy valley floor with cattle grazing; a river winds across the middle ground in large bends, lined by a single row of trees; in the background, a small village of stone houses and a church sits on a low rise above the valley floor; gentle slopes with hedged fields rise behind it.

**4.** Describe the physical landscape shown in Photograph A. **[4]**

**5.** Describe the land use shown in Photograph A. **[3]**

**6.** Suggest **two** reasons why the village was built on the low rise rather than on the valley floor. **[2]**

**7.** On the map extract for Photograph A, the church is at grid reference 452318 and a road bridge is at 448322. In the photograph, the church appears in front of the bridge. State the direction in which the photograph was taken, and explain how you worked it out. **[3]**

**8.** Describe how you would draw a field sketch of Photograph A, and write **two** annotations you would include. **[5]**

**9.** Two satellite images of the fictional city of Ravara, taken in 2005 and 2025, show that the built-up area has spread east along a main highway, replacing farmland, while a lake to the west is unchanged.

**(a)** Describe the change shown. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **one** limitation of satellite images for studying this change. **[1]**

**10.** Two maps of the fictional coastal village of Port Edra show the same area in 1990 and 2020. In 2020: a caravan park has appeared in grid square 6142; housing has spread along the coast road from 6240 to 6440; a sea wall is marked along the beach in 6341. Identify **three** changes and suggest **one** reason for one of them. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a single photograph may give an unreliable impression of a place. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of photographs compared with large-scale maps for studying a coastal landscape. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A label names a feature, for example "river" [1]. An annotation explains or describes it, for example "meandering river with a wide floodplain" [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: a vertical photograph looks straight down, an oblique one at an angle [1]; a vertical photograph shows layout like a map, an oblique one shows the shape and height of the land [1]; scale is fairly even across a vertical photograph but changes from front to back on an oblique one [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any three: title [1]; north arrow [1]; approximate scale [1]; key [1]. **[3]**

**4.** A wide, flat valley floor in the foreground [1]. A river flowing in large meanders across the middle ground [1]. A low rise in the background above the flat land [1]. Gentle slopes rising behind the village; the flat floor suggests a floodplain [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Pasture: cattle grazing on the valley floor [1]. Settlement: a small village with stone houses and a church on the low rise [1]. Farmland with hedged fields on the slopes behind [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two: the rise keeps the village above the level of river floods [1]; the valley floor is better used for grazing [1]; the rise gives drier ground for building [1]. **[2]**

**7.** North-west [1]. The bridge has a lower easting (448) than the church (452), so it is to the west, and a higher northing (322 against 318), so it is to the north [1]. Since the church is in front of the bridge, the camera was facing from the church towards the bridge, which is north-west [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Method, any three: draw a frame the shape of the photograph [1]; draw the skyline and main lines of the land and river [1]; add the village, river and fields as simple outlines [1]; add a title, place and direction of view [1]. Two annotations, one mark each, for example: "Wide, flat floodplain used for grazing" [1]; "Village on a low rise, above flood level" [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** The built-up area has grown eastwards along the highway [1], replacing farmland, while the lake to the west is unchanged [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Any one: images may lack detail to show building types or land use [1]; cloud may hide parts of the area; two dates cannot show when or how fast growth happened. **[1]**

**10.** Changes, one mark each up to three: a caravan park in 6142 [1]; housing along the coast road from 6240 to 6440 [1]; a sea wall in 6341 [1]. Reason, for example: the caravan park suggests growth in tourism, or the sea wall suggests a need to protect new housing from erosion [1]. **[4]**

**11.** It shows only one moment and one season, so conditions such as flooding or crowds may not be typical [1]. The photographer chooses the framing, so features outside the view are left out [1]. Scale and distance can be hard to judge, especially on ground and oblique photographs [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Photographs show what the coast looks like: cliff shape, beach material, vegetation and land use [1].
- They can reveal features too small for the map, such as a slump scar or a new path [1].
- Maps give accurate height from contours, distances and grid references for features [1].
- Maps show a wider area and the names of places, so landforms can be placed in context [1].
- Photographs can be misleading because of framing, season or date; maps may be out of date [1].
- Judgement: each is more useful when used with the other; a map is better for measurement and location, a photograph for appearance and current land use [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing human features in a physical-landscape question, or the reverse.
- No positional language when describing a photograph.
- Getting the direction of view the wrong way round.
- Field sketch methods with no title, direction or annotations.
- Map comparisons without grid references.
- Evaluations that list points without a judgement.
