These notes condense section **3.3.11.2 Base properties** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Base properties study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-amine-base-properties/), and exam-style questions are in the [Base properties practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-amine-base-properties-practice/). The previous sub-topic is in the [Amines revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-amines-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- State that **amines are weak bases**.
- State the **difference in base strength** between ammonia, primary aliphatic amines and primary aromatic amines.
- **Explain** the difference in terms of the **availability of the lone pair** on the N atom.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Base (Brønsted–Lowry) | Proton (H⁺) acceptor |
| Weak base | Only partially accepts protons in water |
| Lone pair | Pair of non-bonding electrons on nitrogen |
| Inductive effect | Electron donation or withdrawal through sigma bonds |
| Delocalised | Spread over several atoms |
| Ammonium salt | Ionic compound containing RNH₃⁺ |

## Amines as bases

```
RNH₂ + H⁺ → RNH₃⁺
RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻
```

- The lone pair on nitrogen forms a **dative covalent bond** to H⁺.
- The reaction with water is **reversible**, so amines are weak bases.
- Aqueous amines are **alkaline**.

## Reactions with acids

| Reaction | Equation |
|---|---|
| Ethylamine + HCl | CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ |
| Phenylamine + HCl | C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻ |
| Ethylamine + H₂SO₄ | 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂SO₄ → (CH₃CH₂NH₃)₂SO₄ |
| Salt + NaOH | RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → RNH₂ + H₂O (free amine released) |

Salts are ionic and soluble in water. Names: **alkylammonium chloride**.

## The strength order

```
phenylamine  <  ammonia  <  primary aliphatic amine
```

| Compound | Reason for strength | Lone pair |
|---|---|---|
| Primary aliphatic amine | Alkyl group donates electron density to N | Most available |
| Ammonia | Reference: no donating group | Intermediate |
| Phenylamine | Lone pair delocalised into the benzene ring | Least available |

## Model explanation

1. A base needs a lone pair to bond to H⁺.
2. The more available the lone pair, the stronger the base.
3. **Alkyl groups donate electron density** to nitrogen, so the lone pair is more available: aliphatic amines are stronger than ammonia.
4. In phenylamine the lone pair **overlaps with the delocalised ring** and is partly delocalised into it, so it is less available: phenylamine is weaker than ammonia.

## Key words to include

- **lone pair on the nitrogen**
- **availability**
- **electron-donating (alkyl group)**
- **delocalised into the ring**
- **proton acceptor**

## Worked example to copy

**Question.** Explain why methylamine is a stronger base than ammonia but phenylamine is a weaker base than ammonia.

1. Say what a base does: it accepts a proton using the lone pair on nitrogen.
2. Methylamine: the methyl group is electron-donating, so it pushes electron density onto nitrogen and the lone pair is more available.
3. Phenylamine: the lone pair on nitrogen overlaps with the delocalised ring electrons and is partly delocalised into the ring, so it is less available.
4. Conclude with the order: phenylamine < ammonia < methylamine.

## Linking to other topics

- The same lone pair makes amines **nucleophiles**: see the Nucleophilic properties notes.
- Amides, made in acylation, are much weaker bases than amines, because the nitrogen lone pair is drawn into the C=O group. This is beyond the specification but shows why availability of the lone pair matters.
- Weak-base behaviour contrasts with strong bases such as sodium hydroxide, which are fully ionised.

## Visual summary

| Compound | Group attached to N | Lone pair availability | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₃CH₂NH₂ | Alkyl (donates) | High | Strongest |
| NH₃ | None | Medium | Middle |
| C₆H₅NH₂ | Phenyl (delocalises the lone pair) | Low | Weakest |

## Calculations

- Amine and acid react 1 : 1 with HCl (monoprotic) and 2 : 1 with H₂SO₄.
- n = c × V (in dm³).
- Mass of salt = moles × Mᵣ of salt.

Example: 20.0 cm³ of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ ethylamine needs 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ HCl for neutralisation. Check: n(amine) = 0.0200 × 0.250 = 0.00500 mol and n(HCl) = 0.0250 × 0.200 = 0.00500 mol.

## Practical uses of salt formation

| Situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| Phenylamine in water | Poorly soluble |
| Add dilute HCl | Dissolves, as soluble phenylammonium chloride |
| Add excess NaOH | Free phenylamine is released and separates |

## Exam traps

- Calling an amine a base because it releases OH⁻ without mentioning proton acceptance.
- Saying alkyl groups are electron-withdrawing.
- Explaining phenylamine's weakness without referring to the lone pair.
- "The lone pair is donated to the ring" instead of delocalised.
- A one-way arrow for the reaction with water.
- Salt written as RNH₂Cl, with no charges.
- Wrong order of strength.
- Describing size or mass as the reason.

## Self-test

1. What is a Brønsted–Lowry base?
2. Why can an amine accept a proton?
3. Write an equation for methylamine with water.
4. Why is a reversible arrow used?
5. Name the product of ethylamine with hydrochloric acid.
6. What is added to the salt to release the free amine?
7. Put phenylamine, ammonia and ethylamine in order of increasing base strength.
8. Why is ethylamine a stronger base than ammonia?
9. Why is phenylamine a weaker base than ammonia?
10. Why does phenylamine dissolve in dilute hydrochloric acid?
11. What volume of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl neutralises 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ propylamine?
12. How many moles of ethylamine react with 1 mole of sulfuric acid?

### Answers

1. A proton acceptor.
2. It has a lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atom.
3. CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻.
4. The amine is only partly protonated; the equilibrium lies to the left.
5. Ethylammonium chloride.
6. Aqueous sodium hydroxide.
7. Phenylamine < ammonia < ethylamine.
8. The alkyl group donates electron density, so the lone pair is more available.
9. The lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available.
10. It forms a soluble ionic salt, phenylammonium chloride.
11. 25.0 cm³.
12. 2 mol.
