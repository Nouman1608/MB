## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by operations management objectives. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** examples of operations management objectives. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs clear operations management objectives. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** trade-off between two operations management objectives. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**5.** Kiln & Co makes ceramic mugs. Its factory can make 10,000 mugs a month but currently makes 7,500. Fixed costs are £180,000 a month. The operations manager sets an objective of 85% capacity utilisation.

**(a)** Calculate the current capacity utilisation. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the monthly output needed to meet the 85% objective. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the fixed cost per mug at the current output and at the new output, to the nearest penny. **[3]**

**(d)** Evaluate the importance of the capacity utilisation objective to Kiln & Co. **[8]**

**6.** Kiln & Co spends £150,000 a year on clay and glaze. Currently 9% is wasted. It sets a target of reducing waste to 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the value of materials wasted at present. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual saving if the target is met. **[2]**

**(c)** Kiln & Co also has an objective to deliver 96% of orders on time. Last month 1,800 of 2,000 orders were on time. Calculate the on-time percentage and the number of extra orders that needed to arrive on time to meet the target. **[3]**

**7.** Ferrous Foods is a new company delivering hot meals to offices. Customers expect delivery within 30 minutes and the company has a small budget. Recommend and justify operations management objectives for Ferrous Foods. **[9]**

**8.** SkyJet is a budget airline. Its business objective is to be the lowest-cost airline on its routes. Evaluate the importance of operations management objectives in the achievement of SkyJet's business objective. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The targets set for the operations function [1] covering how well the business turns inputs into goods or services, such as cost, quality, speed and flexibility [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: cost; quality; speed; dependability; flexibility; capacity utilisation; waste reduction or sustainability; innovation; customer service [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Clear objectives give staff a measurable target [1], so performance can be compared with the target and problems noticed early [1], and they help managers to set priorities and coordinate with other functions such as marketing [1]. **[3]**

**4.** For example speed and cost [1]: delivering quickly may need overtime and faster transport, which raises costs [1], so a business that wants to keep costs low may have to accept slower delivery [1]. Accept quality against cost or flexibility against cost with a similar explanation. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 7,500 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **75%** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 85% × 10,000 = **8,500 mugs** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Current: 180,000 ÷ 7,500 = **£24.00** [1]. New: 180,000 ÷ 8,500 = **£21.18** [1]. Saving £2.82 per mug [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** Using more of the capacity spreads the fixed costs, cutting the fixed cost per mug from £24.00 to £21.18, so unit cost falls and profit per mug rises [1]. Output rises by 1,000 mugs a month (13.3%) without new equipment [1]. This helps Kiln to achieve a profit or price-cutting objective [1].
But the 85% objective needs 1,000 more mugs a month sold, so it depends on marketing [1]; if the extra mugs are sold only by discounting, the unit gain may be lost [1]; running machines near capacity leaves little flexibility for large orders and reduces time for maintenance [1].
Importance: it is important because fixed costs are high (£180,000), so spreading them has a large effect on unit cost [1]. Judgement: the objective is valuable, but only if demand is there, so it should be agreed with the sales team and reviewed monthly [1]. **[8]**

**6. (a)** 150,000 × 0.09 = **£13,500** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Wasted at 5% = 150,000 × 0.05 = £7,500 [1]. Saving = 13,500 − 7,500 = **£6,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 1,800 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **90%** [1]. Target = 0.96 × 2,000 = 1,920 [1]. Extra = 1,920 − 1,800 = **120 orders** [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Speed: for example 95% of orders delivered within 30 minutes, because speed is the order winner for this market [2: 1 for objective with measure, 1 for reason]. Quality: for example complaints about cold or incorrect meals below 2% of orders, because poor quality would lose customers who can easily change [2]. Dependability follows from these, and could be measured by on-time delivery. Cost: for example, reduce delivery cost per order by 10% in a year, since the budget is small and profit depends on cost [2].
Trade-offs: pursuing speed raises costs through extra riders, and a tight cost target may reduce speed in busy periods, so the company needs to decide priorities [1]. Speed and quality come first, with cost as the third [1]. Judgement: objectives should be reviewed after the first three months, once real data is available [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Importance: cost is the order winner for a budget airline, so objectives for cost per seat, fuel efficiency and turnaround time directly affect the business objective [1]. For example, high capacity utilisation (seat load factor) spreads fixed costs over more passengers [1]; fast turnaround at airports allows more flights per aircraft per day, spreading the cost of the aircraft [1]; dependability, such as on-time departures, avoids the costs of compensation and customer loss [1]. Without clear targets, managers could not tell where costs are being lost [1].
Stakeholders: passengers want low fares, but also safety and reliability [1]; staff are affected by pressure for fast turnarounds [1]; shareholders benefit from lower costs.
Limits: cost objectives may conflict with quality and customer service, which could harm reputation [1]; cost depends on factors outside operations' control, such as fuel prices [1]; the pressure to meet fast turnaround targets could affect safety if not controlled [1].
Judgement: operations objectives are central to the cost leadership aim, because most of a budget airline's cost advantage is created by operations, but they must be balanced with safety, dependability and the willingness of passengers to accept low service levels [2]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing objectives without a number, a measure or a date.
- Forgetting that capacity utilisation depends on demand.
- Ignoring trade-offs between objectives.
- Calculating capacity utilisation as output divided by demand instead of by capacity.
