These questions cover sub-section 3.9.3 Cosmology of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards), part of the Astrophysics option (A-level only, Paper 3 Section B).

Revise from the [cosmology study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-cosmology-aqa-alevel/) and [cosmology revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-cosmology-aqa-alevel-revision-notes/) first. Mixed option questions are in the [Astrophysics practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-astrophysics-practice/).

Data: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; 1 Mpc = 3.08 × 10²² m; 1 year = 3.15 × 10⁷ s.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain why light from a receding galaxy is red-shifted. **[2]**

**2.** State Hubble's law and give its simple interpretation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the cosmological microwave background radiation supports the Big Bang theory. **[2]**

**4.** Give two reasons why exoplanets are difficult to detect directly. **[2]**

**5.** State how quasars were first discovered and what they are thought to form from. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A line of rest wavelength 486.1 nm is observed at 501.0 nm in a galaxy's spectrum. Take H = 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate z. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the recession speed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the distance to the galaxy in Mpc. **[2]**

**7.** In a nearby galaxy, a line of rest wavelength 656.3 nm is observed at 655.65 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the galaxy's speed along the line of sight and state its direction. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this does not contradict Hubble's law. **[1]**

**8.** A radio line with rest frequency 1.420 GHz is received from a gas cloud at 1.4135 GHz. Calculate the speed of the cloud along the line of sight and state whether it is approaching or receding. **[2]**

**9.** A binary star system is viewed in the plane of its orbit. A line of rest wavelength 589.0 nm in one star's spectrum varies by up to ±0.030 nm, with a period of 4.0 days.

**(a)** Calculate the orbital speed of the star. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the radius of its circular orbit. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how each spectral line appears over one period if both stars are equally bright. **[1]**

**10.** **(a)** Take H = 72 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. Estimate the age of the Universe in years. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this value is only an estimate. **[1]**

**11.** A quasar has z = 0.12. The intensity of its radiation at Earth is 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ W m⁻². Take H = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate its distance in metres. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate its power output. **[2]**

**12.** A planet causes its star to move towards and away from Earth with a maximum speed of 55 m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum shift of a line of rest wavelength 550 nm. **[2]**
**(b)** State how the planet's orbital period can be found from these observations. **[1]**

**13.** In a transit, the intensity of a star of radius 5.5 × 10⁸ m falls by 0.40%.

**(a)** Calculate the radius of the planet. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the light curve observed over two orbits of the planet. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** As the galaxy moves away, each successive wavefront is emitted from further away, so the wavelengths received are stretched [1]; the observed wavelength is longer than the rest wavelength, shifted towards the red end of the spectrum [1].

**2.** The recession speed of a galaxy is proportional to its distance, v = Hd [1]; the Universe is expanding [1].

**3.** It is black-body radiation at about 2.7 K arriving almost uniformly from all directions [1]; it is explained as radiation from the hot, dense early Universe, stretched to microwave wavelengths as the Universe expanded [1].

**4.** The planet is extremely faint compared with its star [1]; the angular separation between the planet and star is very small, so they cannot be resolved [1].

**5.** As bright radio sources [1]; active supermassive black holes [1].

**6. (a)** z = (501.0 − 486.1)/486.1 = **0.0307** [1]
**(b)** v = zc = **9.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** d = v/H = 9200 km s⁻¹/68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ [1] = **135 Mpc** [1]

**7. (a)** v = cΔλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.65/656.3 [1] = **3.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹, approaching** [1]
**(b)** Hubble's law describes the overall expansion at large distances; a nearby galaxy's own motion through space can exceed its small recession speed [1].

**8.** v = cΔf/f = 3.00 × 10⁸ × (−0.0065/1.420) [1] = −1.4 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹: **1.4 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, receding** [1]

**9. (a)** v = cΔλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.030/589.0 [1] = **1.5 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** T = 4.0 × 86 400 = 3.46 × 10⁵ s [1]; r = vT/2π = **8.4 × 10⁸ m** [1]
**(c)** Each line splits into two components that move apart and back together, merging twice per period when both stars move across the line of sight [1].

**10. (a)** H = 72 × 10³/3.08 × 10²² = 2.34 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ [1]; 1/H = 4.28 × 10¹⁷ s [1]; age = **1.4 × 10¹⁰ years** [1]
**(b)** It assumes H has been constant throughout the history of the Universe, which may not be true [1].

**11. (a)** v = 0.12 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 3.6 × 10⁴ km s⁻¹; d = 3.6 × 10⁴/70 = 514 Mpc [1] = **1.6 × 10²⁵ m** [1]
**(b)** P = 4πd²I = 4π × (1.58 × 10²⁵)² × 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ [1] = **2.5 × 10³⁹ W** [1]

**12. (a)** Δλ = λv/c = 550 × 10⁻⁹ × 55/3.00 × 10⁸ [1] = **1.0 × 10⁻¹³ m** (1.0 × 10⁻⁴ nm) [1]
**(b)** It equals the period of the repeating variation in the Doppler shift [1].

**13. (a)** r_p = r_s √0.0040 [1] = 5.5 × 10⁸ × 0.063 = **3.5 × 10⁷ m** [1]
**(b)** Constant intensity, then a drop to a flat minimum 0.40% lower while the planet crosses the star, then a rise back to the original level [1]; the dip repeats at regular intervals equal to the orbital period, so two identical dips appear [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a speed without saying approaching or receding.
- Mixing km s⁻¹ and m s⁻¹ in Hubble calculations.
- Taking 1/H without converting H to s⁻¹.
- Using 0.40 instead of 0.0040 for a 0.40% dip.
- Forgetting to convert days to seconds for binary stars.
