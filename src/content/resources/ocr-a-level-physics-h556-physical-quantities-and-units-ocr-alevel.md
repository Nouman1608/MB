## A quantity is a number times a unit (2.1.1(a))

Writing "the current is 0.25" says nothing. The quantity is 0.25 A: the number 0.25 multiplied by the unit ampere. Treat units as algebraic symbols that multiply, divide and cancel with the numbers. That habit does three jobs at once:

- it tells you the unit of any answer (a force in N divided by an area in m² must be in N m⁻², the pascal);
- it exposes mixed units, because cm and m will not cancel;
- it explains the labelling convention: if speed = 4.2 m s⁻¹, then speed / m s⁻¹ = 4.2, a pure number that can go in a table or on an axis.

## Estimating quantities (2.1.1(b))

An estimate uses sensible values for everyday quantities to reach an answer to one or two significant figures, or to the nearest power of ten. The method matters more than the exact value.

1. Choose the equation that links the quantity to things you can picture.
2. State each assumed value with its unit.
3. Calculate and round to one or two significant figures.
4. Check the result is plausible.

Reference values worth knowing: mass of an adult about 70 kg; height of a room about 3 m; walking speed about 1.5 m s⁻¹; car at motorway speed about 30 m s⁻¹; density of water 1000 kg m⁻³ and of air about 1.2 kg m⁻³; atmospheric pressure about 1 × 10⁵ Pa; visible wavelengths 400 to 700 nm.

### Worked example 1: three quick estimates

```
Mass of air in a classroom
  volume ≈ 8 m × 6 m × 3 m = 144 m³
  mass = ρV ≈ 1.2 × 144 ≈ 170 kg  (about 2 × 10² kg)

Kinetic energy of a car on a motorway
  m ≈ 1200 kg, v ≈ 30 m s⁻¹
  Ek = ½mv² ≈ 0.5 × 1200 × 900 ≈ 5 × 10⁵ J

Pressure under a standing adult
  weight ≈ 700 N, area of both soles ≈ 0.05 m²
  p = F/A ≈ 700 / 0.05 ≈ 1 × 10⁴ Pa
```

A mark scheme for an estimate accepts a range of answers, but only when the assumptions are stated and reasonable.

## S.I. base quantities and units (2.1.2(a))

| Base quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Length | metre | m |
| Time | second | s |
| Current | ampere | A |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |

Two traps. **Charge is not a base quantity**: the coulomb is derived, 1 C = 1 A s, because current is the base quantity. **Temperature in S.I. is in kelvin**: a temperature in °C must be converted (T/K = θ/°C + 273) before it goes into an equation such as pV = nRT, although a temperature *difference* is the same size in K and °C.

## Derived units (2.1.2(b)-(c))

Every other unit is a product of powers of base units, found from a defining equation. Work from the simplest equation that contains the quantity.

| Quantity | Defining equation | Named unit | Base units |
|---|---|---|---|
| Force | F = ma | N | kg m s⁻² |
| Pressure, stress, Young modulus | F/A | Pa | kg m⁻¹ s⁻² |
| Energy, work | W = Fx | J | kg m² s⁻² |
| Power | P = W/t | W | kg m² s⁻³ |
| Frequency | f = 1/T | Hz | s⁻¹ |
| Charge | Q = It | C | A s |
| Potential difference | V = W/Q | V | kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹ |
| Resistance | R = V/I | Ω | kg m² s⁻³ A⁻² |
| Resistivity | ρ = RA/L | Ω m | kg m³ s⁻³ A⁻² |
| Spring constant | k = F/x | N m⁻¹ | kg s⁻² |
| Specific heat capacity | c = E/(mΔθ) | J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ | m² s⁻² K⁻¹ |

### Worked example 2: base units of the gravitational constant

Newton's law of gravitation is F = GMm/r², so G = Fr²/(Mm).

```
units of G = (kg m s⁻²)(m²) / (kg × kg)
           = kg m³ s⁻² / kg²
           = m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²
```

The same method gives the base units of any constant: make it the subject, substitute base units, then collect the powers of each base unit separately.

## Homogeneity (2.1.2(d))

An equation is **homogeneous** when every term has the same base units. A correct physical equation must be homogeneous. A homogeneous equation can still be wrong, because pure numbers such as ½ or 2π have no units and are invisible to the check.

### Worked example 3: finding the unit of a constant

The drag force on a car is sometimes written F = ½ρAv²C, where ρ is the density of air and A the frontal area. Find the unit of C.

```
ρAv² : (kg m⁻³)(m²)(m² s⁻²) = kg m s⁻²
F    : kg m s⁻²
```

ρAv² already has the base units of force, so C must have **no unit**. It is a pure number.

### Worked example 4: an equation that fails

A student suggests that the energy E stored in a stretched spring is E = kx, where k is the spring constant and x the extension.

kx has base units kg s⁻² × m = kg m s⁻², which is a force, not an energy (kg m² s⁻²). The equation is not homogeneous, so it is wrong. The correct form, E = ½kx², has units kg s⁻² × m² = kg m² s⁻²; the check confirms the power of x but cannot confirm the ½.

## Prefixes (2.1.2(e))

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² | deci | d | 10⁻¹ |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | kilo | k | 10³ |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ | mega | M | 10⁶ |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ | giga | G | 10⁹ |
| centi | c | 10⁻² | tera | T | 10¹² |

A prefix belongs to the unit it is attached to, and a power on the unit applies to the prefix too.

```
0.20 mm²   = 0.20 × (10⁻³ m)² = 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²
3.5 dm³    = 3.5 × (10⁻¹ m)³  = 3.5 × 10⁻³ m³
13.6 g cm⁻³ = 13.6 × 10⁻³ kg / 10⁻⁶ m³ = 1.36 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³
1.5 kW h   = 1.5 × 10³ W × 3600 s = 5.4 × 10⁶ J
```

Upper and lower case matter: m is milli, M is mega; mm is millimetre, Mm would be megametre.

## Labelling axes and table columns (2.1.2(f))

The convention is **quantity / unit**, because dividing the quantity by its unit leaves the pure number that is plotted or tabulated.

| Heading | Meaning of the entry 2.5 |
|---|---|
| t / s | t = 2.5 s |
| I / mA | I = 2.5 mA |
| v / 10³ m s⁻¹ | v = 2.5 × 10³ m s⁻¹ |
| T⁻¹ / s⁻¹ | 1/T = 2.5 s⁻¹ |

A logarithm can only be taken of a pure number, so log axes are labelled with the quantity divided by its unit inside the bracket: **ln (I / A)** or **lg (f / Hz)**. Writing "ln I / A" suggests the unit belongs to the logarithm, which it cannot.

## Common errors

- Treating the coulomb as a base unit; charge is derived from current and time.
- Putting °C into an equation that needs kelvin.
- Converting mm² to m² with a factor of 10⁻³ instead of 10⁻⁶.
- Claiming a homogeneous equation is "proved correct".
- Giving an estimate with no stated assumptions, or to four significant figures.
- Writing units in every cell of a table, or labelling a log axis "log I (A)".

## Exam technique

- **Show that** a unit or equation is homogeneous: start from defining equations, write each step, end on the base units asked for.
- **Estimate**: state assumptions, use one or two significant figures, and give a unit.
- **Define** a derived unit in words from its equation: one volt is one joule per coulomb.
- **State** the base units: give only kg, m, s, A, K and mol, with negative powers rather than fraction bars.
