Section 3.1.3 **Research methods and theory** in OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology
(9690) is examined twice. In **Unit 1 Section B (Research methods)**, the specification describes
"short questions and a structured question based on data about an unseen issue of global
sociological significance". This guide is about that Unit 1 section, which leans on the practical
content: approaches to research (3.1.3.1), the main methods (3.1.3.2), issues in research (3.1.3.3)
and the interpretation of data (3.1.3.4). The theory content (3.1.3.5) is covered in the
[research methods and theory study guide for Unit 2](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-research-methods-and-theory/),
and the unit overview is in the [Introducing Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/).

Because the data are about an **unseen issue**, you cannot revise the topic in advance. What you
can revise is a toolkit: precise definitions, the strengths and limitations of each method, and a
routine for reading tables, charts and qualitative extracts.

## Approaches to research (3.1.3.1)

- **Quantitative data** are numerical (percentages, rates, scores); **qualitative data** are
  words, images or descriptions of meaning.
- **Primary data** are collected by the researcher; **secondary data** already exist (official
  statistics, diaries, other researchers' findings).
- **Positivists** model sociology on natural science and prefer quantitative, reliable methods;
  **interpretivists** seek the meanings people give to actions and prefer qualitative, valid
  methods.

## The main methods (3.1.3.2)

| Method | Key features | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Postal/telephone surveys** | Standard questions sent by post or asked by phone | Wide geographical reach; cheap per respondent | Low response rates (postal); phone excludes those without phones or who screen calls |
| **Online surveys** | Questionnaire completed on a website or app | Fast, cheap, large samples, easy analysis | Excludes people without internet access; self-selection; hard to verify who answers |
| **Self-completion surveys** | Respondent fills in the form without an interviewer | Anonymity reduces social desirability; no interviewer bias | Misunderstood questions cannot be clarified |
| **Structured interviews** | Fixed questions in fixed order, read by an interviewer | Reliable; comparable answers; higher response than postal | Inflexible; interviewer bias still possible |
| **Unstructured interviews** | Conversation guided by themes | Valid, in-depth; respondent can explain meanings | Time-consuming; hard to compare; small samples |
| **Group interviews** | Several respondents together (including focus groups) | Shows how views form in interaction | Dominant voices; peer pressure distorts answers |
| **Participant observation** | Researcher joins the group (an ethnographic approach) | High validity; natural setting | Small, unrepresentative; risk of "going native" |
| **Covert / overt observation** | Group unaware / aware of researcher | Covert avoids the Hawthorne effect; overt allows consent | Covert is ethically problematic; overt may alter behaviour |
| **Non-participant observation** | Researcher watches without joining | Detachment; can use schedules | Presence may still change behaviour; meanings missed |
| **Laboratory experiments** | Controlled, artificial setting, variables manipulated | Establish cause and effect; replicable | Artificial; ethical limits on manipulating people |
| **Field experiments** | Variables manipulated in a natural setting | More realistic | Less control; participants often unaware, raising consent issues |
| **Longitudinal studies** | Same group studied repeatedly over time | Show change and sequence | Expensive; drop-out ("attrition") reduces representativeness |
| **Qualitative secondary sources** | Diaries, letters, personal documents | Insight into meaning, including the past | May be unrepresentative or written for an audience |
| **Quantitative secondary sources** | Official statistics | Cheap, large-scale, show trends | Collected for state purposes; definitions and counting rules shape them |

**Types of survey question**: **closed** questions (fixed choices, easy to count), **open**
questions (respondents answer in their own words), **scaled** questions (for example agree to
disagree), and multiple-choice or ranking questions. A good survey avoids leading, double-barrelled
or ambiguous questions.

**Triangulation** combines methods or data sources so that one checks another, for example a survey
to show how common something is and interviews to explain why.

Classic examples worth knowing: Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson's **field experiment** on
teacher expectations (*Pygmalion in the Classroom*, 1968), in which teachers were told certain
pupils would "spurt" ahead; Laud Humphreys' **covert observation** in *Tearoom Trade* (1970), the
standard example of contested research ethics; and William Thomas and Florian Znaniecki's use of
**letters** in *The Polish Peasant in Europe and America* (1918–1920).

## Issues in research (3.1.3.3)

- **Operationalisation** means turning a concept into something measurable. "Religiosity" might be
  operationalised as frequency of worship, prayer or belief, and each choice gives a different
  result.
- **Population** is the whole group being studied; the **sampling frame** is the list from which a
  sample is drawn (a school register, an electoral roll). A poor frame leaves people out.
- **Types of sample**: **random** (each member has an equal chance), **systematic** (every nth name),
  **stratified random** (random within subgroups in proportion), **quota** (interviewers fill set
  numbers of people with given characteristics, not randomly), **multistage** (sampling in stages,
  such as regions, then towns, then households) and **snowball** (participants recruit others,
  useful for hidden groups).
- **Validity**: a true picture of what is studied. **Reliability**: the same results if repeated.
- **Researcher/interviewer bias**: the researcher's characteristics or wording influence answers.
- **Hawthorne effect**: people change their behaviour because they know they are being studied,
  named after studies of workers at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne Works in the 1920s and
  1930s.
- **Social desirability**: respondents give answers that make them look good.
- **Practical issues**: time, funding, access, the researcher's skills and characteristics.
- **Ethical issues**: informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity and participant wellbeing.
- **Causation and correlation**: two variables moving together (correlation) does not prove one
  causes the other; a third factor may explain both.
- **Representativeness and generalisability**: whether the sample reflects the population, and so
  whether findings can be applied more widely.

## Interpreting data (3.1.3.4)

**Quantitative routine (TREND):**
1. **T**itle and units: what is measured, where, when, in what units (percentages or numbers)?
2. **R**ead extremes: highest and lowest values.
3. **E**xamine change and comparison: differences between groups and over time.
4. **N**ote whether change is in percentage points or relative terms.
5. **D**oubt: who collected it, sample size, definitions, what is missing.

**Qualitative routine**: for field notes or interview extracts, identify what participants say and
do, the meanings they appear to give, the researcher's possible influence, and how far one setting
can be generalised.

## Worked example: reading a table

The data below are fictional, invented for practice.

| Households using mobile money in the fictional country of Tarnia (%) | 2015 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Urban | 30 | 74 |
| Rural | 12 | 51 |

*Identify two trends shown in the table.*

> Use rose in both areas: by 44 percentage points in urban areas (30% to 74%) and by 39 points in
> rural areas (12% to 51%). The gap between urban and rural households widened slightly in
> percentage points, from 18 to 23, even though rural use grew faster in relative terms, more than
> fourfold compared with about two and a half times in urban areas.

*Suggest one limitation of these data.* Percentages alone hide sample size and how "using" was
operationalised (once a year, or weekly?), so they may overstate everyday use.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **reliability** and **validity**.
- Calling a quota sample random.
- Saying a method is "unethical" without naming the principle (consent, confidentiality, anonymity,
  wellbeing).
- Treating a correlation in data as proof of cause.
- Describing a table without quoting figures and units.
- Writing generic strengths that are not applied to the issue in the question.

## Exam technique

Short questions reward precise definitions with an example. Data questions reward accurate
figures: always quote numbers with their units and say whether a difference is in percentage
points. When asked to explain a strength or limitation of a method **for studying the issue in the
item**, link the point to the people involved (are they hard to reach, vulnerable or powerful?)
and the topic (is it sensitive, private or illegal?).
