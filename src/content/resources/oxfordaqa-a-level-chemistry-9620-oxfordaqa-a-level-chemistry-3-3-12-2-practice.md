This set practises **3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 and in the synoptic Unit 5. If you get stuck, go back to the [biodegradability and disposal study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2/) or the [biodegradability and disposal revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2-revision-notes/). Use Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Na = 23.0 and Cl = 35.5.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which polymer is non-biodegradable?

A: poly(propene)

B: nylon 6,6

C: Terylene

D: Kevlar

**[1]**

**2.** Explain why poly(propene) is not hydrolysed when heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide. **[2]**

**3.** Nylon 6 has the repeating unit –[NH–(CH₂)₅–CO]–. Give the structure of the organic product when nylon 6 is completely hydrolysed by:

**(a)** excess hot hydrochloric acid **[1]**

**(b)** excess hot aqueous sodium hydroxide **[1]**

**4.** State why polyesters are described as biodegradable. **[1]**

**5.** Give one disadvantage of incinerating waste PVC, and state how the problem is reduced in practice. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** Explain why nylon 6,10 can be hydrolysed but poly(ethene) cannot. **[4]**

**7.** A polyamide is made from butane-1,4-diamine, H₂N–(CH₂)₄–NH₂, and pentanedioic acid, HOOC–(CH₂)₃–COOH.

**(a)** Name the organic products formed when the polymer is heated with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. **[2]**

**(b)** Give the structures of the organic products formed when the polymer is heated with excess aqueous sodium hydroxide. **[2]**

**8.** Waste nylon 6,6 (repeating unit Mr = 226.0) is chemically recycled by hydrolysis with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of repeating units in 1.00 kg of nylon 6,6. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum mass of hexane-1,6-diamine (Mr = 116.0) recovered. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the maximum mass of sodium hexanedioate, NaOOC–(CH₂)₄–COONa, formed. **[1]**

**9.** A waste company incinerates 250 kg of PVC, poly(chloroethene), repeating unit –[CH₂–CHCl]– (Mr = 62.5).

**(a)** Write an equation for the complete combustion of one repeating unit, C₂H₃Cl. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of hydrogen chloride formed. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest how the hydrogen chloride could be removed from the waste gases. **[1]**

**10.** A coastal town collects two kinds of plastic waste: old nylon fishing nets and broken PVC water pipes. Evaluate landfill, incineration and recycling as ways of dealing with this waste. **[6]**

**11.** A food company replaces its poly(propene) trays with trays made from a polyester. Explain one environmental advantage of this change and suggest one limitation. **[3]**

**12.** Kevlar is heated for a long time with concentrated hydrochloric acid. Give the names of the two organic products. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **A** [1]. The others contain ester or amide links that can be hydrolysed.

**2.** Poly(propene) contains only C–C and C–H bonds, which are non-polar [1]; there is no δ+ carbon for the hydroxide ion (nucleophile) to attack [1]

**3. (a)** ⁺H₃N–(CH₂)₅–COOH (as its chloride) [1]

**(b)** H₂N–(CH₂)₅–COO⁻ (as its sodium salt) [1]

**4.** The ester links can be broken down by hydrolysis (slowly, in the environment) [1]

**5.** Hydrogen chloride, a toxic and acidic gas, is produced [1]; the waste gases are passed through (neutralised by) a base before release [1]

**6.** Nylon 6,10 contains amide links with a polar C=O bond, so the carbonyl carbon is δ+ [1]; water or hydroxide ions (nucleophiles) attack this carbon and the C–N bond breaks [1]; poly(ethene) contains only C–C and C–H bonds, which are non-polar [1]; so there is no δ+ site for a nucleophile to attack [1]

*Examiner insight:* answers that say only "poly(ethene) has strong bonds" score no marks for the second half; the comparison must be about polarity.

**7. (a)** Pentanedioic acid [1]; butane-1,4-diammonium chloride (the butane-1,4-diammonium ion) [1]

**(b)** ⁻OOC–(CH₂)₃–COO⁻ (sodium pentanedioate) [1]; H₂N–(CH₂)₄–NH₂ (butane-1,4-diamine) [1]

**8. (a)** 1000 ÷ 226.0 = **4.42 mol** [1]

**(b)** 4.42 × 116.0 = **513 g** [1]

**(c)** Mr(Na₂C₆H₈O₄) = 190.0; 4.42 × 190.0 = **841 g** [1]

**9. (a)** C₂H₃Cl + 2½O₂ → 2CO₂ + H₂O + HCl (accept doubled: 2C₂H₃Cl + 5O₂ → 4CO₂ + 2H₂O + 2HCl) [1]

**(b)** n(repeating units) = 250 000 ÷ 62.5 = 4000 mol = n(HCl) [1]; mass = 4000 × 36.5 = 146 000 g = **146 kg** [1]

**(c)** Pass the gases through an alkaline or basic substance (for example a calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide scrubber) to neutralise the HCl [1]

**10.** Six marks, for balanced points linked to chemistry. Award one mark for each point, up to 6:

- Landfill is cheap and needs no sorting;
- but PVC, an addition polymer with a C–C backbone, is non-biodegradable and persists, and land is limited; nylon will break down only slowly;
- incineration reduces the volume and the heat can generate electricity;
- but it releases CO₂, and PVC releases toxic HCl that must be removed from the waste gases;
- the two kinds of waste are easy to separate (nets and pipes), so mechanical recycling is practical and saves crude oil;
- nylon, a polyamide, can also be chemically recycled by hydrolysis to its monomers;
- recycling needs collection, cleaning and energy, and quality may fall;
- a reasoned judgement, for example recycling both separately, with incineration (with gas cleaning) for any contaminated material.

*Examiner insight:* the best answers use the fact that the waste is already sorted by type, which removes the usual objection to recycling.

**11.** Advantage: the polyester's ester links can be hydrolysed, so the trays are biodegradable and do not persist in landfill or the environment [1]; this reduces long-term waste [1]. Limitation: hydrolysis in the environment is slow, so the trays still take time to break down (or the polyester may still be made from crude oil) [1]

**12.** Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid [1]; benzene-1,4-diammonium chloride (from benzene-1,4-diamine) [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying polyalkenes have "weak" bonds.
- Not stating that the carbonyl carbon is δ+.
- Free acid in alkali, or free amine in acid.
- One-sided evaluations with no judgement.
- Forgetting HCl when chlorine-containing polymers burn.
