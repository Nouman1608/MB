## 4.1.1.1 Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

| | Eukaryotic (plant, animal) | Prokaryotic (bacteria) |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Larger; can be up to about 100 times wider | Much smaller, a few μm or less |
| Genetic material | Enclosed in a **nucleus** | **Single DNA loop**, not in a nucleus |
| Plasmids | No | May have one or more small rings of DNA |
| Cell membrane and cytoplasm | Yes | Yes |
| Cell wall | Plants and algae (cellulose) | Yes, surrounding the membrane |

### Prefixes and standard form

| Prefix | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| centi | c | 10⁻² |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ |

```
mm  --×1000-->  μm  --×1000-->  nm
mm  <--÷1000--  μm  <--÷1000--  nm
```

**Order of magnitude** = a factor of 10. Divide one size by the other (same units), then write the answer as a power of ten. 10³ means three orders of magnitude.

**Worked reminder:** 30 μm = 30 × 10⁻⁶ m = **3 × 10⁻⁵ m**. Check: the number in front must be at least 1 and less than 10.

## 4.1.1.2 Animal and plant cells

**Animal cells (most):** nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria, ribosomes.

**Plant cells (often) add:** chloroplasts, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.

**Plant and algal cells also have:** a cellulose cell wall, which strengthens the cell.

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Genetic material that controls the cell's activities |
| Cytoplasm | Most chemical reactions happen here |
| Cell membrane | Controls what enters and leaves |
| Mitochondria | Aerobic respiration, releasing energy |
| Ribosomes | Protein synthesis |
| Chloroplasts | Absorb light for photosynthesis |
| Permanent vacuole | Holds cell sap |
| Cell wall (cellulose) | Strengthens the cell |
| Plasmids (bacteria) | Extra small rings of DNA |

### Estimation

Use estimation to judge the **relative** size or area of sub-cellular structures when exact measurement is not possible or not needed, such as irregular shapes or unclear edges.

> **Method in steps -- estimating size or area**
> 1. Find a known length (a scale bar or a given cell size).
> 2. Count how many times the structure fits along it, or treat it as a rectangle or circle.
> 3. Divide or multiply to get the estimate.
> 4. Write "about" or "approximately" and give a sensible number of figures.

## Required practical 1 -- method in steps

> 1. Thin layer of cells (e.g. onion epidermis) on a slide.
> 2. Add a stain (iodine solution for plant cells) so structures show up.
> 3. Lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
> 4. Lowest-power objective first; coarse focus, then fine focus.
> 5. Move to a higher-power objective; refocus with fine focus only.
> 6. Pencil drawing, clear lines, no shading, labels with ruled lines.
> 7. Add a **magnification scale** (scale bar).

Total magnification = eyepiece × objective. ×10 eyepiece and ×10 objective gives ×100.

## 4.1.1.3 Cell specialisation

| Cell | Feature → what it allows |
|---|---|
| Sperm | Tail → swims; many mitochondria → energy for swimming |
| Nerve | Long → carries impulses a long way; branched endings → links to many cells |
| Muscle | Protein fibres → contract; many mitochondria → energy |
| Root hair | Long projection → large surface area to absorb water and mineral ions |
| Xylem | Hollow tubes of dead cells, lignin → water flows freely; tubes are strong |
| Phloem | End walls with pores → dissolved sugars pass from cell to cell |

If the exam describes a cell you have not met, use the same "feature → what it allows" pattern with the information given.

## 4.1.1.4 Cell differentiation

- As an organism develops, cells **differentiate** into different types.
- A differentiating cell gains the sub-cellular structures it needs, and becomes **specialised**.
- Most **animal** cells differentiate early. Many **plant** cells keep the ability **throughout life**.
- In mature animals, cell division is mainly for **repair and replacement**.
- Importance: it produces the different cell types that form tissues and organs.

## 4.1.1.5 Microscopy

| | Light microscope | Electron microscope |
|---|---|---|
| Developed | First | Later |
| Magnification | Lower | Much higher |
| Resolution (resolving power) | Lower; cannot resolve below about 200 nm | Much higher |
| Result | Cells, nucleus, chloroplasts | Many more sub-cellular structures in fine detail |

**Magnification** = how many times bigger the image is. **Resolution** = ability to distinguish two close points as separate.

```
magnification = size of image / size of real object
```

> **Method in steps -- magnification questions**
> 1. Write the equation.
> 2. Convert both sizes to the **same unit** (usually μm).
> 3. Substitute and calculate.
> 4. Check the answer is sensible (a cell is not 3 m wide).
> 5. Give the unit for a size; no unit for magnification. Use standard form if asked.

**Scale bar:** magnification = measured length of bar / length written on bar (same units).

## 4.1.1.6 Culturing microorganisms

- Bacteria divide by **binary fission**, as often as once every **20 minutes** with enough nutrients and a suitable temperature.
- Grow them in **nutrient broth** or as **colonies on agar gel**.
- Uncontaminated cultures are needed to test disinfectants and antibiotics.

| Aseptic step | Why |
|---|---|
| Sterilise Petri dishes and media | Kill microorganisms already present |
| Flame the inoculating loop | Kill microorganisms on the loop |
| Tape the lid | Stop airborne microorganisms getting in |
| Store upside down | Condensation does not drip onto the agar |
| Incubate at 25°C (school labs) | Reduces the chance of growing human pathogens |

> **Method in steps -- bacterial numbers**
> 1. Convert the time to the same unit as the division time.
> 2. Divisions = time / mean division time.
> 3. Final number = starting number × 2^(divisions).
> 4. **Higher tier only:** write the answer in standard form.

> **Method in steps -- area of a colony or clear zone**
> 1. Halve the diameter to get r.
> 2. Area = πr².
> 3. Give the unit (mm² or cm²).

## Required practical 2 -- method in steps

> 1. Spread bacteria evenly on an agar plate using aseptic technique.
> 2. Add discs soaked in different antiseptics or antibiotics, plus a control disc (sterile water).
> 3. Tape the lid, incubate at 25°C.
> 4. Measure the diameter of each clear **zone of inhibition**.
> 5. Calculate each area with πr². Larger area = more effective.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Nucleus vs DNA loop:** bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
- **Cell membrane vs cell wall:** the membrane controls what passes in and out; the wall strengthens.
- **Magnification vs resolution:** bigger image vs finer detail.
- **Image size vs real size:** image size is the one measured on the drawing or micrograph.
- **Radius vs diameter:** πr² needs the radius.
- **Differentiation vs division:** differentiation changes what a cell is; division makes more cells.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 0.004 mm to micrometres.
2. Name the two kinds of DNA a bacterial cell may contain.
3. Name three structures found in plant cells but not animal cells.
4. An image of a cell is 12 mm wide. The real cell is 6 μm wide. Calculate the magnification.
5. A cell's image is 30 mm long at ×1500. Find its real length in μm.
6. A cell is 2 × 10⁻⁵ m long and a bacterium is 2 × 10⁻⁶ m. How many times longer is the cell?
7. Why is a root hair cell long and thin?
8. Why are school cultures incubated at 25°C?
9. A bacterium divides every 25 minutes. Starting with one, how many bacteria are there after 100 minutes?
10. A clear zone has a diameter of 14 mm. Calculate its area to 3 significant figures.
11. **Higher tier only:** a culture of 40 bacteria divides every 30 minutes. How many are there after 4 hours? Give your answer in standard form.
12. State the difference between magnification and resolution.

### Answers

1. 0.004 × 1000 = **4 μm**.
2. A **single DNA loop** and **plasmids**.
3. **Chloroplasts, permanent vacuole, cellulose cell wall.**
4. 12 mm = 12 000 μm; 12 000 / 6 = **×2000**.
5. 30 / 1500 = 0.02 mm = **20 μm**.
6. (2 × 10⁻⁵) / (2 × 10⁻⁶) = **10 times** (one order of magnitude).
7. It gives a **large surface area** for absorbing water and mineral ions.
8. It **reduces the chance of growing pathogens** harmful to humans.
9. 100 / 25 = 4 divisions; 2⁴ = **16 bacteria**.
10. r = 7 mm; π × 7² = **154 mm²**.
11. 4 h = 240 min; 240 / 30 = 8 divisions; 40 × 2⁸ = 10 240 = **1.024 × 10⁴ bacteria**.
12. Magnification is how many times bigger the image is; resolution is the ability to **distinguish two close points** as separate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "bacteria have no DNA" instead of "no nucleus".
- Leaving out "permanent" when naming the plant cell vacuole.
- Mixing units in the magnification equation, for example dividing mm by μm.
- Giving a unit for magnification, or leaving the unit off a real size.
- Using diameter in πr², which makes the area four times too big.
- Counting the wrong number of divisions when the time is in hours and the division time in minutes.
- Giving "best temperature for bacteria" as the reason for 25°C instead of safety.
- Naming a feature of a specialised cell without saying what it allows the cell to do.
- In the required practical, not including a magnification scale on the drawing.
- Saying electron microscopes are better because of "magnification" alone, without resolution.
