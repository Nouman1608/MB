## Section A

**1.** Which case set out the two-limb test for remoteness of damage in contract? A: Hadley v Baxendale. B: Robinson v Harman. C: Payzu v Saunders. D: Beswick v Beswick. **[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is an equitable remedy? A: compensatory damages. B: nominal damages. C: specific performance. D: termination for breach. **[1]**

**3.** State the aim of compensatory damages in the law of contract. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish expectation loss from reliance loss. **[2]**

**5.** Identify two situations in which specific performance will normally be refused. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain when damages for distress and disappointment can be recovered for breach of contract. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the rules on remoteness of damage, referring to two cases. **[5]**

**8.** Explain the duty to mitigate loss. **[4]**

**9.** Explain when an innocent party may terminate a contract for breach. **[4]**

**10.** *Ravi books a two-week "luxury, quiet" spa holiday for £3,000. The hotel is a building site, the spa is closed and the noise is constant. He spends £200 moving to another hotel for the second week.* Suggest what damages Ravi can recover. **[5]**

**11.** *Printwell Ltd agrees to supply a new printing press to Quill Publishing for £50,000, delivery by 1 March. Quill tells Printwell it needs the press for a regular monthly magazine contract. Printwell delivers three months late. Quill loses three months' magazine profit of £8,000 a month and also loses a one-off government contract worth £40,000 profit, which it had not mentioned. In January, Printwell had offered to lend Quill a used press for the delay period, which Quill refused without good reason.* Advise Quill on the damages it can recover. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss whether the remedies available for breach of contract are effective, with particular reference to consumers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A: Hadley v Baxendale. **[1]**

**2.** C: specific performance. **[1]**

**3.** To put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in **if the contract had been performed** [1]; damages compensate and do **not punish** the defendant (Robinson v Harman, 1848) [1].

**4.** Expectation loss is the **benefit the claimant would have received** had the contract been performed, such as lost profit [1]. Reliance loss is **expenditure wasted** in reliance on the contract, as in Anglia Television v Reed (1972) [1].

**5.** Any two of: **personal service** contracts [1]; contracts requiring **constant supervision** (Co-operative Insurance v Argyll Stores); where damages are **adequate**; where the order would cause **severe hardship** (Patel v Ali); where the claimant has acted **unfairly or delayed** [1].

**6.** The general rule is that damages are **not recoverable for injured feelings or distress** [1], as in **Addis v Gramophone (1909)** [1]. The exception is where **a major object of the contract is pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind** [1], as in **Jarvis v Swans Tours (1973)**, a disappointing holiday, or **Farley v Skinner (2001)**, a survey that failed to report aircraft noise as specifically requested [1].

**7.** Under **Hadley v Baxendale (1854)**, loss is recoverable if it arises **naturally** from the breach [1] or was in the **reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting** because of special knowledge [1]. In **Victoria Laundry v Newman (1949)** ordinary lost profits were recoverable but exceptionally lucrative contracts unknown to the seller were too remote [1]. Under **Parsons v Uttley Ingham (1978)**, if the **type** of loss is within contemplation, its full extent is recoverable [1]. **The Achilleas (2008)** added whether the defendant **assumed responsibility** for the type of loss [1].

**8.** A claimant must take **reasonable steps to reduce the loss** caused by the breach [1] and cannot recover loss that **could reasonably have been avoided** (British Westinghouse, 1912) [1]. In **Payzu v Saunders (1919)** the buyer should have accepted the seller's offer to supply for cash [1]. The standard is **reasonableness, not perfection**, and reasonable costs of mitigation are recoverable [1].

**9.** Termination is available for breach of a **condition** (Poussard v Spiers, 1876) [1]; for a breach of an **innominate term** that deprives the innocent party of **substantially the whole benefit** of the contract (Hong Kong Fir, 1962) [1]; and for **renunciation**, including anticipatory breach [1]. The innocent party must **elect** to terminate or affirm; termination for a breach that is not repudiatory is itself a repudiation [1].

**10.** The holiday was sold as "luxury, quiet", so a **major object of the contract was pleasure and relaxation** [1]. As in **Jarvis v Swans Tours (1973)**, Ravi can recover damages for **distress and disappointment**, an exception to Addis [1]. He can recover **expectation loss**: the difference between the holiday promised and the one provided, for at least the first week [1]. The **£200** spent moving was a **reasonable step in mitigation** and is recoverable [1]. Conclusion: damages for diminished value, distress for the week spent there, and the £200 cost of moving [1].

**11.** Late delivery is a **breach**; Quill accepted the press, so it claims **damages** [1]. The aim is to put Quill in the position it would have been in had delivery been on time (**expectation loss**) [1]. Lost magazine profit arises **naturally** and Printwell was **told** of the magazine contract, so it is within **Hadley v Baxendale** [1]. In principle that is 3 × £8,000 = **£24,000** [1]. The **£40,000** government contract was not mentioned and is a one-off, unusually profitable loss, so it is **too remote** (**Victoria Laundry**) [1]. **Mitigation**: refusing the loan of a used press without good reason was unreasonable [1], as in **Payzu v Saunders** [1]. Conclusion: Quill may recover little or none of the £24,000, only any loss the loaned press would not have avoided [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **Damages are available as of right** once breach and loss are shown, which gives certainty [1].
- The **expectation measure** aims at full compensation, and **reliance** and **loss of chance** fill gaps where profit is uncertain [1].
- **Remoteness** and **mitigation** limit recovery, which is fair to defendants but can leave claimants under-compensated [1].
- The **Addis** rule limits damages for distress, a problem for consumers, though **Jarvis** and **Farley** help where pleasure or peace of mind is the object [1].
- Court action is **costly and slow** relative to many consumer losses, so damages may be ineffective in practice [1].
- **Specific performance** is discretionary and rarely available for ordinary goods, so consumers cannot usually insist on performance [1].
- The **Consumer Rights Act 2015** gives clearer self-help remedies: rejection, repair or replacement, price reduction and repeat performance [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that common law remedies suit commercial parties, while statutory consumer remedies are more effective for consumers if they know their rights [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to calculate damages where figures are given.
- Applying remoteness to what the defendant knew at the time of breach.
- Missing the mitigation point when an alternative was offered.
- Treating specific performance as an automatic remedy.
- Confusing termination with rescission.
