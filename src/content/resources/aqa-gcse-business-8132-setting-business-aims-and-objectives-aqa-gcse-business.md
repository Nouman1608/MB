This guide covers section 3.1.3, Setting business aims and objectives, of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, in Topic 1, Business in the real world. The [study guide for 3.1.3 and 3.1.4](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-and-stakeholders/) defines aims and objectives, lists the main objectives with market share and dividend calculations, and outlines why objectives differ and change. This page goes further into the skills that case-study questions demand: judging a business's success against its objectives from data, handling objectives that conflict, and explaining a change of objectives from evidence in a case.

Use it with the [aims and objectives revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) and the [aims and objectives practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-aqa-gcse-business-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Students should be able to:

- understand the main aims and objectives for businesses: **survival, profit maximisation, growth (domestic and international), market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives and shareholder value**
- understand the role of objectives in running a business
- understand how and why the objectives set will differ between businesses, with reasons including **the size of the business, the level of competition faced and the type of business (not-for-profit organisations)**
- understand how and why the objectives set may change as businesses evolve, considering how larger, more established businesses might differ from smaller start-ups, for example **becoming the dominant business in the market, international expansion, increasing shareholder value and ethical and environmental considerations**
- understand that the success of a business can be measured in other ways than profit

## What each objective looks like in practice

An objective is only useful if the business can tell whether it has been met. For each objective, know what a business would measure.

| Objective | What the business would measure |
|---|---|
| Survival | Whether it covers its costs and can pay its bills each month |
| Profit maximisation | Profit compared with previous years and with what is possible |
| Growth (domestic) | Revenue, number of outlets or customers in the home country |
| Growth (international) | Sales or outlets abroad |
| Market share | Its sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Customer satisfaction | Survey scores, repeat customers, complaints, online reviews |
| Social and ethical | Packaging cut, carbon emissions reduced, suppliers paid fairly, money given to local causes |
| Shareholder value | Dividends paid and the change in share price |

Writing a target as a number with a deadline makes it measurable: "raise customer satisfaction to 85% by the end of next year" can be checked; "make customers happier" cannot.

## Judging success from data

The specification says success can be measured in ways other than profit. Case studies often give a table of results and ask whether a business has been successful. Judge it against **its own objectives**, not against profit alone.

### Worked example: Ashdown Juice Co.

Ashdown Juice Co. (fictional) set four objectives for 2026. Its results:

| Objective for 2026 | 2025 | 2026 | Met? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase revenue by 10% | £480,000 | £516,000 | (516,000 − 480,000) ÷ 480,000 × 100 = 7.5%: **missed** |
| Reach 6% market share (market worth £7.8 million in 2026) | – | £516,000 | 516,000 ÷ 7,800,000 × 100 = 6.6%: **met** |
| Customer satisfaction of at least 85% | – | 1,068 of 1,200 surveyed satisfied | 1,068 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 89%: **met** |
| Cut plastic packaging by 25% | 14.0 t | 11.2 t | (14.0 − 11.2) ÷ 14.0 × 100 = 20%: **missed** |

Profit fell from £62,000 to £55,800, a fall of 10%.

**A strong judgement:** Ashdown was partly successful. It met its market share and satisfaction objectives, which suggest a strong position for the future, but missed its revenue and packaging targets. The fall in profit may be explained by the cost of switching packaging and attracting customers, so on its own objectives the year was more successful than the profit figure suggests. Whether it was successful overall depends on which objectives the owners see as most important.

Notice the method: calculate, compare with each target, then weigh them.

## When objectives conflict

A business rarely pursues one objective alone, and objectives can pull in different directions.

| Conflict | Why they clash | How a business might decide |
|---|---|---|
| Market share and profit maximisation | Cutting prices wins customers but lowers profit per sale | Accept lower profit for a period to build share, then raise prices |
| Growth and survival | Opening new outlets ties up cash that may be needed to pay bills | Grow only when cash reserves are safe |
| Social and ethical objectives and profit | Fair pay to suppliers or greener materials raise costs | Use ethics in marketing to justify a higher price |
| Shareholder value and growth | High dividends leave less retained profit to fund expansion | Balance dividends with retained profit |

In an exam, show you understand the conflict and suggest how the business might prioritise, with a reason linked to its situation.

## Explaining why objectives differ

The specification gives three reasons. Apply them to evidence in the case.

- **Size:** a one-person start-up with no reputation and little cash is likely to focus on survival; a national chain may aim to be the dominant business in its market.
- **Level of competition:** a café facing three rivals on the same street may prioritise customer satisfaction to keep its regulars; a business with no nearby rivals may find profit maximisation easier.
- **Type of business:** a not-for-profit organisation such as a charity measures success by what it achieves for its cause, for example meals served or people helped, while still needing to cover its costs.

## Explaining why objectives change

Look for a trigger in the case. Common triggers:

- **The business grows.** A start-up that has survived its first two years can shift from survival to growth, then to market share. A large, established business may add international expansion, increasing shareholder value, or ethical and environmental objectives.
- **A new competitor enters.** Customer satisfaction or market share may become more urgent than profit.
- **The economic situation worsens.** Survival may return to the top of the list, even for an established firm.
- **The ownership changes.** A company that becomes a plc faces shareholders who expect dividends and a rising share price, so shareholder value becomes central.
- **Customer expectations change.** Growing concern about the environment can make social and ethical objectives part of the business's strategy, not an extra.

**Model answer.** *Explain one reason why a business's objectives may change as it grows.* "When a business starts, it has few customers and little cash, so its main objective is survival. Once it is established and making a profit, it can afford to aim for growth, such as opening a second branch, because it is no longer at risk of running out of money."

## The role of objectives in running a business

Objectives give direction and allow results to be measured; they also guide day-to-day decisions. If the objective is market share, a manager deciding on price might choose a lower price; if the objective is profit maximisation, the same manager might hold the price. Objectives can be broken down into targets for each department, so marketing, operations, finance and human resources all work towards the same end.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** an objective from a case: quote the evidence, such as "Ashdown wants to cut plastic", then name it (social and ethical).
- **Calculate** percentages carefully: percentage change uses the starting value; market share uses total market sales.
- **Explain:** link the objective to the business's size, competition or type.
- **Evaluate** success: compare results with each objective, then reach a judgement that recognises success is not only profit.

## Common mistakes

- Judging success by profit alone when the case gives other objectives.
- Using the final value as the base for a percentage change.
- Stating that objectives change without naming the trigger.
- Describing a conflict between objectives without saying how it might be resolved.
- Giving a large company's objectives to a start-up in the case.
