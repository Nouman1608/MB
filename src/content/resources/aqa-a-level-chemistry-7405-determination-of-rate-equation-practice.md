These questions cover section **3.1.9.2 Determination of rate equation** of the AQA A-level Chemistry specification (7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Determination of rate equation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-determination-of-rate-equation/) and the [Rate equations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-rate-equations/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain why the rate equation for a reaction cannot be deduced from the balanced equation. **[1]**

**2.** The table shows initial rates for the reaction P + Q → products.

| Experiment | [P] / mol dm⁻³ | [Q] / mol dm⁻³ | Initial rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.40 | 0.10 | 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ |
| 3 | 0.20 | 0.30 | 9.0 × 10⁻⁵ |

**(a)** Deduce the order with respect to P. **[2]**

**(b)** Deduce the order with respect to Q. **[2]**

**(c)** Write the rate equation. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate k, with units. **[3]**

**(e)** Calculate the initial rate when [P] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³ and [Q] = 0.20 mol dm⁻³. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows initial rates for the reaction R + S → products.

| Experiment | [R] / mol dm⁻³ | [S] / mol dm⁻³ | Initial rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.15 | 0.15 | 4.5 × 10⁻³ |
| 2 | 0.30 | 0.15 | 9.0 × 10⁻³ |
| 3 | 0.30 | 0.45 | 9.0 × 10⁻³ |

**(a)** Deduce the order with respect to R and with respect to S. **[3]**

**(b)** Write the rate equation and calculate k with units. **[3]**

**(c)** State what the order with respect to S tells you about the rate-determining step. **[1]**

**4.** A student follows the reaction of a reactant X by measuring its concentration at intervals. The graph is a straight line sloping down from 0.50 mol dm⁻³ at 0 s to zero at 250 s.

**(a)** State the order with respect to X. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the rate and k. **[2]**

**5.** For another reaction, tangents to the concentration–time graph of a reactant give the following gradients: at [Y] = 0.60 mol dm⁻³ the gradient is −1.8 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, and at [Y] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³ the gradient is −9.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

**(a)** Deduce the order with respect to Y. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate k with units. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how a tangent is used to find the rate of reaction at a particular time on a concentration–time graph. **[3]**

**7.** The reaction of propanone with iodine in acid solution has the rate equation: rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺].

**(a)** State the order with respect to iodine. **[1]**

**(b)** State what this shows about the role of iodine in the rate-determining step. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why H⁺ appears in the rate equation although it is not in the overall equation. **[1]**

**8.** The reaction 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ has the rate equation rate = k[NO]²[O₂].

**(a)** State the overall order. **[1]**

**(b)** Deduce the units of k. **[2]**

**(c)** The concentration of NO is halved and that of O₂ is doubled. Calculate the factor by which the rate changes. **[2]**

**9.** In an initial rate experiment, the total volume of the reaction mixture is kept constant. Explain why. **[2]**

**10.** A student concludes that a reaction is first order in a reactant because the rate doubled when its concentration was doubled. In that experiment, the temperature also rose by 10 °C. Evaluate the conclusion. **[2]**

**11.** The rate equation for a reaction is rate = k[A][B]. A proposed mechanism has a slow first step A + B → C followed by a fast step C + A → D. Comment on whether the mechanism is consistent with the rate equation. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The orders have to be found by experiment, and they are not the same as the coefficients in the balanced equation [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** Experiments 1 and 2: [P] doubles with [Q] constant and the rate increases by 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 4 [1]; second order in P [1]

**(b)** Experiments 1 and 3: [Q] triples with [P] constant and the rate increases by 9.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 3 [1]; first order in Q [1]

**(c)** rate = k[P]²[Q] [1]

**(d)** k = rate ÷ ([P]²[Q]) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.20² × 0.10) [1] = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁻³ = **7.5 × 10⁻³** [1]; units mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ [1]

**(e)** rate = 7.5 × 10⁻³ × 0.30² × 0.20 [1] = **1.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[10]**

**3. (a)** Experiments 1 and 2: [R] doubles, the rate doubles, so first order in R [1]; experiments 2 and 3: [S] triples, the rate does not change, so zero order in S [1]; the other concentration is constant in each comparison [1]

**(b)** rate = k[R] [1]; k = 4.5 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.15 = **0.030 s⁻¹** [1 for value, 1 for units]

**(c)** S is not involved in the rate-determining step [1]. **[7]**

**4. (a)** Zero order [1]

**(b)** Rate = 0.50 ÷ 250 = **2.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]; for a zero-order reaction k = rate = **2.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** When the concentration halves from 0.60 to 0.30, the rate halves (from 1.8 × 10⁻³ to 9.0 × 10⁻⁴) [1]; first order in Y [1]

**(b)** k = rate ÷ [Y] = 1.8 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.60 = **3.0 × 10⁻³** [1]; units s⁻¹ [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Draw a straight line that touches the curve at that time without crossing it [1]; find the gradient of this line by dividing the change in concentration by the change in time [1]; the rate is the magnitude of the gradient (the gradient is negative for a reactant) [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Zero order [1]

**(b)** Iodine does not take part in the rate-determining step [1]

**(c)** H⁺ acts as a catalyst, so it affects the rate although it is not used up [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 3 [1]

**(b)** k = rate ÷ ([NO]²[O₂]) so the units are (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)³ [1] = **mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Factor = (½)² × 2 [1] = **½** (the rate halves) [1]. **[5]**

**9.** The concentrations must be known and only the one being investigated must change [1]; if the total volume changed, the concentrations of all the other reactants would also change [1]. **[2]**

**10.** The conclusion is not valid [1]; the temperature rise would increase the rate as well, so the change in rate cannot be attributed to concentration alone (only one variable should change) [1]. **[2]**

**11.** The slow step involves one A and one B, which fits the first-order dependence on each in the rate equation [1]; the fast step comes after the rate-determining step, so the extra A in it does not appear in the rate equation [1]; the mechanism is consistent with the rate equation, but it is not proved by it [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking orders from the coefficients of the balanced equation.
- Comparing experiments in which more than one concentration changes.
- Wrong units for k.
- Failing to use the magnitude of the gradient for a reactant.
- Saying that a mechanism is proved by a matching rate equation.
