Section 7.1 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 covers how an analyst researches the current system, records what it does and what is wrong with it, and specifies the hardware and software for the new one. These notes condense it for recall. The [analysis study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-analysis/) has the full explanation and a worked example, and the [analysis practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-analysis-practice/) let you apply it.

## Syllabus coverage

| Syllabus wording | Recall |
|---|---|
| Analysis of the current system | Observation, interviews, questionnaires, examination of existing documents: characteristics, uses, advantages, disadvantages |
| Record and analyse information about the current system | Inputs, outputs, processing; problems; user and information requirements |
| System specification | Identify and justify suitable hardware and software |

## The four research methods at a glance

| Method | What the analyst does | Best when | Key advantage | Key disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Observation | Watches users at work | Visible, practical process | Sees what really happens | People act differently when watched |
| Interviews | Asks a few users questions face to face | A few people hold detailed knowledge | Follow-up questions possible | Slow; few people; not anonymous |
| Questionnaires | Gives written questions to many users | Many users, several sites or shifts | Quick to reach many; can be anonymous | Low response; no follow-up; may be misunderstood |
| Examining documents | Studies forms, files, reports | Need exact data items and outputs | Shows real data and volumes | May be out of date; shows nothing about use |

### Extra points that earn marks

- **Observation** is cheap and does not rely on what people say; it can miss problems that happen rarely.
- **Interviews** let the analyst see reactions and ask for examples; answers are harder to compare than ticked boxes.
- **Questionnaires** with **closed questions** are easy to analyse, especially online; **open questions** give detail but take longer to analyse.
- **Documents** feed directly into later design work, because they show field names, data formats and report layouts.

## Recording the current system

Five headings:

1. **Inputs**: data entering the system, and how (forms, phone calls, keyboard).
2. **Processing**: what is done to the data (searching, calculating, sorting, updating).
3. **Outputs**: what is produced (reports, letters, invoices, lists).
4. **Problems**: slow, inaccurate, duplicated or lost data; reliance on one person.
5. **Requirements** for the new system:
   - **User requirements**: what users must be able to *do* ("search by surname").
   - **Information requirements**: what the system must *produce* ("weekly sales report").

Each problem should lead to a requirement. Slow searches → "find a record in seconds".

## The system specification

- Lists the **hardware** (input, output, storage, processing, network devices) and **software** (applications and any specialist programs) for the new system.
- Every item must be **justified** from the scenario: barcode scanner *because* items are sold in large numbers; database software *because* records must be searched and sorted.
- Consider: number of users, volume of data, need for sharing, need for printing, need for back-up, mobility of users.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Systems analyst | Person who investigates the current system and plans the new one |
| Current system | The system in use now, paper-based or computerised |
| Closed question | Question with fixed answers to choose from, such as yes/no or a rating |
| Open question | Question the person answers in their own words |
| Inputs | Data that enters the system |
| Processing | Operations carried out on the data |
| Outputs | Information produced by the system |
| User requirements | What users need to be able to do with the new system |
| Information requirements | What information the new system must produce |
| System specification | The hardware and software identified and justified for the new system |

## Exam traps

- **Analysis is not design.** Drawing data capture forms and choosing validation checks happen in design.
- **"Gets more information"** is too vague as an advantage of interviews; give the reason (follow-up questions).
- **Questionnaire answers are not always more honest.** They *can* be anonymous, which may encourage honesty; say so precisely.
- **Justify with the scenario.** "Laser printer because it is fast" is weaker than "laser printer because 300 invoices are printed every day".
- **Observation's disadvantage** is that people change their behaviour, not that "the analyst might be wrong".

## Self-test

1. Name the four research methods in the syllabus.
2. Give one advantage of observation over interviews.
3. Give two disadvantages of interviews.
4. Why might a questionnaire suit staff who work on different shifts?
5. What is the difference between a closed and an open question?
6. Give one advantage of examining existing documents.
7. State the three things recorded about how the current system works, besides its problems.
8. Define user requirements.
9. Give an example of an information requirement for a school library system.
10. What is a system specification?
11. A shop sells 2000 items a day. Identify one input device for the new system and justify it.
12. Why should every problem found lead to a requirement?

## Self-test answers

1. Observation, interviews, questionnaires, examination of existing documents.
2. It shows what really happens rather than what the person says happens.
3. Any two of: time-consuming; only a few people can be asked; not anonymous, so answers may be less honest; expensive in staff time.
4. They can complete it at a convenient time, and it is hard to arrange interviews across shifts.
5. A closed question has fixed answers to choose from; an open question is answered in the person's own words.
6. Any one of: shows the exact data items and formats; shows the volume of data; does not disturb staff.
7. Inputs, processing and outputs.
8. What the users need to be able to do with the new system.
9. For example, a weekly list of overdue books with borrowers' names.
10. The identified and justified hardware and software needed for the new system.
11. A barcode scanner, because items can be entered quickly and accurately when so many are sold each day.
12. So the new system solves the problems of the current one, and its success can later be checked against those requirements.
