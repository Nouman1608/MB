These questions cover sections 1.1 (Types of business organisation) and 1.2 (Accounting concepts) of Topic 1 in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. All businesses and people are invented and amounts are in dollars ($). The specification does not list business-type characteristics or stakeholder groups, so those questions use standard accounting knowledge.

## Questions

**1.** State two characteristics of a sole trader. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two characteristics of public sector organisations. **[4]**

**3.** Calum Skerritt and Raoul Pemberley plan to open a tile shop together as a partnership.

**(a)** Explain two advantages to them of forming a partnership rather than each trading as a sole trader. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one disadvantage of a partnership. **[2]**

**4.** Mosscroft Bakery is a sole trader business. State one item in its financial statements that each stakeholder might use, and why.

**(a)** The bank manager, considering an overdraft request. **[2]**
**(b)** A baker employed by the business. **[2]**
**(c)** The supplier of flour, who sells on credit. **[2]**
**(d)** The tax authority. **[2]**

**5.** Name the accounting concept shown in each situation.

**(a)** Tideswell Textiles has a highly skilled workforce, but no value for it appears in the statement of financial position. **[1]**
**(b)** An owner's family holiday is paid from the business bank account and recorded as drawings. **[1]**
**(c)** December's electricity is charged to December although the bill is paid in January. **[1]**
**(d)** A box of paper clips costing 8 is treated as an expense. **[1]**
**(e)** Closing inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. **[1]**
**(f)** The same method of calculating depreciation is used as last year. **[1]**

**6.** Tideswell Textiles holds 80 rolls of cloth that cost 25 each. A fall in market prices means each roll now has a net realisable value of 21.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost and total net realisable value of the rolls. **[2]**
**(b)** State the value at which the rolls should appear and the effect on profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain which accounting concept supports this treatment. **[1]**

**7.** Mosscroft Bakery pays 4,800 on 1 April for 12 months' rent. Its year ends on 30 June.

**(a)** Calculate the rent expense for the year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the amount that is a prepayment at 30 June. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the accruals concept. **[2]**

**8.** Calum Skerritt is a sole trader. His opening capital is 38,000 and his profit for the year is 19,500. During the year he took 9,800 in cash, goods that cost 1,500 and paid a 1,100 personal phone bill from the business account.

**(a)** Calculate his total drawings. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate his closing capital. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the personal items are not recorded as business expenses. **[2]**

**9.** Fostergate Joinery has annual revenue of 250,000. During the year it buys a pencil sharpener for 15 and a delivery van for 18,500.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of each item as a percentage of revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** State how each item is likely to be treated, using the concept of materiality. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the same item might be treated differently by a much smaller business. **[2]**

**10.** Raoul Pemberley bought a machine for 36,000. In year 1 he charged depreciation of 10% of cost each year. In year 2 he changed to reducing balance at 20% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the year 1 charge and the net book value at the end of year 1. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the year 2 charge under reducing balance and the difference from the year 1 method. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain which concept the change affects and why it matters to users. **[2]**

**11.** A newsletter states that a business's statement of financial position shows the full worth of the firm. Explain two reasons, based on the money measurement concept, why this claim may be wrong. **[4]**

**12.** Calum Skerritt trades alone as Skerritt Tiling with capital of 18,000 and profit last year of 31,200. He needs 40,000 to buy a workshop. Raoul Pemberley offers to join him as a partner and put in 22,000, with profit shared equally. Evaluate whether Calum should accept. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: owned by one person [1]; the owner keeps all the profit [1]; the owner has unlimited liability, so is personally responsible for the debts; simple to set up. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" asks for short points, so two clear characteristics are enough. Writing "small business" is vague and may not be enough on its own.

**2.** Owned and controlled by the government [1], so decisions follow public policy, not the wishes of private owners [1]. Main aim is to provide a service to the public, for example health care [1], not to make a profit for owners [1]. Funded mainly by taxes is another valid characteristic. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" calls for a point plus a reason or consequence. A list of examples, such as "hospitals and schools", does not explain a characteristic.

**3. (a)** More capital because both contribute [1], which can pay for a larger shop or stock [1]. Different skills, for example one in tiling and one in selling [1], so the business is better run [1]. Shared workload or ideas would also be sensible points. **[4]**
**(b)** Profit must be shared [1], so each receives less than a sole trader would from the same profit [1]. Disagreements, or partners being personally responsible for the debts, are other sound disadvantages. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each advantage needs the point and its effect on the business. "More money" alone is thinner than "more capital, so a bigger shop".

**4. (a)** Profit for the year or assets [1], to judge whether the bakery could repay an overdraft [1].
**(b)** Profit for the year [1], to judge whether the job is secure or a pay rise is affordable [1].
**(c)** Trade payables compared with cash and receivables [1], to judge whether the bakery can pay for flour on time [1].
**(d)** Profit for the year [1], as the basis for calculating the tax due [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Each part asks for an item and a reason. Naming the stakeholder's interest without a figure from the statements leaves half the answer missing.

**5. (a)** Money measurement [1] **(b)** Business entity [1] **(c)** Accruals [1] **(d)** Materiality [1] **(e)** Prudence [1] **(f)** Consistency [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A single concept name is the whole answer here. Naming two concepts for one situation risks losing the credit.

**6. (a)** Cost = 80 × 25 = 2,000 [1]; net realisable value = 80 × 21 = 1,680 [1].
**(b)** The rolls appear at the lower figure, **1,680** [1], so profit falls by 2,000 − 1,680 = **320** [1].
**(c)** Prudence: a likely loss is recognised as soon as it is foreseen. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State both the value and the profit effect separately. Giving only the 320 does not say which value is used.

**7. (a)** Monthly rent = 4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 [1]; April to June is 3 months, so the expense is **1,200** [1].
**(b)** The other 9 months, 9 × 400 = **3,600**, is a prepayment. [1]
**(c)** Income and expenses are recorded in the period in which they are earned or incurred [1], not in the period in which cash is received or paid [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the monthly figure before the final answer. A correct number from a wrong period shows up as a different expense, so the working is worth setting out.

**8. (a)** Total drawings = cash + goods + personal bill [1] = 9,800 + 1,500 + 1,100 [1] = **12,400** [1].
**(b)** Closing capital = 38,000 + 19,500 − 12,400 [1] = **45,100** [1].
**(c)** The business is a separate entity from its owner [1], so private spending is not a cost of the business and is not deducted in working out profit. [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is subtracting drawings from profit before showing profit. Keep profit at 19,500 and deduct drawings from capital.

**9. (a)** Sharpener: 15 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **0.006%** [1]; van: 18,500 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **7.4%** [1].
**(b)** The sharpener is immaterial and is likely to be charged as an expense [1]; the van is material and is likely to be recorded as a non-current asset [1].
**(c)** Materiality depends on the size of the business [1], so an amount that is trivial here could affect the decisions of users of a much smaller business's statements. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Material" needs a comparison with something, usually revenue or profit. A statement that an item is "small" with no comparison is weaker.

**10. (a)** Year 1 charge = 36,000 × 10% = **3,600** [1]; net book value = 36,000 − 3,600 = **32,400** [1].
**(b)** Year 2 charge = 32,400 × 20% = **6,480** [1]; difference = 6,480 − 3,600 = **2,880** higher [1].
**(c)** Consistency [1]: the method has changed, so profit for the two years is not comparable and users cannot tell whether performance or the method caused the change. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the answer to comparability. Saying only "the method should always stay the same" overlooks that a change can be justified.

**11.** Only items that can be measured in money are recorded, so things such as a skilled workforce or a good reputation are not in the statement [1], although they add to the firm's worth [1]. Money changes in value over time, so assets bought at different dates are recorded at figures that are not like for like [1], and may not show current worth [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate reasons are asked for. Two examples of the same reason, such as staff skills and staff loyalty, count as one idea.

**12.** Advantages: capital would be 18,000 + 22,000 = 40,000 [1], exactly the amount needed [1]; Raoul may bring extra skills and share the workload [1]. Disadvantages: if profit stays at 31,200, each partner's share would be 31,200 ÷ 2 = 15,600 [1], so Calum's income falls by 15,600 [1]; he loses sole control of decisions [1]; partners are personally responsible for the debts, which could include debts arising from Raoul's decisions [1]. Judgement: accepting is reasonable only if the workshop is likely to raise profit by more than the share given away, or if no other finance is available, and a partnership agreement should set out how decisions are made [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation answers tend to gain more when the figures are used and a supported decision is reached. A list of advantages and disadvantages with no conclusion stops short of evaluating.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "limited liability" for a sole trader or an ordinary partnership.
- Describing a stakeholder's interest without naming an item in the financial statements.
- Naming a concept without linking it to the facts in the question.
- Treating prudence as always choosing the lowest number.
- Counting the whole payment as this year's expense and forgetting the prepayment.
- Deducting drawings from profit, not from capital.
- Calling an item material or immaterial without any comparison.
- Stating a conclusion in an evaluate question with no figures to support it.
