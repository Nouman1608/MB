This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5.1 General properties of transition metals** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The specification states that "transition metal characteristics of elements Ti–Cu arise from an incomplete sub-level in atoms or ions" and lists four characteristic properties: complex formation, formation of coloured ions, variable oxidation state and catalytic activity. It defines a **ligand**, a **complex** and **co-ordination number**. This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

## The incomplete d sub-level

Titanium to copper all have electrons in the 3d sub-level. What makes them transition metals is that the 3d sub-level is **incomplete** (holds between one and nine electrons) in the atom, or in at least one common ion.

| Element | Atom | A common ion | Ion configuration | 3d incomplete in the ion? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ti | [Ar] 3d² 4s² | Ti³⁺ | [Ar] 3d¹ | yes |
| V | [Ar] 3d³ 4s² | V³⁺ | [Ar] 3d² | yes |
| Cr | [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ | Cr³⁺ | [Ar] 3d³ | yes |
| Mn | [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² | Mn²⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁵ | yes |
| Fe | [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² | Fe³⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁵ | yes |
| Co | [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s² | Co²⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁷ | yes |
| Ni | [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s² | Ni²⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁸ | yes |
| Cu | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ | Cu²⁺ | [Ar] 3d⁹ | yes |

Two rules for writing these configurations:

1. **Chromium and copper** are exceptions in the atom: 3d⁵ 4s¹ and 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
2. When the atoms form ions, **4s electrons are lost before 3d electrons**.

### Copper: why "atoms or ions" matters

A copper atom has a full 3d sub-level (3d¹⁰). On atoms alone, copper would not qualify. But Cu²⁺ is 3d⁹, which is incomplete, so copper shows the transition metal properties: [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is a blue complex ion. Cu⁺ is 3d¹⁰ and its compounds are typically white or colourless.

### Zinc: d block but not a transition metal

Zinc forms only Zn²⁺, which is [Ar] 3d¹⁰. Neither the atom nor its ion has an incomplete d sub-level, so zinc compounds are white and zinc has one oxidation state in its compounds. That is why the specification lists Ti to Cu, not the whole d block.

## The four characteristic properties

Each property is studied in more detail later in section 3.2.5. Here you need to recognise them and give an example.

| Property | Example | Studied in |
|---|---|---|
| Complex formation | [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [CuCl₄]²⁻ | 3.2.5.2, 3.2.5.3 |
| Formation of coloured ions | Cu²⁺(aq) is blue; Ni²⁺(aq) is green | 3.2.5.4 |
| Variable oxidation state | iron as +2 and +3; vanadium from +2 to +5 | 3.2.5.5 |
| Catalytic activity | iron in the Haber process; vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process | 3.2.5.6 |

All four follow from the d electrons. Partly filled d orbitals allow electrons to move between d energy levels (colour), the similar energies of the 4s and 3d electrons allow different numbers to be lost (variable oxidation state), and the ability to change oxidation state or to bond to other species underlies catalysis.

## Ligands, complexes and co-ordination number

The specification's definitions are worth learning word for word.

- **Ligand**: a molecule or ion that forms a **co-ordinate bond** with a transition metal ion by **donating a pair of electrons**.
- **Complex**: a central metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands.
- **Co-ordination number**: the number of co-ordinate bonds to the central metal atom or ion.

A co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond is a shared pair of electrons in which **both electrons come from the same atom**, here the ligand's donor atom. Every ligand must therefore have at least one lone pair.

| Ligand | Donor atom | Charge on ligand |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | O | 0 |
| NH₃ | N | 0 |
| Cl⁻ | Cl | −1 |

Note that co-ordination number counts **bonds**, not ligands. A ligand that forms two co-ordinate bonds (a bidentate ligand, met in 3.2.5.2) contributes two to the co-ordination number.

## Analysing a complex

Four questions can be asked about any complex: what the ligand is and which atom donates, the co-ordination number, the oxidation state of the metal, and the overall charge. Each is found from the formula.

### Worked example 1: [CoCl₄]²⁻

```
ligand:              Cl⁻, donating a lone pair from chlorine
co-ordination number: 4 co-ordinate bonds
oxidation state:     x + 4(−1) = −2, so x = +2
metal ion:           Co²⁺, [Ar] 3d⁷ (incomplete)
```

### Worked example 2: a complex with two kinds of ligand

Chromium(III) is bonded to four ammonia molecules and two chloride ions. Write the formula of the complex and give its co-ordination number.

```
co-ordination number: 4 + 2 = 6
charge:              +3 + 4(0) + 2(−1) = +1
formula:             [Cr(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺
```

### Worked example 3: deducing an oxidation state from a salt

Potassium hexachloroferrate has the formula K₃[FeCl₆]. Deduce the oxidation state of iron and its electron configuration.

```
three K⁺ balance the complex, so its charge is 3−: [FeCl₆]³⁻
x + 6(−1) = −3, so x = +3
Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵
```

## Common mistakes

- Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming ions: Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².
- Writing chromium as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² or copper as [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s².
- Defining a ligand as "something that bonds to a metal" without mentioning the donated electron pair.
- Counting ligands rather than co-ordinate bonds for the co-ordination number.
- Calling zinc a transition metal.
- Saying a co-ordinate bond is "an ionic bond to the metal".

## Exam technique

- **"Define"**: use the specification wording; "donates a pair of electrons" and "co-ordinate bond" are the marking words for a ligand.
- **"Explain why copper is a transition metal"**: refer to the Cu²⁺ ion, 3d⁹, not the atom.
- **"Deduce the oxidation state"**: show the sum of charges, including any counter-ions outside the square brackets.
- **"Give the co-ordination number"**: count bonds to the metal.
