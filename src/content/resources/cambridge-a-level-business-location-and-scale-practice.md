## Questions

**1.** Define *offshoring*. **[2]**

**2.** A chain of bakeries is choosing where to open a new shop. Identify **two** factors, other than the cost of labour, that it should consider. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **two** reasons why an international location decision is more complex than a local one. **[4]**

**4.** Anselby Furniture makes 20,000 chairs a year. At its local site, fixed costs are 150,000 a year and variable cost is 22 per chair. At a site in another country, fixed costs are 190,000 a year, variable cost is 15 per chair, and shipping adds 3.50 per chair. Calculate the total cost and the unit cost at each site and state which is cheaper. **[5]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business might bring production back to its home country (reshoring) and **one** effect this could have on the business. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **one** internal economy of scale that a large food manufacturer might gain. **[3]**

**7.** Distinguish between an internal economy of scale and an external economy of scale. Give an example of each. **[4]**

**8.** Halbury Plastics makes crates. Total cost is 45,000 at 5,000 crates, 70,000 at 10,000 crates and 160,000 at 20,000 crates.
**(a)** Calculate the unit cost at each output. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in unit cost when output rises from 10,000 to 20,000 crates, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** possible cause of the change in (b). **[2]**

**9.** Explain **two** factors that might keep a hair salon small in scale. **[4]**

**10.** Meadowquay Cycles builds bicycles for its home market. Analyse **two** ways in which globalisation could affect where it chooses to make its bicycles. **[6]**

**11.** Glenwhistle Electronics assembles 60,000 units a year at a plant abroad. Unit variable costs are: labour 3.20, components 14.00, shipping and duty 2.10, and extra stock holding 0.90. Fixed costs are 420,000 a year. Wages abroad have now risen by 50%. The directors are considering moving assembly back home, where labour would be 5.60 per unit, components 14.00 and delivery 0.40, with fixed costs of 480,000 a year and a one-off cost of 300,000 to build the new plant. Customers say they want faster delivery.
**(a)** Calculate the unit cost at the overseas plant after the wage rise. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the unit cost at the home plant. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether Glenwhistle should reshore. **[7]**

**12.** Tolmere Bakeries has this unit cost data: 0.82 at 20,000 loaves a week, 0.64 at 40,000, 0.58 at 60,000, 0.61 at 80,000 and 0.69 at 100,000. The owner wants to expand output from 60,000 to 100,000 loaves a week.
**(a)** State the output with the lowest unit cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in unit cost from 60,000 to 100,000 loaves, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the owner should expand to 100,000 loaves. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Offshoring is moving some or all of a business's operations (production or services) [1] to another country, whether to its own overseas site or to a supplier abroad [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a definition tends to need both moving operations and another country. "Using cheap labour abroad" describes a motive, not the meaning.

**2.** Closeness to customers or footfall [1]; rent or cost of premises [1]. (Also valid: transport links, competitors nearby.) **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "identify" asks for a short statement only; explanation adds nothing, and repeating labour, which the question rules out, earns nothing.

**3.** First reason: different currencies [1], so costs and revenues move with exchange rates, which adds uncertainty to the cost comparison [1]. Second reason: different laws, languages and customs, or longer supply chains [1], so contracts, standards and delivery times are harder to manage and delays more likely [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason needs a development to earn the second mark. A list of "currency, law, language" with no consequence for the business usually earns the identification marks only.

**4.** Local: variable cost = 22 × 20,000 = 440,000, so total cost = 440,000 + 150,000 = **590,000** [1]; unit cost = 590,000 ÷ 20,000 = **29.50** [1]. Overseas: variable cost per chair = 15 + 3.50 = 18.50, so total = 18.50 × 20,000 + 190,000 = **560,000** [1]; unit cost = 560,000 ÷ 20,000 = **28.00** [1]. The overseas site is cheaper by 1.50 per chair [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* shipping is part of the overseas cost; leaving it out gives a wrong comparison, and shown working lets a slip be recognised.

**5.** Reason: wages abroad have risen, so the cost advantage of offshoring has shrunk (or: long lead times and quality problems abroad) [1], and bringing production home may now cost little more or even less [1]. Effect: shorter delivery times, so the business can respond faster to customers [1], although it may face the cost of building or fitting out a plant at home [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the reason should be one that arises from being abroad. "Cheaper labour at home" would be an offshoring-style argument and is unlikely to fit.

**6.** Purchasing economy: a large manufacturer buys ingredients in bulk [1], so suppliers give discounts per tonne [1], which lowers the cost of each unit it produces [1]. (Technical, financial, marketing or managerial economies also fit.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the final link to lower unit cost is what separates a described advantage from an economy of scale.

**7.** An internal economy is a fall in unit cost caused by the growth of the firm itself [1]; for example, bulk-buying discounts as it orders more [1]. An external economy is a fall in unit cost caused by the growth of the industry or area, benefiting all firms in it [1]; for example, a local college that trains skilled workers for all the firms nearby [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the distinction turns on the source of the saving (the firm or the industry); examples should make that source clear.

**8. (a)** 45,000 ÷ 5,000 = **9.00** [1]; 70,000 ÷ 10,000 = **7.00** [1]; 160,000 ÷ 20,000 = **8.00** [1]. **(b)** (8 − 7) ÷ 7 × 100 [1] = **+14.3%** [1]. **(c)** Output doubled, but communication and coordination across a larger workforce became harder [1], so total cost rose faster than output, which is a diseconomy of scale [1]. (Lower motivation also fits.) **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* state the direction as well as the size in (b); a rise should be shown as positive. In (c), name the diseconomy and link it to unit cost.

**9.** Factor 1: the product is a personal service [1], so each stylist can serve only a few customers a day, which limits growth without hiring more [1]. Factor 2: the owner may prefer control and a quiet life over growth [1], so would not take on the finance or management load of a bigger salon [1]. (Also valid: small local market, limited finance.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain two" needs two distinct factors, each applied to a salon rather than to business in general.

**10.** First way: lower trade barriers and cheaper transport [1] mean Meadowquay could make frames or whole bicycles abroad where labour is cheaper and ship them home at a competitive cost [1]. Second way: overseas rivals can sell into its home market at lower prices [1], which pressures Meadowquay to cut costs, possibly by offshoring or by moving to a cheaper site [1]. Emerging markets also offer new customers, so a site nearer them could appeal [1], though rising wages abroad or shipping disruption could weaken the case [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* analysis is a chain of cause and effect for the business named; stating a trend and jumping to a conclusion leaves the chain incomplete.

**11. (a)** Wage abroad = 3.20 × 1.5 = 4.80. Variable cost = 4.80 + 14.00 + 2.10 + 0.90 = 21.80 [1]. Total cost = 21.80 × 60,000 + 420,000 = 1,728,000 [1]. Unit cost = **28.80** [1]. **(b)** Variable cost = 5.60 + 14.00 + 0.40 = 20.00 [1]. Total = 20.00 × 60,000 + 480,000 = 1,680,000, so unit cost = **28.00** [1]. **(c)** For: home unit cost is 0.80 lower, a saving of 48,000 a year [1]. Faster delivery meets what customers want and less stock needs to be held [1]. Less exposure to exchange rates, shipping disruption and further wage rises abroad [1]. Against: the one-off 300,000 takes 300,000 ÷ 48,000 = 6.25 years to recover [1], a long period if the market changes. The saving is small (under 3% of unit cost) and home labour cost could rise [1]. Staying keeps the existing set-up, avoiding disruption [1]. Judgement: reshoring is justified only if faster delivery wins or protects sales worth more than the 6.25-year payback implies, since cost alone gives a thin case [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* an evaluate answer needs the calculated figures used as evidence, a balance of both sides, and a final decision with a reason; recalculating (a) and (b) without using them in (c) tends to leave the judgement unsupported.

**12. (a)** **60,000 loaves** (0.58) [1]. **(b)** (0.69 − 0.58) ÷ 0.58 × 100 [1] = **+19.0%** [1]. **(c)** Unit cost rises beyond 60,000, so expansion brings diseconomies of scale, perhaps from communication or coordination problems [1]. Total cost rises from 34,800 to 69,000, a rise of 98.3% for a 66.7% rise in output [1]. Against this, extra sales could raise revenue and market share if demand exists, and profit depends on the selling price relative to 0.69 [1]. Judgement: expand only if the price and demand justify it and managers can offset the diseconomies; otherwise stay near 60,000 [1]. One further point of balance: a gradual step to 80,000, where unit cost is 0.61, would test demand at lower cost risk [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* using the figures in the table strengthens an evaluation. A bare "it depends" with no reason for what it depends on tends to be treated as a weak judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining offshoring as outsourcing, or giving only a motive such as cheap labour.
- Leaving shipping, duty or stock costs out of an overseas cost comparison.
- Calling a saving an internal economy when the cause is the industry or area.
- Giving a percentage change without a sign or direction, or dividing by the new figure instead of the old.
- Evaluating with no judgement, or one not tied to the numbers.
