## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between total utility and marginal utility. **[2]**

**2.** An invented consumer's total utility from weekly visits to a climbing wall is: 1 visit 26, 2 visits 46, 3 visits 60, 4 visits 68, 5 visits 72, 6 visits 72, 7 visits 68 utils.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal utility of the 3rd, 5th and 7th visit. **[3]**
**(b)** State the number of visits that maximises total utility and the marginal utility at that point. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the pattern of marginal utility. **[2]**

**3.** Fran has 40 cu to spend on good F (4 cu each) and good G (6 cu each). Marginal utility in utils:

| Unit | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F | 40 | 32 | 24 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 0 |
| G | 54 | 42 | 30 | 24 | 12 | | |

**(a)** Find the bundle that maximises her total utility and show that the equi-marginal principle holds. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate her total utility at that bundle and at 7 units of F with 2 units of G. Comment. **[3]**

**4.** A good has marginal utility 36, 30, 24, 18, 12 and 6 utils for units 1 to 6. Each cu of income is worth 3 utils to the consumer.

**(a)** Calculate the quantity bought at a price of 9 cu and at 5 cu. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the individual demand curve slopes downward. **[2]**

**5.** Evaluate the limitations of marginal utility theory. **[6]**

**6.** A consumer has income 360 cu. Good X costs 12 cu and good Y costs 18 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the intercepts of the budget line. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate its slope. **[1]**
**(c)** The price of X falls to 9 cu. Describe and quantify the change. **[2]**
**(d)** Both original prices rise by 20 per cent. Describe and quantify the change. **[2]**

**7.** Points on one indifference curve for goods X and Y are A (2, 36), B (4, 18), C (6, 12), D (9, 8) and E (12, 6).

**(a)** Calculate the MRS of X for Y from A to B, B to C and C to D. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what the pattern shows about the shape of the curve. **[1]**
**(c)** Income is 72 cu, X costs 6 cu and Y costs 3 cu. Show that C is the optimum. **[3]**

**8.** A consumer has income 200 cu. Y costs 10 cu. The price of X falls from 10 cu to 5 cu. Quantities of (X, Y): original optimum (8, 12), new optimum (20, 10), and the compensated point at the new price ratio on the original curve (12, 9.5).

**(a)** Calculate the income of the compensated budget line, at the new prices. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the substitution effect, the income effect and the price effect on X. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why X is a normal good. **[2]**
**(d)** State two points on the consumer's demand curve for X. **[2]**

**9.** A consumer has income 150 cu. Y costs 10 cu. The price of Z rises from 5 to 6 cu. Quantities of Z: original optimum 20 (with 5 of Y), compensated point 17 (with 6.8 of Y), new optimum 19 (with 3.6 of Y).

**(a)** Calculate the income that keeps the original bundle affordable at the new prices. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the substitution, income and price effects on Z. **[3]**
**(c)** Classify Z, justifying your answer. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why a Giffen good must be an inferior good, but most inferior goods are not Giffen goods. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate how useful indifference curve analysis is for explaining consumer behaviour. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Total utility is the satisfaction from all units consumed [1]. Marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain credit when both terms are defined in the same sentence structure, with "all units" set against "one more unit".

**2. (a)** MU of 3rd = 60 − 46 = **14** [1]. MU of 5th = 72 − 68 = **4** [1]. MU of 7th = 68 − 72 = **−4** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** TU is highest at **5 or 6 visits** (72) [1] and MU of the 6th is **0** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** MU falls from 26 to 4 and then below zero [1], because each extra visit adds less satisfaction as wants are met and tiredness sets in (diminishing marginal utility) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign of a negative marginal utility, and link the fall to the number of units consumed in a given time.

**3. (a)** MU per cu for F: 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 1, 0. For G: 9, 7, 5, 4, 2 [1]. Buy the units with the highest ratios until 40 cu is spent: 4 units of F and 4 units of G [1]. Cost = 16 + 24 = 40 cu, and the last unit of each has MU per cu = 4, so the ratios are equal [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** TU at (4, 4) = (40 + 32 + 24 + 16) + (54 + 42 + 30 + 24) = 112 + 150 = **262** [1]. TU at (7, 2) = 124 + 96 = **220**, which also costs 28 + 12 = 40 cu [1]. The alternative gives 42 fewer utils at the same cost, so the equal-ratio bundle is better [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the ratio for each unit and compare bundles of equal cost. Comparing bundles of different cost proves nothing.

**4. (a)** At 9 cu, buy while MU ≥ 27: units 1 and 2, so **2 units** [1]. At 5 cu, buy while MU ≥ 15: units 1 to 4, so **4 units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Marginal utility diminishes with each extra unit [1]. A lower price makes lower-value units worth buying, so quantity demanded rises as price falls [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the assumption that the marginal utility of money stays constant. Without it the derivation is incomplete.

**5.** Utils cannot be measured, so the numbers are not observable [1]. The marginal utility of money is unlikely to be constant when prices change a lot [1]. Consumers may be influenced by habit, advertising, or limited information, so they may not equalise MU per cu [1]. Goods are not independent: complements and substitutes affect each other's MU [1]. Judgement: the theory still explains why demand slopes downward and gives a clear rule for allocation [1], so it is a useful model, but it is weaker as a tool for predicting precise choices [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A list of limitations gains little. Weigh how far each one damages the theory's main result.

**6. (a)** X intercept = 360 ÷ 12 = **30** [1]. Y intercept = 360 ÷ 18 = **20** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Slope = 12 ÷ 18 = **0.67** (2/3) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The line pivots outward on the Y intercept [1]. The X intercept becomes 360 ÷ 9 = **40**, and the Y intercept stays at 20 [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The line shifts inward in parallel [1]. New intercepts are 360 ÷ 14.4 = **25** for X and 360 ÷ 21.6 = **16.7** for Y, equal to an income of 300 cu at the old prices [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the intercept that stays fixed in a pivot, and give the numbers for both intercepts.

**7. (a)** A to B: 18 Y given up for 2 X, so MRS = **9** [1]. B to C: 6 ÷ 2 = **3** [1]. C to D: 4 ÷ 3 = **1.33** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The MRS diminishes, so the curve is convex to the origin [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** C costs 6 × 6 + 3 × 12 = 72 cu, so it is affordable [1]. B and D cost 78 cu each, which is unaffordable [1]. The price ratio is 6 ÷ 3 = 2, which lies between the MRS from B to C (3) and from C to D (1.33), so C is the best affordable point [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the MRS with the price ratio and say which way the consumer would move. A statement that C is "tangent" needs this support.

**8. (a)** 5 × 12 + 10 × 9.5 = **155 cu** [1]. This is slightly below the 160 cu that would rebuy the original bundle. **[1]**
**(b)** Substitution effect = 12 − 8 = **+4** [1]. Income effect = 20 − 12 = **+8** [1]. Price effect = **+12** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** A fall in price raises real income, and quantity rises through the income effect [1]. Both effects raise quantity, so X is a normal good [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At 10 cu, **8 units** [1]. At 5 cu, **20 units** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give price and quantity together for each demand point. Pairs reversed or without units lose clarity.

**9. (a)** 6 × 17 + 10 × 6.6 = **168 cu** [1]. This is slightly below the 170 cu that would rebuy the original bundle. **[1]**
**(b)** Substitution effect = 17 − 20 = **−3** [1]. Income effect = 19 − 17 = **+2** [1]. Price effect = **−1** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Z is an inferior good, because the fall in real income raises its quantity (income effect +2) [1]. It is not a Giffen good, because the income effect is smaller than the substitution effect and quantity falls when price rises [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the sizes of the two effects. Naming the signs without comparing them does not settle whether the good is Giffen.

**10.** A Giffen good is one for which quantity demanded rises when price rises [1]. This requires an income effect that outweighs the substitution effect [1]. The income effect works against the substitution effect only for an inferior good, where lower real income raises demand [1]. Most inferior goods take a small share of the budget, so the income effect is too small to outweigh the substitution effect [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the sequence price rise, fall in real income, extra demand for the inferior good, and compare the two effects.

**11.** Strengths: it separates the income and substitution effects and shows how a change in price or income alters choice [1]. It needs only a ranking of bundles, not measurable utils [1]. It explains why a Giffen good is possible, which MU theory cannot [1]. Weaknesses: only two goods can be drawn [1], the curves cannot be observed [1], and it assumes rational, consistent, well-informed consumers with stable tastes [1]. Judgement: it is a model for understanding the logic of choice, so it is most useful for explaining why behaviour changes [1]; it is less reliable for predicting exact choices without real data [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluation reaches a conclusion that depends on what the model is being used for.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total utility where marginal utility is asked for.
- Choosing units by highest MU rather than highest MU per cu.
- Comparing bundles that cost different amounts.
- Pivoting the budget line on the wrong intercept.
- Forgetting that the substitution effect holds real income constant.
- Calling every inferior good a Giffen good.
- Listing limitations without weighing them.
