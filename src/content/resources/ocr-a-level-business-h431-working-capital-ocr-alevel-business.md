## What working capital is

**Working capital** is the money a business has available to pay its day-to-day running costs. It is calculated as:

**Working capital = current assets − current liabilities**

- **Current assets** are assets the business expects to turn into cash within a year: inventory (stock), receivables (money owed by customers) and cash.
- **Current liabilities** are debts that must be paid within a year: payables (money owed to suppliers), an overdraft, short-term loans and tax due.

Working capital is shown on the statement of financial position. A positive figure means the business has enough short-term assets to cover its short-term debts. A negative figure means it may struggle to pay what it owes.

### Worked example 1: calculating working capital

Fenn Furniture has these figures at the year end.

| Current assets | £ | Current liabilities | £ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory | 120,000 | Payables | 60,000 |
| Receivables | 135,000 | Overdraft | 40,000 |
| Cash | 15,000 | | |
| **Total** | **270,000** | **Total** | **100,000** |

**Working capital** = £270,000 − £100,000 = **£170,000**.

The current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) is 270,000 ÷ 100,000 = 2.7, so for every £1 owed in the short term Fenn has £2.70 of short-term assets. But most of this working capital is tied up in inventory and receivables, and only £15,000 is cash. Working capital is therefore not the same as cash: a business can have a large working capital figure and still be unable to pay a bill tomorrow if the assets cannot be turned into cash quickly.

## The working capital cycle

The **working capital cycle** (also called the cash operating cycle) is the time between paying for the materials or stock a business buys and receiving the cash from customers who buy the finished goods. Cash is turned into stock, then into goods sold, then into receivables, and finally back into cash.

The stages for a manufacturer such as Fenn:

1. **Cash is paid** to buy materials (or the business buys them on credit, delaying the payment).
2. **Materials are held** as inventory until they are used.
3. **Production**: labour and other costs turn materials into finished goods.
4. **Finished goods are held** until they are sold.
5. **Goods are sold**, usually on credit, creating a receivable.
6. **The customer pays**, and cash returns to the business.

The longer the cycle, the more cash is tied up in inventory and receivables, and the more the business must fund from its own resources or borrowing. The cycle is shortened by moving stock faster, collecting payment sooner and taking longer to pay suppliers.

### Measuring the length of the cycle

The cycle can be measured in days (these are the efficiency ratios you meet in the ratio analysis content):

| Stage | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Inventory days | Inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 |
| Receivable days | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 |
| Payable days | Payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 |
| **Cycle length** | Inventory days + receivable days − payable days |

### Worked example 2: Fenn Furniture's cycle

Fenn's revenue is £1,095,000 and its cost of sales is £730,000.

- Inventory days = 120,000 ÷ 730,000 × 365 = **60 days**.
- Receivable days = 135,000 ÷ 1,095,000 × 365 = **45 days**.
- Payable days = 60,000 ÷ 730,000 × 365 = **30 days**.
- Cycle length = 60 + 45 − 30 = **75 days**.

Fenn pays its suppliers 30 days after buying, but does not receive cash from customers until 75 days after that payment. It must finance 75 days of operations from its own resources.

## Managing the cycle

Fenn considers three changes.

| Change | New figure | Cash released |
|---|---|---|
| Cut inventory to 45 days | 45 ÷ 365 × 730,000 = £90,000 | £120,000 − £90,000 = **£30,000** |
| Collect from customers in 36 days | 36 ÷ 365 × 1,095,000 = £108,000 | £135,000 − £108,000 = **£27,000** |
| Pay suppliers in 40 days | 40 ÷ 365 × 730,000 = £80,000 | £80,000 − £60,000 = **£20,000** |

Together they release £30,000 + £27,000 + £20,000 = **£77,000** and shorten the cycle to 45 + 36 − 40 = **41 days**, which is 34 days shorter.

**Risks of each change.**

- *Holding less inventory* risks running out of stock and lost sales.
- *Collecting faster* may annoy customers or need discounts; competitors may offer longer credit.
- *Paying suppliers later* may damage relationships, lose early-payment discounts or lead to a loss of supply.

**Why it matters for growth.** Suppose Fenn's sales rise 20%, to revenue of £1,314,000 and cost of sales of £876,000. At the same days, inventory becomes £144,000, receivables £162,000 and payables £72,000. The net amount tied up (inventory + receivables − payables) rises from £195,000 to £234,000, an extra **£39,000**. A growing business therefore needs more working capital just to stand still in terms of days. If it cannot find that money, it may be profitable on paper but run out of cash. This is sometimes called over-trading.

**Too much working capital** is also a problem: money in excess inventory or idle cash earns nothing, and could be invested or used to repay debt.

## Evaluating working capital

When asked to evaluate, consider:

- **What is the right level?** It depends on the industry. A supermarket sells for cash and holds fast-moving stock, so it can run with low or even negative working capital. A manufacturer of made-to-order machinery will need a lot.
- **The balance between liquidity and profitability.** More inventory and credit to customers may increase sales but tie up cash.
- **The source of finance.** Overdrafts are a quick but expensive way of covering a gap.
- **Stakeholders.** Suppliers want prompt payment; customers like long credit; lenders look at working capital before lending; employees depend on wages being paid.

A balanced judgement says that the working capital cycle should be as short as can be achieved without harming sales, supplier relations or quality, and that the right target depends on the industry and the business's stage of growth.

## Common mistakes

- Treating working capital as the same as cash.
- Including non-current assets or long-term loans in the calculation.
- Calculating the cycle with payable days added instead of subtracted.
- Forgetting that growth increases the need for working capital.
- Recommending that a business simply delay paying suppliers without noting the risks.

## Exam technique

For calculations, write the formula and list the items that go into current assets and current liabilities. For the cycle, name the stages in order and tie them to the case. For short evaluations, give a benefit, a risk, and a judgement tied to the type of business.
