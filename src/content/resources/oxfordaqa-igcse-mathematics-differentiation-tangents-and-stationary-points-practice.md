## Questions

**1.** (Extension only)

**(a)** Differentiate y = 6x⁵ − 4x² + 9. **[2]**
**(b)** Differentiate y = 3(x + 2)(x − 5). **[3]**

**2.** (Extension only) Given y = (8x⁵ + 12x³)/(4x²), find dy/dx and work out the gradient of the curve where x = −1. **[4]**

**3.** (Extension only) A curve has equation y = x³ − 5x².

**(a)** Find the gradient of the curve where x = 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the x-coordinates of the points on the curve where the gradient is 8. **[3]**

**4.** (Extension only) Find the equation of the tangent to the curve y = 2x³ − 5x² + 4 at the point where x = 2. Give your answer in the form y = mx + c. **[4]**

**5.** (Extension only) A curve has equation y = x⁴ − 3x + 2.

**(a)** Find the equation of the tangent to the curve at the point where x = −1. **[4]**
**(b)** This tangent crosses the x-axis at the point T. Find the coordinates of T. **[2]**

**6.** (Extension only) The curve C has equation y = x³ − 6x + 1. Two tangents to C are parallel to the line y = 6x + 3. Find the coordinates of the two points of contact and the equations of the two tangents. **[7]**

**7.** (Extension only) Find the coordinates of the stationary points of y = 2x³ − 9x² + 12x − 1 and decide whether each is a maximum or a minimum. **[6]**

**8.** (Extension only) Show that the curve y = x³ − 3x² + 3x + 5 has exactly one stationary point, and that it is a point of inflection. State its coordinates. **[4]**

**9.** (Extension only) A curve has equation y = x⁴ − 8x² + 3.

**(a)** Find the coordinates of the stationary points. **[4]**
**(b)** Determine the nature of each stationary point. **[3]**
**(c)** Describe a sketch of the curve, naming its key features. **[1]**

**10.** (Extension only) The curve y = x³ + px² + qx has stationary points where x = 1 and x = 3.

**(a)** Find the values of p and q. **[4]**
**(b)** Find the coordinates of the two stationary points. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine the nature of each stationary point. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe a sketch of the curve that shows the position of the stationary points and the point where the curve meets the y-axis. **[1]**

**11.** (Extension only) A curve has equation y = x⁴ + 4x³.

**(a)** Find the coordinates of its stationary points. **[3]**
**(b)** Determine the nature of each stationary point. **[3]**
**(c)** Describe a sketch of the curve. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 6x⁵ → 30x⁴ [1]. −4x² → −8x, and the constant 9 disappears [1].
dy/dx = **30x⁴ − 8x**
**(b)** Expand: 3(x² − 3x − 10) = 3x² − 9x − 30 [2]. Differentiate: **6x − 9** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Expanding the brackets first is the safe route and it shows your method. Differentiating each bracket separately and multiplying does not give the gradient function.

**2.** Divide each term by 4x²: y = 2x³ + 3x [2]. dy/dx = 6x² + 3 [1]. At x = −1: 6 × 1 + 3 = **9** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the simplified form of y. If the division goes wrong, the line of working shows where, and an answer with no working has nothing to build on.

**3. (a)** dy/dx = 3x² − 10x [1]. At x = 2: 12 − 20 = **−8** [1].
**(b)** 3x² − 10x = 8, so 3x² − 10x − 8 = 0 [1]. (3x + 2)(x − 4) = 0 [1]. x = **4** or x = **−2/3** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Where the gradient is 8" means set dy/dx equal to 8. A common slip is to set y equal to 8, which uses the wrong equation.

**4.** At x = 2: y = 16 − 20 + 4 = 0 [1]. dy/dx = 6x² − 10x [1]. At x = 2: 24 − 20 = 4 [1]. y − 0 = 4(x − 2), so **y = 4x − 8** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out the point on the curve before the gradient. Here y is 0, so check that your final line also passes through (2, 0).

**5. (a)** At x = −1: y = 1 + 3 + 2 = 6 [1]. dy/dx = 4x³ − 3 [1]. At x = −1: −4 − 3 = −7 [1]. y − 6 = −7(x + 1), so **y = −7x − 1** [1].
**(b)** On the x-axis y = 0, so −7x − 1 = 0 [1]. x = −1/7, so **T = (−1/7, 0)** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the tangent from part (a) in part (b). Writing y = 0 and the coordinates of T as a pair makes the answer clear.

**6.** The tangents have gradient 6, the gradient of y = 6x + 3 [1]. dy/dx = 3x² − 6 [1]. 3x² − 6 = 6, so x² = 4 and x = 2 or x = −2 [1].
x = 2: y = 8 − 12 + 1 = −3. x = −2: y = −8 + 12 + 1 = 5. Points **(2, −3)** and **(−2, 5)** [2].
At (2, −3): y + 3 = 6(x − 2), so **y = 6x − 15** [1].
At (−2, 5): y − 5 = 6(x + 2), so **y = 6x + 17** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Parallel lines have equal gradients. Check both roots of x² = 4, because stopping at x = 2 loses the second tangent.

**7.** dy/dx = 6x² − 18x + 12 = 6(x − 1)(x − 2) [1]. So x = 1 or x = 2 [1].
y at x = 1: 2 − 9 + 12 − 1 = 4. y at x = 2: 16 − 36 + 24 − 1 = 3. Points **(1, 4)** and **(2, 3)** [1].
Test x = 0, 1.5, 3: dy/dx = 12, −1.5, 12 [1].
Positive then negative at x = 1: **(1, 4) is a maximum** [1].
Negative then positive at x = 2: **(2, 3) is a minimum** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the gradient values you tested. A conclusion with no evidence is harder to credit than one backed by numbers.

**8.** dy/dx = 3x² − 6x + 3 = 3(x − 1)² [1]. This is zero only when x = 1, so there is one stationary point. y = 1 − 3 + 3 + 5 = 6, so it is **(1, 6)** [1].
At x = 0, dy/dx = 3. At x = 2, dy/dx = 3 [1].
The gradient is positive on both sides, so the point is a **point of inflection**, not a maximum or minimum [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that" question, state the reason as well as the result. Here that means saying the gradient does not change sign.

**9. (a)** dy/dx = 4x³ − 16x [1]. = 4x(x − 2)(x + 2) = 0, so x = 0, 2, −2 [1].
x = 0: y = 3 [1]. x = ±2: y = 16 − 32 + 3 = −13 [1]. Points **(0, 3), (2, −13), (−2, −13)**.
**(b)** Test x = −3, −1, 1, 3: dy/dx = −60, 12, −12, 60 [1].
At x = −2 the gradient goes − then +: **minimum** [1]. At x = 0 it goes + then −: **maximum** [1]. At x = 2 it goes − then +: **minimum**.
**(c)** Symmetric about the y-axis, both ends rising, minima at (±2, −13), maximum at (0, 3), which is also the y-intercept [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Choose test values that sit between the stationary x-values. Testing x = 0.5 and x = 3 only would miss the sign change at x = −2.

**10. (a)** dy/dx = 3x² + 2px + q [1]. x = 1: 3 + 2p + q = 0 [1]. x = 3: 27 + 6p + q = 0 [1]. Subtracting gives 24 + 4p = 0, so **p = −6** and **q = 9** [1].
**(b)** The curve is y = x³ − 6x² + 9x. At x = 1: 1 − 6 + 9 = 4. At x = 3: 27 − 54 + 27 = 0. Points **(1, 4)** and **(3, 0)** [2].
**(c)** dy/dx = 3(x − 1)(x − 3). Test x = 0, 2, 4: dy/dx = 9, −3, 9 [1]. So **(1, 4) is a maximum** and **(3, 0) is a minimum** [1].
**(d)** The curve passes through the origin (y = x(x − 3)²), rises to the maximum at (1, 4), falls to touch the x-axis at the minimum (3, 0), then rises again [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (a), "stationary at x = 1" means dy/dx = 0 when x = 1. Substitute into dy/dx, never into y.

**11. (a)** dy/dx = 4x³ + 12x² = 4x²(x + 3) [1]. So x = 0 or x = −3 [1]. y at x = 0 is 0. y at x = −3 is 81 − 108 = −27. Points **(0, 0)** and **(−3, −27)** [1].
**(b)** Test x = −4, −1, 1: dy/dx = −64, 8, 16 [1]. At x = −3 the gradient goes − then +: **(−3, −27) is a minimum** [1]. At x = 0 the gradient is positive on both sides: **(0, 0) is a point of inflection** [1].
**(c)** Both ends rise. The curve falls to the minimum at (−3, −27), then rises through a flat spot at the origin and keeps rising [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A repeated factor such as x² in dy/dx often points to an inflection, but the sign test is what proves it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Differentiating brackets one at a time instead of expanding the product first.
- Dropping the multiplication by the power, so 6x⁵ becomes 6x⁴.
- Treating a constant as if it still contributes a term in the gradient.
- Stopping at the stationary x-values and not giving the y-coordinates.
- Putting x-values into dy/dx to find heights, when y = f(x) is needed.
- Declaring a maximum or minimum with no sign test, or testing values that straddle two stationary points.
- Missing the second solution when dy/dx = k gives a quadratic, so only one tangent is found.
- Forgetting that a stationary point of inflection exists: the gradient is zero but no sign change occurs.
- Sketches with sharp corners at stationary points, or ends that go the wrong way for the highest power.
