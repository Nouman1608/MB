Sub-topic 7.2 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) has two outcomes. Outcome 7.2.1 asks you to describe and explain the factors that change human populations: birth rates, death rates, migration, rural and urban migration, and urbanisation. Outcome 7.2.2 asks you to describe twelve named impacts of human population growth, from informal urban settlements to changes in employment opportunities. This guide explains each factor using the syllabus's own measures, works through population-change and urbanisation calculations, and links the impacts into chains that earn marks.

For the whole of Topic 7, see the [human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/).

## The population change equation

Every change in the size of a population comes from four flows:

**Population change = (births - deaths) + (immigrants - emigrants)**

The first bracket is **natural change**; the second is **net migration**. For the world as a whole there is no migration, so world population changes only through births and deaths. For a country, region or city, migration can matter as much as natural change.

## Factors that affect population change (7.2.1)

### (a) Birth rates: children per woman

The syllabus links birth rates to **children per woman**: the average number of children a woman has during her lifetime (often called the fertility rate). A population roughly replaces itself, without migration, when this is about 2.1; the extra 0.1 allows for children who die before adulthood.

Explaining high or low values:

- Higher children per woman: high child death rates (parents have more children so that some survive), children working on farms or supporting parents in old age, limited access to contraception, early marriage, limited education for girls, and cultural or religious values that favour large families.
- Lower children per woman: better child survival, more education and paid work for women, the high cost of raising children in cities, access to contraception, and later marriage.

### (b) Death rates: life expectancy

The syllabus links death rates to **life expectancy**, the average number of years a person can expect to live. When death rates fall, life expectancy rises. Falls come from better nutrition, clean water and sanitation, vaccination, medicines and healthcare. Life expectancy can fall because of famine, conflict, epidemics or the collapse of health services.

A crucial idea for explaining growth: in many countries death rates fell before birth rates did. For a period, many more people were born than died, so the population grew rapidly. Birth rates usually fall later, once people are confident their children will survive and as education spreads.

### (c) Migration: emigration, immigration, push and pull factors

- **Emigration** is leaving a country; **immigration** is arriving in one.
- **Push factors** drive people away: unemployment, poverty, conflict, persecution, natural hazards and environmental damage such as drought or soil erosion.
- **Pull factors** attract people: jobs, higher wages, safety, education, healthcare and family already living there.

Migrants are often young adults, so migration changes the population *structure* as well as its size. The receiving area gains workers and, later, births; the sending area loses young adults and can be left with mainly children and elderly people.

### (d) Rural and urban migration

Movement from countryside to towns and cities is **rural-urban migration**. Typical push factors in rural areas are few jobs, low farm incomes, lack of services, and land shortages or degradation. Typical pull factors in cities are jobs in factories and services, schools and hospitals, and the hope of a better life. The syllabus heading is "rural and urban migration", so be ready to explain movement in the opposite direction too, from cities to rural areas, which happens when people seek cheaper housing or a quieter life.

### (e) Urbanisation

**Urbanisation** is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas. It results from rural-urban migration and from natural increase within cities, since many migrants are young adults who then have children in the city.

### Worked example 1: a town's population change

*The figures are invented for practice.* A town of 250 000 people records, in one year: 7 500 births, 2 000 deaths, 6 000 immigrants and 1 500 emigrants.

- Natural change = 7 500 - 2 000 = **+5 500**.
- Net migration = 6 000 - 1 500 = **+4 500**.
- Total change = 5 500 + 4 500 = **+10 000**, so the population becomes **260 000**.
- Growth rate = 10 000 / 250 000 x 100 = **4%** in one year.

Migration contributes 4 500 / 10 000 = 45% of the growth, which shows why a town can grow quickly even if birth rates fall.

### Worked example 2: urbanisation

A country had 40 million people, 14 million of them urban. Twenty years later it had 55 million, 27.5 million of them urban.

- Urban share before = 14 / 40 x 100 = **35%**; after = 27.5 / 55 x 100 = **50%**.
- Urban population increase = 27.5 - 14 = 13.5 million, which is 13.5 / 14 x 100 = **96.4%**.

Urbanisation is the rise in **share** from 35% to 50%. The near-doubling of the urban population is urban growth. Keep the two ideas separate in answers.

## Impacts of human population growth (7.2.2)

The syllabus names twelve impacts. Group them to remember them, then use chains to explain.

**Where people live**
- **Informal urban settlements**: when cities grow faster than affordable housing can be built, migrants build homes on unused land, often on steep slopes or floodplains, without legal ownership or services.
- **Megacities**: cities with more than 10 million people.
- **Availability of land**: more land is needed for housing, farming and industry, so farmland and natural habitats are lost and land prices rise.

**Resources and services**
- **Availability of resources**: more demand for food, water, minerals and timber.
- **Energy usage**: more people and more industry use more electricity and fuel.
- **Water supply**: demand can exceed what rivers, reservoirs and aquifers provide.
- **Sanitation**: sewers and toilets may not keep pace, especially in informal settlements.
- **Management of waste**: more household and industrial waste to collect and dispose of.
- **Facilities and infrastructure**: schools, hospitals, roads and public transport become overcrowded.

**Environment and daily life**
- **Air, land, light, noise, visual and water pollution**: from traffic, industry, waste, lighting and construction.
- **Traffic congestion**: more vehicles on roads that were not designed for them.
- **Changes in employment opportunities**: more workers can attract new industries and services, but if jobs do not keep pace there is unemployment and much informal work.

### Model answer chain

**Question:** Explain how rapid population growth in a city can affect people's health. **[4]**

Population grows faster than housing, so migrants build informal settlements *(1)*. These lack sanitation and safe water supply *(1)*, so drinking water becomes contaminated with sewage *(1)*, and water-related diseases spread quickly among crowded households *(1)*. A chain like this earns more than four separate impacts, because "explain" needs the links.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "birth rate" as a number per 1 000 when the question uses children per woman; read which measure is given.
- Forgetting that life expectancy rises when death rates fall.
- Treating urbanisation and urban growth as the same thing.
- Listing push factors when the question asks for pull factors, or the reverse.
- Describing impacts in a list with no link to the cause (growth outpacing provision).
- Assuming growth has only negative impacts: employment opportunities can increase.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a factor with its measure (children per woman, life expectancy). **Explain** change with the equation and a reason chain. **Calculate**: show natural change and net migration separately before adding. **Describe impacts** with specific examples, and in longer answers show that impacts are greatest where growth is fastest and provision weakest.

## Check your understanding

1. State the population change equation. *Change = (births - deaths) + (immigrants - emigrants).*
2. Why might children per woman be high where child death rates are high? *Parents have more children so that some survive.*
3. Give two pull factors for rural-urban migration. *Jobs and better healthcare (or schools, higher wages).*
4. What is a megacity? *A city with more than 10 million people.*
5. Births 3 000, deaths 1 200, immigrants 500, emigrants 900. What is the change? *1 800 - 400 = +1 400.*
