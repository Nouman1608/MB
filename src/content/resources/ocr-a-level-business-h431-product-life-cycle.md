## The model and its purpose

The **product life cycle** shows how the sales of a product change over time, from launch until it is withdrawn. A typical curve has these stages.

| Stage | Sales | Profit and cash flow | Typical marketing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Development | None | Negative: money is spent on research, design and testing | Market research, testing, trial |
| Introduction | Low, slow | Often losses: high promotion and set-up costs | Build awareness; limited distribution; price may be high (skimming) or low (penetration) |
| Growth | Rising fast | Profit grows as sales rise and unit costs fall | Widen distribution; build brand preference; competitors enter |
| Maturity | Peak, then flat | Highest profit; cash generated | Defend share; differentiate; promotions to retain; possible price competition |
| Decline | Falling | Profit falls; may become a loss | Cut costs; reduce promotion; withdraw or use an extension strategy |

Some textbooks add **saturation** between maturity and decline. The specification names the stages generally, so use the labels your teacher gives and be consistent.

**Purpose.** The model helps a business to:

- plan the marketing mix to suit the stage;
- forecast the likely pattern of sales and cash flow;
- decide when to launch the next product so that sales do not fall away;
- decide when to withdraw or support a product;
- balance its **product portfolio**, so that new products are growing as old ones decline.

A **life cycle diagram** is a graph with time on the horizontal axis and sales (or sales and profit) on the vertical axis. To **complete** one, plot the figures and label the stages. To **interpret** one, describe what the shape shows, name the stage, and say what the business should do.

## Worked example 1: interpreting sales data

**Case.** Zest Fizz is a sparkling soft drink. Annual sales (£000) and the change from the previous year are below. The year-on-year change is (this year ÷ last year − 1) × 100.

| Year | Sales (£000) | Change in sales (£000) | Change (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 120 | | |
| 2 | 480 | +360 | +300.0 |
| 3 | 1,150 | +670 | +139.6 |
| 4 | 2,100 | +950 | +82.6 |
| 5 | 2,900 | +800 | +38.1 |
| 6 | 3,350 | +450 | +15.5 |
| 7 | 3,420 | +70 | +2.1 |
| 8 | 3,380 | −40 | −1.2 |
| 9 | 3,100 | −280 | −8.3 |
| 10 | 2,550 | −550 | −17.7 |

**Reading the table.**

- **Year 1: introduction.** Sales are small (£120,000).
- **Years 2 to 5: growth.** Sales rise every year. The biggest jump in pounds comes in year 4 (+£950,000), but the percentage growth is already falling: 300%, 139.6%, 82.6%, 38.1%.
- **Years 6 to 8: maturity.** The rate of growth drops to 15.5%, then 2.1%, and sales peak at £3.42 million in year 7. Year 8 shows a slight fall of 1.2%.
- **Years 9 to 10: decline.** Falls of 8.3% and then 17.7%, which is accelerating.

**Key lesson.** Sales keep rising until year 7, but the rate of growth begins to fall from year 3. A manager looking only at the pound change would think growth continued until year 6; the percentage trend gives earlier warning. The boundaries between stages are judgements, and another analyst might place the start of decline in year 8 instead of year 9.

**Marketing implications.** In years 6 to 8 Zest Fizz should defend share against rivals with promotions and loyalty offers, and in year 9 begin to decide on an extension strategy, since the decline has started. A new product should have been in development by year 5 or 6, so that it reaches growth as Zest Fizz declines.

## Extension strategies

An **extension strategy** is an action to lengthen the life of a product by sustaining or restoring sales, usually when it is mature or beginning to decline. Its **purpose** is to keep earning from a product whose development costs have already been paid, which is usually cheaper than launching a new one.

Common strategies:

- **product modification:** new flavours, sizes, features or packaging;
- **new markets:** a new country, region or customer group;
- **new uses:** promoting a different use (such as a drink as a cocktail mixer);
- **price changes:** a discount to stimulate demand, or a higher price for a refreshed premium image;
- **promotion and repositioning:** new advertising or a new brand message;
- **new distribution:** selling online or through a new retailer.

### Worked example 2: comparing extension strategies by payback

At the end of year 8, Zest Fizz's managers consider two strategies. All figures are forecasts.

| | A: new flavour | B: enter a new country |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | £300,000 | £600,000 |
| Extra sales a year | £500,000 | £900,000 |
| Margin on extra sales | 35% | 30% |
| Extra contribution a year | £500,000 × 35% = **£175,000** | £900,000 × 30% = **£270,000** |
| Payback | £300,000 ÷ £175,000 = **1.71 years** | £600,000 ÷ £270,000 = **2.22 years** |

**Test the forecast.** Suppose extra sales are only half the forecast.

- A: contribution £87,500 a year; payback £300,000 ÷ £87,500 = **3.43 years**.
- B: contribution £135,000 a year; payback £600,000 ÷ £135,000 = **4.44 years**.

**Analysis.** Strategy B earns more each year, but costs twice as much and takes longer to pay back. It is also riskier because it depends on a new market, with unknown tastes and rivals. A is cheaper and quicker, but a new flavour may only move sales between flavours and may not reverse a decline that is caused by changing tastes. Both would be worthwhile only if the product keeps selling for longer than the payback period. With the drink already in decline (falling 17.7% in year 10), a payback of 3.4 or 4.4 years looks hard to justify, while 1.7 years may be acceptable.

**Judgement.** Strategy A is the safer choice, particularly if a small trial confirms demand. Strategy B should be considered only after market research in the new country. Neither should replace work on a new product.

## Evaluating the model

| Useful | Limits |
|---|---|
| Helps plan the mix and cash flow for each stage | Real products do not always follow the curve: fashion products rise and fall quickly, and some products last for decades |
| Shows when new products are needed | Stages are hard to identify as they happen; a dip may be temporary |
| Warns that profits are likely to change | The length of each stage cannot be predicted |
| Allows portfolio balance | Ignores external events, such as a recession or a rival launch |
| Supports communication with investors and lenders | Business action changes the curve, so it is not a fixed law |

For **stakeholders**, the model helps shareholders to see whether a firm has products at several stages, helps staff to plan jobs and workloads, and helps suppliers to forecast orders. But it should be combined with sales data, market research and finance. It describes a likely pattern; it does not make decisions.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the stages without saying what the business should do in each.
- Judging the stage by the size of sales rather than by the trend in growth.
- Assuming every product follows the same smooth curve.
- Treating extension strategies as free. They cost money and must pay back.
- Confusing the life cycle of a single product with the life of the business.

## Exam technique

When interpreting a diagram or table, name the stage, support it with figures, and state the implication. When analysing an extension strategy, link the action to the cause of the decline and give the cost and the likely effect. For "evaluate", give a judgement about how useful the model is for this product.
