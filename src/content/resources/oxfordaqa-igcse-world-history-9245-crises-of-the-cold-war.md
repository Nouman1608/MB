**This guide covers the whole of the Section A option Crises of the Cold War, 1960-1975 in OxfordAQA International GCSE History (9245).** The specification divides it into two parts: Threats to peace in the 1960s, and Conflict in Vietnam. Section A is assessed entirely through sources (AO3), so this page gives you the knowledge you need to use as contextual knowledge, and shows how to turn it into marks on the four source questions. The [Paper 1 Section A study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-paper-1-section-a/) explains the question types for both Section A options. Follow up with the [Crises of the Cold War revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-crises-of-the-cold-war-revision-notes/) and the [Crises of the Cold War practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-crises-of-the-cold-war-practice/).

## What the specification says

The option "enables students to understand the complex and diverse interests of different states, ideologies and the individuals who represented them". It considers revolutionary movements and their impact on East-West rivalry, and it focuses on "global crises of the Cold War" and why conflict occurred and proved hard to resolve.

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Part 1: Threats to peace in the 1960s | Causes of tension (the U2 Crisis, Berlin and the Wall, the space and arms races); the Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962; Czechoslovakia, 1968 |
| Part 2: Conflict in Vietnam | The US escalation of conflict; the ending of the conflict |

Each of the source questions targets Part 1 or Part 2. Question 1 (4 marks) uses an unseen source; Question 2 (6 marks) compares one unseen and one pre-released source; Question 3 (8 marks) asks how useful an unseen source is; Question 4 (12 marks) asks you to analyse and evaluate an issue using a range of sources and your own knowledge.

## Part 1: Threats to peace in the 1960s

### The U2 Crisis and the Paris Summit, 1960

In May 1960 the USSR shot down an American U2 spy plane over its territory and captured the pilot, Gary Powers. President Eisenhower first said it was a weather research flight. Khrushchev then revealed that the pilot was alive and had confessed, and Eisenhower had to admit that the USA carried out spy flights. At the Paris Summit in the same month, Khrushchev demanded an apology; Eisenhower would not give one, and the summit collapsed. A planned visit by Eisenhower to the USSR was cancelled. The crisis ended hopes of a thaw, showed how mistrust could wreck talks, and led both sides to argue over who was to blame.

### Berlin and the Wall, 1961

Berlin was divided into four sectors but lay deep inside East Germany. Between 1949 and 1961 around three million East Germans left through West Berlin, many of them skilled workers, teachers and doctors. The flow embarrassed the communist regime and weakened the East German economy. Khrushchev demanded that the western powers leave Berlin but was resisted. In the early hours of 13 August 1961 East German troops closed the border with barbed wire, and soon after the Berlin Wall was built.

**Kennedy's response.** Kennedy protested but did not use force. His priority was to stop the Soviets from taking West Berlin, not to stop them closing the border. In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, but both sides drew back. In June 1963 Kennedy visited West Berlin and declared his support for its people, giving a boost to their morale. The Wall became a symbol of the division of Europe and of communist repression.

### The space and arms races

Each side wanted to show the superiority of its system. In 1957 the USSR launched Sputnik 1; in April 1961 Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space. Kennedy responded by committing the USA to landing a man on the Moon by the end of the decade. The Apollo programme achieved this in July 1969.

The nuclear arms race was a race to build more and better weapons. Both sides built intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), able to reach the other's cities, and the USA developed Polaris missiles that could be fired from submarines. The idea of the **nuclear deterrent** was that neither side would attack because the response would destroy it as well. The result was a balance of terror, but it also raised the risk of accident and miscalculation.

### The Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962

In January 1959 Fidel Castro came to power in Cuba, 90 miles from Florida. He took over American-owned businesses, and the USA responded with a trade embargo. Castro turned to the USSR for aid. In April 1961 about 1,400 Cuban exiles trained by the CIA landed at the Bay of Pigs. They were defeated within days, and Kennedy was embarrassed. The invasion made Castro more dependent on the Soviets and may have convinced Khrushchev that Kennedy was weak.

In 1962 Khrushchev secretly placed nuclear missiles in Cuba. Possible reasons include protecting Cuba from invasion, matching American missiles in Turkey, and improving the Soviet position in the arms race. In mid-October American U2 photographs revealed the missile sites. Kennedy's advisers considered air strikes and invasion, but he chose a naval blockade, which he called a quarantine, and announced it on television on 22 October. The world came close to nuclear war for about two weeks. On 28 October Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American promise not to invade Cuba; the USA also secretly agreed to withdraw its missiles from Turkey.

**Results.** A telephone hotline linked Washington and Moscow in 1963, and a Partial Test Ban Treaty was signed in the same year. Kennedy's standing rose. Khrushchev's reputation was damaged, and he was removed from power in October 1964.

### Czechoslovakia, 1968

In January 1968 Alexander Dubček became leader of the Czechoslovak Communist Party. His reforms, known as the **Prague Spring**, promised "socialism with a human face": less censorship, more freedom of speech and the right to travel. Dubček did not plan to leave the Warsaw Pact. But the USSR and its allies feared that reform would spread and weaken communist control in Eastern Europe. On the night of 20-21 August 1968 Warsaw Pact troops invaded and occupied the country. Dubček was taken to Moscow and later replaced by a hardliner.

The **Brezhnev Doctrine** stated that the USSR had the right to intervene in any communist country if socialism was under threat. The West protested but did nothing, because it did not want a war over a country in the Soviet sphere of influence. The invasion damaged the reputation of the USSR in the West and among some communist parties, but détente talks went ahead.

## Part 2: Conflict in Vietnam

### The US escalation

The USA feared the **Domino Theory**: if one country in South-East Asia fell to communism, its neighbours would follow. Kennedy increased the number of American advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 and supported the Strategic Hamlets programme, which moved peasants into fortified villages to cut off the Vietcong. The programme was unpopular because it uprooted people from their land and pushed many towards the Vietcong.

The North Vietnamese, led by Ho Chi Minh, were supported by the USSR and China. The Vietcong used **guerrilla tactics**: ambushes, booby traps and tunnels, hiding among the peasants, and supplies sent down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. The USA used overwhelming firepower and **Search and Destroy** missions, which burned villages and killed civilians and made the Vietcong more popular.

After the **Gulf of Tonkin incident** in August 1964, in which American ships were reported to have been attacked, Congress gave Johnson wide powers to fight in Vietnam. A bombing campaign, Operation Rolling Thunder, began in March 1965 and the first combat troops arrived; by 1968 there were over half a million. At **My Lai** in March 1968 American soldiers killed hundreds of unarmed villagers, and the news, when it emerged in 1969, shocked the American public. In January 1968 the **Tet Offensive** saw surprise attacks on cities across the South. The communists suffered heavy losses, but the attacks showed that the USA was not winning and turned American opinion against the war. A strong **protest movement** grew among students and many others.

### The ending of the conflict

Nixon's policy of **Vietnamisation** from 1969 handed more of the fighting to the South Vietnamese army while American troops left. He also used heavy bombing, chemical weapons such as napalm and Agent Orange, and widened the war into Cambodia and Laos in 1970-72. In May 1970 National Guardsmen shot four student protesters dead at **Kent State University**, and protests grew. Television brought the war into American homes, and the media played a part in reducing support.

Nixon improved relations with China, visiting it in 1972, which put pressure on North Vietnam. After **Paris peace talks**, an agreement was signed in January 1973 and the last American troops left. Fighting continued, and Saigon fell to the North Vietnamese in April 1975.

## Using the knowledge with sources

Section A tests knowledge only in service of the sources. Your contextual knowledge must be linked to the source, not recited.

### Worked example: Question 1 (4 marks)

*Source A (invented): from a newspaper editorial imagined as published in West Berlin, August 1961. "Overnight, a barrier of barbed wire has cut our city in two. Families cannot visit each other, and workers cannot reach their jobs. The communists say it is for our protection. The refugees who crossed in their thousands in the past weeks know the truth."*

*Use Source A and your contextual knowledge to support the claim that the Berlin Wall was built to stop people leaving East Germany.* **[4]**

> The source says "the refugees who crossed in their thousands in the past weeks know the truth", which suggests that the Wall was built because people were leaving, not for protection **[1 source]**. The editorial dismisses the communist claim of "protection", which supports the claim by treating that explanation as false **[1 explained]**. My own knowledge supports this: around three million East Germans left through West Berlin between 1949 and 1961, including skilled workers, which weakened the East German economy **[1 knowledge]**. In the early hours of 13 August 1961 East German troops closed the border, which shows that stopping the flow of people was the aim **[1 link]**.

### Worked example: Question 3 (8 marks), planning

For a question on how useful a source is, use content, provenance and knowledge. Never write about what is missing, as limitations are not required. For a source on the Tet Offensive written by a journalist in Saigon, plan like this.

- **Content:** what it says about attacks on cities and the reaction of the public; knowledge to confirm the date (January 1968), the heavy communist losses and the shock in the USA.
- **Provenance:** a journalist in Saigon was close to events and reported to the American public; knowledge that television and newspapers affected opinion.
- **Judgement:** explain why content and provenance make it useful to a historian studying why American support for the war fell.

### The 12-mark question

Question 4 asks for a line of reasoning that uses several sources. Group the sources by what they show. For "How far do the sources support the view that the Cuban Missile Crisis was mainly a victory for Kennedy?" put the evidence for (the missiles were removed; the USA did not give way publicly) against the evidence against (the secret deal over Turkey; Khrushchev's guarantee for Cuba), weigh the sources by their provenance, add your own knowledge, and reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the story of the crisis without linking it to the source.
- Writing about what a source leaves out in Question 3. This is not required.
- Mixing Part 1 and Part 2 dates: the Wall (1961), Cuba (1962), Czechoslovakia (1968), Tet (1968), Kent State (1970), Saigon (1975).
- Confusing the Strategic Hamlets (Kennedy era) with Vietnamisation (Nixon).
- Saying the USA won the Cuban Missile Crisis outright, ignoring the Turkey deal.
- Treating provenance as only "who wrote it" and not the purpose, audience and date.
