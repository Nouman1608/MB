These questions practise the presentation in Component 3, the Team Project, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, in which each candidate presents "their research and preferred solution (10 minutes maximum)" and argues "for an effective and workable solution to the problem". For the component's format, see the [Team Project study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-team-project/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The presentation is coursework, so these questions practise the decisions behind it; they do not supply a problem statement or content for your own project, which must be your team's own work. All towns, teams and sources are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [presentation study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-team-project-presentation/) and the [presentation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-team-project-presentation-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the maximum length of the presentation and the **two** formats it may take. **[3]**

**2.** Name the **four** AO1 skills assessed in the presentation. **[4]**

**3.** State **two** items submitted with the recording of the presentation. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** A team's problem statement is: "Flooding of homes on the low-lying east side of Marrow Bridge is getting worse." A candidate presenting from a social perspective opens with three minutes describing photographs of flooded streets and residents' stories.

**(a)** Identify **two** parts of an individual analysis that this opening leaves out. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how the candidate could cover them within the same time. **[2]**

**5.** For the same problem, a candidate proposes: "The world's governments should agree to stop climate change." Evaluate this as an "effective and workable" solution. **[4]**

**6.** Write one spoken sentence evaluating each source. **[4]**

- **Source P:** the town council's flood map, updated last year, based on river-level records.
- **Source Q:** a news report on a flood-defence scheme in a much larger coastal city abroad.

**7.** A candidate's timing plan is: problem, 6 minutes; research, 3 minutes; solution, 1 minute. Evaluate the plan and suggest a better one. **[3]**

**8.** A slide contains four paragraphs of text, a stock photograph of rain and no sources. Suggest **two** improvements, explaining how each supports AO3. **[4]**

**9.** Rewrite this sentence for a non-specialist listener: "Impermeable surfaces increase runoff and reduce lag time, raising peak discharge." **[2]**

**10.** Using the six moves of a justification, outline a solution to the Marrow Bridge problem from an economic perspective. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why acknowledging a teammate's alternative solution can strengthen your own justification. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate this passage from a presentation transcript as a justification of a solution. **[6]**

> "My solution is rain gardens on every street in the east side. Rain gardens are really popular now and look nice. They are used in lots of places around the world. Residents will love them. Some people say they are expensive, but I think they are worth it. So that is why rain gardens are the best solution."

## Answers

**1.** Maximum 10 minutes [1]. Either a live recording of the candidate presenting [1] or a presentation with a recorded voiceover by the candidate [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Individual analysis of the problem identified by the team [1]; range of research undertaken [1]; evaluation of sources [1]; justification for their individual solution [1]. **[4]**

**3.** The audio and/or visual materials used [1]; a verbatim transcript of the presentation [1]. (The reflective paper is also submitted for the component, but it is a separate piece of work, not part of the presentation.) **[2]**

**4. (a)** Any two: the causes of the worsening flooding [1]; evidence of the scale of the problem (how many homes, how often) [1]; its global relevance [1]; what the social perspective highlights, such as which residents are most affected [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Replace most of the photographs with one image and one chart of flood frequency [1], then use the time saved to explain causes and link them to similar problems elsewhere, keeping one resident's story to show the social impact [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It is not effective for this problem in a measurable way, because it does not act on the local causes, such as drainage or building on the floodplain [1], and any effect would be distant and uncertain [1]. It is not workable for the team's context: no one the candidate can address could carry it out, and there is no cost, timescale or responsible body [1]. Judgement: it states a global aim rather than a solution to the local problem, so it cannot be justified as effective and workable [1]. **[4]**

**6.** P, for example: "The council's flood map was updated last year from river-level records [1], so it is the most current and locally specific evidence we have [1]." Q, for example: "This scheme worked in a much larger coastal city [1], so it shows the idea can succeed, but a river town like ours may not get the same results [1]." **[4]**

**7.** The plan gives 1 minute to the solution, where justification, an AO1 skill, is shown, while the problem takes 6 minutes [1]. Research with evaluation also needs more time than this allows if sources are to be assessed [1]. A better plan: problem about 2 minutes, perspective and causes about 2, research 2-2.5, solution and justification 3, conclusion 1 [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Any two, 2 marks each: replace paragraphs with a few key words or a chart of local flood data [1], so the visual carries evidence the listener can take in while hearing the explanation [1]; replace the stock photograph with a map showing the affected streets [1], so the image makes a point about where the problem is [1]; add short source references on the slide [1], meeting the requirement to cite references clearly and accurately [1]. **[4]**

**9.** For example: "Hard surfaces such as roads and car parks stop rain soaking into the ground [1], so water reaches the river faster and in greater amounts, which makes floods higher [1]." **[2]**

**10.** One mark per move, for example:
- Solution: the council pays householders a grant to fit flood barriers and raise electrical sockets in the 200 homes most at risk [1].
- Cause: frequent flooding of the same homes, where large defences would cost more than the property they protect [1].
- Evidence: a similar grant scheme elsewhere reduced repair claims, though in a different country with different insurance rules [1].
- Workable: one-off grants, run by the council's existing housing team, installed within a year [1].
- Alternative: a flood wall protects more homes but costs far more and takes years to approve [1].
- Objection answered: grants do not stop streets flooding, but on economic criteria they reduce the largest cost, damage to homes, soonest [1]. **[6]**

**11.** It shows the candidate has weighed an alternative from another perspective, not ignored it [1]. Explaining why one's own solution is better on stated criteria turns a preference into a reasoned justification [1]. It also anticipates the obvious question an audience would ask, which strengthens the argument's organisation (AO3) [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- The solution is stated but not precisely: who would build and pay for "every street" is not given [1].
- No link is made to the causes of the flooding [1].
- "Popular" and "look nice" are not evidence of effectiveness against flooding [1].
- "Lots of places around the world" is vague; no source is named or evaluated [1].
- The cost objection is raised but answered only with an opinion [1].
- Judgement: the passage asserts rather than justifies; it shows neither effectiveness nor workability, so it would gain little credit for justification [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Analyses that describe impacts at length but never explain causes.
- Global aims offered as local solutions.
- Spoken source evaluation that names a source without saying why it can or cannot be trusted.
- Visual improvements that make slides prettier but not more informative.
- Justifications that answer an objection with "I think it is worth it".
