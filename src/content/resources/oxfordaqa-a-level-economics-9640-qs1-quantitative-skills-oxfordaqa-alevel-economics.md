## One idea, four forms

A ratio, a fraction, a decimal and a percentage can all express the same relationship. Economics data switch between them, so move between them confidently.

| Ratio (part : whole) | Fraction | Decimal | Percentage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 : 4 | 3/4 | 0.75 | 75% |
| 7 : 20 | 7/20 | 0.35 | 35% |
| 1 : 8 | 1/8 | 0.125 | 12.5% |

A **part : part** ratio is different. If exports : imports = 3 : 5, exports are 3/5 of imports, but exports are 3/8 of total trade (exports plus imports). Always ask, "a fraction of what?"

## 1. Concentration ratios

The specification lists "concentration ratios and how to calculate a concentration ratio" in 3.1.4.4. An **n-firm concentration ratio** is the combined market share of the n largest firms in a market. It is a fraction of total market sales (or output), usually written as a percentage.

### Worked example 1

Seven firms sell in the market for bottled water in Country A. Annual sales (cu million): 150, 110, 90, 60, 40, 30, 20.

```
Total market sales = 150 + 110 + 90 + 60 + 40 + 30 + 20 = 500
Three-firm ratio = (150 + 110 + 90) / 500 = 350/500 = 7/10 = 70%
Four-firm ratio  = (350 + 60) / 500 = 410/500 = 82%
```

A high concentration ratio is consistent with an oligopoly: a few firms supply most of the market.

**What the ratio cannot show.** In a second market, the four largest firms might each hold 20.5%, also giving a four-firm ratio of 82%. In the first market one firm alone holds 30%. The same ratio can hide very different patterns of market power among the leading firms. The ratio also depends on how the market is defined: "bottled water" gives a higher ratio than "soft drinks".

## 2. Opportunity cost as a ratio

On a production possibility diagram, the opportunity cost of one good is the amount of the other good given up. When the boundary is a straight line, that opportunity cost is a constant ratio. This underlies comparative advantage in 3.4.1.2, where the specification asks you to "use a simple numerical example to illustrate the principle of comparative advantage".

### Worked example 2

In one day, a worker in Country A can make 6 tonnes of wheat or 3 bolts of cloth. A worker in Country B can make 2 tonnes of wheat or 2 bolts of cloth.

```
Opportunity cost of 1 bolt of cloth:
  A: 6/3 = 2 tonnes of wheat
  B: 2/2 = 1 tonne of wheat
Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat:
  A: 3/6 = 1/2 bolt of cloth
  B: 2/2 = 1 bolt of cloth
```

Country A has an **absolute** advantage in both goods, because its workers make more of each. But B gives up less wheat to make cloth (1 tonne against 2), so B has the **comparative** advantage in cloth. A gives up less cloth to make wheat (1/2 a bolt against 1), so A has the comparative advantage in wheat.

Note that the two opportunity costs for each country are reciprocals: 2 and 1/2 for A, 1 and 1 for B. This is a fast check on your arithmetic.

**Mutually beneficial terms of trade.** Trade benefits both countries if 1 bolt of cloth exchanges for between 1 and 2 tonnes of wheat. At 1.5 tonnes per bolt, B gets more wheat for its cloth than the 1 tonne it would give up producing it, and A pays less than the 2 tonnes it would give up producing cloth itself.

## 3. Marginal propensities and the multiplier

Section 3.2.2 lists the marginal propensities to consume, save, tax and import, and says students should be able to calculate the value of the multiplier. Each marginal propensity is a fraction: the change in one variable divided by the change in income.

```
MPC = ΔC / ΔY
```

If income rises by 200 cu million and consumption rises by 150 cu million, MPC = 150/200 = 3/4 = 0.75.

### Worked example 3: the multiplier

**Closed economy with no government.** The extra income not consumed is saved, so MPS = 1 − MPC.

```
MPC = 0.8, so MPS = 0.2
Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / 0.2 = 5
An injection of 40 cu million raises national income by 5 × 40 = 200 cu million
```

**Open economy with a government.** Leakages are saving, taxation and imports, so the multiplier uses the sum of the marginal propensities to withdraw:

```
MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.25, MPM = 0.1
Multiplier = 1 / (MPS + MPT + MPM) = 1 / 0.5 = 2
```

The fraction 1/0.5 is the reciprocal of the share of each extra unit of income that leaks out of the circular flow. The bigger the leakage fraction, the smaller the multiplier.

## 4. Productivity as a ratio

Section 3.1.3.1 covers the meaning of productivity, including labour productivity. Labour productivity is output per worker (or per hour worked):

```
Labour productivity = total output / number of workers
```

### Worked example 4

A factory's 8 workers produce 240 units a day: productivity is 240/8 = 30 units per worker. After hiring a ninth worker, output rises to 300 units: productivity is 300/9 = 33⅓ units per worker. Output rose by 60 units, but the ninth worker did not produce 60 units alone; the ratio tells you about the average across all workers, not about the last one hired.

## 5. Ratios to GDP

Governments and international organisations compare debt, deficits and trade balances with the size of the economy. Dividing by GDP turns a sum of money into a fraction that can be compared across years and countries.

### Worked example 5

Country B's national debt is 450 cu billion and its GDP is 600 cu billion: the debt-to-GDP ratio is 450/600 = 3/4, or 75%. A year later, debt is 480 and GDP is 660: the ratio is 480/660 = 0.727, or 72.7%. The debt rose by 30, yet the ratio fell, because GDP grew proportionally faster. A question that asks whether the debt problem "has worsened" needs both readings.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a part : part ratio as a part : whole fraction.
- Adding the market shares of the wrong firms in a concentration ratio: they must be the n **largest**.
- Calculating opportunity cost upside down, for example giving cloth per wheat when the question asks for wheat per cloth.
- Confusing absolute and comparative advantage.
- Using 1/MPC as the multiplier instead of 1/(1 − MPC).
- Treating average productivity as the output of the last worker.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out the fraction before simplifying it, and give the unit (cu million, units per worker, tonnes per bolt).
- **Using the data** or **using the extract**: take the figures from the data provided and say which ones you used.
- **Explain**: after the number, state what it means in economic terms, such as "the market is highly concentrated".
- **Analyse**: link the ratio to a concept, for example from concentration ratio to likely market power.
- State what the ratio leaves out when a question asks you to assess the evidence.
