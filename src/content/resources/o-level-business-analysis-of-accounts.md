## Profitability (5.5.1)

Profitability is how well a business earns profit compared with the revenue it makes or the money invested in it. A profit figure alone does not tell you this. A profit of 50 000 is excellent for a business with 200 000 of revenue and weak for one with 20 million.

Profitability matters because:

- owners and shareholders want a return for taking the risk of running or funding the business
- retained profit is a source of finance for growth without borrowing
- lenders look for profit to be confident that interest can be paid
- falling profitability is an early warning that costs are rising or prices are too low

Profitability is judged with ratios that compare profit to something else: revenue (the two margins) or capital employed (ROCE).

## Liquidity (5.5.2)

Liquidity is the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts when they fall due. It depends on having enough cash, or assets that can quickly be turned into cash, compared with the bills due soon.

Liquidity matters because a business that cannot pay wages, suppliers or the electricity bill on time can be forced to stop trading, even if it is profitable on paper. Profit and cash are different things (see section 5.3). A business can sell on credit, record profit, and still have an empty bank account while customers delay payment.

Liquidity is judged with the current ratio and the acid test ratio. Both compare current assets with current liabilities. These come from the statement of financial position (section 5.4): current assets are items such as inventories, receivables and cash, and current liabilities are debts due within a year.

## The five ratios (5.5.3)

### Formulas

| Ratio | Standard form | Shown as |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 | % |
| Profit margin | (profit ÷ revenue) × 100 | % |
| Return on capital employed | (profit ÷ capital employed) × 100 | % |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | a ratio, such as 1.5 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilities | a ratio, such as 0.8 : 1 |

Supporting definitions:

- Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales.
- Profit in the profit margin and ROCE is the profit figure taken from the income statement. If a question gives a single "profit" figure, use that figure; if it gives several profit figures, state which one you use.
- Capital employed is the long-term finance in the business. The standard form is total assets − current liabilities, which equals owners' capital plus non-current liabilities.
- Acid test removes inventories because they are the current asset that is slowest and least certain to be turned into cash.

### Worked example: Hollin Cycles

Hollin Cycles sells bicycles and repairs. Its figures for two years:

| Item (in $000) | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 480 | 540 |
| Cost of sales | 288 | 351 |
| Profit | 57.6 | 43.2 |
| Capital employed | 360 | 432 |
| Inventories | 70 | 105 |
| Receivables | 30 | 36 |
| Cash | 20 | 9 |
| Current liabilities | 80 | 125 |

Step 1, gross profit. Year 1: 480 − 288 = 192. Year 2: 540 − 351 = 189.

Step 2, gross profit margin. Year 1: 192 ÷ 480 × 100 = 40%. Year 2: 189 ÷ 540 × 100 = 35%.

Step 3, profit margin. Year 1: 57.6 ÷ 480 × 100 = 12%. Year 2: 43.2 ÷ 540 × 100 = 8%.

Step 4, ROCE. Year 1: 57.6 ÷ 360 × 100 = 16%. Year 2: 43.2 ÷ 432 × 100 = 10%.

Step 5, current assets. Year 1: 70 + 30 + 20 = 120. Year 2: 105 + 36 + 9 = 150.

Step 6, current ratio. Year 1: 120 ÷ 80 = 1.5. Year 2: 150 ÷ 125 = 1.2.

Step 7, acid test ratio. Year 1: (120 − 70) ÷ 80 = 0.625, which rounds to 0.63. Year 2: (150 − 105) ÷ 125 = 0.36.

Step 8, analyse. Revenue grew by 12.5%, but every profitability measure fell. Gross profit margin fell by five percentage points, so the cost of the bicycles and parts sold rose faster than prices, or discounts were given to win sales. Profit margin fell by four percentage points, so overheads did not shrink either. ROCE fell from 16% to 10%, because capital employed grew by 72 while profit fell: the business put in more money and earned less from it. Liquidity also weakened. Current ratio fell from 1.5 to 1.2, and the acid test ratio fell to 0.36, which means that without selling inventories the business has only about 36 cents of liquid assets for each dollar owed in the short term. Cash fell from 20 to 9 while inventories rose from 70 to 105, so money has been tied up in stock.

Step 9, a decision. A bank asked for a new loan would see falling returns and weak liquidity, and would probably ask for more information or security before lending.

### Interpreting each ratio

Gross profit margin shows how much of each dollar of revenue is left after the direct cost of the goods or services sold. A fall points to cheaper pricing, dearer suppliers or wastage. A rise can come from higher prices or cheaper purchasing.

Profit margin shows how much is left after all costs. If gross profit margin is steady but profit margin falls, the cause is in overheads such as rent, wages or marketing, not in the cost of sales.

ROCE shows the return on the money invested in the business. Higher is better. It is useful for comparing with earlier years, with similar businesses, and with what the money could earn elsewhere, such as in a savings account.

Current ratio shows whether current assets cover current liabilities. A result below 1 means that current liabilities are larger than current assets. A result far above what is usual for the industry can mean cash or inventories are sitting idle. There is no single "correct" figure that applies to every business.

Acid test ratio is the stricter test. A business with high inventories may have a comfortable current ratio but a weak acid test ratio. A supermarket and a furniture maker will not have the same typical figures.

### Comparing and judging

A ratio on its own says little. Make a judgement by comparing with:

- the same business in earlier years (trend)
- similar businesses (competitors)
- the business's own targets

Then ask why the figure changed, using the other information in the case. Look at rising costs, a change in prices, extra borrowing, a new loan, slower payment by customers or large purchases of inventories. Link the cause to the business in the question.

## Why and how accounts are used (5.5.4)

Different users want different things from the same set of accounts and ratios.

| User | What they want to know | Example decision |
|---|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Is the business making a good return on their investment? | Keep, increase or sell the investment; vote on dividends |
| Managers | Which products and costs need attention? How does this year compare with the plan? | Cut costs, change prices, set targets |
| Lenders such as banks | Can the loan and interest be repaid? | Whether to lend, how much, and on what terms |
| Suppliers | Will invoices be paid on time? | Whether to allow credit, and for how long |
| Employees | Is the business secure and profitable enough to keep jobs and pay rises? | Whether to ask for higher pay or look for another job |
| Government | Is the right amount of tax being paid? | Tax assessment |
| Potential investors | Is the return high enough to justify the risk? | Whether to invest |

These are examples. The syllabus asks for the needs of different users and how they might use results, and gives lending and investing as examples.

Match the ratio to the user. A lender or supplier cares most about liquidity (current ratio, acid test) and profit that can cover interest. An investor cares most about ROCE and profit margin. A manager looks at all five, mainly to spot problems early.

## Common errors

- Dividing gross profit by cost of sales instead of revenue.
- Using revenue as the profit figure in ROCE.
- Writing a % sign after the current ratio or acid test ratio, or leaving it off the margins and ROCE.
- Leaving inventories in the acid test calculation.
- Describing a figure without explaining what it means for the business.
- Saying a high current ratio is always good, with no comment on idle assets.
- Naming a user without saying which result that user would look at and what they would decide.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between profitability and liquidity?**
Profitability is how well a business earns profit compared with its revenue or the money invested in it. Liquidity is its ability to pay short-term debts when they fall due, using cash or assets that can quickly become cash.

**What does it mean if gross profit margin is steady but profit margin falls?**
The cause lies in overheads such as rent, wages or marketing, not in the cost of sales. A fall in gross profit margin itself points to cheaper pricing, dearer suppliers or wastage.

**Can a profitable business run out of cash?**
Yes. Profit and cash are different: a business can sell on credit and record a profit while customers have not yet paid, and it can be forced to stop trading if it cannot pay wages or suppliers on time.

**How do you judge whether a ratio is good or bad?**
A ratio on its own says little. Compare it with the same business in earlier years, with similar businesses and with the business's own targets, then explain why it changed using the case.
