**Condensed notes on the source questions in Paper 1 Section B.** The specification lists question 1, "Source analysis" (4 marks), and question 2, "How useful are sources..." (12 marks), both testing AO3, with "one source... for the first question and two sources for the second" and "visual and written sources". The method is explained in full, with a Korean War example, in the [source analysis and usefulness study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-world-history-8145-source-analysis-and-usefulness/); the other two Section B questions are summarised in the [wider world depth studies revision notes](/resources/gcse-history-wider-world-depth-studies-revision-notes/).

## The two questions

| Question | Marks | Wording pattern | Core task |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | "Source A supports/opposes... How do you know?" | Show how content and provenance reveal the view, with context |
| 2 | 12 | "How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying...?" | Evaluate each source's content, provenance and context for the named enquiry |

Together they are 16 of the 40 Section B marks: around 5 minutes for question 1, 15 for question 2.

## Content, provenance, context

- **Content**: what it says or shows; the message; what you can infer.
- **Provenance**: nature (type), origin (author, place, date), purpose (why made, for whom). Remember **NOP**.
- **Context**: what was happening at the time; your knowledge that confirms, explains or adds.

## Cartoon checklist

1. Caption and labels
2. Figures: size, expression, caricature
3. Symbols: bear (Russia), Uncle Sam (USA), dove (peace), chains, bombs
4. Action: what is happening, what might happen next
5. Message in one sentence
6. Provenance: country, publication, date

## Question 1 recipe

- Detail from the source **+** what it shows about the view.
- Second detail **+** contextual knowledge explaining why this view was held then.
- Provenance (publication, country, date) **+** why the maker would take this line.

## Question 2 recipe (for each source)

1. **Useful for** -- content linked to the enquiry, confirmed or extended by knowledge.
2. **Because of who made it** -- provenance explained: what the type, author and date make it good evidence of.
3. **Less useful for** -- gaps or one-sidedness, explained by provenance.
4. **Compare** -- a final sentence on how the two sources work together or which is more useful for this enquiry.

## What different types of source are useful for

| Type | Good evidence of | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Propaganda poster | What a government wanted people to believe | Exaggeration; deliberate omissions |
| Cartoon | Public or press opinion in one country | Simplification; satire |
| Speech | A leader's public aims and justifications | Purpose to persuade |
| Private letter or diary | Personal experience and feelings | Narrow viewpoint |
| Official report | Government information and decisions | What officials wanted recorded |
| Photograph | A moment, place or scale | Selection, staging, caption |
| Newspaper report | Events as reported, and the paper's stance | Editorial line; censorship |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Source | Evidence produced at the time being studied |
| Provenance | Nature, origin and purpose of a source |
| Inference | A conclusion drawn from what a source suggests, not just states |
| Usefulness | What a source can tell an historian about a particular enquiry |
| Reliability | How far a source can be trusted as an accurate account |
| Propaganda | Material designed to persuade |
| Caricature | Exaggerated drawing of a person for effect |
| Contextual knowledge | Your own knowledge of the period |
| Enquiry | The issue the historian is studying, named in the question |

## Usefulness versus reliability

An unreliable source can be **very useful**. A Nazi poster attacking the Treaty of Versailles is unreliable as an account of the treaty, but it is excellent evidence of how the Nazis exploited German anger. Always answer the enquiry in the question: useful *for what*? A strong sentence pattern: "Source C is useful for showing how the East German government justified the Berlin Wall, precisely because it is government propaganda."

## Exam traps

- Describing the cartoon without stating its message.
- Writing "biased so not useful" with no explanation.
- Using provenance that could fit any source ("it is primary so it is reliable").
- Leaving out contextual knowledge.
- Answering on only one source in question 2.
- Ignoring the named enquiry and evaluating the source in general.

## Self-test

1. How many sources does question 1 use, and how many does question 2 use?
2. Which assessment objective do the source questions test?
3. What does NOP stand for?
4. Name three things to check in a cartoon.
5. What is the difference between usefulness and reliability?
6. Why can a propaganda poster be useful?
7. What is an inference?
8. What should a question 2 answer include for each source?
9. What is a weak way to use provenance?
10. Roughly how long should you spend on question 2?
11. What makes a private letter useful?
12. What is the "enquiry" in a usefulness question?

## Answers

1. One for question 1; two for question 2.
2. AO3.
3. Nature, origin, purpose.
4. Any three: caption, labels, figures, symbols, action, message, provenance.
5. Usefulness is what a source can tell an historian about an enquiry; reliability is how far it can be trusted as accurate.
6. It shows what its makers wanted people to believe, and how they tried to persuade them.
7. A conclusion drawn from what a source suggests rather than states directly.
8. Content linked to the enquiry, contextual knowledge, provenance and what it is less useful for.
9. A general comment that could apply to any source, such as "it was written at the time so it is true".
10. About fifteen minutes.
11. It gives a frank, personal reaction, not written for publication.
12. The issue the historian is studying, named in the question.
