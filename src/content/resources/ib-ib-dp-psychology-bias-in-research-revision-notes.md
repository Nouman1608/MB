These notes cover **bias** in research for IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027), one of the three research considerations that question 3 of HL Paper 3 (6 marks) can ask about ("discuss how the researcher could avoid bias"). Bias is also one of the six concepts of the course. The other research ideas are in the [approaches to researching behaviour revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-approaches-to-researching-behaviour-revision-notes/); the full explanations and a worked Paper 3 answer are in the [bias in research study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-bias-in-research/).

## Core definitions

- **Bias:** a systematic influence that pushes results away from the truth, consistently in one direction.
- **Random error:** unsystematic variation that makes results less precise but not consistently wrong.
- **Participant bias:** comes from how participants respond.
- **Researcher bias:** comes from how the researcher designs, collects, analyses or reports.
- **Reflexivity:** the researcher's reflection on their own influence on the research.

## Participant bias: type, trigger, fix

| Type | Typical trigger in a study | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Demand characteristics | participants know or guess the hypothesis | cover story; independent samples; debrief |
| Social desirability | sensitive topic; others can overhear; interviewer has status | anonymity; private setting; neutral questions; rapport |
| Acquiescence | all statements worded in the same direction; yes/no questions | balanced wording; open questions |
| Dominant respondent | focus group with one confident member | skilled moderator; smaller groups; extra individual interviews |
| Sensitivity bias | painful or embarrassing subject | rapport; trained interviewer; right to skip questions |
| Placebo effect | participants expect a treatment to work | placebo control group; blind procedures |

## Researcher bias: type, trigger, fix

| Type | Typical trigger in a study | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | researcher has a strong expectation or personal connection | reflexive diary; second analyst; recordings and transcripts |
| Expectancy effects | researcher knows which condition a participant is in | double-blind procedure; scripted instructions |
| Leading questions | questions suggest an answer | open, neutral wording; pilot study |
| Question order | early questions frame later ones | general to specific; vary order |
| Sampling bias | volunteers or one convenient group | random or stratified sampling; clear purposive criteria |
| Biased reporting | only supportive findings reported | report all themes; supporting quotations |
| Cultural bias | outsider interprets behaviour by own norms | emic approach; local co-researchers; reflexivity |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Personal reflexivity | how the researcher's own background and beliefs may shape the research |
| Epistemological reflexivity | how the chosen method and design may have shaped the findings |
| Researcher triangulation | more than one researcher analyses the data independently and results are compared |
| Rich description | detailed reporting of context and quotations so readers can judge interpretations |
| Rapport | trust between interviewer and participant, which encourages open answers |
| Pilot study | a small trial run used to test procedures and questions before the main study |
| Double-blind | neither participants nor the researcher collecting data know which condition each participant is in |

## Quantitative versus qualitative

| | Experiments | Qualitative studies |
|---|---|---|
| Main source of bias | design and expectations | the researcher as the instrument; social setting of interviews |
| Main strategies | random allocation, standardisation, blind procedures, control groups | reflexivity, triangulation, recording, neutral questions, rapport |
| What a good answer shows | controls are built into the procedure | bias is made visible and checked, not removed |

## Paper 3 answer recipe

1. Read the summary twice; mark the setting, recruitment, researcher background and analysis.
2. Pick three or four biases that genuinely apply.
3. For each: where it arises in this study, a realistic strategy, and how far the strategy would work.
4. End with which bias matters most and why.

## Exam traps

- **Generic lists.** Every bias must be tied to a detail of the study.
- **Wrong toolkit.** Double-blind procedures belong to experiments; reflexivity belongs to qualitative research.
- **Drifting into generalisation.** Sampling can be discussed, but explain how it biases results, not only who the results apply to.
- **"Reflexivity removes bias."** It makes bias visible so it can be judged.
- **Changing the aim.** Do not turn an interview study into an experiment to remove bias.

## Self-test

1. What makes an error a bias rather than random error?
2. Define social desirability bias.
3. How can acquiescence bias be reduced in a questionnaire?
4. What is a dominant respondent, and in which method does it occur?
5. Give one way to reduce demand characteristics in an experiment.
6. What is confirmation bias in qualitative analysis?
7. Distinguish personal and epistemological reflexivity.
8. What is researcher triangulation?
9. Why is question order a possible source of bias?
10. Why is double-blind a poor suggestion for an interview study?
11. How can an interview setting increase social desirability?
12. What should the final sentence of a Paper 3 bias answer do?

**Answers**

1. A bias pushes results consistently in one direction; random error varies unpredictably.
2. Participants answer in a way that presents them favourably rather than truthfully.
3. Word statements in both directions so agreeing with all of them is inconsistent, or use open questions.
4. One confident member steering the discussion in a focus group.
5. For example: a cover story; independent samples; standardised instructions that do not reveal the hypothesis.
6. Noticing or interpreting data in ways that fit what the researcher expects.
7. Personal reflexivity concerns the researcher's own background and beliefs; epistemological reflexivity concerns how the method and design shaped the findings.
8. Two or more researchers analyse the same data independently and compare their conclusions.
9. Earlier questions can frame how participants think about later ones.
10. In an interview the researcher is present and asks the questions, so neither side can be "blind"; reflexivity and a second analyst are more appropriate.
11. If others can overhear or the interviewer has authority over participants, they may give answers that protect their image.
12. Give a judgement about which bias is most serious in this study and which strategy matters most.
