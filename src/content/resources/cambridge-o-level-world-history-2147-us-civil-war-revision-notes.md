**These notes condense key question 1.4 of Cambridge O Level History 2147: why the United States fought a civil war
and what the war changed.** The question is the Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2026, so treat the dates and names
below as the checklist you test sources against. For full explanation, read the
[US Civil War study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-us-civil-war/); for exam practice, use the
[US Civil War practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-us-civil-war-practice/).

## Condensed recall by focus point

**How far did slavery cause the war?**
- Every major crisis from 1820 concerned slavery in the West: Missouri (1820), the Wilmot Proviso (1846), 1850,
  Kansas–Nebraska (1854), Dred Scott (1857).
- States' rights meant, above all, the right to keep slavery.
- Economic differences (industry against cotton, tariffs) mattered, but the cotton economy depended on enslaved labour.
- The breakdown of national parties (Whigs collapse, Republicans purely Northern, Democrats split in 1860) removed the
  means to compromise.

**What was the significance of Lincoln's election?**
- Won with no Southern electoral votes: the North could govern alone.
- Republicans would stop slavery's spread, which Southerners saw as the start of its end.
- Triggered secession: seven states by February 1861, eleven after Fort Sumter.
- Lincoln rejected the Crittenden Compromise and would fight to keep the Union.

**Why did the North win?**
- Northern strengths: population, industry, railways, navy and blockade, finance.
- Confederate weaknesses: states' rights disputes with governors, inflation, conscription exemptions for large
  slaveholders, desertion, escape of enslaved people.
- Turning points: Antietam and emancipation (1862–63); Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863); Atlanta and the March to
  the Sea (1864); Lincoln's re-election (November 1864).

**Did the war change anything?**
- Yes, legally: Union preserved, slavery abolished, citizenship and voting rights written into the Constitution.
- Less in practice: Black Codes, sharecropping, Klan violence, withdrawal of federal troops in 1877.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Free state / slave state | A state that banned or allowed slavery |
| Abolitionist | Someone who campaigned to end slavery completely |
| Popular sovereignty | Letting a territory's settlers vote on slavery |
| Wilmot Proviso | 1846 proposal to ban slavery in land gained from Mexico |
| Secession | A state's formal withdrawal from the Union |
| Confederacy | The Confederate States of America, 1861–65 |
| Border states | Slave states that stayed in the Union: Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri |
| Anaconda Plan | Union strategy to blockade the coast and control the Mississippi |
| Total war | Destroying the enemy's economic ability to fight, as in Sherman's march |
| Ten Percent Plan | Lincoln's 1863 terms for readmitting Southern states |
| Black Codes | Southern laws of 1865–66 restricting freed people |
| Radical Republicans | Members of Congress who wanted equal rights and a tough Reconstruction |
| Sharecropping | Farming a landowner's land for a share of the crop, often trapping families in debt |
| Redeemers | White Southern Democrats who regained control of state governments in the 1870s |

## Confederate weaknesses at a glance

| Weakness | Evidence |
|---|---|
| States' rights | Governors resisted central conscription and kept supplies |
| Finance | Paper money and severe inflation |
| Unequal sacrifice | Exemption for those with twenty or more enslaved people; desertion |
| Labour | Enslaved people fled to Union lines |
| Foreign help | Britain and France never recognised the Confederacy |

## Exam traps

- **Answering "how far did slavery cause the war" with only slavery.** Explain states' rights, economic differences
  and political breakdown, then show how they relate to slavery.
- **Confusing emancipation and abolition.** Emancipation Proclamation 1863 (rebel areas only); 13th Amendment 1865
  (whole nation).
- **Calling Fort Sumter the cause.** It was the start of fighting; secession had already happened.
- **Dating Reconstruction 1865–70.** It begins with Lincoln's plan in 1863 and ends in 1877.
- **Paper 2 provenance.** Lincoln's public letters were written for audiences; read them as political statements.

## Self-test

1. What did the Wilmot Proviso propose?
2. Why did the Fugitive Slave Act anger Northerners?
3. What was the "Freeport doctrine", and why did it hurt Douglas?
4. Why could the Crittenden Compromise not succeed?
5. Why did Lincoln's victory alarm the South even though he promised not to touch slavery where it existed?
6. Name the four border states.
7. Give **two** weaknesses of the Confederate government.
8. Why was July 1863 a turning point?
9. What did the Ten Percent Plan require?
10. What did the Reconstruction Acts of 1867 do?
11. What was sharecropping?
12. What happened in the Compromise of 1877?

**Answers:** 1. A ban on slavery in all land gained from Mexico. 2. It forced Northern officials and citizens to help
return people who had escaped slavery. 3. Settlers could keep slavery out by refusing laws to protect it; Southern
Democrats saw it as betrayal. 4. Lincoln and the Republicans would not allow slavery to extend into any territory.
5. Stopping slavery's spread meant free states would eventually dominate and might abolish it; he had won without any
Southern votes. 6. Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri. 7. Any two: states' rights quarrels; inflation; unfair
conscription exemptions; desertion; loss of enslaved labour. 8. Gettysburg ended Lee's invasion of the North and
Vicksburg gave the Union the Mississippi. 9. A state could rejoin when 10% of its voters (of 1860) swore loyalty and
accepted emancipation. 10. Placed the South under military districts and required new state constitutions accepting
Black male voting and the 14th Amendment. 11. Farming land for a share of the crop, usually leaving families in debt.
12. Hayes became president and the last federal troops left the South.
