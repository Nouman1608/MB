This set covers one content point of key development 3.1.3.1: "Muhammad bin Qasim, the expansion of
the Umayyad Caliphate in the subcontinent, and its religious impact". It is part of the optional
topic examined in Paper 1 Section C, which has short and extended response questions but no multiple
choice; the multiple-choice items below are recall drills. The questions do not repeat those in the
[Islam in the Subcontinent practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-pakistan-studies-9236-islam-subcontinent-practice/).
Learn the content from the [Muhammad bin Qasim study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-pakistan-studies-9236-muhammad-bin-qasim/).

## Section A — short-answer questions

**1.** Which Umayyad Caliph ruled when Muhammad bin Qasim invaded Sindh? **[1]**

A: Sulaiman &nbsp; B: al-Walid I &nbsp; C: Umar bin Abdul Aziz &nbsp; D: Muawiya

**2.** Which port near the mouth of the Indus did Muhammad bin Qasim capture first? **[1]**

A: Debal &nbsp; B: Multan &nbsp; C: Alor &nbsp; D: Sehwan

**3.** Which city in southern Punjab did he capture in 713? **[1]**

A: Lahore &nbsp; B: Multan &nbsp; C: Peshawar &nbsp; D: Thatta

**4.** What name, meaning "gateway of Islam", is given to Sindh? **[1]**

A: Dar-ul-Islam &nbsp; B: Bab-ul-Islam &nbsp; C: Dar-ul-harb &nbsp; D: Bait-ul-Hikmah

**5.** State **two** reasons why Hajjaj bin Yusuf sent an army to Sindh. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** features of the way Muhammad bin Qasim governed Sindh. **[2]**

**7.** State **two** reasons why Muhammad bin Qasim's career in Sindh ended in 715. **[2]**

## Section B — extended questions

**8.** Identify and explain **two** reasons why Muhammad bin Qasim was able to defeat Raja Dahir.
**[4]**

**9.** Identify and explain **two** ways in which the conquest linked Sindh to the wider Muslim world.
**[4]**

**10.** Analyse **two** reasons why Muhammad bin Qasim allowed Hindus and Buddhists to keep their
religion. **[6]**

**11.** Analyse **two** ways in which the conquest of Sindh had a religious impact on the
subcontinent. **[6]**

**12.** "The capture of ships by pirates near Debal was the main reason for the Umayyad invasion of
Sindh."

How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer using the statement above and your
contextual knowledge. **[9]**

**13.** "Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest had only a limited religious impact on the subcontinent."

How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. **[9]**

## Answers

Multiple-choice questions score 1 mark. "State" questions score 1 mark per valid point. "Identify
and explain" questions score 1 mark for each point identified and 1 for its explanation. The 6- and
9-mark questions are marked by levels; the level descriptions follow the answers. Credit other
accurate points.

**1.** **B: al-Walid I** [1]. Sulaiman succeeded him in 715 and recalled Muhammad bin Qasim. **[1]**

**2.** **A: Debal** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** **B: Multan** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **B: Bab-ul-Islam** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Two reasons, 1 mark each: pirates near Debal had seized ships carrying gifts and Muslim
families, and Raja Dahir refused responsibility [1]; earlier expeditions had failed, damaging Umayyad
prestige [1]. Also credit: Dahir sheltered Arab rebels; the wealth and trade of Sindh; the wider
expansion of the caliphate. **[2]**

**6.** Two features, 1 mark each: Hindus and Buddhists were given protected (dhimmi) status in return
for jizya [1]; local officials, including Brahmins, were kept in charge of revenue [1]. Also credit:
temples could be repaired; Arab garrisons and mosques were established in key towns. **[2]**

**7.** Two reasons, 1 mark each: his patron Hajjaj died in 714 [1]; the new Caliph Sulaiman, hostile to
Hajjaj's family, recalled and imprisoned him in 715 [1]. Also credit: the death of al-Walid ended
the policy of expansion that had supported him. **[2]**

**8.** Two reasons, each identified (1) and explained (1):
- his army was well equipped, with siege engines sent by sea [1], so strongly defended towns such as
  Debal could be taken quickly [1];
- he offered terms to towns that surrendered [1], so places such as Nerun gave in without fighting and
  Dahir's support weakened [1].

Also credit: strong cavalry; backing and supplies from Hajjaj; divisions within Sindh. **[4]**

**9.** Two ways, each identified (1) and explained (1):
- Sindh became part of the caliphate's trading network [1], so goods and merchants moved between the
  Indus and the Arab world [1];
- Arab scholars and settlers came to Sindh [1], and Indian learning in mathematics and astronomy later
  reached Baghdad by these routes [1].

Also credit: Islamic law and Arabic introduced through mosques and courts. **[4]**

**10.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- *Practical government:* the Arabs were a small minority ruling a large population; tolerance reduced
  rebellion and kept the land productive.
- *Revenue:* jizya from protected subjects and taxes collected by experienced local officials provided
  income for the new government.
- *Islamic principles:* treating non-Muslims as protected people paying jizya followed the existing
  practice of the caliphate towards Christians and Jews, extended here to Hindus and Buddhists.
- *Winning support:* fair terms encouraged other towns to surrender rather than resist. **[6]**

**11.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- *First Muslim rule:* Sindh became the first region of the subcontinent ruled by Muslims, giving
  Islam a permanent foothold.
- *Mosques and settlers:* mosques in towns such as Debal and Arab settlers introduced Islamic worship,
  law and the Arabic language.
- *Gradual conversion:* some local people converted through contact with traders, officials and
  scholars, over generations.
- *A model for rule:* the dhimmi settlement became a pattern for later Muslim rulers governing
  non-Muslim majorities. **[6]**

**12.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* the seizure of the ships and Dahir's refusal to act gave Hajjaj a clear reason and a
  public justification; it was the immediate trigger.
- *Disagree:* earlier failed expeditions had to be avenged to protect prestige; Dahir sheltered
  rebels; Sindh's wealth and trade routes were attractive; the invasion was one part of a much wider
  Umayyad expansion under al-Walid.
- *Judgement:* for example, the pirate incident explains why the invasion happened in 711, but the
  deeper causes explain why the Umayyads were ready to invade at all. **[9]**

**13.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* conversion was slow and partial; the conquest reached only Sindh and Multan; Arab rule
  later broke into smaller states; the main spread of Islam came later through the Ghaznavids, the
  Delhi Sultanate and Sufi saints.
- *Disagree:* Sindh became *Bab-ul-Islam*, the first Muslim-ruled region; mosques, settlers and
  scholars introduced Islamic institutions; links with the caliphate brought trade and learning; the
  dhimmi settlement offered a lasting model of tolerant rule.
- *Judgement:* for example, the impact was limited in extent but important as a foundation, because
  later Muslim rulers built on an established Muslim presence in the Indus valley. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Claiming he converted the population of Sindh.
- Mixing up Hajjaj, al-Walid and Sulaiman.
- Explaining only the pirate incident in a question about causes.
- In judgement questions, describing the conquest without assessing its impact.

## Frequently asked questions

**How reliable are the details of the conquest?**
Much comes from the *Chachnama*, written centuries later. Use phrases such as "according to
traditional accounts" for details like the fall of the flag at Debal.

**Do I need exact dates?**
Know 711-712 for Debal and Dahir's defeat, 713 for Multan and 715 for the recall.
