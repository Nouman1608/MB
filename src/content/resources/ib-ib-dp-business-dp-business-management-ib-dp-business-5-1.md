This guide goes deeper into section 5.1 (Introduction to operations management) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The section applies to SL and HL. It has one content point, the role of operations management, set at AO2. That means you must apply the idea to the organisation in the case: what its operations do, what they must achieve, and how they affect the rest of the business.

The broader [5.1 and 5.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-introduction-to-operations-management-and-operations-methods/) defines operations management and lists the decisions operations managers take. This page adds how operations differ between goods and services, a calculation of value added, the trade-offs between performance aims, and an annotated AO2 answer. ## What section 5.1 asks for

| Content point | Depth |
|---|---|
| The role of operations management | AO2 |

The guide gives no breakdown of this point. The ideas below are the standard ways of explaining the role so that it can be applied to a case.

## The transformation process, applied

Operations management plans, organises and controls the process that turns **inputs** into **outputs**. Applying this to a case means naming the actual inputs, process and outputs of that organisation.

| Organisation | Inputs | Transformation | Outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Candle maker | Wax, wicks, fragrance, moulds, labour, energy | Melting, blending, pouring, curing, packing | Finished candles |
| Hospital | Doctors, nurses, equipment, medicines, patient information | Diagnosis, treatment, care | Treated patients |
| Online retailer | Stock, warehouse space, software, staff | Storing, picking, packing, dispatching | Delivered orders |
| School | Teachers, buildings, materials, students' time | Teaching and assessment | Educated students |

Some inputs are themselves transformed (wax becomes a candle; a patient becomes healthier). Others do the transforming and are reused (moulds, staff, buildings). Recognising both kinds shows a precise understanding of the process.

## Value added

Value added is the difference between the price customers pay and the cost of the bought-in inputs. Raising value added is a central purpose of operations.

### Worked example: Copperleaf Candles

Copperleaf Candles is invented, and all figures are invented (money in dollars). One candle sells for 18.00. Bought-in materials and packaging cost 6.50.

* Value added per candle = 18.00 − 6.50 = **11.50**.
* At 4,000 candles a month, total value added = 4,000 × 11.50 = **46,000**.

Value added must still cover wages, rent and other costs before any profit is made, so it is not the same as profit. Operations can raise value added in two ways:

* **Lower the cost of inputs**, for example by reducing wax wasted when pouring.
* **Make the output worth more to customers**, for example with hand-finished decoration that supports a higher price.

If Copperleaf cut waste so that materials cost 6.00, value added would rise to 12.00 per candle, or 48,000 a month at the same output.

## Operations in goods and services

Every organisation has operations, but the role looks different when the output is a service.

| Feature | Goods (e.g. candles) | Services (e.g. a hair salon) |
|---|---|---|
| Tangibility | Physical product that can be inspected | Intangible experience |
| Storage | Can be stored as stock | Cannot be stored; an empty appointment slot is lost |
| Production and consumption | Made before it is bought | Produced and consumed at the same time |
| Customer involvement | Customer usually absent from production | Customer present and part of the process |
| Quality control | Products can be checked before sale | Quality depends on each interaction |

Implications for the role of operations:

* A goods producer can use stock to absorb changes in demand. A service provider must match staff to demand hour by hour, because it cannot store output.
* In services, staff behaviour is part of the product, so operations and human resource decisions are closely linked.
* Many organisations deliver both: a restaurant produces food (a good) and table service (a service).

## Performance aims and their trade-offs

A practical way to describe what operations must achieve is through five aims that customers notice. These are a standard textbook framework, not guide wording.

| Aim | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Getting things right | Candles burn evenly; salon cuts match the request |
| Speed | Short time from order to delivery | Same-day dispatch |
| Dependability | Keeping promises on time | Deliveries arrive on the day stated |
| Flexibility | Ability to change products, volume or timing | Custom scents; extra stock before holidays |
| Cost | Producing at low cost per unit | Less waste, efficient use of labour |

The aims often conflict. Copperleaf could offer custom scents (flexibility), but each change of fragrance means cleaning equipment, which slows output and raises cost. An online retailer promising next-day delivery (speed) may need more staff on standby, raising cost. The role of operations management is to decide which aims matter most for the organisation's customers and objectives, and to accept the trade-offs that follow.

## Links to other business functions

Operations decisions ripple through the organisation:

* **Marketing**: the promises made to customers (price, quality, delivery times) must be ones operations can keep.
* **Finance**: equipment, premises and stock require capital expenditure and working capital.
* **Human resources**: methods of working determine the skills, numbers and training of staff.

The guide's conceptual understandings for this unit stress change, creativity, ethics and sustainability. In operations these appear as: new processes that change other functions; creative processes that can disrupt markets; ethical choices about working conditions and sourcing; and sustainable practices, such as cutting waste and energy use, that often reduce cost as well as harm.

## Annotated AO2 answer

*Question: Explain the role of operations management at Copperleaf Candles.*

> Operations management at Copperleaf turns inputs such as wax, fragrance and labour into finished candles, and its job is to do this at the quality and cost the business needs. **[transformation applied]** Each candle sells for 18.00 but its materials cost 6.50, so operations add 11.50 of value per candle; reducing wasted wax would raise this. **[value added with data]** Operations must also balance aims: offering custom scents would appeal to customers, but frequent changes of fragrance would slow production and raise costs. **[trade-off in context]** Because Copperleaf's marketing promises hand-finished quality, operations must protect that quality even if it limits how fast output can grow. **[link to another function]**

Each sentence applies the role to Copperleaf, which is the AO2 requirement. A generic description of operations would earn less.

## Common mistakes

* Treating operations as factory work only; services have operations too.
* Equating value added with profit.
* Listing operations activities with no link to the organisation in the case.
* Assuming all performance aims can be maximised at once.
* Ignoring the effect of operations decisions on marketing, finance and HR.

## Exam technique

* **Describe** or **outline**: state the inputs, process and outputs of the case organisation.
* **Explain**: link an operations decision to a consequence for customers or another function.
* **Analyse**: identify a trade-off between performance aims and show its effect, using case data where possible.
