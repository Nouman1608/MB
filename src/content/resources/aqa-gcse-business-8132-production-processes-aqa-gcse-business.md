## Job production

Job production makes a single product, or a very small number, to a customer's individual order. Each job is different. Examples include a made-to-measure wedding dress, an extension to a house, a bespoke website, or a portrait painted from a photograph.

**Features:**

- Work starts only when an order is received.
- Each product is designed to meet a particular customer's needs.
- Workers are usually highly skilled and do many different tasks.
- Tools and equipment are general purpose, so they can be used for different jobs.

**Advantages:**

- The customer gets exactly what they want, so the business can charge a high price.
- Workers find varied, skilled work more satisfying, which can help motivation.
- Little finished stock is held, because each item is sold before it is made.
- The business can respond to changing tastes easily.

**Disadvantages:**

- High cost per unit, because skilled labour takes a long time on each item.
- Slow, so output is low.
- Few economies of scale, since materials are bought in small amounts.
- Revenue can be irregular, depending on when orders arrive.

## Flow production

Flow production makes large quantities of identical products continuously, with each item moving from one stage to the next along a production line. Examples include bottled water, chocolate bars, tinned food and newspapers.

**Features:**

- Production runs continuously, often with heavy use of machinery.
- Each worker or machine does one specialised task.
- Products are standardised, so every unit is the same.
- Output is made in anticipation of demand, so stock is held.

**Advantages:**

- Low cost per unit, because fixed costs such as machinery are spread over a huge output.
- Fast, high output to meet large demand.
- Bulk buying of materials brings lower prices.
- Consistent quality, as every unit follows the same process.

**Disadvantages:**

- High start-up cost for machinery and the production line.
- Inflexible: changing the product can mean stopping the line and re-equipping it.
- Repetitive work can lower motivation.
- A breakdown at one stage can stop the whole line.
- Needs high, steady demand to justify the investment.

## When each is appropriate

| Ask this about the business | Points to job | Points to flow |
|---|---|---|
| Does each customer want something different? | Yes | No |
| How large and steady is demand? | Small or irregular | Large and steady |
| Can customers wait? | Yes, they expect to | No, they buy from the shelf |
| Is the price premium or competitive? | Premium | Competitive |
| Can the business afford expensive machinery? | Not needed | Required |

Some businesses use both. A furniture maker might make standard chairs on a line and also take one-off commissions. In an exam, choose the method that fits the product and market described, and say why the other is less suitable.

## Efficiency and lean production

Efficiency means producing output with as few resources as possible: less time, fewer materials, less space, lower cost. One common measure is **productivity**, the output per worker in a given period.

Productivity = total output ÷ number of workers

**Lean production** is an approach that aims to cut waste of every kind. Waste includes:

- making more than customers want, which ties up money in stock;
- waiting, when workers or machines sit idle;
- unnecessary movement of goods or people around a site;
- defects that must be fixed or thrown away;
- holding more materials than needed.

### Lean techniques

- **Just in time (JIT):** materials and components arrive just before they are needed, and products are made to meet orders rather than to build up stock. This cuts storage costs and waste, but depends on reliable suppliers.
- **Continuous improvement (often called kaizen):** workers are encouraged to suggest many small improvements to their own tasks. Over time, these add up to large savings.
- **Getting it right first time:** building quality checks into each stage, so faults are found early and fewer products are wasted.
- **Reorganising the workplace:** placing tools and stages close together to reduce movement and waiting.

### Benefits and drawbacks of lean production

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Lower costs from less waste and storage | Depends on suppliers delivering reliably |
| Less money tied up in stock | Little spare stock if demand suddenly rises |
| Quicker production and delivery | Takes time and training to introduce |
| Staff involvement can raise motivation | Staff may resist change or worry about jobs |
| Better quality, fewer defects | More frequent deliveries can add transport costs |

## Worked example: measuring a lean improvement

Figures invented. Penrose Bottling fills bottles of fruit cordial on a flow line with 20 workers. It produces 48,000 bottles a week, and 1,200 a week are rejected for underfilling.

Before changes:

- Productivity = 48,000 ÷ 20 = 2,400 bottles per worker per week.
- Reject rate = 1,200 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 2.5%.

Penrose introduces continuous improvement. Workers suggest moving the labelling machine next to the filler and checking fill levels at each station. A week later the line produces 52,000 bottles with the same 20 workers, and rejects fall to 520.

After changes:

- Productivity = 52,000 ÷ 20 = 2,600 bottles per worker per week, a rise of 200, or about 8.3%.
- Reject rate = 520 ÷ 52,000 × 100 = 1%.

Wage costs are unchanged, but output is higher and less is wasted, so the cost per good bottle falls. Penrose could cut its price to win supermarket contracts, or keep the price and earn a higher profit margin.

## Exam technique

- **State or identify:** name a method or a lean technique.
- **Explain:** link a feature to an effect, such as "flow uses machinery → fixed costs spread over high output → low unit cost".
- **Analyse:** follow the effect into another function, such as lower unit costs letting marketing set a lower price.
- **Evaluate or recommend:** decide which method or technique suits the business, using the case study's evidence about demand, customers and finance, and acknowledge the main drawback.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up production methods (job, flow) with stock methods (JIT and just in case).
- Saying flow production is always better because it is cheaper per unit. It needs high, steady demand.
- Describing lean production only as JIT. There are several techniques.
- Calling job production "low quality" or "small business only". Large firms also use it for one-off projects.
- Forgetting the human side: repetitive work in flow production can lower motivation.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between job and flow production?**
Job production makes one-off products to individual orders, giving flexibility but a high unit cost. Flow production makes large quantities of identical products continuously, giving a low unit cost but requiring costly machinery and steady demand.

**Is JIT a production method or a stock method?**
It is both a lean production technique in section 3.3.1 and a stock management method in section 3.3.2. In both, materials arrive just before they are needed.

**How do you measure efficiency in production?**
A common measure is productivity, which is output per worker. A fall in the reject rate also shows less waste.
