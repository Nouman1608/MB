Subtopic 2.4 Climate and biomes is part of Topic 2 Ecology in IB Diploma Programme Environmental
Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The IB's public subject brief names Topic 2 but
not its subtopics, so this title follows the course's provisional checklist; confirm it in your

The [climate, biomes and succession study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-climate-biomes-succession/)
explains insolation, the tricellular model, ocean currents and the seven main terrestrial biomes,
and identifies a rainforest from climate data. This guide goes further: a step-by-step method for
reading climate graphs, the limiting factors and adaptations that define each biome, aquatic
biomes, how altitude creates biome zones on mountains, and how to explain and calculate biome
shifts under climate change.

## What 2.4 asks you to do

- Distinguish weather from climate and define a biome.
- Explain how temperature, precipitation and insolation determine the distribution of biomes.
- Interpret climate graphs and biome diagrams.
- Explain how organisms in each biome are adapted to its limiting factors.
- Explain how climate change shifts biomes and affects their productivity.

## Reading a climate graph: a method

A climate graph shows mean monthly temperature (line) and mean monthly precipitation (bars).

1. **Total precipitation:** add the twelve monthly values.
2. **Seasonality of rain:** find the driest month and look for a dry season.
3. **Mean temperature:** average of the twelve monthly values.
4. **Annual range:** warmest month minus coldest month.
5. **Growing season:** count months warm enough for plant growth (often taken as above about 5 °C)
   with enough water.
6. **Hemisphere:** a warm July suggests the northern hemisphere; a warm January the southern.
7. **Match to a biome,** then explain using limiting factors.

### Worked example 1: two stations (fictional data)

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Station L temp (°C) | −28 | −27 | −24 | −15 | −4 | 4 | 8 | 6 | 0 | −10 | −20 | −25 |
| Station L precip (mm) | 8 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 12 | 22 | 30 | 28 | 20 | 14 | 10 | 8 |
| Station M temp (°C) | 15 | 17 | 21 | 25 | 30 | 33 | 34 | 33 | 31 | 26 | 20 | 16 |
| Station M precip (mm) | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |

```
Station L: total precip = 176 mm; range = 8 − (−28) = 36 °C;
           months above 5 °C = 2 (July, August)
Station M: total precip = 16 mm;  range = 34 − 15 = 19 °C;
           mean temp = 301 / 12 = 25.1 °C (3 s.f.)
```

Station L has a very short growing season, long severe winters and low precipitation: **tundra**.
Station M has almost no rain and high temperatures: **hot desert**. Both have low NPP, but for
different reasons: temperature and light limit L, water limits M.

## Biome diagrams

A biome diagram (often called a Whittaker diagram) plots mean annual temperature against annual
precipitation. Each biome occupies a region. Use it to explain that the same rainfall can support
forest in a cool climate but only grassland in a hot one, because higher temperatures increase
evaporation and so reduce the water actually available to plants.

## Limiting factors and adaptations by biome

| Biome | Main limiting factor | Plant adaptations | Animal adaptations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | Light at ground level; nutrients in poor soils | Tall trees and emergents; drip-tip leaves; buttress roots; epiphytes and climbers reaching light | Many arboreal species; specialised diets in a crowded community |
| Hot desert | Water; extreme heat | Deep or wide roots; water storage in succulent tissue; small or spiny leaves; short life cycles after rain | Nocturnal activity; burrowing; concentrated urine |
| Tundra | Low temperature; short growing season; permafrost | Low, cushion-shaped growth; shallow roots above permafrost; rapid flowering in summer | Thick fur or feathers; fat layers; migration or hibernation |
| Boreal forest | Cold, short summers; acidic, nutrient-poor soils | Needle leaves reducing water loss; evergreen to photosynthesise as soon as it warms; conical shape shedding snow | Seasonal coat changes; hibernation; food storing |
| Temperate deciduous forest | Winter cold and low light | Leaves shed in autumn to avoid frost damage and water loss when soil is frozen | Hibernation or migration; food caching |
| Tropical grassland | Seasonal drought; fire | Deep roots; fire-resistant bark; grasses regrowing from the base | Migration following rains; grazing herds |
| Temperate grassland | Summer drought; cold winters | Deep, dense roots; growth from the base, tolerating grazing | Burrowing; herd behaviour |

Adaptation answers earn marks only when the feature is linked to the factor: "needle leaves have a
small surface area, so they lose less water when the soil is frozen".

## Productivity and biodiversity across biomes

NPP is highest where light, warmth and water are all plentiful for most of the year (tropical
rainforest) and lowest where one factor is severely limiting (desert, tundra). Biodiversity follows a
similar pattern. Explanations include high productivity supporting more trophic levels, a stable
climate allowing specialisation, and many layers creating more niches.

## Aquatic biomes

In water, light and nutrients become the key limiting factors:

- **Open ocean:** light only in the surface layer; nutrients often scarce, so productivity per area is
  low, but the huge area makes total productivity large.
- **Upwelling zones:** cold, nutrient-rich water rises to the surface, supporting very productive
  fisheries.
- **Coral reefs:** shallow, warm, clear water; very high biodiversity despite low nutrient levels in
  the surrounding water, because nutrients are recycled tightly within the reef.
- **Freshwater lakes and rivers:** temperature, oxygen, flow and nutrients vary widely.

## Altitude: biomes on a mountain

Temperature falls with height. A typical average rate, the environmental lapse rate, is about
6.5 °C per 1,000 m. Climbing a tropical mountain can therefore pass through zones resembling
rainforest, temperate forest, boreal forest and tundra, compressed into a few kilometres.

### Worked example 2: a rising tree line (assumed values)

A tree line sits at 2,100 m. Local mean temperature rises by 1.3 °C. Assuming a lapse rate of
6.5 °C per 1,000 m and that the tree line follows temperature:

```
Height change = 1.3 / 6.5 × 1000 = 200 m
New tree line ≈ 2100 + 200 = 2300 m
```

Alpine species above the tree line lose area as the forest moves up, and those near the summit have
nowhere higher to go. In reality the tree line lags behind warming, because trees grow and disperse
slowly and soils take time to develop.

## Climate change and biomes: further points

- Biomes shift **poleward and upslope**, but species move at different speeds, so communities may
  reassemble rather than move as a unit.
- **Fire regimes** change: hotter, drier summers increase fire frequency in boreal forest and
  savanna.
- **Productivity** may rise where warmth was limiting (parts of the tundra) and fall where drought
  increases (parts of the tropics).
- **Coral reefs** face both warming (bleaching) and ocean acidification.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a climate graph without quoting values.
- Confusing weather with climate.
- Listing adaptations without linking each to a limiting factor.
- Assuming desert means hot: deserts are defined by low precipitation.
- Saying biomes "move" as a whole community.

## Exam technique

"Describe" a climate graph: totals, range, seasonality, with numbers. "Explain" a biome's
distribution: link temperature, precipitation and insolation to the tricellular model. "Suggest" an
adaptation: name the feature and the factor it overcomes.
