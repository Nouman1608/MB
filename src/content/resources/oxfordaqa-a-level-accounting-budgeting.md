## The need for budgeting

A **budget** sets out, in money or in units, what a business intends to happen over a future period. **Budgetary control** is
the process of comparing actual results with the budget, finding the differences and acting on them.

The purpose of budgeting is to help managers run the business deliberately rather than react to events:

- **Planning.** Managers must think ahead about sales, resources and finance.
- **Co-ordination.** Each department's budget must fit the others. Production cannot exceed what
  purchasing can supply or what sales can sell.
- **Communication.** Staff learn what is expected of them.
- **Control.** The budget is a yardstick against which actual results are measured.
- **Motivation.** A realistic target gives managers something to aim for.
- **Responsibility.** Each budget has a named holder who answers for its differences.

## Benefits and limitations

### Generic benefits and limitations

| Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Problems are spotted before they happen | A budget is only as good as its forecasts |
| Departments work towards the same goals | Preparing budgets takes time and money |
| Spending is authorised in advance | Targets that are too hard demotivate; too easy and staff coast |
| Performance can be measured against a standard | Managers may pad budgets with slack to make targets easy |
| Managers are accountable for their areas | A rigid budget can stop managers reacting to new opportunities |

### Benefits and limitations of specific budgets

- **Sales budget.** Usually prepared first, because demand normally limits everything else. Its weakness
  is that it is the hardest forecast to get right, and every other budget inherits its errors.
- **Production budget.** Plans output so finished-goods inventory stays at the target level and the
  factory is neither idle nor overstretched. Limited if machine breakdowns or staff shortages occur.
- **Purchases budget.** Avoids running out of materials and avoids tying cash up in excess inventory.
  Limited by supplier price changes and delivery delays.
- **Labour budget.** Shows when to recruit, train or plan overtime. Limited by staff turnover and
  absence, and by how accurately the time per unit is estimated.
- **Cash budget.** Shows when an overdraft or loan will be needed, so finance can be arranged early,
  and when surplus cash could be invested. Limited because customers may pay late.
- **Budgeted income statement and statement of financial position.** Reveal whether the combined plans
  give an acceptable profit and a healthy financial position. Limited because they rest on every
  other forecast.

### Incremental and zero-based budgeting

With **incremental budgeting**, the starting point is the previous period's figure, which is then
adjusted, for example by an allowance for inflation. It is quick and cheap and suits stable costs. But it carries past
waste forward and gives managers no reason to question spending.

**Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)** starts every budget from nil. Managers must defend every item of spending
before it is included. This strips out waste and moves money to current priorities. But it is slow, needs skilled
managers, and can favour spending whose benefits are easy to measure.

## Budgeted financial statements (International AS)

A budgeted income statement and statement of financial position use the same layouts as the real
statements. The skill is working out each figure from the plan.

### Worked example 1: Ravensmere Kites

Odette Brannigan owns Ravensmere Kites. Her statement of financial position at 1 January 2027 showed:
equipment at cost 40,000, accumulated depreciation 12,000; inventory 6,000; trade receivables 4,500;
bank 2,300; trade payables 3,800; capital 37,000.

Budget for the year ending 31 December 2027:

- Revenue 96,000, all on credit; closing trade receivables 8,000.
- Purchases 52,000, all on credit; closing trade payables 4,300.
- Closing inventory 7,500.
- General expenses paid 21,400; a further 600 will be owing at the year end.
- Depreciation 10% a year on cost (straight line). Drawings 15,000. No new non-current assets.

**Step 1: budgeted income statement**

```
Ravensmere Kites
Budgeted income statement for the year ending 31 December 2027
Revenue                                   96,000
Cost of sales: Opening inventory   6,000
               Purchases          52,000
                                  58,000
               Closing inventory  (7,500)  (50,500)
Gross profit                              45,500
Expenses: General expenses        22,000
          Depreciation             4,000   (26,000)
Profit for the year                       19,500
```

General expenses are 21,400 + 600 = 22,000 (accruals). Depreciation is 10% x 40,000 = 4,000.

**Step 2: budgeted bank balance**

- Receipts from customers: 4,500 + 96,000 - 8,000 = 92,500.
- Payments to suppliers: 3,800 + 52,000 - 4,300 = 51,500.
- Closing bank: 2,300 + 92,500 - 51,500 - 21,400 - 15,000 = **6,900**.

**Step 3: budgeted statement of financial position**

```
Ravensmere Kites
Budgeted statement of financial position as at 31 December 2027
Non-current assets: Equipment (40,000 - 16,000)         24,000
Current assets: Inventory            7,500
                Trade receivables    8,000
                Bank                 6,900
                                    22,400
Current liabilities: Trade payables  4,300
                     Other payables    600
                                    (4,900)
Net current assets                              17,500
Net assets                                      41,500
Capital: Opening balance                        37,000
         Add profit for the year                19,500
         Less drawings                         (15,000)
                                                41,500
```

The budget shows a profit of 19,500 but cash rises by only 4,600, because receivables and inventory
grow and drawings take 15,000 out.

## Functional budgets (Assessed at A-level)

The order matters: sales, then production, then purchases and labour, then cash.

**Production (units)** = budgeted sales + closing inventory of finished goods - opening inventory of finished goods

**Purchases (units)** = materials used in production + closing materials inventory - opening materials inventory

### Worked example 2: Quillfield Lanterns

Quillfield Lanterns plans sales of 1,200 lanterns in January 2027, 1,500 in February, 1,400 in March
and 1,600 in April, at 45 each. Closing finished-goods inventory is 20% of next month's sales; opening
inventory on 1 January is 250. Each lantern uses 0.5 kg of tin sheet at 8 per kg; closing materials
inventory is 25% of next month's usage; 150 kg is held on 1 January. Each lantern takes 0.8 labour
hours. Up to 1,100 hours a month are paid at 15 an hour; extra hours are overtime at time-and-a-half.

| Sales budget | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Units | 1,200 | 1,500 | 1,400 |
| Revenue (x 45) | 54,000 | 67,500 | 63,000 |

| Production budget (units) | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 1,200 | 1,500 | 1,400 |
| Add closing inventory | 300 | 280 | 320 |
| Less opening inventory | (250) | (300) | (280) |
| Production | 1,250 | 1,480 | 1,440 |

| Purchases budget | January | February |
|---|---|---|
| Usage (kg) | 625 | 740 |
| Add closing inventory (kg) | 185 | 180 |
| Less opening inventory (kg) | (150) | (185) |
| Purchases (kg) | 660 | 735 |
| Purchases (x 8) | 5,280 | 5,880 |

| Labour budget | January | February | March |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours (production x 0.8) | 1,000 | 1,184 | 1,152 |
| Basic hours at 15 | 15,000 | 16,500 | 16,500 |
| Overtime hours at 22.50 | 0 | 84 hours: 1,890 | 52 hours: 1,170 |
| Labour cost | 15,000 | 18,390 | 17,670 |

February's closing inventory is 20% x 1,400 = 280. Carry each closing inventory figure forward: it is
the opening figure for the following month.

## The cash budget (Assessed at A-level)

A cash budget lists receipts and payments in the month the cash actually moves. Non-cash items, such
as depreciation and irrecoverable debts, never appear. Capital items, such as an asset purchase or a
loan received, do appear.

## Budgets in planning and control

Planning happens before the period: the budget sets targets and resources. Control happens during and
after it:

1. Record actual results.
2. Compare each line with the budget. A difference that raises profit is **favourable**; one that
   reduces profit is **adverse**.
3. Investigate the causes of significant differences.
4. Act: change methods, retrain staff, renegotiate prices, or revise the budget if conditions changed.

In January Quillfield's actual revenue was 52,650 against a budget of 54,000: 1,350 adverse. Actual
labour cost was 15,840 against 15,000: 840 adverse. The production manager answers for labour and the
sales manager for revenue. Splitting differences into detailed variances is section 3.2.1, Standard
costing and variance analysis, which is International A-level only.

## Common errors to avoid

- Putting depreciation in a cash budget, or leaving it out of a budgeted income statement.
- Using sales instead of production to calculate materials usage and labour hours.
- Forgetting that closing inventory becomes next month's opening inventory.
- Entering a credit sale in the month of sale in the cash budget.
- Using the cash paid for an expense in the income statement when there is an accrual or prepayment.
- Leaving drawings out of the bank balance, or treating them as an expense.
- Losing the bracket on an overdrawn balance carried into the next month.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a budget?**
A budget sets out, in money or in units, what a business intends to happen over a future period. Budgetary control compares actual results with the budget so that differences can be investigated.

**Why is the sales budget usually prepared first?**
Demand normally limits everything else the business can do, so the other budgets are built from it. Its weakness is that it is the hardest forecast to get right, and every other budget inherits its errors.

**How do you calculate the purchases budget for materials?**
Purchases (units) = materials used in production + closing materials inventory − opening materials inventory. Materials usage must be based on production units, not sales units.

**When is a budget variance favourable or adverse?**
A difference that raises profit compared with the budget is favourable, and one that reduces profit is adverse. The causes of significant differences are then investigated and acted on.

**Why can a budget show a profit but only a small rise in cash?**
Profit includes credit sales and expenses when earned or incurred, while cash only moves when money is received or paid. Growing receivables and inventory, and drawings, all use cash without reducing profit.
