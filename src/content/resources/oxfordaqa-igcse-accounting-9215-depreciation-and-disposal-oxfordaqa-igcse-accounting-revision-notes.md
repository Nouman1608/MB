These notes cover the section 3.3 statement "Accounting for depreciation and disposal of non-current assets". For the full ledger examples read the [depreciation and disposal study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-depreciation-and-disposal-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting/), then try the [depreciation and disposal practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-depreciation-and-disposal-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/). Figures are invented and in dollars.

## The essentials

- Depreciation spreads cost less residual value over the useful life (accruals concept).
- Causes: **wear and tear**, **obsolescence**, **passage of time**.
- It is a **non-cash expense**: it lowers profit but moves no money. The cash left when the asset was bought.
- **Net book value (NBV)** = cost − provision for depreciation. It is not market value (historic cost concept).
- Use the same method every year (consistency concept).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Residual value | Amount expected when the asset is no longer needed |
| Useful life | Number of years the business expects to use the asset |
| Provision for depreciation | Credit balance account holding total depreciation to date on a group of assets |
| Net book value | Cost less provision for depreciation; also called carrying amount |
| Disposal account | Account that removes a sold asset's cost and provision and finds the profit or loss |
| Profit on disposal | Proceeds greater than NBV; other income in the income statement |
| Loss on disposal | Proceeds less than NBV; an expense in the income statement |

## The two methods

| | Straight line | Reducing balance |
|---|---|---|
| Calculation | (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life | Rate × NBV at start of year |
| Pattern | Same charge every year | Highest in Year 1, then falls |
| Residual value | Deducted first | Not deducted; the rate allows for it |
| Suits | Assets used evenly: fittings, furniture | Assets that lose value fast or need more repairs later: vehicles, computers |

Shortcut: NBV after n years on reducing balance = cost × (1 − rate)ⁿ. For example, 40,000 at 20% for 2 years: 40,000 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 25,600.

## Part-year policies

- **Time basis**: months owned ÷ 12 × annual charge, in the year of purchase and the year of sale.
- **Full year in year of purchase, none in year of sale**: charge on assets held at the year end only.

Read the policy before any calculation. Most lost marks in disposal questions start here.

## Recording depreciation each year

1. Debit depreciation (or income statement), credit provision for depreciation.
2. The asset account stays at cost.
3. Statement of financial position: cost, less provision, equals NBV.

## Recording a disposal: four entries

| Step | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Remove cost | Disposal | Asset |
| 2 Remove that asset's depreciation | Provision for depreciation | Disposal |
| 3 Record proceeds | Bank (or cash, or receivable) | Disposal |
| 4a Loss | Income statement | Disposal |
| 4b Profit | Disposal | Income statement |

Check: profit or loss = proceeds − NBV of the asset sold.

On a time basis, post the depreciation for the months owned in the year of sale **before** step 2, so the provision transferred is up to date.

## Mini example

Cost 15,000 bought 1 January Year 1, straight line 10% of cost, time basis. Sold 30 September Year 3 for 10,500.

- Years 1 and 2: 2 × 1,500 = 3,000. Year 3, nine months: 1,500 × 9/12 = 1,125.
- Provision transferred: 4,125. NBV: 15,000 − 4,125 = 10,875.
- Loss on disposal: 10,875 − 10,500 = 375.

## Effects of errors

| Error | Profit | NBV |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation omitted | Overstated | Overstated |
| Reducing balance rate applied to cost after Year 1 | Understated | Understated |
| Whole provision moved to disposal account | Profit on disposal overstated (or loss understated) | Remaining assets' NBV overstated |
| Proceeds credited to sales | Revenue overstated; disposal result missing | Sold asset still shown |

## Exam traps

- Taking only the sold asset's provision, not the group's whole balance.
- Using opening cost when the policy says "assets held at the year end".
- Deducting residual value before applying a reducing balance rate.
- Putting the profit on disposal on the credit side of the disposal account. It is a debit, because it balances the account.
- Saying depreciation provides cash for replacement.
- Calling NBV the asset's market value.

## Self-test

1. Name three causes of depreciation.
2. Why is depreciation called a non-cash expense?
3. A machine cost 24,000, residual value 4,000, life 8 years. Calculate the straight line charge.
4. A car cost 20,000, reducing balance 30%. Calculate the Year 2 charge.
5. Using question 4, calculate NBV at the end of Year 3.
6. Give one reason a business might choose reducing balance for vans.
7. Which concept means NBV is not market value?
8. State the double entry to remove the cost of a sold asset.
9. An asset had cost 6,000 and provision 4,200. It sold for 2,100. Calculate the profit or loss.
10. On which side of the disposal account does a profit on disposal appear?
11. Equipment at cost 30,000 includes an item costing 5,000 sold during the year. The policy is 10% of cost on assets held at the year end, none in the year of sale. Calculate the year's charge if no assets were bought.
12. What is the effect on profit of omitting a year's depreciation?

**Answers**

1. Wear and tear, obsolescence, passage of time.
2. It reduces profit, but no money is paid when it is charged; the cash was paid when the asset was bought.
3. (24,000 − 4,000) ÷ 8 = 2,500.
4. Year 1: 6,000, NBV 14,000. Year 2: 30% of 14,000 = 4,200.
5. 20,000 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 6,860.
6. Vans lose most value early and need more repairs later, so falling charges give a more even yearly cost.
7. Historic cost.
8. Debit disposal, credit the asset account.
9. NBV 1,800; profit on disposal 2,100 − 1,800 = 300.
10. The debit side.
11. 10% of (30,000 − 5,000) = 2,500.
12. Profit is overstated by that year's charge, and NBV is overstated by the same amount.
