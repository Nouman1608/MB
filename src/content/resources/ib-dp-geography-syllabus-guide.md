This guide covers the full syllabus structure of **IB Diploma Programme
Geography**, first assessment 2019, for both Standard Level (SL) and
Higher Level (HL) students. It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-geography-subject-guide/) and the
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by listing what is actually taught -- the optional
geographic themes, the shared core, and the HL-only extension.

All students study a common core plus a set of optional geographic
themes; HL students additionally study an HL extension not seen by SL
students at all. Total teaching hours are 150 at SL and 240 at HL.

## Geographic themes -- seven options (60 hours SL / 90 hours HL)

SL students choose **two** options; HL students choose **three**. The
seven available options are:

- **Freshwater** -- the distribution, use and management of freshwater
  resources.
- **Oceans and coastal margins** -- the physical processes and human
  pressures shaping coastlines and ocean systems.
- **Extreme environments** -- how people live in, and adapt to, hostile
  physical environments.
- **Geophysical hazards** -- the causes, impacts and management of
  hazards such as earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.
- **Leisure, tourism and sport** -- the geography of how and where
  people spend leisure time, and its economic and environmental impact.
- **Food and health** -- the geography of food production, security and
  its links to health outcomes.
- **Urban environments** -- the growth, structure and challenges of
  cities.

Because your teacher selects which two (SL) or three (HL) of these seven
your class actually studies, always confirm with your teacher which
specific options apply to you -- revision material for an option your
class did not study is wasted effort. Each option combines physical and
human geography around one theme, so revising an option well means being
able to move between the physical processes at work (for example, wave
action in Oceans and coastal margins) and their human and management
consequences, rather than treating either half in isolation.

## SL and HL core -- Geographic perspectives: global change (70 hours, both SL and HL)

- **Population distribution** -- changing population, examining why
  people are distributed unevenly and how that distribution changes
  over time.
- **Global climate** -- vulnerability and resilience, examining how
  different places and communities are affected by, and adapt to,
  climate change.
- **Global resource consumption and security** -- examining how
  resource demand and supply are distributed globally, and the
  tensions this creates.

This core is identical in content and hours for SL and HL students, and
is examined in Paper 2.

## HL extension only -- Geographic perspectives: global interactions (60 hours, HL only)

- **Power, places and networks** -- how power is exercised and
  contested across geographic scales and networks.
- **Human development and diversity** -- how development is measured
  and how it varies across and within societies.
- **Global risks and resilience** -- how interconnected global systems
  create shared risks, and how societies build resilience to them.

This entire component does not exist for SL students. It is examined
separately in Paper 3, which SL students do not sit.

## Internal assessment -- fieldwork (20 hours, both SL and HL)

A single written report based on a fieldwork question of the student's
choosing, drawing on information collection and analysis with
evaluation. The report can be based on any suitable syllabus topic --
option, core or (at HL) extension. Because the fieldwork question is
student-designed rather than set by the IB, it rewards early planning:
students who settle on a genuinely testable geographic question and a
realistic data-collection method well before the fieldwork trip itself
tend to produce stronger reports than those who collect data first and
try to fit a question to it afterwards.

## How to approach it

Because Paper 1 examines the two (SL) or three (HL) chosen options and
Paper 2 examines the shared core, make sure you know which of these two
papers is actually testing which content before you start revising --
confusing an option topic with the core global-change content is a
common and avoidable error, especially since some themes (such as
climate-related content) can appear to overlap between an option like
Extreme environments and the core's Global climate content. HL students
should treat the HL extension as functionally a fourth, separate subject
within geography: it has its own paper (Paper 3), its own three
sub-themes, and 60 hours of dedicated teaching time that SL students
never encounter, so it cannot be revised as an afterthought to the core
and options.

Across every option and core theme, DP Geography questions consistently
reward students who can support an argument with named, specific
real-world case studies rather than generalised description -- a strong
answer on Geophysical hazards, for instance, names an actual earthquake
or eruption with real dates, magnitudes and management responses, not a
generic account of "an earthquake in a developing country". Building a
personal case-study bank as you work through each option and the core --
one or two well-researched, specific examples per theme -- is one of the
highest-value uses of revision time in this subject, because the same
case study can often be reused flexibly across Paper 1 option questions
and Paper 2 core questions if it is chosen to illustrate more than one
theme. For the fieldwork report specifically, revisit your data
collection methodology critically before submission: evaluation of your
own method's limitations is assessed as its own skill, separate from
simply presenting your results.
