## Section A

**1.** Define environmental justice. **[1]**

**2.** State what values of 0 and 1 mean on the index of dissimilarity. **[2]**

**3.** Outline two features of crime prevention through environmental design. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between in-situ upgrading and sites-and-services schemes. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why heat island measurements should be taken on calm, clear nights. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by compound stress in an urban district. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows two groups in an invented city with three districts.

| District | Group A | Group B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6,000 | 1,000 |
| 2 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| 3 | 1,000 | 6,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the index of dissimilarity. **[3]**

**(b)** Interpret your answer. **[1]**

**(c)** Outline one limitation of the index. **[1]**

**8.** A 15 km commute takes 20 minutes on empty roads and 52 minutes in the evening peak.

**(a)** Calculate the travel time index. **[1]**

**(b)** After a bus lane opens, the peak bus journey takes 34 minutes. Calculate the new index for bus users. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one reason why car drivers might oppose the bus lane. **[2]**

**9.** Readings taken at the same time give: rural site 9.5 degrees C; suburban street 11.0 degrees C; large urban park 10.5 degrees C; city centre 13.5 degrees C.

**(a)** Calculate the heat island intensity. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the park is cooler than the suburban street. **[2]**

**10.** An agency has 48 million currency units. Upgrading costs 4,000 per household; new apartments cost 24,000 per household. Calculate how many more households can be helped by upgrading, and outline one reason why upgrading may still be rejected for a particular settlement. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the responses to social stresses in one or more named cities. **[10]**

**12.** "Urban environmental stresses are fundamentally social problems." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The principle that no social group should bear a disproportionate share of environmental harm [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 0 means the two groups are distributed identically across districts [1]; 1 means they are completely separated, with no district containing both [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two, for example: natural surveillance through windows overlooking streets and good lighting [1]; clear boundaries between public and private space [1]; good maintenance [1]; mixed land use so streets are busy at different times [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**4.** In-situ upgrading improves services, paths and tenure in an existing settlement without moving residents [1]; sites-and-services schemes provide new serviced plots on which households build their own homes, often on a different site [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Wind mixes urban and rural air, and cloud reduces night-time cooling in the countryside [1], so both reduce the temperature difference; calm, clear nights show the full effect of urban surfaces releasing stored heat [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The same households or districts face several stresses at once, such as pollution, heat, flood risk and poor services [1], so the stresses reinforce each other and the total harm is greater than any single stress suggests [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** A = 10,000; B = 10,000 [1]. Differences: |0.6 - 0.1| = 0.5; |0.3 - 0.3| = 0; |0.1 - 0.6| = 0.5; sum = 1.0 [1]. D = 0.5 x 1.0 = **0.50** [1].

**(b)** Half of either group would need to move to another district for the two groups to be evenly distributed, a moderately high level of segregation [1].

**(c)** For example, the value depends on where district boundaries are drawn, or it ignores whether segregated districts are next to each other [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** 52 / 20 = **2.6** [1].

**(b)** 34 / 20 = **1.7** [1].

**(c)** The bus lane removes road space from cars [1], so remaining car lanes may become more congested and car journeys slower [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 13.5 - 9.5 = **4.0 degrees C** [1].

**(b)** Vegetation cools the air through evapotranspiration [1], and grass and trees store and release less heat than concrete, asphalt and brick [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Upgrading: 48,000,000 / 4,000 = 12,000 households [1]. Apartments: 48,000,000 / 24,000 = 2,000 households [1]. Upgrading helps 12,000 - 2,000 = **10,000 more households** [1]. Reason for rejection: for example, the settlement is on an active floodplain or unstable slope, so upgrading would leave residents exposed to hazards [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Criteria:** who benefits, whether causes or symptoms are tackled, whether the response lasts [1].
- **Informal settlement upgrading:** Indonesia's Kampung Improvement Programme in Jakarta improved services in existing neighbourhoods at low cost [1]; depends on maintenance and may not address hazard exposure [1].
- **Clearance:** frees land but destroys homes, livelihoods and networks, and residents often rebuild elsewhere [1].
- **Deprivation targeting:** using multiple deprivation indices to direct spending at the neediest areas [1].
- **Design and crime:** defensible-space and design measures reduce opportunity [1], but do not tackle unemployment or inequality [1].
- **Segregation:** gated communities may reduce crime inside but increase segregation [1].
- **Comparison:** responses combining physical improvement with jobs and services are more lasting [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion about which responses worked, where and for whom [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Social causes:** pollution exposure follows income, because poorer households live near roads and industry [1]; informal settlements occupy hazardous land because of poverty and lack of affordable housing [1].
- **Social consequences:** older and isolated people suffer most in heatwaves, as in France in August 2003 [1]; compound stress concentrates in deprived districts [1].
- **Social solutions:** upgrading, affordable housing and public transport tackle root causes [1].
- **Physical causes:** heat islands, inversions and heavy rainfall are physical processes that affect whole cities [1]; site and climate, such as Mexico City's basin, shape stress regardless of social structure [1].
- **Technical solutions:** cleaner fuels and drainage address stresses directly [1].
- **Interaction:** physical processes create the hazard, social conditions decide who is harmed [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, agree largely: the physical processes are real, but their impacts and the ability to respond are shaped by social inequality [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "discuss" requires both sides; using named cities and showing which groups are affected lifts answers to the top bands.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to halve the sum when calculating the index of dissimilarity.
- Calculating heat island intensity from the coolest urban site rather than the rural one.
- Listing responses without saying who benefits.
- Treating environmental stresses as affecting everyone equally.
