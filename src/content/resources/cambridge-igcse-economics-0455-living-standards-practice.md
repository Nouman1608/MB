These questions cover section 5.1 (living standards) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [living standards study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-living-standards/) and the [living standards revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-living-standards-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which of the following is a component of the Human Development Index? **[1]**

A: The rate of inflation

B: Life expectancy at birth

C: The unemployment rate

D: The share of income received by the richest 10%

**2.** A country's nominal GDP rises by 8% in a year in which prices rise by 8% and population is unchanged. What happens to real GDP per head? **[1]**

A: It rises by 16%

B: It rises by 8%

C: It stays about the same

D: It falls by 8%

**3.** Define real GDP per head. **[2]**

**4.** Country F has nominal GDP of 1,260 billion, a price index of 105 and a population of 60 million. Calculate real GDP per head. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why subsistence farming causes real GDP per head to understate living standards. **[2]**

**6.** In Country G, 25% of households live in the capital city with average income of 36,000. The rest live in rural areas with average income of 8,000. Calculate the average income for the whole country. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Country H has the following data (invented).

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | 300 billion | 315 billion |
| Population | 25 million | 26 million |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in real GDP. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate real GDP per head in each year. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain whether average living standards rose in Country H. **[2]**

**8.** Country J ranks 40th in the world on real GDP per head but 75th on the HDI.

**(a)** Explain two possible reasons for the difference in ranks. **[4]**

**(b)** State one piece of extra information that would help judge living standards in Country J. **[1]**

**9.** Explain two disadvantages of using real GDP per head to compare living standards between two countries. **[4]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why living standards may differ between regions of the same country. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse how an increase in government spending on healthcare could raise a country's HDI. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether the HDI is a better indicator of living standards than real GDP per head. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Inflation, unemployment and income shares are not components. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. The rise in nominal GDP is entirely due to higher prices, so real output and real output per head are unchanged. **[1]**

**3.** The total output of a country, adjusted for inflation [1], divided by the size of the population [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Real GDP = 1,260 ÷ 105 × 100 = 1,200 billion [1]; real GDP per head = 1,200 billion ÷ 60 million = **20,000** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Food grown for the family's own use is not sold, so it is not recorded in GDP [1]. Families consume more than the GDP figure shows, so measured income per head is lower than the true level of consumption [1]. **[2]**

**6.** (0.25 × 36,000) + (0.75 × 8,000) [1] = 9,000 + 6,000 = **15,000** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** (315 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **5%** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 300 billion ÷ 25 million = **12,000** [1]; Year 2: 315 billion ÷ 26 million = **12,115** (to the nearest whole number) [1].

**(c)** Real GDP per head rose by only about 1% (12,115 ÷ 12,000 = 1.0096) [1], so average material living standards rose slightly, but this tells us nothing about health, education or how the extra income was shared [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Any two developed reasons, 2 marks each:

**Reason 1:** Income may be concentrated among a small group [1], so most people do not benefit through better health or schooling, keeping life expectancy and schooling low [1].

**Reason 2:** The government may spend little on health and education, for example using revenue on defence [1], so high income is not turned into longer lives or more years of schooling [1].

Other creditable reasons: high death rates from disease or conflict; income from one export earned by foreign firms.

**(b)** Any one: the distribution of income; access to clean water; levels of pollution; average hours worked [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Each reason must link the gap in ranks to the HDI components. "Country J is unequal" alone scores one mark at most.

**9.** Disadvantage 1: figures must be converted into one currency using exchange rates [1], which do not reflect differences in what money buys in each country, so one country's living standards may be overstated or understated [1]. Disadvantage 2: the share of non-market output may differ [1]; a country with much subsistence farming or unpaid work will appear poorer than it is compared with a country where most output is sold [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Reason 1: cities usually have more firms and better-paid jobs [1], so incomes and spending power are higher there than in rural areas [1]. Reason 2: access to public services may differ [1]; rural households may lack clinics or schools, so health and education are worse even at similar incomes [1]. Other creditable reasons: wealth ownership, discrimination, skills. **[4]**

**11.** More spending pays for clinics, doctors, vaccinations or clean water [1]. This reduces deaths from preventable illness, especially of children [1], so life expectancy at birth rises [1]. As life expectancy is one of the three HDI components, the HDI rises; healthier workers may also be more productive, raising income per head [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For the HDI: it includes life expectancy and schooling as well as income [1], which people value for their own sake and which show whether income reaches people [1]; a country with high income from exports owned by few can rank lower on the HDI, revealing a problem GDP per head hides [1]. For real GDP per head: data are published more often, so it is better for tracking short-term change [1]; it is simpler and less dependent on judgement about combining different components [1]. Shared limits: both are averages that hide inequality [1]; neither includes freedom, pollution or hours worked [1]. Judgement: the HDI is the better indicator of overall well-being, but a sound comparison uses both together with data on income distribution [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* The final mark needs a judgement that says what the answer depends on, such as whether the aim is to measure well-being or short-term income change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the population before removing inflation, then forgetting one of the steps.
- Mixing billions and millions when dividing real GDP by population.
- Saying the HDI measures happiness.
- Listing a disadvantage without saying how it makes a comparison misleading.
- Giving both sides of a "Discuss" question with no conclusion.
