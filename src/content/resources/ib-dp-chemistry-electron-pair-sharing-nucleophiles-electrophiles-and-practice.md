## Questions

**1.** Explain what an electrophile is, and name one electrophile that carries no charge. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the nucleophile in each reaction and state whether it is neutral or negatively charged.

**(a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl + CH₃O⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂OCH₃ + Cl⁻ **[1]**
**(b)** (CH₃)₂CHI + H₂O → (CH₃)₂CHOH + HI **[1]**
**(c)** CH₃CH₂Br + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ + HBr **[1]**

**3.** Classify each species as a nucleophile or an electrophile: Cl⁻, NO₂⁺, NH₃, (CH₃)₃C⁺. **[4]**

**4.** This question is about heterolytic fission.

**(a)** Define heterolytic fission. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the heterolytic fission of the C–Cl bond in 2-chloro-2-methylbutane, CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂Cl, and describe where the curly arrow starts and ends. **[2]**
**(c)** State how the products would differ if the same bond broke homolytically. **[1]**

**5.** 1-Chlorohexane, CH₃(CH₂)₅Cl, undergoes nucleophilic substitution.

**(a)** Write an equation for its reaction with hydroxide ions. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for its reaction with cyanide ions and name the organic product. **[2]**
**(c)** Write an equation for its reaction with excess ammonia. **[2]**

**6.** 2-Iodobutane reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions to form butan-2-ol. Describe and explain the movement of electron pairs in this reaction, and write the equation. **[4]**

**7.** Cycloheptene, C₇H₁₂, is a symmetrical cyclic alkene. Deduce equations, using molecular formulas, for its reaction with:

**(a)** chlorine **[1]**
**(b)** hydrogen bromide **[1]**
**(c)** water, stating the conditions used. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why alkenes are attacked by electrophiles, and why the reactions are classed as additions. **[3]**

**9.** A student has two colourless liquids, hexane and hex-1-ene. She shakes a sample of each with bromine water in a dark cupboard.

**(a)** Describe and explain what she sees with hex-1-ene. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe and explain what she sees with hexane. **[2]**

**10.** Butan-2-ol can be made from but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃, by two routes.

**(a)** Route 1 is a single step. Write the equation and state the conditions. **[2]**
**(b)** Route 2 has two steps: but-2-ene reacts with hydrogen bromide, and the product then reacts with hydroxide ions. Write an equation for each step. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the type of reaction in each step of route 2. **[2]**
**(d)** Identify the electrophile in step 1 and the nucleophile in step 2 of route 2. **[2]**

**11.** A sample of 2.52 g of 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=C(CH₃)₂, reacts with excess hydrogen bromide. The mass of 2-bromo-2,3-dimethylbutane collected is 3.29 g.

**(a)** Explain why only one organic product forms. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[4]**
**(d)** State the role of hydrogen bromide in this reaction. **[1]**

**12.** A chemist heats 3.75 g of 2-chlorobutane, CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃, with excess cyanide ions and isolates 2.80 g of the organic product.

**(a)** Give an equation for this substitution. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the IUPAC name of the nitrile formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify the nucleophile and the leaving group. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A species that accepts an electron pair from its partner (the nucleophile) to form a new covalent bond [1]. Neutral example: Br₂, HBr, HCl or the δ+ carbon of a halogenoalkane [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "A positive ion" is not a definition; an answer has to mention accepting an electron pair to describe what an electrophile does.

**2. (a)** CH₃O⁻ (methoxide ion), negatively charged [1]
**(b)** H₂O, neutral [1]
**(c)** CH₃NH₂ (methylamine), neutral [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Look for the atom that ends up bonded to carbon with its own lone pair; naming the halogenoalkane as the nucleophile scores nothing.

**3.** Cl⁻: nucleophile [1]. NO₂⁺: electrophile [1]. NH₃: nucleophile [1]. (CH₃)₃C⁺: electrophile [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Charge is a guide, not the rule: decide by whether the species has a pair to give (nucleophile) or room to accept one (electrophile).

**4. (a)** A covalent bond breaks with both bonding electrons going to one fragment, forming two ions [1]
**(b)** CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂Cl → CH₃CH₂C⁺(CH₃)₂ + Cl⁻ [1]; the curly arrow begins on the C–Cl bond and its head points at chlorine [1]
**(c)** Homolytic fission would give two radicals (one electron each), not ions [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Arrows drawn from the carbon, or from the plus sign, claim electrons come from somewhere they do not, so they earn nothing.

**5. (a)** CH₃(CH₂)₅Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃(CH₂)₅OH + Cl⁻ [1]
**(b)** CH₃(CH₂)₅Cl + CN⁻ → CH₃(CH₂)₅CN + Cl⁻ [1]; heptanenitrile [1]
**(c)** CH₃(CH₂)₅Cl + 2NH₃ → CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂ + NH₄Cl: hexan-1-amine as the organic product [1]; 2NH₃ and NH₄Cl to balance [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The nitrile name counts the CN carbon, so six carbons plus one gives heptanenitrile; "hexanenitrile" loses the naming mark.

**6.** Iodine is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon bonded to iodine carries δ+ [1]. A lone pair on the oxygen of OH⁻ is donated to this δ+ carbon, forming a C–O bond [1]. The C–I bonding pair moves onto the iodine atom and the bond breaks; iodide is the leaving group [1]. CH₃CH₂CHICH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ + I⁻ [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe and explain" needs the reason (bond polarity), not just the arrows; an answer that only lists what moves gives no explanation.

**7. (a)** C₇H₁₂ + Cl₂ → C₇H₁₂Cl₂ [1]
**(b)** C₇H₁₂ + HBr → C₇H₁₃Br [1]
**(c)** C₇H₁₂ + H₂O → C₇H₁₃OH [1]; steam with an acid catalyst such as phosphoric acid, heated [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that each product formula equals alkene plus reagent; a missing hydrogen in the HBr or water product is the most common slip.

**8.** The C=C bond contains a pi bond whose electrons lie above and below the plane of the molecule, giving a region of high electron density [1]. This electron-rich region attracts electron-poor species and donates a pair to them, so electrophiles attack [1]. The pi bond breaks and the whole reagent bonds to the two carbons with nothing removed, so the product is a single saturated molecule: addition [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying "a double bond is strong" works against you; the point is high electron density, and the pi bond is the part that breaks.

**9. (a)** Bromine water turns from orange-brown to colourless [1]. The electron-rich C=C bond reacts with bromine by electrophilic addition, which needs no light, so the bromine is used up [1].
**(b)** No colour change; the mixture stays orange-brown [1]. Hexane contains only C–C and C–H bonds, strong and hardly polar; it can only react by radical substitution, which needs UV light to break the Br–Br bond homolytically [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write "colourless", not "clear": a clear liquid can still be coloured.

**10. (a)** CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ [1]; steam with an acid catalyst (for example phosphoric acid) [1]
**(b)** Step 1: CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + HBr → CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ [1]. Step 2: CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ + Br⁻ [1]
**(c)** Step 1: electrophilic addition [1]. Step 2: nucleophilic substitution [1]
**(d)** Step 1: HBr (its δ+ hydrogen) [1]. Step 2: the hydroxide ion, OH⁻ [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Both words of each reaction type are needed: "addition" alone or "substitution" alone does not say who attacks.

**11. (a)** The alkene is symmetrical: both carbons of the C=C carry the same groups, so H and Br can add only one way round [1]
**(b)** (CH₃)₂C=C(CH₃)₂ + HBr → (CH₃)₂CBrCH(CH₃)₂ [1]
**(c)** M(C₆H₁₂) = 84.18 g mol⁻¹, so n = 2.52 / 84.18 = 0.0299 mol [1]. M(C₆H₁₃Br) = 165.09 g mol⁻¹ [1]. Theoretical mass = 0.0299 × 165.09 = 4.94 g [1]. Percentage yield = (3.29 / 4.94) × 100 = **66.6%** [1]
**(d)** Electrophile [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the moles and the theoretical mass on separate lines, so your method is visible to the examiner even if the final percentage goes wrong.

**12. (a)** CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃ + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH(CN)CH₃ + Cl⁻ [1]
**(b)** 2-methylbutanenitrile [1]
**(c)** Nucleophile: the cyanide ion, CN⁻ [1]. Leaving group: the chloride ion, Cl⁻ [1]
**(d)** M(C₄H₉Cl) = 92.58 g mol⁻¹, so n = 3.75 / 92.58 = 0.0405 mol [1]. M(C₅H₉N) = 83.15 g mol⁻¹ [1]. Theoretical mass = 0.0405 × 83.15 = 3.37 g [1]. Percentage yield = (2.80 / 3.37) × 100 = **83.1%** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The nitrile has one more carbon than the chloroalkane, so its molar mass must be worked out from C₅H₉N; reusing the C₄ formula gives the wrong theoretical mass.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining nucleophiles and electrophiles by charge instead of by donating or accepting an electron pair.
- Naming the halogenoalkane as the nucleophile; it supplies the δ+ carbon and is the electrophile.
- Not recognising that the alkene's C=C bond acts as the nucleophile in electrophilic addition.
- Curly arrows that start at an atom or a positive charge instead of at a lone pair or a bond.
- Fish-hook arrows or radical products in a heterolytic fission answer.
- Missing HX or NH₄X when the nucleophile is neutral, leaving the equation unbalanced.
- Naming nitriles without counting the CN carbon.
- Calling halogen addition to an alkene a substitution, or stating the hydration conditions without a catalyst.
- Writing "clear" instead of "colourless" for decolourised bromine water.
- Rounding moles too early in yield questions; keep at least three significant figures through the working.
