These questions cover section 3.1.9, Electrode potentials and electrochemical cells (3.1.9.1 and 3.1.9.2), of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards. It is International A2 content, so every question is **International A-level only**. The specification places it in Unit 3, and Unit 5 can assess all content, including required practical 6. Use only the E⦵ data given.

## Questions

**1.** Define the standard electrode potential of a half-cell. **[3]**

**2.** A student writes these three electrode reactions as oxidations. Rewrite each one using the IUPAC convention.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cr²⁺ → Cr³⁺ + e⁻
2SO₄²⁻ → S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ **[3]**

**3.** Data: Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni, E⦵ = −0.26 V; Au³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Au, E⦵ = +1.52 V.

**(a)** Write the conventional representation of a cell made from these two half-cells. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the overall equation for the cell reaction. **[2]**

**4.** Data: V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺, E⦵ = −0.26 V; Co²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Co, E⦵ = −0.28 V.

A student writes the cell as Pt(s) | V²⁺(aq), V³⁺(aq) || Co²⁺(aq) | Co(s).

**(a)** Calculate the EMF of the cell as the student has written it. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what your answer shows, and write the correct representation. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the overall equation for the reaction when the cell delivers a current. **[1]**

**5.** A student sets up a reference electrode: hydrogen gas at 100 kPa is bubbled over a copper electrode dipping into 1.00 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid at 298 K.

**(a)** Identify two errors in this set-up and explain why each is an error. **[4]**
**(b)** A correctly built standard hydrogen electrode is connected to a standard cadmium half-cell. The voltmeter reads 0.40 V and the hydrogen electrode is positive. State E⦵ for Cd²⁺/Cd. **[1]**

**6.** Data:

S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2SO₄²⁻, E⦵ = +2.01 V
VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O, E⦵ = +1.00 V
Cd²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd, E⦵ = −0.40 V
Cr³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cr²⁺, E⦵ = −0.41 V

**(a)** Identify the strongest reducing agent in the data. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce whether cadmium metal can reduce Cr³⁺ ions to Cr²⁺ ions under standard conditions. Explain. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that Cr²⁺ ions can reduce VO₂⁺ ions. Calculate the EMF and write the overall equation. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain how increasing the concentration of Cr³⁺ ions could change your answer to (b). **[2]**

**7.** In required practical 6, a student builds the cell Ni(s) | Ni²⁺(aq) || Sn²⁺(aq) | Sn(s). Data: Ni²⁺/Ni −0.26 V; Sn²⁺/Sn −0.13 V. Three readings are 0.116 V, 0.120 V and 0.118 V.

**(a)** Calculate the expected EMF under standard conditions. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean of the readings. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why the mean is lower than expected. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a high-resistance voltmeter is used. **[1]**

**8.** The simplified electrode reactions in a lithium cell are:

Positive electrode: Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻

**(a)** Write the overall equation for discharge. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how these electrode reactions generate an electric current. **[3]**
**(c)** Write the equation for the reaction at the cobalt-containing electrode when the cell is recharged. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one benefit and one risk to society of using rechargeable lithium cells. **[2]**

**9.** A non-rechargeable zinc–air cell uses these half-equations:

[Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn + 4OH⁻, E⦵ = −1.20 V
O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻, E⦵ = +0.40 V

**(a)** Deduce the reaction at each electrode during discharge. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the overall equation. **[2]**
**(c)** Deduce the EMF of the cell. **[1]**
**(d)** Oxygen is taken in from the air continuously. Explain why this cell is still not a fuel cell. **[1]**

**10.** An alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell is based on these half-equations:

O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻
2H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ + 2OH⁻

**(a)** Write the reaction at the negative electrode and at the positive electrode. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the overall equation and show that hydroxide ions are not used up. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the cell does not need to be electrically recharged. **[1]**
**(d)** State one risk to society of using hydrogen as the fuel. **[1]**

**11.** Data:

Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ca, E⦵ = −2.87 V
Cd²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd, E⦵ = −0.40 V
VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O, E⦵ = +1.00 V
S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2SO₄²⁻, E⦵ = +2.01 V

**(a)** Identify the pair of half-cells that would give the largest EMF and calculate it. Suggest why a calcium half-cell with an aqueous solution is not practical. **[3]**
**(b)** A cell is made from the cadmium and VO₂⁺/VO²⁺ half-cells. Write its conventional representation, calculate its EMF and write the overall equation. **[5]**
**(c)** Predict, with a reason, the effect on the EMF of the cell in (b) of adding more acid to the vanadium half-cell. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The EMF of a cell made from the half-cell and the standard hydrogen electrode [1]; measured at 298 K and 100 kPa [1]; with 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition listing only the conditions, without the hydrogen electrode, is incomplete.

**2.** **Na⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Na** [1]; **Cr³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cr²⁺** [1]; **S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2SO₄²⁻** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Flipping only the arrow leaves an oxidation; the electrons must move to the left.

**3. (a)** Ni is more negative, so it is the negative electrode on the left [1]; **Ni(s) | Ni²⁺(aq) || Au³⁺(aq) | Au(s)** [1]
**(b)** EMF = 1.52 − (−0.26) = **+1.78 V** [1]
**(c)** Ni × 3 and Au × 2 to balance 6e⁻ [1]; **3Ni + 2Au³⁺ → 3Ni²⁺ + 2Au** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The half-equations are scaled to match electrons but the E⦵ values are not; scaling them gives a wrong EMF in (b).

**4. (a)** EMF = E(right) − E(left) = −0.28 − (−0.26) = **−0.02 V** [1]
**(b)** A negative EMF means the cell is the wrong way round: cobalt is negative [1]; **Co(s) | Co²⁺(aq) || V³⁺(aq), V²⁺(aq) | Pt(s)** [1]
**(c)** **Co + 2V³⁺ → Co²⁺ + 2V²⁺** [1]
*Examiner insight:* With close E⦵ values, check the order; a working cell always has a positive EMF.

**5. (a)** Copper electrode [1]; copper is not inert and could set up its own Cu²⁺/Cu equilibrium, so platinum is needed [1]; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid [1]; it releases more than one H⁺ per formula unit, so [H⁺] is above 1.00 mol dm⁻³; use, for example, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl [1]
**(b)** **−0.40 V** [1]
*Examiner insight:* 298 K and 100 kPa are correct here; each real error needs a reason as well as a name.

**6. (a)** **Cr²⁺** (its half-equation is the most negative, −0.41 V) [1]
**(b)** Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ (−0.41 V) is less positive than Cd²⁺/Cd (−0.40 V) [1]; so the reaction is **not feasible** under standard conditions; EMF would be −0.01 V [1]
**(c)** VO₂⁺/VO²⁺ (+1.00 V) is more positive than Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ (−0.41 V), so feasible [1]; EMF = 1.00 − (−0.41) = **+1.41 V** [1]; **VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + Cr²⁺ → VO²⁺ + H₂O + Cr³⁺** [1]
**(d)** Higher [Cr³⁺] shifts Cr³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cr²⁺ to the right, making its potential more positive [1]; it could become more positive than −0.40 V, so cadmium could then reduce Cr³⁺ [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the reducing agent is a right-hand species; choosing Cd because it is a metal misreads the table.

**7. (a)** −0.13 − (−0.26) = **0.13 V** [1]
**(b)** (0.116 + 0.120 + 0.118) ÷ 3 = **0.118 V** [1]
**(c)** Any two of: oxide on an electrode; solutions not exactly 1.00 mol dm⁻³ or temperature not 298 K; the voltmeter draws some current [2]
**(d)** So that almost no current flows and the reading equals the EMF [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "human error" is too vague; name a specific cause linked to this cell.

**8. (a)** **Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻** [1]
**(b)** Lithium is oxidised at the negative electrode, releasing electrons [1]; the electrons flow through the external circuit to the positive electrode, where CoO₂ is reduced (cobalt +4 to +3) [1]; Li⁺ ions move through the electrolyte to the positive electrode to balance the charge [1]
**(c)** **Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻ → Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻** [1]
**(d)** Benefit: reused over many charge cycles, so fewer cells are discarded [1]; risk: an overcharged or crushed cell can overheat and ignite [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), say where electrons go and where ions go; they take different paths.

**9. (a)** Negative electrode: **Zn + 4OH⁻ → [Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ + 2e⁻** [1]; positive electrode: **O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻** [1]
**(b)** Double the zinc equation to balance 4e⁻ [1]; **2Zn + O₂ + 2H₂O + 4OH⁻ → 2[Zn(OH)₄]²⁻** [1]
**(c)** 0.40 − (−1.20) = **+1.60 V** [1]
**(d)** The zinc is not supplied continuously; once it is used up, the cell stops [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), 8OH⁻ on the left and 4OH⁻ on the right should cancel to leave 4OH⁻; check that charge balances (−4 each side).

**10. (a)** Negative: **H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻** [1]; positive: **O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻** [1]
**(b)** Doubling the negative-electrode equation and adding gives **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** [1]; the 4OH⁻ made at the positive electrode equals the 4OH⁻ used at the negative electrode [1]
**(c)** Hydrogen and oxygen are supplied continuously, so the reactants are never used up [1]
**(d)** Any one: hydrogen–air mixtures are explosive; it is bulky to store; producing it may use fossil-fuel energy [1]
*Examiner insight:* Reverse the hydrogen half-equation for the negative electrode.

**11. (a)** Ca²⁺/Ca and S₂O₈²⁻/SO₄²⁻ [1]; EMF = 2.01 − (−2.87) = **+4.88 V** [1]; calcium reacts with water, so it would not stay in equilibrium with its ions [1]
**(b)** Cadmium is the negative electrode on the left; the vanadium half-cell needs platinum [1]; **Cd(s) | Cd²⁺(aq) || VO₂⁺(aq), H⁺(aq), VO²⁺(aq) | Pt(s)** [1]; EMF = 1.00 − (−0.40) = **+1.40 V** [1]; vanadium equation × 2 [1]; **Cd + 2VO₂⁺ + 4H⁺ → Cd²⁺ + 2VO²⁺ + 2H₂O** [1]
**(c)** H⁺ is on the left of the vanadium half-equation, so more acid shifts it right and its potential becomes more positive [1]; the EMF **increases** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), include H⁺ in the cell representation because it appears in the half-equation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing an electrode reaction as an oxidation instead of an IUPAC reduction.
- Scaling an E⦵ value when the half-equation is scaled.
- Leaving out Pt(s) or H⁺(aq) from a cell representation.
- Using sulfuric acid in the hydrogen electrode without noticing that [H⁺] is not 1.00 mol dm⁻³.
- Saying electrons travel through the electrolyte or the salt bridge.
- Leaving spare OH⁻ or electrons uncancelled in an overall equation.
- Explaining a concentration change without stating the direction of the equilibrium shift.
