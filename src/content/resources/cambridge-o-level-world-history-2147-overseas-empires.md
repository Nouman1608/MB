**Key question 5 of Core Content Option A in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks why nations built overseas empires
in the nineteenth century and what those empires did to the people they ruled.** It is a comparison question at
heart: three European case studies in Africa, one British case study in India and one American case study in Cuba
and the Philippines. Examiners reward answers that compare motives and impacts across cases rather than writing five
separate stories. The question was the Paper 2 prescribed topic for November 2024 and remains on Paper 1 throughout
2024–2026.

For quick recall, use the [overseas empires revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-overseas-empires-revision-notes/);
for exam questions with mark schemes, use the
[overseas empires practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-overseas-empires-practice/).

this question to the causes of the First World War.

## What the specification sets

Focus points:

1. What were the motives behind European and US imperialism?
2. How varied were the impacts of European imperialism on Africans?
3. Why, and with what effects, did Indians resist British rule?
4. Why, and with what effects, did the United States gain former Spanish colonies?

Specified content: economic, military, geopolitical, religious and cultural motives; the French model of assimilation
and direct rule (Faidherbe and Senegal); the British model of indirect rule (Lugard and Nigeria); the Belgians and
private imperialism (Leopold II and the Congo); the British in India, the events of 1857 and the changes they brought;
the wars of independence in the Philippines and Cuba, the Spanish–American War and the Treaty of Paris, the debate in
the USA over imperialism, the consequences of American control for Filipinos, and American intervention in Cuba to
1906.

## Weighing the motives

The syllabus names five motives. A (c) question will usually ask you to weigh one against the others, so you need an
argument, not a list.

- **Economic.** Industrial economies wanted raw materials (palm oil, rubber, cotton), markets for manufactured goods
  and places to invest. The English writer J. A. Hobson argued in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902) that empire mainly
  served investors seeking higher profits abroad. Against this, many colonies cost more to run than they returned, and
  most British overseas investment went to independent countries such as the United States and Argentina.
- **Military.** Steamships needed coaling stations, and navies needed bases on trade routes. The American naval
  officer Alfred Thayer Mahan argued in *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890) that national greatness
  depended on a strong navy with bases overseas, an argument that shaped US interest in the Pacific and the Caribbean.
- **Geopolitical.** Rivalry drove powers to claim territory before others did. At the **Berlin Conference
  (1884–85)** the European powers agreed that a claim to African territory required "effective occupation", which
  sped up the Scramble.
- **Religious.** Missionary societies wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade, and missionaries often
  pressed their governments to take control.
- **Cultural.** Many Europeans and Americans believed in a "civilising mission", backed by racial ideas that ranked
  peoples as superior and inferior. The French politician Jules Ferry defended colonial expansion in the 1880s partly
  in these terms.

**A useful line of argument:** economic interests usually explain *where* powers expanded, but rivalry and prestige
explain *why so fast* after 1880, and cultural and religious ideas explain *why so many people at home supported it*.

## How varied were the impacts on Africans?

Use the three cases to show variety along clear criteria.

| Criterion | French Senegal (Faidherbe) | British Northern Nigeria (Lugard) | Leopold's Congo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who ruled | French officials directly | Existing emirs under British supervision | Leopold's private state and concession companies |
| Aim stated | Assimilation into French culture | Cheap, stable rule through local institutions | Profit for the king |
| Local elites | Chiefs who resisted were replaced or defeated | Emirs kept their courts and collected taxes | Ignored, used or destroyed |
| Economy | Groundnut cultivation for export expanded | Taxes and trade through existing structures | Forced rubber and ivory collection |
| Violence | Military conquest inland against Umar Tall's forces | Conquest of Kano and Sokoto in 1903, then relative stability | Hostage-taking, mutilation and killing to enforce quotas |
| Rights | Citizenship for a small minority in the Four Communes | None in the modern sense; rule through tradition | None; reports by Casement and Morel exposed abuses |

**Faidherbe in Senegal** pushed French control up the Senegal River with forts, relieved the siege of Médine in 1857
and raised African troops, the tirailleurs sénégalais. He opened a school for the sons of chiefs at Saint-Louis to
train an elite loyal to France. Assimilation, though, reached only a few: most Senegalese remained subjects without
citizenship.

**Lugard in Nigeria** governed through emirs because Britain had few officials and little money. In the north this
preserved Islamic law and courts and limited missionary activity. In the south-east, where there were no powerful
chiefs, Britain appointed "warrant chiefs" with little local legitimacy.

**Leopold II in the Congo** treated a vast territory as a business. The Congo Free State, recognised in 1885, paid
for itself through forced labour. Early critics included the African American journalist George Washington Williams,
whose open letter to Leopold appeared in 1890. After Roger Casement's report (1904) and the campaign of E. D. Morel's
Congo Reform Association, Belgium annexed the colony in 1908.

**Judgement:** impacts were varied in method but similar in their main effect, the loss of African political
independence and the reshaping of economies for European needs. The Congo was the extreme case because no state,
however distant, held the ruler to account.

## Why, and with what effects, did Indians resist British rule?

The rising of 1857 combined military, religious, political and economic grievances: the greased-cartridge rumour,
annexations under the Doctrine of Lapse and the takeover of Awadh in 1856, fears that Western reforms and missionaries
threatened caste and religion, and resentment among landholders who had lost estates. It began at Meerut in May 1857
and was centred on Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi. It was largely confined to northern and central India; the
Punjab and the south stayed mostly quiet, the Madras and Bombay armies largely remained loyal, and many princes
supported Britain.

**Effects:** East India Company rule ended with the Government of India Act 1858; a Viceroy now governed for the
Crown; the Queen's Proclamation of November 1858 promised religious toleration and respect for the princes; the army
was reorganised with more British troops and recruitment from groups considered loyal; the last Mughal emperor was
exiled. British rule became more cautious about social reform but also more distant and racially separate. In 1877
Victoria took the title Empress of India.

## Why, and with what effects, did the USA gain Spanish colonies?

**Why:** sympathy for the Cuban war of independence (from 1895), inflamed by sensational newspapers; American
investments in Cuban sugar; Mahan's case for bases; the explosion of the USS *Maine* in Havana harbour in February
1898. Congress passed the **Teller Amendment**, promising not to annex Cuba, and war followed in April 1898.

**The debate:** after the Treaty of Paris (December 1898) gave the USA the Philippines, Puerto Rico and Guam,
imperialists argued for trade, bases and a duty to govern. The Anti-Imperialist League argued that ruling people
without consent contradicted the Declaration of Independence, and some opponents also used racist arguments against
bringing new peoples under US rule.

**Effects on Filipinos:** Emilio Aguinaldo's independence movement fought the USA from 1899. American forces used
harsh counter-insurgency methods, and civilian deaths from fighting, disease and famine were very high. Aguinaldo was
captured in 1901. US rule then built schools and roads and created an elected assembly (1907), but power stayed in
American hands.

**Cuba to 1906:** US troops occupied the island until 1902. The **Platt Amendment** (1901), written into the Cuban
constitution, gave the USA the right to intervene and a naval base at Guantánamo Bay. US troops returned in 1906.
Cuba was formally independent but under American control.

## Annotated model answer: a (c) question

*"Economic motives were the most important reason for imperialism in the nineteenth century." How far do you agree?
Explain your answer.*

> Economic motives were important because industrial countries needed raw materials and markets. Leopold II's Congo
> was run almost entirely to extract rubber and ivory for profit. **[Point with specific evidence.]** However, the
> speed of the Scramble after 1880 is better explained by rivalry. At the Berlin Conference the powers agreed that
> claims required effective occupation, so each rushed to take land before another could, even land of little value.
> **[Counter-factor explained.]** In the case of the USA in 1898, military and strategic motives, such as Mahan's
> argument for naval bases, help explain why it kept the Philippines. **[Second case broadens the answer.]** Overall
> economic motives were the most common starting point, but rivalry decided the timing and scale of expansion, so I
> partly agree. **[Judgement with reasons.]**

## Common mistakes

- Listing motives without examples from the named case studies.
- Describing the Congo as a Belgian colony before 1908.
- Calling 1857 a "national" rising of all India.
- Forgetting the Teller Amendment when explaining why Cuba was not annexed.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe**: precise features, such as the terms of the Platt Amendment.
- **Explain**: show cause and effect; for example, why indirect rule suited Britain.
- **How far**: compare cases and conclude with a criterion.
- **Sources**: imperial propaganda, cartoons and reformers' reports each had a purpose; identify it before judging
  reliability.
