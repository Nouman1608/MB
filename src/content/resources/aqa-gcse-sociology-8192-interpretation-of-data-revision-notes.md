These notes cover the "interpretation of data" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: interpreting graphs, diagrams, charts and tables to discern patterns and trends. Read the [interpretation of data study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-interpretation-of-data/) for worked examples, then practise with the [interpretation of data practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-interpretation-of-data-practice/). The [research methods revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-revision-notes/) cover all of section 3.7. All figures below are fictional.

## What you must be able to do

Interpret **graphs, diagrams, charts and tables** to **discern patterns and trends** in statistical data, in the context of any topic in the course.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Data | Information collected for research |
| Trend | General direction of data over time |
| Pattern | A regular relationship in data, such as one group being consistently higher |
| Percentage | A proportion out of 100 |
| Percentage point | The unit for the difference between two percentages |
| Rate | A number compared with population size, such as per 1,000 |
| Mean | Total divided by the number of values |
| Median | Middle value when values are in order |
| Mode | Most common value |
| Correlation | Two things changing together |
| Causation | One thing causing another |
| Representative data | Data that reflect the wider population |

## The seven-step routine: TUSPQEL

1. **T**itle: what, where, when?
2. **U**nits: numbers, percentages, rates?
3. **S**ource and sample size.
4. **P**attern or trend in one sentence.
5. **Q**uote figures: first, last, highest, lowest.
6. **E**xceptions.
7. **L**imitations: what can the data not show?

## Calculations at a glance

| Task | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage of total | part ÷ total × 100 | 45 of 180 = 25% |
| Difference in percentage points | new % − old % | 18% to 27% = 9 percentage points |
| Percentage change | change ÷ original × 100 | 18% to 27%: 9 ÷ 18 × 100 = 50% increase |
| Rate per 1,000 | number ÷ population × 1,000 | 96 in 12,000 = 8 per 1,000 |
| Times more likely | larger % ÷ smaller % | 48% and 12%: 4 times as likely |
| Mean | total ÷ count | 4, 6, 8, 10, 12: 40 ÷ 5 = 8 |
| Median | middle value in order | 3, 5, 9, 14, 20: median 9 |

**Always show the sum, the answer and the unit.**

## Reading each format

- **Table:** read row and column headings; check whether figures are numbers or percentages; look for totals.
- **Bar chart:** compare bar heights against the axis scale.
- **Line graph:** follow direction; note gaps between years; check where the vertical axis starts.
- **Pie chart:** shows shares of a whole, not how big the whole is; two pies of equal size can represent very different totals.

## Describing a trend: sentence frame

"Between [first year] and [last year], [what is measured] [rose / fell / stayed stable] from [first figure + unit] to [last figure + unit]. The exception was [year or group], when [figure]."

**Example:** "Between 2010 and 2025 the proportion of Year 11 pupils in Stonebridge saying they planned to go to university rose from 31% to 44%. The exception was 2020, when it dipped to 29%."

## Traps

- **Numbers versus rates:** bigger populations produce bigger numbers.
- **Percentage versus percentage points:** 20% to 30% is 10 points, a 50% increase.
- **Truncated axis:** small changes look dramatic.
- **Missing years:** you cannot describe what happened between data points.
- **Correlation is not causation.**
- **Small samples:** percentages swing with one or two answers.
- **Changed definitions:** create false trends.
- **Missing groups:** data only describe who was included.

## Linking data to sociology

- Rising lone-parent households: New Right reading (family breakdown) versus a reading based on choice and acceptance of family diversity.
- Higher recorded crime in an area: more crime, or more policing and recording?
- Class gaps in university entry: support for Marxist criticism of meritocracy.

## Exam traps in data questions

- Describing every figure instead of the overall pattern.
- No units, or "it went up a lot".
- Calculating correctly but not showing working when asked.
- Explaining a limitation with no sociological concept.
- Forgetting to use figures from the item when explaining a pattern.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the difference between a pattern and a trend?
2. In a survey of 160 pupils, 40 say they are bullied. What percentage is this?
3. A rate rose from 12 to 18 per 1,000. By how much did it rise, and what is the percentage increase?
4. Unemployment fell from 8% to 6%. Is that a fall of 2% or 2 percentage points?
5. A town of 50,000 people records 400 burglaries. What is the rate per 1,000?
6. Why are rates better than numbers for comparing areas?
7. What is the median of 7, 2, 11, 5, 9?
8. Why might the median be used for income data?
9. What does a truncated axis do?
10. What does "correlation is not causation" mean?
11. What can a pie chart not show?
12. Give one question to ask about the source of a data item.

### Answers

1. A pattern is a regular relationship at one time; a trend is a direction over time.
2. 40 ÷ 160 × 100 = 25%.
3. It rose by 6 per 1,000; 6 ÷ 12 × 100 = a 50% increase.
4. 2 percentage points.
5. 400 ÷ 50,000 × 1,000 = 8 per 1,000.
6. They take population size into account, so areas of different sizes can be compared fairly.
7. In order: 2, 5, 7, 9, 11, so the median is 7.
8. A few very high incomes pull the mean upwards.
9. It starts the axis above zero, making small differences look large.
10. Two things changing together does not prove that one causes the other.
11. The size of the total; it shows shares only.
12. Who produced it and why; how large the sample was; when it was collected.
