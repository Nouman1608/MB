This guide covers the **Law making: delegated legislation** row of AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1. The specification lists four things: **types of delegated legislation: orders in council, statutory instruments, bylaws (from local authorities and public bodies)**; **parliamentary and judicial controls on delegated legislation**; **the reasons for the use of delegated legislation**; and **the advantages and disadvantages of delegated legislation**. The [Nature of Law and the English Legal System study guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-law-nature-of-law-and-english-legal-system/) gives a one-paragraph overview. This page explains each part with examples and cases, and shows how to evaluate.

## What delegated legislation is

**Delegated legislation** (also called secondary or subordinate legislation) is law made by a person or body other than Parliament, using power that Parliament has given in an Act. The Act that gives the power is the **enabling Act** (or parent Act). Delegated legislation has the same legal force as an Act, but only if it stays within the powers the enabling Act grants.

```
Parliament
   passes ENABLING ACT (sets the limits of the power)
      -> Privy Council: Order in Council
      -> Minister / department: statutory instrument
      -> Local authority or public body: bylaw
```

## The three types

### Orders in Council

Made by the **King and the Privy Council**, in practice on the advice of government ministers. Uses include:

- transferring responsibilities between government departments;
- bringing some Acts into force;
- making law in an emergency: the Civil Contingencies Act 2004 allows emergency regulations by Order in Council where there is an emergency and Parliament cannot act quickly enough.

### Statutory instruments

Made by **government ministers and their departments** under powers in an enabling Act. They are by far the most common type. Examples:

- the National Minimum Wage Act 1998 allows the government to set the minimum wage rates each year by regulations, so rates can change without a new Act;
- the Health Protection (Coronavirus, Restrictions) (England) Regulations 2020 were made under the Public Health (Control of Disease) Act 1984, and imposed the first national lockdown;
- commencement orders bringing parts of Acts into force.

### Bylaws

Made by **local authorities** for their own area and by **public bodies** for the places they manage. A district council may make bylaws on matters such as dog fouling or the use of parks; transport bodies make bylaws governing conduct on their railways and stations. Bylaws usually need approval from the relevant government department before they take effect.

## The reasons for using delegated legislation

The specification separates reasons from advantages, so keep them distinct: reasons explain **why** Parliament delegates; advantages are **benefits** of the result.

1. **Lack of parliamentary time.** Parliament could not pass every detailed rule as an Act; it sets the framework and leaves detail to others.
2. **Technical expertise.** Ministers can draw on civil servants and experts; for example, detailed safety rules for machinery or medicines.
3. **Local knowledge.** Local authorities know their area's needs better than Parliament.
4. **Speed and emergencies.** A statutory instrument can be made in days, which was why most coronavirus restrictions were imposed this way.
5. **Flexibility.** Rules that must change often, such as minimum wage rates, can be updated without a new Act.
6. **Consultation.** Many enabling Acts require consultation with interested parties before rules are made.

## Parliamentary controls

| Control | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| The enabling Act | Sets the scope of the power, who can use it and the procedure required | The most basic and important limit | Can be drafted very widely |
| Negative resolution | The instrument becomes or remains law unless either House annuls it within a set period (usually 40 days) | Covers most instruments | Few are ever debated; annulment is rare |
| Affirmative resolution | The instrument must be approved by Parliament (usually both Houses) to become or remain law | Forces a vote | Parliament cannot amend; it must accept or reject the whole instrument |
| Super-affirmative procedure | Extra scrutiny and a longer period, used for some powers to amend Acts | Detailed scrutiny | Rarely used |
| Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments | Checks instruments on technical grounds, such as unusual use of a power or defective drafting | Expert, cross-party | Cannot change an instrument; reports on legality, not merits |
| Lords Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee | Reports on instruments of interest, including policy merits | Considers merits | Recommendations are not binding |
| Questions and debates | MPs can question ministers | Publicity | Limited time |

The House of Lords **Delegated Powers and Regulatory Reform Committee** also examines Bills before they become Acts, checking whether the powers they would delegate are appropriate. This is a control at source.

## Judicial controls

Anyone with sufficient interest can ask the High Court for **judicial review**. If the court finds the delegated legislation **ultra vires** ("beyond the powers"), it can declare it void.

### Procedural ultra vires

The body did not follow the procedure the enabling Act required. In *Agricultural, Horticultural and Forestry Industry Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms Ltd* (1972) the Minister was required to consult organisations representing those affected. The Mushroom Growers' Association was not consulted, so the order did not apply to its members.

### Substantive ultra vires

The body made rules beyond the powers given.

- *Customs and Excise Commissioners v Cure & Deeley Ltd* (1962): the commissioners gave themselves power to decide the amount of tax due, which went beyond their power to make regulations for collecting tax.
- *R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor* (2016): the Supreme Court held that a draft order introducing a residence test for civil legal aid was outside the powers in the Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012, because the Act allowed changes based on need and merit, not on where a person lived.

### Unreasonableness

The courts will also strike down delegated legislation that is so unreasonable no reasonable body could have made it, the standard set in *Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation* (1948). In *Strickland v Hayes Borough Council* (1896) a bylaw banning singing or reciting obscene songs in any public **or private** place was held unreasonable because it was too wide. The courts are slow to interfere with bylaws, however: in *Kruse v Johnson* (1898) a bylaw against playing music or singing near a dwelling house after being asked to stop was upheld as reasonable.

**Limit of judicial control:** it is retrospective (the court acts only after a challenge), depends on someone bringing a case, and cannot be used against the enabling Act itself.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Saves parliamentary time for major policy | **Undemocratic**: made by unelected civil servants or ministers rather than debated by Parliament |
| Uses expertise and local knowledge | **Volume**: large numbers are made each year, and few receive any debate |
| Quick to make and amend, essential in emergencies | **Lack of publicity**: people may break rules they have never heard of |
| Flexible: easy to update | **Sub-delegation**: power passed down again, further from Parliament |
| Consultation can improve quality | **Henry VIII powers** allow ministers to amend Acts themselves, weakening parliamentary supremacy in practice |
| Controls exist in Parliament and the courts | **Weak controls**: negative procedure rarely leads to debate; Parliament cannot amend; judicial review depends on a challenge |

## Worked example: applying judicial control

**Scenario:** The (fictional) Community Safety Act 2026 lets the Home Secretary make regulations "to restrict the sale of fireworks to persons under 18, after consulting retailers' organisations". She makes regulations banning all sales of fireworks to anyone, without consulting anyone.

```
Procedural ultra vires: duty to consult retailers' organisations ignored
   -> Aylesbury Mushrooms: regulations may not bind those not consulted
Substantive ultra vires: power limited to sales to under-18s;
   a ban on all sales goes beyond it -> Cure & Deeley
Unreasonableness: possibly also argued, but the clearer grounds are ultra vires
Conclusion: a retailer could seek judicial review; regulations likely void
```

## Common mistakes

- Describing delegated legislation as made by Parliament.
- Mixing up the affirmative and negative procedures.
- Saying the Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments can amend or veto instruments.
- Listing reasons and advantages as identical lists without explanation.
- Using procedural and substantive ultra vires without a case for each.

## Exam technique

**Explain** a type by saying who makes it, under what, with an example. **Explain** a control by saying how it works, adding a case for judicial controls. **Discuss** or **evaluate** by weighing the controls' effectiveness and the advantages against the disadvantages, then judging.
