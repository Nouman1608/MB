## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between static efficiency and dynamic efficiency. **[4]**

**2.** In the market for a good in Country A, demand is P = 90 - 3Q and the marginal cost of supply is MC = 18 + 3Q (Q in thousands of units, P in currency units, so surplus is in thousands).

**(a)** Find the competitive output and price, and calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[4]**
**(b)** A single firm takes over the whole market and has the same MC. Find the output and price that maximise its profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the deadweight loss and the fall in consumer surplus. **[3]**

**3.** A small economy imports a good at the world price of 40. Domestic demand is P = 100 - 5Q and domestic supply is P = 10 + 5Q. The government imposes a tariff of 10 per unit. Calculate the change in consumer surplus, the change in producer surplus, the tariff revenue and the deadweight loss. **[6]**

**4.** A seller has constant MC = 12 and two groups of buyers. Group A has demand P = 56 - 2Q and Group B has P = 32 - 2Q.

**(a)** Find the price and quantity in each group if the seller charges each group a separate profit-maximising price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the price and total quantity if one price must be charged to both groups. **[2]**
**(c)** Compare profit, consumer surplus and total surplus in the two cases. **[4]**

**5.** A firm has total cost TC = 500 + 4Q + 0.05Q², so MC = 4 + 0.1Q.

**(a)** Work out the output that minimises average total cost, and the minimum cost per unit. **[3]**
**(b)** Is the firm productively efficient at an output of 80? **[2]**
**(c)** At 100 units the firm's actual average total cost is 16. Calculate the cost of its X-inefficiency and express the excess as a percentage of the minimum. **[2]**

**6.** A firm's unit cost is 20. It invests in research so that unit cost falls by 5% a year, on 30,000 units a year.

**(a)** Calculate its unit cost after four years. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate its annual cost saving in the fourth year compared with the original cost. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why the investment might not make the firm dynamically efficient. **[2]**

**7.** Firm P is the only seller of a product. It sells 20,000 units at 25 with a unit cost of 15. Firm Q enters with a unit cost of 12. The price falls to 20, Firm P sells 12,000 units and Firm Q sells 14,000.

**(a)** Calculate Firm P's profit before and after entry. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in Firm P's profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage changes in price and in market output. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how this illustrates creative destruction. **[2]**

**8.** Market A: price 18, marginal cost 15, R&D spending 2 million, sales 150 million. Market B: price 16, marginal cost 14, R&D spending 6 million, sales 120 million.

**(a)** Calculate the price-cost margin as a percentage of price in each market. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate R&D spending as a percentage of sales in each market. **[1]**
**(c)** Use the figures to compare the two markets for allocative and dynamic efficiency. **[2]**
**(d)** State one limitation of these indicators. **[1]**

**9.** Using the market in question 2, a firm practises perfect price discrimination. Find output, consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss, and give one criticism. **[5]**

**10.** Evaluate whether the tariff in question 3 improves the welfare of the small economy. **[8]**

**11.** Return to question 2. Innovation lowers the firm's marginal cost to MC = 9 + 3Q.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising monopoly output and price, and the total surplus. **[5]**
**(b)** The competitive market on the old costs gave total surplus of 432. Evaluate whether monopoly can be better for society than competition. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Static efficiency judges one moment, with technology and resources given [1], judged by productive efficiency (lowest ATC) and allocative efficiency (P = MC) [1]. Dynamic efficiency is efficiency over time [1], through R&D, investment in human and non-human capital and technological change that lowers costs or improves products [1].
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs both sides. Defining one term well and the other vaguely is hard to reward fully.

**2. (a)** 90 - 3Q = 18 + 3Q, so Q = 12 [1]. P = 90 - 36 = 54 [1]. CS = half x 12 x (90 - 54) = 216 [1]. PS = half x 12 x (54 - 18) = 216 [1].
**(b)** MR = 90 - 6Q [1]. 90 - 6Q = 18 + 3Q, so Q = 8 [1]. P = 90 - 24 = 66 [1].
**(c)** MC at 8 = 42 [1]. DWL = half x (12 - 8) x (66 - 42) = 48 [1]. CS falls from 216 to half x 8 x 24 = 96, a fall of 120 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show each triangle as half x base x height with the numbers, so your working is easy to follow and check.

**3.** At P = 40: demand 12, supply 6 [1]. At P = 50: demand 10, supply 8 [1]. Loss of CS = half x (12 + 10) x 10 = 110 [1]. Gain in PS = half x (6 + 8) x 10 = 70 [1]. Revenue = 10 x (10 - 8) = 20 [1]. DWL = half x 2 x 10 + half x 2 x 10 = 20 (check: 70 + 20 + 20 = 110) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check that the CS loss equals PS gain plus revenue plus DWL. If not, an area is wrong.

**4. (a)** Group A: 56 - 4Q = 12, so Q = 11 and P = 34 [1]. Group B: 32 - 4Q = 12, so Q = 5 and P = 22 [1].
**(b)** Market demand is Q = 44 - P, so P = 44 - Q and MR = 44 - 2Q [1]. MR = 12 gives Q = 16 and P = 28 [1].
**(c)** Profit: discrimination 11 x 22 + 5 x 10 = 292; one price 16 x 16 = 256 [1]. CS: discrimination half x 11 x 22 + half x 5 x 10 = 146; one price half x 14 x 28 + half x 2 x 4 = 200 [1]. Total surplus: 438 against 456 [1]. Profit rises, consumers lose, and total surplus falls by 18: total quantity is 16 either way, but it is split unevenly between groups (valued at different prices) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say whether total welfare rises or falls and why, not only that the firm gains.

**5. (a)** ATC = 500/Q + 4 + 0.05Q. Set MC = ATC: 500/Q = 0.05Q, so Q² = 10,000 and Q = 100 [1]. ATC = 5 + 4 + 5 = 14 [1]. MC = 4 + 10 = 14, matching [1].
**(b)** ATC at 80 = 6.25 + 4 + 4 = 14.25 [1]. This is above the minimum of 14, so no: output is too low to spread the fixed cost [1].
**(c)** Excess = 16 - 14 = 2 per unit, so 2 x 100 = 200 [1]. 2/14 = 14.3% above the minimum [1].
*Examiner insight:* Productive efficiency is the lowest average cost, not the lowest marginal cost.

**6. (a)** 20 x 0.95⁴ [1] = 16.29 [1].
**(b)** Saving per unit = 20 - 16.29 [1]; times 30,000 = about 111,296 (using the unrounded cost) [1].
**(c)** The spending might be larger than the savings, so the firm gains nothing net [1]. Rivals may copy the innovation, or the firm's R&D may fail to produce a usable product or process [1].
*Examiner insight:* Two reasons need two different points; rewording one earns little extra.

**7. (a)** Before: (25 - 15) x 20,000 = 200,000 [1]. After: (20 - 15) x 12,000 = 60,000 [1].
**(b)** (60,000 - 200,000)/200,000 = -70% [1].
**(c)** Price falls 20% and output rises from 20,000 to 26,000, which is 30% [1].
**(d)** The incumbent's large profit signalled a reward to a new firm [1], which entered with a cheaper process; the incumbent's profit fell as consumers gained lower prices and more output [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link the data to the concept: say why entry happened, not only that profit fell.

**8. (a)** A: 3/18 = 16.7%. B: 2/16 = 12.5% [1] [1].
**(b)** A: 2/150 = 1.3%. B: 6/120 = 5.0% [1].
**(c)** Market B has a lower margin, so price is closer to marginal cost and it is nearer allocative efficiency [1]. Its higher R&D share suggests more dynamic efficiency [1].
**(d)** Any one: R&D spending does not guarantee success; margins do not show productive efficiency [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say that indicators suggest efficiency. They do not prove it, so cautious wording is safer.

**9.** Output 12, where the last buyer pays 54 = MC [1]. CS = 0 [1]. PS = half x 12 x (90 - 18) = 432 [1]. DWL = 0 [1]. Criticism: all the gain goes to the firm, and the firm must know each buyer's willingness to pay [1].
*Examiner insight:* Allocative efficiency and fairness are different. Say that the deadweight loss disappears yet consumers are worse off.

**10.** The tariff cuts CS by 110 but raises PS by 70 and revenue by 20, a net loss of 20 [1]. This net loss is the DWL from extra higher-cost home production and lost consumption [1]. Consumers lose and producers and the government gain, so the effect depends on whose welfare counts [1]. The revenue can fund spending that benefits society [1]. A small economy cannot lower the world price, so there is no terms-of-trade gain [1]. An infant industry may need time to grow, though protected firms may lose the pressure to cut costs [1]. Retaliation could reduce welfare further [1]. Overall the static effect is a net loss, so a tariff needs dynamic or non-economic justification [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the calculated figures in the argument and reach a clear conclusion, rather than listing points.

**11. (a)** MR = 90 - 6Q = 9 + 3Q [1]. Q = 9 [1], P = 90 - 27 = 63 [1]. MC at 9 = 36. CS = half x 9 x 27 = 121.5 [1]. PS = 9 x 27 + half x 9 x 27 = 364.5, so total surplus = 486 [1].
**(b)** Total surplus of 486 is above 432 [1], because the cost cut raises the surplus available [1]. A competitive market on the new cost would still give more (546.75) [1]. Gains require the innovation to happen, and a monopoly may do less R&D without a threat of entry [1]. Dynamic gains can outweigh static loss when innovation is large, but this depends on the firm passing on benefits and on the threat of entry [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare like with like, and say what the comparison depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong height in a surplus triangle.
- Reading the monopoly price from MR instead of from demand.
- Locating productive efficiency at the lowest MC.
- Counting tariff revenue as a loss to society.
- Forgetting the second DWL triangle in a tariff diagram.
- Calling perfect discrimination fair because the deadweight loss disappears.
- Judging monopoly on static grounds only.
