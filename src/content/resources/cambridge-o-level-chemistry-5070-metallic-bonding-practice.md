## Answers

**1.** Electrostatic **attraction** [1] between **positive ions** in a giant metallic lattice [1] and a sea of **delocalised electrons** [1]. **[3]**

**2. (a)** 1+ [1]

**(b)** 2+ [1]

**[2]**

**3.** Can be hammered (or pressed) into shape without breaking [1]. **[1]**

**4.** 12 × 2 = **24** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** **Delocalised electrons** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** The bond is the **strong electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons [1]; the electrons can move, but they are still **attracted to many ions** throughout the lattice, so the bonding remains strong [1]. **[2]**

**7.** A **giant lattice** of **positive aluminium ions** [1] with charge **3+** [1], arranged in regular **layers** [1], surrounded by a **sea of delocalised electrons** (three from each atom) held by electrostatic attraction [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Metals contain **delocalised electrons** [1] that are **free to move** through the lattice and carry charge [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** The copper ions are arranged in **layers** [1]; when the metal is pulled, the **layers slide** over each other [1]; the **delocalised electrons** keep attracting the ions in their new positions, so the bonding is not broken [1]

**(b)** Copper contains **delocalised electrons** [1] that **move through the lattice** and carry charge when a potential difference is applied [1]

**[5]**

**10. (a)** **A** [1]; it conducts **when solid** (and when molten) [1]; it **flattens** when hammered, showing it is malleable [1]

**(b)** **Ionic** (giant ionic lattice) [1]

**[4]**

*Examiner insight:* D conducts when solid but flakes into layers rather than flattening: graphite, not a metal. C is likely to be a giant covalent substance such as silicon(IV) oxide.

**11.** In a metal, the **layers of positive ions slide** over each other when hammered [1] and the **delocalised electrons** continue to hold the ions together, so the bonding remains [1]. In potassium chloride, moving a layer brings **ions of the same charge** next to each other [1], which **repel**, so the crystal breaks apart [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Solid magnesium **conducts**; solid magnesium chloride **does not** [1]. Magnesium has **delocalised electrons** that are free to move and carry charge [1]. Magnesium chloride contains **ions** [1], but in the solid they are held in **fixed positions** and cannot move; there are no delocalised electrons [1]. **[4]**

**13.** Lithium provides **1** delocalised electron per atom, forming **Li⁺** ions [1]; aluminium provides **3** per atom, forming **Al³⁺** ions [1]. **[2]**

**14. (a)** A **giant lattice** of **Mg²⁺ ions** in layers [1]; each atom has released its **two outer electrons**, which form a sea of **delocalised electrons** [1]; held together by **electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons [1]

**(b)** The **layers of ions slide** over each other when the ribbon is bent [1]; the **delocalised electrons** still attract the ions in their new positions, so the **bonding is not broken** [1]

**(c)** The **delocalised electrons** [1] are **free to move** through the lattice and carry charge [1]

**[7]**

*Examiner insight:* parts (b) and (c) say "use your answer to (a)": answers that introduce new ideas instead of building on the delocalised-electron model lose marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "sea of ions" or describing negative ions in the metal.
- Saying metals conduct because ions move.
- Explaining malleability with "layers slide" but not saying the bonding remains.
- Confusing malleable and ductile.
- Calling metallic bonds weak.
