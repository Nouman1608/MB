## Questions

**1.** The north pole of bar magnet A is held close to the south pole of bar magnet B. State what happens and name the type of force. **[2]**

**2.** (a) From copper, nickel, aluminium, cobalt and zinc, name the two magnetic materials. **[2]**
(b) A student has a metal bar that is attracted to a magnet. State the test that shows whether the bar is itself a magnet. **[1]**

**3.** Two bar magnets lie in line with their north poles facing, 4 cm apart. Describe the magnetic field pattern in the gap. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how a bar magnet picks up an unmagnetised steel paper clip. **[4]**

**5.** (a) Explain why the needle of a plotting compass settles pointing north. **[2]**
(b) A hiker's compass gives a wrong reading when held next to a steel flask. Explain why. **[2]**

**6.** A long straight wire carries a current.
**(a)** Describe what the magnetic field lines around the wire look like. **[2]**
**(b)** State one way of making the field stronger at a point, and state the effect of reversing the current. **[2]**

**7.** A plotting compass is placed at different distances from a straight wire carrying a steady current. The needle turns through 48 degrees at 3 cm, 30 degrees at 6 cm and 21 degrees at 9 cm.
**(a)** State the trend and explain it. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage decrease in the turn from 3 cm to 9 cm. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**8.** A wire is wound into a solenoid.
**(a)** Explain why a solenoid gives a stronger field than a single straight wire carrying the same current. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the field inside the solenoid. **[1]**
**(c)** State how the field outside compares with that of a bar magnet, and what happens to it when the current is reversed. **[2]**

**9.** A scrapyard crane uses an electromagnet with a soft iron core to move steel. Explain why an iron core is used, and how the load is released. **[4]**

**10.** A student uses a coil on an iron nail and a constant current. She counts the paper clips lifted in three trials for four numbers of turns.

| Turns | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| 30 | 6 | 7 | 7 |
| 45 | 10 | 9 | 10 |
| 60 | 13 | 13 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean clips lifted for 15 turns and for 60 turns. Give each to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** State the conclusion the data support, using the ratio of the two means. **[2]**
**(c)** Give two variables the student must keep the same. **[2]**

**11.** Plan and analyse an investigation into how the current affects the strength of an electromagnet.
**(a)** Describe a method, naming the apparatus, the variable you change, the variable you measure and how you improve reliability. **[5]**
**(b)** Mean clips lifted for 0.4, 0.8, 1.2 and 1.6 A were 2.0, 4.3, 6.1 and 8.0. Calculate the percentage increase in clips from 0.8 A to 1.6 A, and say what 8.0 compared with 2.0 shows. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The magnets attract [1]. It is a non-contact force [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the poles as unlike, and the force as non-contact, are separate points, so give both.

**2.** (a) Nickel [1] and cobalt [1]. **[2]**
(b) See whether it repels a known pole of a magnet [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "It is attracted" does not answer part (b), since iron is attracted too. Name repulsion.

**3.** Lines leave each north pole and curve away from the other magnet [1]. The lines do not cross, and arrows point away from each north pole [1]. There is a point midway where the field is zero [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A sketch is easier to credit when arrows are shown and no lines cross. In words, name the neutral point.

**4.** The magnet's field magnetises the clip, so it becomes an induced magnet [1]. The end nearest the magnet's pole becomes the opposite pole [1]. Opposite poles attract [1]. Induced magnetism always gives attraction, so the clip is pulled to the magnet whichever pole is used [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The step that the nearer end takes the opposite pole is the one most often left out. Write it out.

**5.** (a) The needle is a small magnet that can turn freely [1]. It lines up with the Earth's magnetic field, so its north-seeking end points towards the Earth's north [1]. **[2]**
(b) The steel flask is made of a magnetic material, which becomes an induced magnet [1]. Its field acts on the needle and changes the direction it points [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that a second field acts on the needle. "The flask interferes" is too vague to gain credit.

**6.** (a) Concentric circles [1] in a plane perpendicular to the wire, centred on the wire [1]. **[2]**
(b) Increase the current (or move closer to the wire) [1]. Reversing the current reverses the field direction [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Circles" alone is incomplete. Give the orientation to the wire, or the centre.

**7.** (a) The turn decreases as the distance increases [1] because the field is weaker further from the wire [1]. **[2]**
(b) (48 − 21) / 48 × 100 [1] = **56.3%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the unrounded working first. Then give the answer to the 3 significant figures requested.

**8.** (a) Each turn produces its own circular field [1]. Inside the coil the fields add together in the same direction [1]. **[2]**
(b) The field is strong and uniform [1]. **[1]**
(c) The field outside has a similar shape to that of a bar magnet [1]. Reversing the current swaps the poles [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the solenoid with a straight wire at the same current. A bare statement that it is "stronger" is unlikely to be enough.

**9.** Iron becomes a strong induced magnet in the coil's field, so the core increases the field strength [1], and it can lift more steel [1]. When the current is switched off, the iron loses most or all of its magnetism quickly [1]. The load is no longer held and drops [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two ideas are needed: a stronger magnet when on and loss of magnetism when off. Do not say the current "stops attracting".

**10.** (a) 15 turns: (3 + 4 + 3) / 3 = **3.33** clips [1]. 60 turns: (13 + 13 + 14) / 3 = **13.3** clips [1]. **[2]**
(b) 13.33 / 3.33 = 4.0, the same as 60 / 15 [1], so more turns give a stronger electromagnet, with strength close to proportional to the number of turns [1]. **[2]**
(c) Any two: the current, the core, the nail and clips [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data in the conclusion. A conclusion with no figures from the table is hard to credit in full.

**11.** (a) Wind insulated wire on an iron nail and connect it to a dc supply, ammeter, variable resistor and switch [1]. Change the current with the variable resistor and read it on the ammeter [1]. Measure the clips lifted [1]. Keep the turns, the core and the clips the same [1]. Repeat each setting at least three times and take a mean [1]. **[5]**
(b) (8.0 − 4.3) / 4.3 × 100 [1] = **86.0%** [1]. Quadrupling the current from 0.4 A to 1.6 A gives four times the clips (8.0 / 2.0 = 4.0), so the strength is proportional to the current over this range [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A good plan names the apparatus, what changes, what is measured and what stays the same. State all four.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "attracts metal" and not naming iron, steel, cobalt or nickel.
- Describing attraction without saying the poles are unlike.
- Missing arrows on field lines, or drawing lines that cross.
- Claiming that induced magnetism can give repulsion.
- Giving the wire's field as "around the wire" instead of concentric circles perpendicular to it.
- Writing "stronger" with no comparison.
- Giving "more power" in place of a larger current or more turns.
- Taking one reading in an investigation instead of repeating it and averaging.
- Forgetting to control a variable.
