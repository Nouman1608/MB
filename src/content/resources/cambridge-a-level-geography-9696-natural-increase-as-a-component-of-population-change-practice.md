Revise first with the [4.1 natural increase study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-natural-increase-as-a-component-of-population-change/) and the [4.1 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-natural-increase-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "infant mortality rate". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between the crude birth rate and the total fertility rate. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the natural increase rate of a country is not the same as its rate of population change. **[2]**

**4.** A fictional country has a population of 8 million. In one year there were 96 000 live births and 104 000 deaths.

**(a)** Calculate the natural increase rate per 1000. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** State the term used to describe this situation. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why replacement-level fertility is about 2.1 children per woman in countries with low mortality. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a high-income country may have a higher crude death rate than a low-income country. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows the age/sex structure of fictional country K as a percentage of the total population.

| Age band | Males (%) | Females (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | 21.0 | 20.0 |
| 15-29 | 14.0 | 13.0 |
| 30-44 | 9.0 | 9.0 |
| 45-64 | 5.0 | 5.0 |
| 65+ | 1.8 | 2.2 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the population aged 0-14. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the dependency ratio. Show your working. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe the population structure of country K. **[3]**

**(d)** Suggest **three** challenges this structure creates for the government. **[3]**

**8.** In fictional country G, males aged 25-49 make up 30% of the total population, but females of the same age make up only 9%.

**(a)** Suggest the main reason for this imbalance. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **three** consequences of this imbalance for country G. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how environmental and political factors affect levels of mortality. **[6]**

**10.** The table shows female literacy and infant mortality for five fictional countries.

| Country | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Female literacy (%) | 35 | 55 | 70 | 88 | 98 |
| Infant mortality (per 1000 live births) | 85 | 60 | 38 | 15 | 4 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship shown. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain this relationship. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how a sustained fall in fertility changes a country's age structure over time. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of deaths of infants under one year old [1] per 1000 live births in a year [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The crude birth rate is the number of live births per 1000 people in the total population in a year [1]. The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current rates, so it is not affected by the age structure of the population [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Natural increase measures only births minus deaths [1], whereas population change also includes net migration, the difference between immigration and emigration [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Birth rate = 96 000 / 8 000 000 x 1000 = 12 and death rate = 104 000 / 8 000 000 x 1000 = 13 [1]; natural increase rate = 12 - 13 = -1 per 1000 [1].

**(b)** Natural decrease [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Two children are needed to replace the two parents [1]; the extra 0.1 allows for some children dying before reaching reproductive age and for slightly more boys than girls being born [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The high-income country may have an ageing population with a large share of elderly people [1], and older people have a higher risk of death [1], whereas the low-income country has a youthful population in which most people are at ages with low risk of death, even though health care is poorer [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 21.0 + 20.0 = 41.0% [1].

**(b)** Dependents = 41.0 + 4.0 = 45.0%; working age = 27.0 + 18.0 + 10.0 = 55.0% [1]; dependency ratio = 45.0 / 55.0 x 100 = 81.8 [1].

**(c)** A youthful population with a very wide base: 41.0% are aged 0-14 [1]. Each older band is much smaller, giving a classic pyramid shape that suggests high birth rates and relatively high death rates [1]. Only 4.0% are aged 65 and over, and there are slightly more females than males in that group [1].

**(d)** Providing enough school places and teachers [1]; providing health care for children, such as vaccination and maternal care [1]; creating enough jobs as the large young cohort reaches working age [1]. **[9]**

**8. (a)** In-migration of male workers [1], for example to work in construction or oil industries, without their families [1].

**(b)** A large labour force supports economic growth but much of the migrants' income may be sent home as remittances [1]. Demand for single-person housing and services rises, and migrant workers may face poor living conditions [1]. Few women of this age means low numbers of births among the migrant population, so natural increase is low relative to total population [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Environmental: disease environments such as areas with malaria raise mortality [1]. Natural hazards such as floods, droughts and earthquakes cause deaths directly and through food shortages [1]. Air and water pollution increase respiratory and water-borne diseases [1]. Political: conflict causes deaths directly and destroys health services and food supplies [1]. Government spending on health care, vaccination and sanitation lowers mortality [1]. Policies on issues such as food subsidies or tobacco control also affect death rates [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** As female literacy increases, infant mortality decreases, a strong negative relationship [1], from 85 per 1000 at 35% literacy to 4 per 1000 at 98% literacy [1].

**(b)** Literate mothers can read health information and understand hygiene, nutrition and the need for vaccination [1]. Educated women tend to marry later and have fewer, more widely spaced children, so each child receives more care [1]. Higher literacy often reflects wider development, such as better health services and clean water [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Fewer births mean the base of the pyramid narrows [1]. The large cohorts born before the fall move up into working ages, so the share of working-age people rises for a time [1]. Youth dependency falls [1]. Later, these large cohorts reach old age, so the top of the pyramid widens [1] and old-age dependency rises, giving an ageing population [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the view: in farming economies, children provide labour, so large families are an economic advantage [1]. Where there are no pensions, children support parents in old age [1]. In urban economies children are costly to raise and educate, so families choose to have fewer [1]. Women's paid employment raises the opportunity cost of having children [1].

Against the view: female education strongly reduces fertility, even at similar income levels [1]. Religious and cultural norms can keep fertility high or low regardless of income [1]. Government policies, both anti-natalist and pro-natalist, can change fertility [1].

Judgement: economic factors are important, but they work alongside social factors such as education and the status of women, which often explain differences between countries at similar income levels [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the total population as the base for infant mortality.
- Forgetting that a negative natural increase rate is natural decrease.
- Adding the wrong age bands when calculating the dependency ratio.
- Describing a pyramid without quoting any percentages.
- Explaining factors without linking them clearly to higher or lower fertility or mortality.
