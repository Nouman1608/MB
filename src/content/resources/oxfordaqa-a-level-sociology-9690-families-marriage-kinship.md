Section **3.1.1.1 Families, marriage and kinship** opens the Families content of OxfordAQA
International AS/A level Sociology (9690) and is examined in Unit 1 Section A. The specification
lists five things: types of family (extended, "including vertically and horizontally extended",
nuclear and single parent); alternatives to families ("communes, living alone, and foster
care/children's homes"); Therborn's types of family system; variations between cultures
(matriarchal and patriarchal families); and types of marriage (arranged, love and monogamy). For
the unit as a whole, see the [Introducing Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/).

This section is mostly definitions and comparison, but it is not a vocabulary list. Every later
part of the Families topic, from functionalist "fit" to family diversity, depends on being able to
say precisely what kind of family or household you mean.

## Families, households and kinship

Three terms are often blurred and should be kept apart.

- **Kinship** is the network of people recognised as related by blood, marriage or adoption, and
  the rights and duties that come with that recognition (who inherits, who must be cared for, who
  may marry whom).
- A **family** is a group of people linked by kinship, though they need not live together.
- A **household** is a person or group sharing a home and usually some daily activities. A student
  flat-share is a household but not a family; a family split across two countries is a family but
  not one household.

Keeping these apart lets you answer questions about living alone or communes, which are household
arrangements outside the family rather than family types.

## Types of family

**Nuclear family.** Two generations: parents (traditionally a married couple) and their dependent
children. George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949), a comparative study of around 250
societies, claimed the nuclear family was universal. Critics point to societies and groups where
child-rearing is shared beyond the couple, which is why "alternatives to families" matter.

**Extended family.** Any family wider than the nuclear unit. The specification asks for two forms.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Vertically extended | Three or more generations, e.g. grandparents, parents and children | A grandmother living with her son, daughter-in-law and grandchildren |
| Horizontally extended | Extra members from the same generation, e.g. brothers, their wives, or cousins | Married brothers and their families sharing one compound, as in the traditional joint family of parts of South Asia |

Extended families may share a home (classic extended) or live nearby and keep frequent contact and
support (sometimes called the modified extended family). Michael Young and Peter Willmott's
*Family and Kinship in East London* (1957) found dense mother-daughter networks in Bethnal Green
before families were rehoused to new estates.

**Single-parent family.** One parent with dependent children, created by divorce, separation, death
of a partner or birth outside a couple. Most are headed by mothers in most societies, though the
reasons for single parenthood, and how it is viewed, differ widely.

## Alternatives to families

| Alternative | What it involves | Sociological point |
|---|---|---|
| **Communes** | Groups sharing property, work and often child-rearing, such as the Israeli kibbutz, where children traditionally spent much of their time in collective children's houses | Challenges Murdock's claim that the nuclear family is universal and necessary |
| **Living alone** | One-person households, often young professionals, divorced adults or widowed older people | Eric Klinenberg's *Going Solo* (2012) argues living alone can be a chosen, socially connected lifestyle, not just isolation |
| **Foster care / children's homes** | The state or agencies place children with carers or in residential institutions | Shows that child-rearing can be organised outside kinship, and raises questions about outcomes for children in care |

A useful evaluation point: alternatives often depend on family-like relationships. Kibbutz children
still knew and visited their parents; people living alone usually keep kin and friendship ties.

## Therborn's types of family system

Göran Therborn's *Between Sex and Power: Family in the World, 1900–2000* (2004) compares family
systems across world regions on three dimensions: **patriarchy** (the power of fathers and
husbands), **marriage** (who marries, when, and whether it can be ended) and **fertility**. He
identifies five major systems, broadly European (including its New World offshoots), East Asian,
sub-Saharan African, West Asian/North African and South Asian, plus two "interstitial" systems,
Southeast Asian and Creole American, which mix features of others.

His main argument is that patriarchy declined across the twentieth century in every system, but at
very different speeds and from different starting points; marriage patterns diverged; and fertility
fell almost everywhere. For exam purposes, Therborn is valuable because he:

- shows family forms are shaped by religion, law, colonial history and economy, not by
  industrialisation alone;
- gives you a framework for comparing your own society with others, which the specification
  requires in selected extended answers;
- warns against treating the Western nuclear family as the "normal" end point.

## Matriarchal and patriarchal families

A **patriarchal** family is one in which men, usually the eldest male, hold authority over decisions,
property and the behaviour of women and younger members. Descent and inheritance often pass through
the male line (**patrilineal**) and wives often move into the husband's household (**patrilocal**).

A **matriarchal** family is one in which women hold the main authority. Strictly matriarchal
societies are hard to find; most examples are **matrilineal** (descent and property pass through the
female line) rather than ruled by women. Examples sociologists use include the Khasi of Meghalaya in
north-east India, where property traditionally passes to the youngest daughter, the Minangkabau of
West Sumatra, and the Mosuo of south-west China, whose "walking marriage" lets partners live apart in
their maternal households. In several matrilineal societies, mothers' brothers still hold
significant authority, which is a strong evaluation point.

## Types of marriage

- **Monogamy**: marriage to one partner at a time. **Serial monogamy** (a sequence of marriages,
  separated by divorce) is common where divorce is easy.
- **Arranged marriage**: families play a leading role in choosing or introducing partners, often
  stressing compatibility of background, religion and status. Arranged marriage is not the same as
  **forced marriage**, which lacks the free consent of one or both partners and is a form of abuse.
  Many arranged marriages involve the couple's consent and veto ("assisted" or semi-arranged
  marriage).
- **Love marriage**: partners choose each other on the basis of romantic attraction, with families
  playing a smaller role.

The specification notes that other types, such as polygamy, may be taught where appropriate to
the local context; they are not directly assessed but are credited if used relevantly.

## Worked example: an 8-mark answer

*Explain two differences between arranged marriages and love marriages.* (practice question)

> **Who chooses.** In an arranged marriage, parents and wider kin take a leading role in choosing or
> introducing a partner, so marriage joins two families as well as two individuals. In a love
> marriage, the couple choose each other, reflecting individualisation, where personal feelings
> outweigh family strategy. However, the line is blurred: many arranged marriages give the couple a
> veto, and "love" choices are still shaped by class, religion and ethnicity.
>
> **The basis of the relationship.** Love marriage rests on romantic attraction before marriage,
> while arranged marriage often expects affection to grow afterwards and stresses compatibility of
> background and the couple's duties to both families. This can make arranged marriages more
> embedded in extended kin support, whereas love marriages depend more on the couple's emotional
> satisfaction, which some sociologists link to higher divorce risk.

Why it works: each difference is identified, explained and developed with a qualification, and
neither confuses arranged with forced marriage.

## Common mistakes

- Defining an extended family without saying whether it is **vertically** or **horizontally**
  extended when the question asks.
- Calling living alone or communes "family types": they are **alternatives to families**.
- Treating matrilineal and matriarchal as identical.
- Equating arranged marriage with forced marriage.
- Mentioning Therborn by name only; examiners reward his dimensions (patriarchy, marriage,
  fertility) and his comparative argument.
- Describing every society as moving towards the Western nuclear family.

## Exam technique

The Families section mixes short and extended questions. In this course's Marlbridge practice,
**define** questions need a precise definition plus, ideally, an example; **outline** and
**explain** questions need each point developed (why, with what effect); and **evaluate**
questions need arguments for and against, a named society and a justified conclusion. Section
3.1.1.1 often provides the examples for extended answers elsewhere: Therborn and matrilineal
societies are strong evidence against functionalist claims of a universal nuclear family.
