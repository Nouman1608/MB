## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of the place element of the marketing mix? **[1]**

A: Offering a 10% discount

B: Selling through a website with home delivery

C: Adding a new flavour

D: Posting adverts on social media

**2.** Define the term marketing mix. **[2]**

**3.** Which **two** of the following are examples of digital communication? **[2]**

A: Email newsletters

B: A printed leaflet

C: A social media page

D: A shop window display

E: A loyalty stamp card

**4.** Explain **one** reason why price is an important element of the marketing mix for a start-up. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way that e-commerce can affect the price element of the marketing mix. **[3]**

**6.** Brookside Bagels sold 1,500 bagels a week at £1.80. After changing to locally baked bread and raising the price to £2.10, it sells 1,350 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the weekly revenue before and after the change. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the number of bagels sold. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Jade has opened Glowfit, a small gym. The only other gym in town is a large, low-cost chain open 24 hours. Outline **one** way Jade could balance Glowfit's marketing mix to compete with the chain. **[2]**

**8.** Outline **one** way changing consumer needs could affect the product element of the marketing mix for a sandwich shop. **[2]**

**9.** Mill Street Books is an independent bookshop. It is considering starting a weekly email to customers about new titles and events. Analyse the impact on Mill Street Books of using digital communication in this way. **[6]**

**10.** Explain **one** reason why a change to one element of the marketing mix may require changes to other elements. **[3]**

**11.** Pebble Pottery sells handmade mugs at a craft market for £18. It spends £120 on a month of online adverts. 1,600 people view its website as a result, and 3% place an order for one mug.

**(a)** Calculate the revenue from these orders. **[2]**

**(b)** Each mug costs £7 to make and £4 to post. Calculate the profit or loss from the adverts after the cost of the adverts. **[3]**

**12.** Tilly owns Greenleaf, a start-up café. A national coffee chain has opened two doors away, charging 20% less for coffee. Tilly is considering two options.

Option 1: cut her coffee price from £3.00 to £2.40 to match the chain. She forecasts weekly coffee sales would stay at 900 cups.

Option 2: keep her price at £3.00 and spend £150 a week promoting locally roasted beans and homemade cakes. She forecasts weekly coffee sales would fall from 900 to 820 cups.

Justify which option Tilly should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The combination of price, product, promotion and place [1] that a business uses to meet the needs of its target market and persuade customers to buy [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The price must be high enough to cover the start-up's costs [1], otherwise each sale adds to losses and the business may not survive [1]. At the same time it must not be so high that new customers, who do not yet know the business, refuse to try it [1]. **[3]**

**5.** E-commerce lets customers compare prices from many sellers quickly [1]. This puts pressure on a business to keep its prices close to rivals' [1], or to justify a higher price through product quality or service, otherwise customers buy elsewhere [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Before: 1,500 × £1.80 = **£2,700** [1]. After: 1,350 × £2.10 = **£2,835** [1]

**(b)** Change = 1,350 − 1,500 = −150 [1]. −150 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **−10%** (a 10% fall) [1] **[4]**

**7.** Rather than competing on price with the low-cost chain, Glowfit could offer small classes with personal attention (product) [1], and promote this as a friendly, supported service, which justifies a higher price to customers who want more help than the chain gives [1]. **[2]**

**8.** If customers want healthier food, the sandwich shop could add wholemeal bread and lower-fat fillings [1], so it keeps health-conscious customers who might otherwise go to a rival offering healthier choices [1]. **[2]**

**9.** A weekly email is a cheap way for Mill Street Books to reach customers who already like the shop [1], so it can promote new titles and events without paying for printed adverts [1]. Regular reminders could bring customers back more often, increasing sales [1]. The shop can also see who opens the emails and which titles they click on, so it can stock more of what customers want [1]. However, writing a weekly email takes the owner's time [1], and if customers find it too frequent they may unsubscribe, reducing its effect [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The four elements must be consistent with each other [1]. For example, if a business raises its price, customers expect a better product or service [1], so it may need to improve quality and change promotion to explain why the product is worth more, or customers will see it as poor value [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Orders = 3% × 1,600 = 48 [1]. Revenue = 48 × £18 = **£864** [1]

**(b)** Costs per mug = £7 + £4 = £11; total = 48 × £11 = £528 [1]. £864 − £528 = £336 [1]. £336 − £120 = **£216 profit** [1] **[5]**

**12.** Option 1 revenue = 900 × £2.40 = £2,160 a week [1]. Option 2 revenue = 820 × £3.00 = £2,460, minus £150 promotion = £2,310 [1]. Option 2 leaves £150 more each week before the cost of making the coffee, and as it sells 80 fewer cups, its ingredient costs would also be lower [1].

For Option 1: matching the chain's price removes a reason for customers to switch [1], and it protects the number of customers, who may also buy cakes [1].

For Option 2: Greenleaf cannot easily win a price war with a national chain, which can probably afford lower prices for longer [1], whereas promoting locally roasted beans and homemade cakes gives customers a reason to choose Greenleaf that the chain cannot copy easily, keeping the mix balanced [1].

Judgement: Tilly should choose Option 2, because it is forecast to bring in more money after the promotion cost and avoids a price war she is likely to lose [1]. It depends on whether sales really fall only to 820; if more customers switch to the cheaper chain, Option 2's advantage could disappear, so she should track weekly sales and adjust the promotion [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* A Justify answer compares both options with figures from the source. Noting that the chain could keep cutting prices shows understanding of the competitive environment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling e-commerce a form of promotion rather than a change to place.
- Giving a generic answer to Question 7 that does not use the chain's low price or long opening hours.
- Treating revenue from a promotion as profit, as tested in Question 11.
- Recommending a price cut without considering a rival's ability to cut further.
