**This guide covers one element AQA lists for Option 2B Elements of Political and Social Protest
Writing:** "gender politics and issues of social class". The
[Elements of Political and Social Protest Writing study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-2b-elements-of-political-and-social-protest-writing-7717/)
gives one example of each from four texts; this page explains how the two ideas work as systems of
power, how they intersect, and how to write about them without reducing a text to a message.

## Gender politics and social class as systems

AQA's option is about "issues of power and powerlessness". Gender and class are two of the most
common ways power is organised in the set texts. Treat each as a **system**, not a theme:

- **Who is given power** by the system, and how is it justified (nature, law, God, money, tradition)?
- **How is the system maintained**: by violence, by law, by language, by the cooperation of the
  powerless?
- **What does resistance look like**, and what does it cost?
- **Where does the text place the reader**: inside the system, outside it, or moving between?

"Politics" in "gender politics" means relations of power, not parties. A marriage, a household or a
classroom can be a political space.

## Gender politics in the set texts

**A Doll's House.** Nora has secretly borrowed money to save her husband's life, forging her
father's signature because the law did not let a wife borrow without her husband's consent. The
secret shows both her capability and the legal system that denies it. Torvald's pet names for her,
and his pleasure in instructing her as she rehearses the tarantella, present a marriage built on
performance. Mrs Linde, a widow who has worked to support her family, offers a contrast: a woman who
has known independence and chooses partnership. The final scene turns the living room into a
political space, as Nora claims duties to herself and leaves.

**The Handmaid's Tale.** Gilead organises women into ranks marked by colour: Wives in blue, Handmaids
in red, Marthas in green, Aunts in brown, Econowives in stripes. Gender politics and class are
fused: a woman's function decides her status. Women are forbidden to read, and the Handmaids lose
their names, so "Offred" names her as belonging to the Commander. Atwood also shows the regime
controlling men, such as Guardians and Nick, so the novel asks whether patriarchy serves anyone
fully.

**Henry IV Part I.** Politics is a male world of rebellion and war. Lady Percy's scene with Hotspur
shows her shut out of his plans, and Lady Mortimer speaks only Welsh, so she literally cannot take
part in the English conversation of power. The marginal women are a quiet comment on whose voices the
history play records.

**The Kite Runner.** Hosseini shows gender politics in the Afghan community in California: Soraya's
past relationship is treated as a lasting shame, while men's pasts are not judged in the same way.
Amir's own guilt over Hassan gives the novel a male narrator whose double standard the reader can
test.

## Social class in the set texts

**Hard Times.** Coketown is divided between masters and "Hands", a word that reduces workers to body
parts. Stephen Blackpool, trapped in a marriage he cannot afford to end, is told by Bounderby that
the law offers release only to those who can pay; the scene exposes a legal system with one rule for
the rich. Bounderby's boast of being a self-made man is later exposed as a lie when his mother,
Mrs Pegler, appears, so the myth that justifies class power is shown to be false.

**Songs of Innocence and of Experience.** Blake gives voice to the poor of London. In "London", the
chimney-sweeper's cry and the soldier's sigh are linked to the Church and the palace, so suffering is
traced to institutions. The paired "Chimney Sweeper" poems show the same child exploited by a
system that tells him to accept his duty.

**Tony Harrison's Selected Poems.** "Them and [uz]" turns accent into a class battle: the poet,
corrected as a schoolboy for his northern speech, claims the right to write poetry in his own voice.
"Marked with D" mourns his father, a baker, in images of the oven, so a working life is given
elegiac dignity. "V", written during the miners' strike of the mid-1980s and set in a Leeds
cemetery, stages a debate between the educated poet and a skinhead who might be his other self.

**Harvest.** Crace's narrator, Walter Thirsk, watches a village lose its common land as the new owner
plans to turn fields over to sheep. Enclosure shows class as ownership: those without land lose not
only work but a way of life.

**The Kite Runner.** Amir is a wealthy Pashtun and Hassan a Hazara servant. Class and ethnicity are
bound together, and Amir's betrayal of Hassan is made possible by the hierarchy both boys grow up in.

## Where gender and class meet

The strongest answers notice that the two systems interact.

- In *Hard Times*, Louisa's marriage to Bounderby is arranged by her father on the basis of "facts",
  so a woman is exchanged within the middle-class world of money; Stephen's marriage trap shows the
  same institution from below.
- In *The Handmaid's Tale*, Wives and Econowives are both women, but their class separates them, so
  gender alone does not create solidarity.
- In *A Doll's House*, Mrs Linde and the nursemaid who gave up her own child to work show that a
  woman's choices depend on money as well as law.
- In Blake's "London", the "youthful Harlot" is a figure of both gender and class exploitation.

## Worked example: a set-text paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *A Doll's House*, Nora is oppressed less by Torvald than by the
society he represents." Discuss.

> Ibsen makes Torvald the visible face of power, but the play's structure shows that his authority
> rests on laws and expectations larger than him. Nora's secret debt exists only because the law did
> not allow a wife to borrow without her husband's consent, so her forgery is a crime created by the
> system. Krogstad's threat to expose her is powerful because Torvald's position at the bank and his
> idea of respectability depend on reputation, not on love. When the truth comes out, Torvald's first
> concern is for his own name, and the speed of his forgiveness once the danger passes shows that his
> love was conditional on social safety. Yet Torvald is not merely a mouthpiece. His pet names and
> his delight in directing Nora's dance are personal choices, and Nora's final judgement is addressed
> to him as an individual as well as to the institution of marriage. The play suggests that society
> creates the cage, but Torvald enjoys keeping the key.

**Why it works.** It ties context (nineteenth-century marriage law) to a specific plot point (AO3),
analyses structure and character (AO2), and weighs both sides of the view (AO5).

## Common mistakes

- **Reducing the text to a message** ("Ibsen is saying women should be free"). Analyse how power is
  constructed and resisted.
- **Treating gender or class as background** rather than as systems the text examines.
- **Ignoring men** in gender politics and the middle class in class politics.
- **Generalised context.** Tie history to specific scenes and words.
- **Missing intersections** between gender and class.

## Exam technique

When a question names gender or class, open by defining the system in the text and who benefits from
it. In Section C, pair texts that show the same system from different positions: a wife and a
worker, an insider and an outsider. Use the open book to find short, exact quotations.
