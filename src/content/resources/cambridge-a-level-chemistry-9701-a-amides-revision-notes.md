**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level** subtopic
34.3: production from acyl chlorides, hydrolysis, LiAlH₄ reduction, and "why amides are much weaker
bases than amines".

## Naming in three lines

- RCONH₂ is a **primary amide**: ethanamide, propanamide, benzamide.
- RCONHR′ is **N-substituted**: CH₃CONHCH₃ is N-methylethanamide.
- RCONR′₂ is **N,N-disubstituted**: CH₃CON(CH₃)₂ is N,N-dimethylethanamide.

## Production (room temperature)

    RCOCl + NH₃    → RCONH₂  + HCl
    RCOCl + R′NH₂  → RCONHR′ + HCl

| Target | Acyl chloride | Nitrogen reagent |
|---|---|---|
| ethanamide | ethanoyl chloride | ammonia |
| N-methylpropanamide | propanoyl chloride | methylamine |
| N-phenylethanamide | ethanoyl chloride | phenylamine |
| benzamide | benzoyl chloride | ammonia |

**Disconnection:** break the **C–N** bond; carbonyl side + Cl = acyl chloride; N side + H = ammonia
or amine.

## Hydrolysis: heat under reflux

| Amide | Aqueous acid (HCl) gives | Aqueous alkali (NaOH) gives |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃CONH₂ | CH₃COOH + NH₄Cl | CH₃COONa + NH₃ |
| CH₃CONHCH₃ | CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻ | CH₃COONa + CH₃NH₂ |
| C₆H₅CONH₂ | C₆H₅COOH + NH₄Cl | C₆H₅COONa + NH₃ |

Rule: **acid protonates the nitrogen product; alkali deprotonates the acid product.**

    CH₃CONHCH₃ + H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻
    CH₃CONHCH₃ + NaOH       → CH₃COONa + CH₃NH₂

**Test:** warm a primary amide with NaOH(aq): NH₃ released turns **damp red litmus blue**.

## Reduction with LiAlH₄

- **C=O → CH₂**; skeleton and N substituents kept.
- RCONH₂ + 4[H] → RCH₂NH₂ + H₂O (**primary amine**)
- RCONHR′ + 4[H] → RCH₂NHR′ + H₂O (**secondary amine**)
- Hydrolysis **splits** the molecule; reduction **does not**.

## Basicity: amides ≪ amines

- Amide N lone pair is **delocalised into C=O** (over N, C and O).
- Electronegative O **withdraws** electron density from N.
- So the lone pair is **much less available** to accept H⁺.
- C–N has partial double-bond character; the amide group is planar.
- Amides: about neutral in water. Amines: alkaline.

## Comparing three related reactions of the C=O group

| Starting compound | Reagent | Product | Bond that changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| acyl chloride | NH₃, room temperature | amide | C–Cl replaced by C–N |
| amide | NaOH(aq), heat | carboxylate + NH₃ | C–N broken |
| amide | LiAlH₄ | amine | C=O becomes CH₂ |

## Identifying an unknown amide in four steps

1. **Hydrolyse** (usually NaOH(aq), heat). Note the gas: ammonia (primary amide) or an amine
   (N-substituted amide). Both turn damp red litmus blue.
2. **Acidify** the residue to release the carboxylic acid and identify it.
3. **Count carbons:** acid carbons + carbons on N = carbons in the amide. Check against the molecular
   formula.
4. **Rebuild** the amide by joining the acyl group to the nitrogen part: RCO– + –NHR′.

Example: C₄H₉NO gives butanoic acid and ammonia, so it is butanamide, CH₃CH₂CH₂CONH₂; if it gave
propanoic acid and methylamine instead, it would be N-methylpropanamide, CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃.

## Where amides appear elsewhere in 9701

- **Amino acids (34.4):** the peptide bond, –CONH–, is an amide link; peptides hydrolyse to amino
  acids just as amides hydrolyse to acids and amines.
- **Polyamides (35.1 and 35.3):** nylons and proteins are chains of amide links; their acidic and
  alkaline hydrolysis follows the same rule as for small amides.
- **Synthesis (36.1):** acyl chloride → amide → LiAlH₄ is a clean route to a single amine.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| amide | compound containing –CONH₂, –CONHR or –CONR₂ |
| primary amide | RCONH₂ |
| N-substituted amide | amide with a carbon group on N, RCONHR′ |
| hydrolysis | splitting a bond by reaction with water, here catalysed or driven by acid or alkali |
| reduction | here, conversion of C=O to CH₂ by LiAlH₄ |
| delocalisation | spreading of electrons over more than two atoms |
| reflux | heating with a condenser so that volatile substances return to the flask |

## Exam traps

- Hydrolysis needs **heat**; amide formation from acyl chlorides is at **room temperature**.
- Acid hydrolysis gives the **salt** of the amine (NH₄⁺ or RNH₃⁺), not the free amine.
- Alkaline hydrolysis gives the **carboxylate salt**, not the acid.
- LiAlH₄ on an N-substituted amide gives a **secondary** amine.
- "Amides have no lone pair" is wrong; the lone pair is **delocalised**.
- Propanamide and N-methylethanamide are **isomers** with different products.

## Self-test

1. Name CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃.
2. Give the reagents to make N-ethylethanamide.
3. Give the conditions for amide hydrolysis.
4. Give the products of heating propanamide with HCl(aq).
5. Give the products of heating N-methylethanamide with NaOH(aq).
6. What is seen when damp red litmus is held over warm ethanamide and NaOH(aq)?
7. Give the product of reducing propanamide with LiAlH₄.
8. Give the product of reducing N-ethylethanamide with LiAlH₄ and classify it.
9. Why is ethanamide a much weaker base than ethylamine?
10. Benzamide is hydrolysed in acid. Name the organic product.
11. Give one structural consequence of the delocalisation in an amide.
12. An amide gives butanoic acid and methylamine on hydrolysis. Give its structure.

**Answers:**

1. N-methylpropanamide.
2. Ethanoyl chloride and ethylamine, room temperature.
3. Heat under reflux with aqueous acid or aqueous alkali.
4. Propanoic acid and ammonium chloride.
5. Sodium ethanoate and methylamine.
6. The litmus turns blue (ammonia released).
7. Propylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂.
8. Diethylamine, (CH₃CH₂)₂NH; secondary amine.
9. Its N lone pair is delocalised into C=O and O withdraws electron density, so the lone pair is much
   less available to accept H⁺; in ethylamine the lone pair is localised and the ethyl group donates
   electron density.
10. Benzoic acid (with ammonium chloride).
11. The C–N bond has partial double-bond character (shorter, restricted rotation), and the N–C=O
    unit is planar.
12. CH₃CH₂CH₂CONHCH₃ (N-methylbutanamide).
