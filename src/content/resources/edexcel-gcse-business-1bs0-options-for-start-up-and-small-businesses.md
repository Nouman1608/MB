This guide covers content item 1.4.1, The options for start-up and small businesses, in Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It sits in Theme 1, Investigating small business, which is examined in Paper 1. The broader [guide to limited liability and business location](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-limited-liability-and-business-location/) introduces each form of ownership. This page goes further on one skill the examiners reward: choosing the right option for a particular owner and backing the choice with reasons. Every business and figure below is invented.

## What 1.4.1 contains

The specification groups this item under three headings.

| Heading in the specification | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The concept of limited liability | Explain limited and unlimited liability, and the implications of each for the business owner or owners |
| The types of business ownership for start-ups | Compare sole trader, partnership and private limited company, with the advantages and disadvantages of each |
| The option of starting up and running a franchise operation | Weigh the advantages and disadvantages of franchising |

Public limited companies are not part of 1.4.1. They appear in Theme 2 (2.1.1) as an option for growing businesses, so keep them out of start-up answers unless a question asks you to compare.

## Liability is about the owner, not the business

Liability decides whose money pays the debts when a business cannot. The key is to separate the business from the person.

- A sole trader and the partners in an ordinary partnership are not legally separate from their businesses. Their liability is **unlimited**: a debt of the business is a debt of the owner.
- A private limited company is a separate legal body. The shareholders' liability is **limited** to the money they invested in shares.

Three implications follow, and each makes a good expansion point in an Explain answer.

1. **Personal risk.** With unlimited liability, a failed business can cost the owner savings, a car or a house. With limited liability, the owner's loss stops at the value of their shares.
2. **Willingness to take risks.** An owner with unlimited liability may avoid borrowing or expanding, even when the idea is sound. Limited liability encourages enterprise because failure is less catastrophic for the owner.
3. **Attracting investors.** People are more willing to buy shares when they know they cannot lose more than they put in, so a limited company can usually raise more capital.

A trap to avoid: limited liability does not stop the business losing money. The company can still fail. It is the owner's personal loss that is capped.

## Matching the form of ownership to the owner

Examiners rarely ask "What is a sole trader?" on its own. More often a source describes an owner and asks which option suits them. Use four questions to decide.

| Question to ask about the owner | Points towards |
|---|---|
| Is the owner risking a large sum or borrowing heavily? | Private limited company, for the protection of limited liability |
| Does the owner want complete control and to keep every pound of profit? | Sole trader |
| Does the owner lack a skill or capital that another person could bring? | Partnership, or a company with a second shareholder |
| Does the owner want a ready-made idea with a known name and support? | Franchise |

### Worked example: Tamsin's mobile bike repair

Tamsin plans to repair bikes from a van. She has £6,000 of savings, needs no loan and will work alone.

- **Sole trader** fits well. Start-up is simple, she keeps all the profit and the risk is small because she is not borrowing. Unlimited liability matters less when the possible debts are small.
- **Partnership** offers little. She has the skills already, so sharing profit with a partner gives her nothing she needs.
- **Private limited company** brings protection she barely needs, plus extra paperwork and less privacy.

Now change one fact: Tamsin wants a workshop with a £45,000 bank loan and two employees. The possible debts are now far larger than her savings, so the case for a private limited company becomes much stronger. The best option depends on the facts in the source, and that is exactly what an Analyse or Justify answer should show.

## Partnerships: shared skills, shared risk

A partnership brings in more capital and a wider mix of skills than one person can offer, and the workload and decisions are shared. The less obvious disadvantage is that, in an ordinary partnership, each partner can be responsible for debts that another partner ran up for the business. Disagreements over how hard each partner works, or what to do with profit, are a common reason partnerships end. Many partners therefore write a partnership agreement covering how profit is shared and what happens if one partner wants to leave. Mentioning that agreement shows you understand how owners reduce the risks of this option.

## Private limited companies: protection with conditions

A private limited company protects the owners' personal wealth and can raise capital by selling shares to people the owners choose, such as family members or a business contact. It also continues if one shareholder leaves, because the company exists separately from its owners.

The costs of that protection are real for a small start-up: more formal set-up and record keeping, some company information becoming publicly available, and profit and control shared among shareholders. Selling a large share of the company to raise capital may mean the founder no longer has the final say. In general, a private limited company cannot offer its shares to the general public, which limits how much it can raise.

## Franchising: buying a proven model

In a franchise, the **franchisor** owns the brand and the way of running the business, and the **franchisee** pays to run an outlet using them. Always say whose side you are writing from.

| | Franchisee (the person starting up) | Franchisor (the brand owner) |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Known brand attracts customers from day one; a tested format lowers the chance of failure; training, supplies and national advertising may be provided | The brand expands using franchisees' money; receives fees and royalties; franchisees are motivated because their own capital is at risk |
| Disadvantages | Initial fee and ongoing payments reduce profit; little freedom over products, prices or decor; a problem elsewhere in the brand can damage local sales | Less direct control over quality; one poor franchisee can harm the whole brand's reputation; shares the revenue from each outlet |

### Worked example: the cost of a franchise

Dev compares opening an independent sandwich shop with buying a sandwich franchise. The franchise has an initial fee of £18,000 and a royalty of 6% of sales revenue. He forecasts sales revenue of £150,000 a year with the franchise, and profit before the royalty of £27,000.

1. Royalty = 6% × £150,000 = £9,000
2. Profit after royalty = £27,000 − £9,000 = £18,000 a year
3. The fee is covered by one year's profit after royalty: £18,000 ÷ £18,000 = 1 year

As an independent, Dev forecasts sales revenue of only £110,000 and a profit of £16,000, with no fee or royalty. The franchise gives £2,000 more profit a year once the fee is paid off, but only if the brand really does lift his sales to the forecast level. In an answer, point out that the comparison rests on forecasts.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse style):** Analyse the impact on Priya of running her new dog-grooming salon as a private limited company. [6]

> Priya will have limited liability, so if the salon fails her loss is limited to the money she put into shares. *(Knowledge.)* This matters because she is taking out a loan to fit out the salon, so without protection her savings could be used to repay it. *(Applied to the context.)* As a result she may feel more confident about borrowing to buy better equipment, which could help the salon grow. *(Developed chain.)* However, she will face more paperwork than a sole trader, which takes time away from grooming. *(Counter-point.)* If she sells shares to her brother to raise extra capital, she will have to share profit and some control, which could slow decisions. *(Second chain, in context.)*

Why it works: every point is tied to Priya's loan, salon or brother, and each point is followed by "so" or "as a result" to show the consequence.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a partnership has limited liability. An ordinary partnership does not.
- Treating a public limited company as a start-up option.
- Listing franchise advantages without stating whether they apply to the franchisor or the franchisee.
- Saying limited liability means the business "cannot go bust".
- Recommending an option without using the owner's situation from the source.

## Exam technique

- **Define** (for example, unlimited liability): give the meaning in one precise sentence.
- **Explain**: one fact, then two expansion points that build on it. There is no context to apply.
- **Outline** and **Analyse**: use the business in the question in every point.
- **Justify**: you will be given two options, for example sole trader or franchise. Recommend one, compare it with the other using the information provided, and say what the recommendation depends on.
