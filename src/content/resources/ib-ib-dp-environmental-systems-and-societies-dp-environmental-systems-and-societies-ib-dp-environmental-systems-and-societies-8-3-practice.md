This set covers subtopic 8.3, Urban air pollution, from Topic 8 of IB DP Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. Every question suits SL and HL. All monitoring data are fictional; take the WHO 2021 annual guideline for PM2.5 as 5 µg/m³. Learn the content first in the [8.3 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-8-3/) and the [8.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-8-3-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between primary and secondary pollutants, giving **one** example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Outline why fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a particular concern for human health. **[2]**

**3.** Table 1 shows hourly readings at a fictional city-centre monitor on a hot, sunny, still day.

| Time | 05:00 | 07:00 | 09:00 | 11:00 | 13:00 | 15:00 | 17:00 | 19:00 | 21:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NO₂ (µg/m³) | 25 | 70 | 90 | 60 | 45 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 50 |
| Ozone (µg/m³) | 15 | 20 | 45 | 95 | 130 | 125 | 85 | 45 | 25 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern of NO₂ concentration. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the timing of the ozone peak. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why ozone falls after 15:00. **[2]**

**4.** The annual mean PM2.5 in a fictional city fell from 48 µg/m³ in 2015 to 30 µg/m³ in 2025.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times the WHO guideline the 2025 value is. **[1]**
**(c)** Comment on whether the city's air quality policy has been successful. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a thermal inversion forms and how it affects urban air quality. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why cities in basins surrounded by mountains are prone to air pollution episodes. **[2]**

**7.** Compare the London smog of December 1952 with photochemical smog. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how emissions from a city can cause environmental damage in a different region. **[3]**

**9.** Outline the causes of indoor air pollution in homes that cook with solid fuels, and identify who is most affected. **[3]**

**10.** A fictional city introduced a low-emission zone. Annual mean NO₂ inside the zone fell from 60 to 42 µg/m³. On the ring road just outside it rose from 45 to 50 µg/m³.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change at each location. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate the low-emission zone using these data. **[4]**

**11.** Outline how a catalytic converter reduces vehicle pollution, and state **one** limitation. **[3]**

**12.** To what extent can urban air pollution be solved by technology alone? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A primary pollutant is released directly from a source, such as SO₂ or carbon monoxide [1]; a secondary pollutant forms when primary pollutants react in the atmosphere, such as tropospheric ozone or PANs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Ozone given as a primary pollutant loses the second mark.

**2.** Its particles are so small that they penetrate deep into the lungs and can pass into the bloodstream [1], where they are linked to heart and lung diseases such as asthma and heart attacks [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "It is harmful" needs the reason: particle size and depth of penetration.

**3. (a)** Two peaks, at 09:00 (90 µg/m³) and 19:00 (80 µg/m³) [1], with a low of 40 µg/m³ at 15:00 between them [1].
**(b)** Ozone peaks at 13:00 (130 µg/m³), about four hours after the NO₂ peak [1]. Ozone is a secondary pollutant formed when sunlight splits NO₂, releasing oxygen atoms that join O₂ [1]. Sunlight is strongest around midday, so formation is fastest then, using up NO₂, which falls as ozone rises [1].
**(c)** Sunlight weakens in the late afternoon, so less ozone forms [1], and NO from evening traffic reacts with ozone, removing it (NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂), which also explains the second NO₂ peak [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), quote times and values from the table; "it goes up and down" earns nothing.

**4. (a)** (30 − 48) ÷ 48 × 100 [1] = **−37.5%** [1]
**(b)** 30 ÷ 5 = **6 times** [1]
**(c)** Partly successful: PM2.5 fell by over a third [1], but the 2025 level is still six times the WHO guideline, so health risks remain high and further action is needed [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided "yes, it fell" misses the guideline comparison that the data invite.

**5.** Normally air temperature falls with height, so warm air near the ground rises and carries pollutants away [1]. On calm, clear nights the ground loses heat quickly and cools the air above it, or warm air moves over cool air, so a layer of warm air lies above cooler air [1]. The cool, dense air cannot rise through the warm layer [1], so pollutants emitted near the ground are trapped and concentrations build up, sometimes for days [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the reason the air cannot rise, not just "the pollution is trapped".

**6.** Cool, dense air drains down the slopes into the basin at night, so inversions form often [1], and the mountains block winds that would otherwise disperse pollutants, so emissions accumulate [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming Los Angeles or Mexico City is creditworthy but does not replace the explanation.

**7.** Similarity: both were made worse by still air and an inversion that trapped pollutants [1]. Differences: the 1952 smog came from coal smoke and SO₂ in cold, damp winter weather [1], while photochemical smog forms from vehicle NOx and VOCs reacting in strong sunlight, producing ozone and PANs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs at least one similarity.

**8.** SO₂ and NOx from power stations, industry and traffic are carried downwind [1]. In the atmosphere they are oxidised and dissolve in water to form sulfuric and nitric acids [1], which fall as wet or dry deposition in other regions, acidifying lakes and soils and damaging forests and buildings, so the damage is transboundary [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The final mark needs an effect at the receiving area.

**9.** Burning wood, dung, charcoal, coal or kerosene on open fires or simple stoves [1] in poorly ventilated rooms produces high concentrations of particulates and carbon monoxide indoors [1]. Women and young children are most affected because they spend most time near the cooking fire [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Both the fuel and the lack of ventilation are needed for the causes.

**10. (a)** Inside: (42 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = **−30%** [1]; ring road: (50 − 45) ÷ 45 × 100 = **+11.1%** [1]
**(b)** The zone cut NO₂ strongly where it applies, by 30%, improving air for people inside it [1]. However, NO₂ rose by 11.1% outside, suggesting traffic was displaced to the ring road rather than removed [1]. Even inside, 42 µg/m³ is well above the WHO annual NO₂ guideline of 10 µg/m³ [1]. A judgement: the zone works locally but needs wider measures, such as better public transport or a larger zone, and one year's data could also reflect weather [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers use both locations and finish with a conditional judgement.

**11.** It contains catalysts that convert carbon monoxide to CO₂, nitrogen oxides to nitrogen, and unburned hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water [1], reducing harmful exhaust emissions [1]. Limitation, any one: it works poorly until the engine warms up, so short trips gain little; it does not reduce CO₂; it does not stop brake and tyre particles [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It filters the exhaust" is incorrect; it converts gases chemically.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- Technology has cut emissions per vehicle and per power station: catalytic converters, particulate filters and flue-gas scrubbers [1].
- Switching to renewable electricity and electric vehicles removes much exhaust pollution at street level [1].
- Monitoring and alerts help reduce exposure, though not emissions [1].
- Limitation: growth in the number of vehicles can cancel gains per vehicle [1].
- Limitation: electric vehicles still produce brake and tyre particles, and their benefit depends on the electricity mix [1].
- Limitation: weather and relief, such as inversions in basin cities, mean even low emissions can build up [1].
- Changes in human activity are also needed: compact planning, public transport, congestion charges and cleaner household fuels [1].
- Historical evidence: the Clean Air Act 1956 combined new rules with fuel switching, showing law and behaviour change alongside technology [1].
- Judgement: technology is necessary but not sufficient; lasting improvement needs planning, regulation and behaviour change together [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" needs a clear judgement and evidence from named examples or data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing data without quoting times and values.
- Stating that ozone peaks at rush hour.
- Inversion explanations that never say why the air cannot rise.
- Confusing the 1952 smog with photochemical smog.
- Evaluations that ignore displacement or the guideline benchmark.
- Calling a catalytic converter a filter.
