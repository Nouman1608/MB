This guide teaches section **3.1.2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 1, and every later calculation in the course uses it. This page goes further into the parts of the specification students find hardest: what "the mole as applied to electrons, atoms, molecules, ions, formulas and equations" means in practice, finding the mass of a single particle, solution problems with more than one step, and reporting answers to the right precision. The skills column adds working in standard and ordinary form, and reporting results only to the limits of the least accurate measurement.

## Three relationships, one idea

```
n = m ÷ Mᵣ                  (m in g)
n = c × V                   (c in mol dm⁻³, V in dm³)
N = n × L                   (N = number of particles, L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)
```

The amount in moles, n, is the bridge. Any problem that starts with a mass, a solution or a count of particles and ends with another of them passes through n.

## "Which particle?": the mole applied to different entities

A number of moles is meaningless until you say *of what*. One mole of water molecules contains one mole of oxygen atoms, two moles of hydrogen atoms, three moles of atoms in total, and ten moles of electrons (8 from O and 1 from each H).

### Method in steps

1. Find the amount of the substance named in the data (molecules or formula units).
2. Multiply by the number of the required particle in **one** formula unit.
3. Multiply by L only if a number of particles, not an amount, is asked for.

### Worked example 1: atoms inside a molecule

How many hydrogen atoms are in 4.90 g of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ (Mᵣ = 98.1)?

```
n(H₂SO₄)       = 4.90 ÷ 98.1 = 0.04995 mol
n(H atoms)     = 2 × 0.04995 = 0.09990 mol
number of H    = 0.09990 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² (3 s.f.)
```

### Worked example 2: electrons

How many electrons are in 0.135 g of aluminium ions, Al³⁺? (Aᵣ Al = 27.0)

```
n(Al³⁺)        = 0.135 ÷ 27.0 = 0.00500 mol
electrons/ion  = 13 − 3 = 10
n(electrons)   = 10 × 0.00500 = 0.0500 mol
number         = 0.0500 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²²
```

The mass of the ion is taken as the mass of the atom: removing electrons makes no measurable difference.

### The mole applied to equations

The coefficients in a balanced equation are amounts in moles. In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, two moles of hydrogen molecules react with one mole of oxygen molecules to give two moles of water molecules. Read the equation in molecules too: 2 molecules with 1 molecule gives 2 molecules. Using these ratios to find reacting masses and volumes is 3.1.2.5; here you need only to read them correctly.

## The mass of one particle

Dividing the mass of one mole by the number of particles in it gives the mass of a single particle.

```
mass of one particle (g) = Mᵣ ÷ L
```

### Worked example 3

The mass of one molecule of buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀ (Mᵣ = 720.0):

```
720.0 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.196 × 10⁻²¹ g
```

If a question wants the mass in kg, divide by a further 1000. This is the step used in time-of-flight calculations in 3.1.1.2.

## Going backwards from a number of particles

### Worked example 4

A sample of iron contains 1.505 × 10²³ atoms. Find its mass. (Aᵣ Fe = 55.8)

```
n = 1.505 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 0.2499 mol
m = 0.2499 × 55.8 = 13.9 g
```

## Solutions with more than one step

Concentration is the amount of solute in each dm³ of solution. Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.

### Worked example 5: concentration of an ion

8.71 g of potassium sulfate, K₂SO₄ (Mᵣ = 174.3), is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Find the concentration of potassium ions.

```
n(K₂SO₄)  = 8.71 ÷ 174.3 = 0.04997 mol
c(K₂SO₄)  = 0.04997 ÷ 0.500 = 0.09994 mol dm⁻³
c(K⁺)     = 2 × 0.09994 = 0.200 mol dm⁻³
```

Each formula unit releases two K⁺ ions, so the ion concentration is double the salt concentration.

### Worked example 6: mixing two solutions

100 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ MgCl₂ is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaCl. Find the concentration of chloride ions in the mixture.

```
n(Cl⁻) from MgCl₂ = 2 × 0.200 × 0.100  = 0.0400 mol
n(Cl⁻) from NaCl  = 0.100 × 0.0500     = 0.00500 mol
total n(Cl⁻)                           = 0.0450 mol
total volume                           = 0.150 dm³
c(Cl⁻) = 0.0450 ÷ 0.150 = 0.300 mol dm⁻³
```

Add amounts, not concentrations. Averaging 0.400 and 0.100 gives a wrong answer because the volumes differ.

## Significant figures and standard form

- Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the **least precise** piece of data. A mass of 4.90 g (3 s.f.) and an Mᵣ of 98.1 (3 s.f.) support 3 s.f.
- Carry at least one extra figure through intermediate steps and round once at the end. In Worked example 4, rounding 0.2499 mol to 0.25 mol early gives 14.0 g instead of 13.9 g.
- In standard form, the significant figures are in the number before the "× 10". 6.02 × 10²² is 3 s.f.; 6.0 × 10²² is 2 s.f.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at moles of molecules when atoms, ions or electrons are asked for.
- Counting electrons in an ion as the proton number, ignoring the charge.
- Multiplying by L when the question asks for an amount in moles.
- Using cm³ directly in c × V.
- Averaging concentrations when mixing solutions.
- Writing too many significant figures, or rounding mid-calculation.
- Writing the unit of L as "mol" instead of mol⁻¹.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate**: write each quantity with its symbol and unit, then substitute. Label which particle each amount refers to: "n(H atoms)", not just "n".
- **Give your answer to an appropriate number of significant figures**: identify the least precise data value and say so if asked to justify.
- **Show that**: give one more significant figure than the value you are shown, so the examiner can see you calculated it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to remember the Avogadro constant?**
No. The specification says you will not be expected to recall it, so the value is given in any question that needs it.

**How many electrons are in one mole of a molecule?**
Add up the electrons in one molecule and multiply by one mole. Water has 10 electrons per molecule, so one mole of water contains ten moles of electrons.

**Why do you add moles, not concentrations, when mixing solutions?**
Concentration depends on volume. Adding the amounts of the ion and dividing by the total volume gives the correct concentration, whatever volumes were mixed.
