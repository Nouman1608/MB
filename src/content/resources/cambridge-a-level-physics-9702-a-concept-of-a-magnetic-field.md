This guide covers subtopic **20.1 Concept of a magnetic field** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Magnetic Fields study guide](/resources/a-physics-magnetic-fields/) introduces the whole of Topic 20 and defines a magnetic field in a sentence; this page goes further into the two 20.1 outcomes: what makes a magnetic field a field of force, how it compares with gravitational and electric fields, what moves and what does not feel it, and the rules for drawing field lines that earn marks. For quick recall use the [Concept of a magnetic field revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-concept-of-a-magnetic-field-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Concept of a magnetic field practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-concept-of-a-magnetic-field-practice/).

## What 20.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists two outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- **understand that a magnetic field is an example of a field of force produced either by moving charges or by permanent magnets**
- **represent a magnetic field by field lines**

These outcomes are rarely worth many marks on their own, but every later part of Topic 20 depends on them. A field-line sketch is often the first part of a longer question about forces or induction, and a poor sketch can make the rest of the answer hard to follow.

## A field of force

A **field of force** is a region of space in which a particular kind of object experiences a force without contact. By A Level you have met three:

| Field | Produced by | Acts on | Defined strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational (Topic 13) | mass | mass | g, force per unit mass (N kg⁻¹) |
| Electric (Topics 10 and 18) | charge | charge (moving or stationary) | E, force per unit charge (N C⁻¹) |
| Magnetic (Topic 20) | moving charge, including currents; permanent magnets | moving charge, including currents; magnetic materials and magnets | B, magnetic flux density (T), defined in 20.2 |

The key difference is the role of **motion**. A stationary charge in a magnetic field experiences no magnetic force. A charge must be moving (and, as 20.3 shows, moving with a component of velocity at right angles to the field) for a magnetic force to act. In the same way, a magnetic field is only produced by charges in motion: a stationary charged sphere produces an electric field but no magnetic field.

## The two sources

**Moving charges.** Any flow of charge produces a magnetic field around it: a current in a wire, a beam of electrons in a vacuum tube, ions moving through a solution, or charged particles streaming through space. A larger current produces a stronger field. The patterns for wires, coils and solenoids are the subject of 20.4.

**Permanent magnets.** A bar magnet, horseshoe magnet or magnetised needle produces a field with no current in any external wire. At the atomic scale, this magnetism also comes from the electrons in the material's atoms, but you do not need that detail for 9702: the syllabus simply names permanent magnets as the second source.

A useful link: because both sources make the same kind of field, a coil carrying a current behaves like a magnet. A current-carrying solenoid has a north end and a south end and attracts or repels a bar magnet just as another bar magnet would.

## Field lines: what they show

Field lines are a model. They are not physical threads, and particles do not travel along them. They encode two pieces of information:

1. **Direction.** The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the tangent to the line, with the arrow pointing the way the **north pole of a small compass** would point there (the direction of the force on an isolated north pole).
2. **Strength.** The closer together the lines, the stronger the field (the greater the flux density). Widely spaced lines mean a weak field.

From these two meanings follow the drawing rules.

## Rules for drawing field lines

- **Arrows on every line**, outside a magnet pointing from N to S.
- **Lines never cross.** At any point the field has a single direction; two lines crossing would give two directions.
- **Lines are closed loops.** Outside a magnet they run from N to S; inside they continue from S back to N. Unlike electric field lines, they do not begin or end on anything. (On exam sketches you usually draw only the part outside a bar magnet, but you should not draw lines stopping in mid-air.)
- **Spacing shows strength.** Crowd the lines near the poles of a bar magnet, where the field is strongest, and spread them out further away.
- **A uniform field** is drawn as parallel, equally spaced straight lines with arrows all the same way, as between the flat pole faces of a large U-shaped magnet.
- **A neutral point** is where two fields cancel. No line passes through it, and the lines nearby bend away from it.

## Field patterns to recognise

**Single bar magnet.** Lines leave the north pole, curve round and enter the south pole; they are densest at the poles and symmetrical about the long axis.

**Two unlike poles facing each other (N facing S).** Lines run straight across the gap from N to S. Near the middle of a narrow gap the field is nearly uniform. The poles attract.

**Two like poles facing each other (N facing N).** Lines from each pole curve away sideways. Halfway between the poles there is a **neutral point** where the fields are equal and opposite. The poles repel.

**Between the poles of a U-shaped magnet.** Nearly uniform across the central region, with the lines bulging outwards at the edges, where the field is weaker. This is the standard "uniform field" used in force and induction questions.

**Representing three dimensions on paper.** Many questions need a field at right angles to the page. The conventions are:

- a **cross** (×) means the field points **into** the page (you see the tail of the arrow);
- a **dot** (•) means the field points **out of** the page (you see the tip).

A uniform field into the page is drawn as a regular grid of crosses, evenly spaced. The same symbols are used for currents into and out of the page.

## Worked example 1: an annotated "describe" answer

*Question.* Two identical bar magnets lie in a straight line with their north poles facing each other, 10 cm apart. Describe the magnetic field between them and explain what would happen to a small compass placed exactly halfway between the poles.

*Model answer.* The field lines leave each north pole and curve outwards and away from the line joining the magnets, ending on the south poles at the far ends [field pattern]. Along the line joining the poles, the field from each magnet points away from its own north pole, so the two fields are in opposite directions [direction]. At the midpoint they are equal in magnitude, so they cancel: this is a neutral point with zero resultant field [neutral point]. A compass there experiences no turning effect from the magnets and settles in the direction of the Earth's field (or any other background field) instead [consequence].

*Annotation.* Each bracket marks one creditable idea. The final point is a classic trap: "the compass points nowhere" is wrong, because the Earth's field is still present.

## Worked example 2: adding fields

A long straight wire carries a large current vertically upwards through a horizontal card. The horizontal component of the Earth's field at the card points north. Small compasses placed close to the wire line up along circles round it; compasses placed far from the wire all point north.

*Explanation.* The current produces its own field, in circles round the wire (see 20.4). Close to the wire this field is much stronger than the Earth's field, so the resultant is almost along the circle. Far from the wire the current's field is weak, so the resultant is almost the Earth's field. On the side of the wire where the wire's field points south, there is one distance at which it equals the Earth's horizontal field in size: the resultant is zero and there is a neutral point there. This illustrates both 20.1 outcomes at once: moving charges produce a magnetic field, and the resultant field is represented by lines found by combining the two fields at each point.

## The size of magnetic fields

Field strength is measured as magnetic flux density B in tesla (T); the definition comes in 20.2. To give a sense of scale: the Earth's field at its surface is of the order of 10⁻⁵ T, and strong laboratory magnets give fields of the order of a tenth of a tesla to around one tesla. You will not be asked to recall these values, but they help you judge whether a calculated answer is sensible.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying a stationary charge feels a magnetic force.** Only moving charges (and currents) do.
- **Drawing lines that cross**, or lines without arrows.
- **Drawing arrows from S to N outside a magnet.**
- **Starting or stopping lines in empty space.** Magnetic field lines form closed loops.
- **Treating field lines as paths of particles.** A charged particle moving at right angles to a uniform field moves in a circle, not along the lines.
- **Forgetting spacing.** A "uniform" field drawn with uneven spacing loses the mark.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (what is meant by a magnetic field): "a region in which a moving charge, a current-carrying conductor or a magnetic material experiences a force". Including "moving" is essential.
- **Sketch** (a field pattern): at least four or five lines, arrows on each, correct spacing, symmetry, and any neutral point labelled.
- **Describe**: give the shape, the direction and where the field is strongest.
- **Explain** (why a compass or charge behaves in a certain way): link the behaviour to the direction of the resultant field or to whether the charge is moving.
- **Compare** (magnetic with electric or gravitational fields): give the source and the object acted on for each, and point out that only moving charges are affected by a magnetic field.
