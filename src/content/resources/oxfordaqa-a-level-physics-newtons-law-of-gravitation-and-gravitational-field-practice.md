These questions cover sections 3.7.1 (Newton's gravitational law) and 3.7.2 (Gravitational field strength) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, Version 4.4, for exams from May/June 2020 onwards. Section 3.7 is International A-level only. Start with the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-newtons-law-of-gravitation-and-gravitational-field/) and the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-newtons-law-of-gravitation-and-gravitational-field-revision-notes/). The [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/physics/) and [printable checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/physics/) show where this unit sits.

Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². Other data is given in each question. Give answers to two or three significant figures, as the data allows.

## Questions

**1.** State Newton's law of gravitation. **[3]**

**2.** Two ships of masses 3.0 × 10⁴ kg and 4.5 × 10⁴ kg are moored with their centres 5.0 m apart. Treat them as point masses.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational force between them. **[2]**
**(b)** The ships drift apart until their centres are 15 m apart. Calculate the new force. **[1]**

**3.** **(a)** Define gravitational field strength, and state its unit. **[2]**
**(b)** State the direction of the gravitational field strength at a point near a planet. **[1]**

**4.** **(a)** Describe the pattern of gravitational field lines around an isolated spherical planet. **[2]**
**(b)** State what the spacing of the lines shows. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the field lines in a small region near the planet's surface are drawn parallel and equally spaced. **[1]**

**5.** Planet X has a surface gravitational field strength of 2.5 N kg⁻¹ and a radius of 3.0 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of planet X. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gravitational field strength at a point 4.5 × 10⁶ m from the centre of planet X. **[2]**

**6.** The Earth has mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and the Moon has mass 7.35 × 10²² kg. Their centres are 3.84 × 10⁸ m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational force that the Earth exerts on the Moon. **[2]**
**(b)** State the size of the force that the Moon exerts on the Earth. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the gravitational field strength of the Earth at the Moon's distance. **[2]**
**(d)** State the direction of the force on the Moon. **[1]**

**7.** A planet is a uniform sphere of radius R and density ρ.

**(a)** Show that the gravitational field strength at its surface is g = (4/3)πGρR. **[3]**
**(b)** Earth has radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m and mean density 5510 kg m⁻³. Use the equation to calculate g at Earth's surface. **[1]**

**8.** An astronaut has a mass of 65 kg. The gravitational field strength at the Moon's surface is 1.62 N kg⁻¹.

**(a)** State the astronaut's mass on the Moon. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the astronaut's weight on the Moon. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the astronaut's weight at Earth's surface and the factor by which the weight is greater. **[2]**

**9.** Earth has radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m and surface field strength 9.81 N kg⁻¹. A satellite is at a point where the field strength is 4.9 N kg⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the distance of the satellite from Earth's centre. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate its height above Earth's surface. **[1]**

**10.** A uniform sphere A of mass 5.0 kg and a uniform sphere B of mass 0.50 kg are placed with their centres 0.12 m apart. Ignore all other forces.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational force on B due to A. **[2]**
**(b)** The weight of B at Earth's surface is 4.9 N. Calculate the ratio of this gravitational force to B's weight. **[2]**
**(c)** The centres are moved to 0.24 m apart. Calculate the new force. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why B would accelerate more than A if both were free to move, although the forces on them are the same size. **[2]**

**11.** A gas giant has mass 5.7 × 10²⁶ kg and radius 6.0 × 10⁷ m. A probe of mass 350 kg hovers at the top of its atmosphere, so that its distance from the centre equals the radius.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational field strength at the probe's position. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gravitational force on the probe. **[1]**
**(c)** The probe moves to a distance of three times the planet's radius from the centre. Calculate the gravitational field strength there. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate how many times greater the field strength in part (a) is than the value at Earth's surface. **[1]**
**(e)** Compare the field lines at the two positions in parts (a) and (c), in terms of direction and spacing. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Two point masses attract with a force proportional to the product of their masses [1] and falling off with the square of the separation, F ∝ 1/r² [1]. The force is attractive [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers usually gain credit for each of the three ideas stated separately; "proportional to the masses and the distance" misses the inverse square and the product.

**2. (a)** F = Gm₁m₂/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.0 × 10⁴ × 4.5 × 10⁴ / 5.0² [1] = **3.6 × 10⁻³ N** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** The distance is three times larger, so the force is 1/9 as large: 3.60 × 10⁻³ / 9 = **4.0 × 10⁻⁴ N** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution before the answer. In part (b), saying "one ninth" and then giving the new value makes the reasoning clear.

**3. (a)** The force per unit mass [1] acting on a small mass placed at that point; unit N kg⁻¹ [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Towards the centre of the planet, the direction of the force [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns credit when it says force per unit mass; "the force of gravity" alone is too vague.

**4. (a)** The lines are straight and radial [1], with arrows pointing inwards towards the centre of the planet [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Field strength: stronger where the lines are packed more closely [1] **[1]**
**(c)** The region is tiny compared with the planet's radius, so the field strength and direction hardly change over it [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A sketch or description earns credit only with arrowheads and a clear statement that lines point towards the centre. Linking spacing to field strength is a separate point.

**5. (a)** M = gR²/G [1] = 2.5 × (3.0 × 10⁶)² / 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ = **3.4 × 10²³ kg** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** g ∝ 1/r², so g = 2.5 × (3.0 / 4.5)² [1] = **1.1 N kg⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A ratio method using the given data avoids carrying a rounded mass into part (b). Using the rounded mass gives the same answer to two significant figures.

**6. (a)** F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 7.35 × 10²² / (3.84 × 10⁸)² [1] = **1.98 × 10²⁰ N** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** The same size, 1.98 × 10²⁰ N [1], because they are a Newton's third law pair **[1]**
**(c)** g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ / (3.84 × 10⁸)² [1] = **2.70 × 10⁻³ N kg⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** Towards the Earth [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* For "state the size of the force on the other body", examiners usually expect the equal-and-opposite pair idea, not a recalculation with a different mass. Mention that the forces act on different bodies.

**7. (a)** Mass M = density × volume = ρ × (4/3)πR³ [1]. Surface field g = GM/R² [1]. Substituting: g = G × ρ(4/3)πR³ / R² = **(4/3)πGρR** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** g = (4/3) × π × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5510 × 6.37 × 10⁶ = **9.81 N kg⁻¹** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In "show that" questions, write every algebra step, including where R³ divided by R² leaves R. A final line that only restates the given result gains little.

**8. (a)** Mass is unchanged: **65 kg** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** W = mg = 65 × 1.62 = **105 N** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** W = 65 × 9.81 = **638 N** [1]. The factor is 638 / 105 = **6.1** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the roles clear: kg for mass, N for weight. A weight given in kg, or a mass said to change, is easy to avoid.

**9. (a)** g ∝ 1/r², so (R/r)² = 4.9 / 9.81 [1]. r = R × √(9.81 / 4.9) [1] = **9.0 × 10⁶ m** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** h = r − R = 9.01 × 10⁶ − 6.37 × 10⁶ = **2.6 × 10⁶ m** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (a) gives the distance from the centre, and the question asks for height. Subtracting the radius at the end is the step most often forgotten.

**10. (a)** F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.0 × 0.50 / 0.12² [1] = **1.2 × 10⁻⁸ N** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Ratio = 1.16 × 10⁻⁸ / 4.9 [1] = **2.4 × 10⁻⁹** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The distance doubles, so the force falls by a factor of 4: 1.16 × 10⁻⁸ / 4 = **2.9 × 10⁻⁹ N** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** The forces form an equal and opposite pair [1]. a = F/m, so the smaller mass B has the larger acceleration [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying the force is "very small" is stronger with a number, such as the ratio in part (b). In part (d), give both ideas: equal forces, then a = F/m.

**11. (a)** g = GM/R² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.7 × 10²⁶ / (6.0 × 10⁷)² [1] = **10.6 N kg⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** F = mg = 350 × 10.6 = **3.7 × 10³ N** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** The distance is three times larger, so g falls to one ninth [1]: 10.56 / 9 = **1.17 N kg⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** 10.6 / 9.81 = **1.08 times greater** [1] **[1]**
**(e)** Both sets of lines are radial and point inwards towards the centre [1]. At the greater distance the lines are further apart, showing the weaker field [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the rounded 10.6 in part (c) can change the third figure compared with a one-step calculation. Show your working and avoid rounding in the middle of a long calculation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using altitude, not the distance from the centre, as r.
- Squaring only the number in r and not its power of ten.
- Swapping G and g in a formula.
- Giving the unit of g as N, kg or m, or leaving it out.
- Saying that a heavier object feels a larger g, instead of explaining that m cancels.
- Treating the two forces in a gravitational pair as unequal because the masses are unequal.
- Drawing field lines without arrows, or with arrows pointing away from the mass.
- Giving a mass in newtons or a weight in kilograms.
- Rounding mid-calculation, then reporting too many figures.
- Skipping steps in a "show that" argument.
