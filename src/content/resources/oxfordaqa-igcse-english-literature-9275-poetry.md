This guide covers the studied anthology poetry in OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature
(9275). It serves **both routes**, because the same content appears twice in the specification:

- **Route A: 3.2.1 Poetry**, assessed in Section A of Paper 2a (Poetry and Unseen Texts);
- **Route B: 3.3.1 Poetry**, assessed in Section A of Paper 2b (Poetry).

The anthology, the skills and the 30-mark question are the same on both routes. What differs is the
paper around the question, which is explained below. For the broader picture, see the
[Poetry and Unseen Texts study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-english-literature-poetry-and-unseen-texts/)
(Route A) or the
[Route B Poetry and non-exam assessment study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-route-b-poetry-and-non-exam-assessment-study-guide/).

## What this section covers

You study the OxfordAQA Exams poetry anthology ***People and Places***: 20 thematically linked poems
from different times and contexts. The specification's aim is that you explore connected ideas and
themes across poets, so the anthology is designed for comparison. OxfordAQA's revised specification
for first teaching from September 2026 refreshes the anthology, so check which version applies to
your exam year.

In the exam you answer **one question from a choice of two**, worth **30 marks**. The papers are
**open book**: the anthology is supplied, so you are not expected to memorise the poems word for
word. You are expected to know them well enough to choose the right poems and find evidence quickly.

## Route A and Route B compared

| | Route A | Route B |
|---|---|---|
| Specification reference | 3.2.1 Poetry | 3.3.1 Poetry |
| Paper | Paper 2a: Poetry and Unseen Texts | Paper 2b: Poetry |
| Paper length and weight | 2 h 15 min, 90 marks, 60% | 1 h 30 min, 60 marks, 40% |
| Other sections on the paper | Unseen poetry and unseen prose | Unseen poetry |
| Anthology question | 30 marks, one from a choice of two | 30 marks, one from a choice of two |
| Sensible time on the anthology question | About 45 minutes | About 45 minutes |

On both routes the anthology question is one of several 30-mark sections, so the time split is the
same: about 45 minutes. Route A candidates have a longer paper to manage, which makes it easier to
overrun on the familiar anthology question and leave too little time for unseen prose.

## Assessment objectives

- **AO1**: understanding of, and engagement with, themes, ideas and contexts. Here that means
  choosing poems that genuinely fit the question and saying something precise about each.
- **AO2**: analysis of how poets create meanings and effects through language, form and structure.
- **AO3**: an informed personal response, using appropriate terminology in coherent, accurate writing.
  In a comparison, AO3 is also about **organisation**: an argued comparison rather than two separate
  essays.

## Key ideas

### Revise by theme, not poem by poem

Because the poems are grouped around connected ideas, group your revision the same way. Typical
thematic strands in an anthology about people and places include identity, belonging and
displacement, memory, the relationship between a person and a landscape, and change over time. For
each strand, know which poems belong to it and how they differ.

### Build a poem profile

For each poem, keep a short profile: speaker and situation; theme and attitude; form and structure;
three or four key language choices; any context you need; and links to other poems. The
[Route B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-route-b-poetry-and-non-exam-assessment-study-guide/)
sets out this profile in detail.

### Choose the right partner poem

If the question names one poem and asks you to choose another, or leaves both choices to you, pick
the poem that gives you the **best contrast within the theme**, not your favourite. Two poems that
treat the same idea in very different ways give you more to say than two near-identical poems.

### Compare methods, not just meanings

"Both poems are about home" is a starting point, not a comparison. The marks come from explaining how
each poet's choices of voice, imagery, form and structure lead to a different view of the shared
idea.

## Worked example: an integrated comparison

The two extracts below are original lines written for this guide, not anthology poems. Imagine they
open two poems about leaving a place.

> **Poem X:** *The bus pulls out. I count the roofs / until the roofs run out.*
>
> **Poem Y:** *What I carry is not the street / but the weight of being from it.*

An integrated comparison paragraph:

> Both poets present leaving as a loss that the speaker cannot fully control, but they locate that
> loss in different places. In Poem X the loss is external and visual: the speaker "count[s] the
> roofs", and the repetition of "roofs" across the line break mimics the town shrinking from view,
> so the reader watches the place disappear. Poem Y, by contrast, moves the loss inside the speaker.
> The negative construction "not the street / but..." rejects the physical place in favour of an
> abstract "weight", suggesting that identity, not geography, is what the speaker takes away.
> Whereas Poem X ends its image when the roofs "run out", Poem Y implies the weight never runs out,
> so leaving becomes permanent rather than momentary.

Notice that the paragraph moves between both poems within a single point, uses comparative
connectives ("but", "by contrast", "whereas"), quotes briefly and analyses structure (line break,
sentence construction) as well as language.

## Planning a 30-mark answer

Spend about five minutes planning:

1. Underline the theme and command words in the question.
2. Choose your poems, if the question leaves the choice to you.
3. Write a one-sentence argument that covers both poems ("Both present..., but X... whereas Y...").
4. Plan three comparative points, each covering both poems: for example, voice and perspective,
   imagery, and form or structure.
5. Note one piece of context for each poem only if it explains a choice in the poem.

Then write a short introduction, three developed comparative paragraphs and a brief conclusion that
returns to your argument.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing two separate essays.** Discussing one poem fully and then the other, with a final
  sentence of comparison, weakens AO3 organisation and AO2 comparison.
- **Theme only.** Comparing what poems are about without comparing how they are written.
- **Quoting whole stanzas.** The anthology is in front of you, so there is no excuse for long
  quotation: choose the key words.
- **Treating the speaker as the poet.** Write "the speaker" unless the poem clearly speaks in the
  poet's own voice.
- **Context instead of analysis.** A biography of the poet does not earn AO2 marks.
- **Overrunning.** On Route A especially, time spent polishing the anthology answer is taken from the
  unseen sections.

## Exam technique and command words

- **Compare** asks you to explore similarities and differences throughout, not at the end.
- **How do the poets present...?** asks about methods: every point should name a choice and its
  effect.
- **Explore** invites more than one interpretation of a line or image.

Use the open-book format well. Annotate your two poems for two or three minutes before writing, then
keep quotations short and precise.
