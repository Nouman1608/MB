"Colonial rule and the nationalist challenge in India, 1919–47" is Depth Study 4 on Paper 1 of
Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1). It explains how British rule was challenged
by the Indian National Congress under Gandhi and Nehru and by the Muslim League under Jinnah, and
why independence in August 1947 came with partition into India and Pakistan. Questions test the
causes and effects of key events and your judgement on the relative importance of individuals,
British policy and the Second World War.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics:

1. **The Rowlatt Acts, Amritsar and the Government of India Act, 1919** — the First World War's
   impact and the growth of nationalism, the Rowlatt Acts, dyarchy, and the Amritsar Massacre.
2. **Gandhi and Congress, 1919–27** — the rise of nationalism, Gandhi's aims and methods, swaraj,
   civil disobedience and satyagraha, Congress's attitude to the British, Muslims and
   untouchables, and the role of Jinnah.
3. **Key developments, 1927–39** — the Simon Commission, the Salt March, the Round Table
   Conferences, the Government of India Act 1935 and the 1937 elections; Gandhi, Jinnah and the
   Muslim League.
4. **The impact of the Second World War** — Gandhi, Nehru and Jinnah; the war's political and
   economic impact; the Lahore Resolution (1940); Quit India; the Cripps Mission; Wavell; Subhas
   Chandra Bose.
5. **Communal violence, independence and partition, 1945–47** — the Simla Conference, the Labour
   government, the Cabinet Mission, Direct Action, the demand for Pakistan, Mountbatten,
   partition and its consequences, and the Princely States.

## How did 1919 change Indian nationalism?

Over a million Indian soldiers served in the First World War, and India paid heavily in taxes and
prices. Congress and the Muslim League cooperated in the Lucknow Pact (1916), and in 1917 the
British promised the "progressive realisation of responsible government". Expectations were high.

Instead, the **Rowlatt Acts** (March 1919) extended wartime powers to imprison political suspects
without trial and try cases without juries. Gandhi called a national *hartal* (a strike and day of
protest). The **Government of India Act 1919** introduced **dyarchy** in the provinces: Indian
ministers controlled "transferred" subjects such as education and health, while British
governors kept "reserved" subjects such as finance and law and order. Its weaknesses were a small
electorate, a Viceroy who kept final power at the centre, and separate electorates that
reinforced religious division.

On **13 April 1919**, at the **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar, General Dyer ordered troops to fire on
an unarmed crowd without warning. The official Hunter Committee recorded 379 deaths; Indian
estimates were far higher. Dyer was censured and removed from command but praised by many in
Britain. The massacre turned moderates, including Gandhi, against British rule.

## What were Gandhi's aims and methods?

Gandhi aimed at **swaraj** (self-rule) achieved through **satyagraha** ("truth-force") and **ahimsa**
(non-violence). His methods included non-cooperation, boycotts of British cloth and institutions,
*swadeshi* (Indian-made goods, symbolised by the spinning wheel) and civil disobedience. He turned
Congress into a mass movement, reaching peasants as well as the educated elite.

The **Non-Cooperation Movement** (1920–22) was linked with the Muslim **Khilafat** campaign, a rare
moment of Hindu–Muslim unity. Gandhi called it off after the killing of policemen at **Chauri
Chaura** (February 1922), and he was imprisoned from 1922 to 1924. Gandhi campaigned against the
treatment of untouchables, whom he later called *Harijans*. **Jinnah**, at this stage a Congress
member who believed in constitutional methods, left Congress in 1920 because he opposed mass
non-cooperation; Hindu–Muslim cooperation broke down in the mid-1920s.

## What happened between 1927 and 1939?

The **Simon Commission** (appointed 1927) had no Indian members and was boycotted with the slogan
"Simon go back". In 1929 Congress, now led by younger figures including **Jawaharlal Nehru**,
demanded *purna swaraj* (complete independence). In the **Salt March** (March–April 1930) Gandhi
walked about 240 miles to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax; tens of
thousands were arrested in the civil disobedience campaign that followed.

The **Round Table Conferences** in London (1930–32) were weakened by disagreements. Congress
boycotted the first; after the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact** (1931) Gandhi attended the second but it failed
over minority representation; Congress was absent from the third. The **Government of India Act
1935** gave elected Indian governments control of the provinces and proposed an all-India federation
(never achieved). In the **1937 elections** Congress formed governments in most provinces, while
the Muslim League did poorly. Congress's refusal to share power with the League in provinces such
as the United Provinces convinced Jinnah that Muslims needed their own protection.

## How did the Second World War affect India?

In September 1939 the Viceroy declared war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders;
Congress ministries resigned. The Muslim League's **Lahore Resolution** (March 1940) demanded
independent states in Muslim-majority areas — the basis of the demand for **Pakistan**. Japan's
advance in 1942 led Churchill's government to send the **Cripps Mission**, offering dominion status
after the war with the right of provinces to opt out; Congress rejected it. Congress then launched
**Quit India** (August 1942); its leaders were arrested and the protests were crushed, while the
League cooperated with the British and grew stronger. The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated two to three
million people. **Wavell** became Viceroy in 1943. **Subhas Chandra Bose** led the Indian National
Army alongside Japan; the trials of its officers in 1945–46 caused nationwide protest.

## Why did independence bring partition in 1947?

Wavell's **Simla Conference** (1945) failed over Jinnah's claim that the League alone should
nominate Muslim members of a new council. The **Labour government** under Attlee (elected July 1945)
was committed to leaving India; Britain was also exhausted by war. In the 1945–46 elections the
League won the great majority of Muslim seats. The **Cabinet Mission** (1946) proposed a united
India with groups of provinces; it broke down when Nehru suggested Congress would not be bound by
the grouping plan. Jinnah called for **Direct Action Day** (16 August 1946), and the Great
Calcutta Killings were followed by violence in Noakhali and Bihar.

In February 1947 Attlee announced that Britain would leave by June 1948. **Mountbatten**, Viceroy
from March 1947, concluded that partition was the only solution and brought the date forward.
India and Pakistan became independent on **14–15 August 1947**. The boundary lines were drawn in
haste, and partition violence killed hundreds of thousands — estimates range up to a million or
more — and displaced over ten million people. More than 500 **Princely States** had to choose; most
acceded to India, but Kashmir became a lasting dispute.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did Gandhi call off non-cooperation in 1922?** Because the violence at Chauri Chaura breached
his principle of non-violence.

**Was partition inevitable?** It is a common essay debate. Evidence for: separate electorates, the
1937 Congress ministries, the Lahore Resolution and the League's wartime growth. Evidence against:
the Cabinet Mission's near-acceptance in 1946.

**Who mattered most?** Gandhi (mass movement), Jinnah (Pakistan), Nehru, Attlee and Mountbatten
can all be weighed; good answers link individuals to circumstances.
