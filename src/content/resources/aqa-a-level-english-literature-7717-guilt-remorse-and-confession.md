**This guide covers one element AQA lists for Option 2A Elements of Crime Writing:** "guilt and
remorse, confession and the desire for forgiveness". The
[Elements of Crime Writing study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-2a-elements-of-crime-writing-7717/)
groups all the elements and gives one example of guilt in three texts; this page separates the four
ideas in AQA's phrase, shows how each set text handles them, and models how to write about guilt in
the set-text essay and the unseen passage.

## Four ideas, not one

AQA's wording contains four distinct ideas, and the best answers keep them apart.

| Idea | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Guilt | Is the character guilty in law, in morality, or only in their own mind? |
| Remorse | Does the guilty character feel sorrow for the act, or only fear of its consequences? |
| Confession | To whom is the crime confessed: a priest, a court, a listener, the reader, no one? |
| Desire for forgiveness | Is forgiveness sought, and from whom: God, the victim, society, oneself? |

A character can be guilty without remorse (Browning's Duke), confess without repenting (Claudius),
or feel guilt for a crime others would not call one (Briony). Those mismatches are where the
interesting arguments are.

## Confession without repentance: *Hamlet*

Claudius's soliloquy in the prayer scene (Act 3 Scene 3) is a confession made to God and overheard
by the audience. He names his crime as a brother's murder, but he recognises that he cannot be
forgiven while he keeps what the murder won him: his crown, his ambition and his queen. The couplet
that ends the scene, in which his words fly up while his thoughts remain below, makes the gap between
confession and repentance explicit. The scene's dramatic irony is sharp: Hamlet, entering, spares
Claudius because he thinks a man at prayer would go to heaven, while the audience has just heard
that the prayer has failed. Guilt here is a structural force: it delays revenge.

## Confession by accident: the dramatic monologue

Browning's speakers confess without meaning to. The form, a single voice addressing a silent listener,
lets the reader judge what the speaker does not see.

- In "My Last Duchess", the Duke's account of commands after which "all smiles stopped together"
  reveals a killing as a matter of taste. There is guilt, but no remorse: the confession is a boast.
- In "Porphyria's Lover", the speaker ends by noting that God has not said a word. The line can be
  read as defiance, as a wait for judgement, or as a sign that he half expects punishment.
- In "The Laboratory", the speaker relishes the poison she is buying. Her pleasure in the details is
  the opposite of remorse, and the reader's repugnance does the moral work.

**Method point:** in a monologue, analyse the gap between what the speaker intends to say and what the
reader understands. That gap is the confession.

## Guilt as haunting: Crabbe and Coleridge

In Crabbe's "Peter Grimes", the fisherman whose apprentices die is shunned by the Borough and haunted
on the water by visions of his father and the dead boys. His dying account is a confession wrung
from him by guilt that has taken physical form.

*The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* shows guilt, penance and release as stages. After shooting the
albatross, the Mariner suffers on the becalmed sea; the turning point comes when he blesses the
water-snakes "unaware", and the albatross falls from his neck. On land he begs the Hermit to shrive
him, and afterwards an agony compels him to retell the tale to chosen listeners. Forgiveness is
partial: the confession must be repeated, so remorse becomes a lifelong condition.

## Guilt in the novels

**Oliver Twist.** After murdering Nancy, Sikes is pursued less by the law than by her eyes, which he
imagines staring at him wherever he goes. Dickens externalises guilt as an image the murderer cannot
escape, and Sikes's flight back to London ends in his own death. Fagin's night in the condemned cell
shows a different state: terror of punishment rather than sorrow for the victims.

**Brighton Rock.** Greene sets guilt inside a Catholic framework of sin, damnation and grace. Pinkie
believes in hell and chooses it; Rose, who loves him, would rather share his damnation than be saved
without him. In the final chapter Rose confesses to an old priest, who speaks of "the appalling
strangeness of the mercy of God". The novel asks whether forgiveness is possible for someone who
never seeks it.

**The Murder of Roger Ackroyd.** Christie's narrator, Dr Sheppard, is revealed as the murderer, and the
narrative turns out to be his own account, closing with a chapter titled "Apologia". The whole novel
becomes a confession disguised as a detective story, which changes how every earlier page reads.

**Atonement.** Briony's crime is a false accusation, and her guilt drives the whole novel. The final
section, dated 1999, reveals that she is the author of what we have read, and that the reunion of
Robbie and Cecilia was invented. Her desire for forgiveness takes the form of fiction, and she
herself questions whether a novelist, who decides every outcome, can achieve atonement. The novel
therefore asks whether writing can be a confession at all.

## Worked example: a set-text paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *Hamlet*, guilt is more powerful than revenge." Discuss.

> Shakespeare makes guilt the force that shapes the play's central act, the failure to kill Claudius.
> In the prayer scene, Claudius's soliloquy confesses the murder plainly, yet his reasoning exposes
> the limits of confession: he cannot ask pardon while he keeps the crown, his ambition and his
> queen. The scene is built on dramatic irony, since Hamlet arrives, sees a man apparently at prayer,
> and decides not to send him to heaven, while the audience already knows that the prayer has failed.
> Claudius's guilt is not strong enough to make him repent, but the appearance of guilt is strong
> enough to stop Hamlet's revenge. In this sense guilt is more powerful than revenge, because it
> controls the timing of the tragedy. Yet the counter-view is strong: guilt does not save Claudius,
> who goes on to plot Hamlet's death in England and at the duel, so revenge is only delayed, and the
> final scene completes it.

**Why it works.** It distinguishes confession from repentance (AQA's separate terms), analyses
dramatic irony and structure (AO2), and weighs the view (AO5).

## Guilt in the unseen passage

When Section A prints an unseen extract, look for these signals of guilt and confession:

- a first-person voice explaining or justifying an act;
- gaps, hesitations or omissions in an account;
- a listener inside the text (a priest, a magistrate, a friend) or the reader as confessor;
- religious, legal or medical vocabulary (sin, plea, fever);
- repetition or return to a scene, suggesting a memory that cannot be escaped;
- imagery of stains, marks, eyes or weight.

Then argue about the significance: is the confession sincere, strategic, or involuntary?

## Common mistakes

- **Using "guilt" and "remorse" as synonyms.** A character can be guilty without remorse.
- **Assuming every confession leads to forgiveness.** Most of these texts withhold it.
- **Ignoring the listener.** The person confessed to shapes what confession means.
- **Forgetting that the reader can be the confessor**, as in Christie and the monologues.
- **Moralising.** Analyse how guilt is constructed; do not simply condemn the criminal.

## Exam technique

Paper 2 is open book, so use clean copies to find short, exact quotations. In Section B, plan around
AQA's four ideas, using at least two of them. In Section C, connect texts through different kinds of
confession, for example an involuntary monologue against a deliberate written account.
