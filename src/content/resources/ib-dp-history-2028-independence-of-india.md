This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The independence of India (1857 to 1964) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers the growth of Indian nationalism, British political reforms, the methods and leaders of the independence movement, the Partition of 1947 and the challenges of the first years of independence.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-independence-of-india-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-independence-of-india-practice/).

## Reasons for the emergence of Indian nationalism

**The 1857 uprising and its aftermath.** The rebellion of 1857 ended the East India Company and brought direct Crown rule in 1858. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised religious tolerance. British officials, however, grew wary of reform and suspicious of Indian society, and the army was reorganised with more European troops. For later nationalists, 1857 became a symbol of resistance.

**British policies (1858 to 1885).** India was governed by a Viceroy with an Indian Civil Service that was almost entirely British. Railways, telegraphs and an English-language education system created an educated Indian middle class, but this class found the top posts closed to it. In 1876 the age limit for the civil service examination was lowered. Lord Lytton's Vernacular Press Act (1878) restricted Indian-language newspapers, and a serious famine in 1876 to 1878 raised questions about British policy. Lord Ripon's attempts at local self-government (1882) and the Ilbert Bill (1883), which would have let Indian judges try Europeans, met angry European opposition. Dadabhai Naoroji's "drain of wealth" argued that India's resources were being taken to Britain. These grievances produced political organisation.

**Violence and the First World War.** About 1.3 million Indians served overseas in the war, and India paid heavy costs, but hopes of reward were disappointed. Wartime prices and taxes rose. The Rowlatt Acts (1919) extended wartime emergency powers. On 13 April 1919, at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, troops under General Reginald Dyer fired on a crowd that had gathered, killing hundreds (the official figure was 379; Indian estimates were higher). The massacre, and the way it was defended by some in Britain, turned many moderate Indians against British rule.

## Impact of political developments in the British Raj

**The Indian National Congress (1885).** Founded by A. O. Hume, a retired British official, and Indian leaders, it began by asking for reform and a larger Indian role. The "moderates" (such as Gokhale and Naoroji) used petitions. The "extremists" (such as Tilak) demanded self-rule, and the movement split at Surat in 1907.

**Partition of Bengal and the Muslim League.** Lord Curzon divided Bengal in 1905 on administrative grounds, but Bengalis saw it as an attack on their unity. A *Swadeshi* campaign boycotted British goods. The All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906 to protect Muslim interests. The Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 gave Muslims separate electorates. Britain reversed the partition in 1911 and moved the capital to Delhi. "Divide and rule" is a common claim, and you should test it against evidence.

**British reforms.** The Government of India Act (1919) introduced dyarchy, a split of power in the provinces between elected Indian ministers and British officials. The Act of 1935 gave the provinces more self-government, with elected ministries, a larger electorate and a plan for an all-India federation that was never put into effect. The **Round Table Conferences (1930 to 1932)** in London brought together British politicians, princes and Indian leaders. Congress boycotted the first, Gandhi attended the second, and no agreement was reached on the main question of minority representation.

## Methods used to achieve independence

**Non-cooperation and civil disobedience.** Gandhi, who returned from South Africa in 1915, led the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920 to 1922), asking Indians to boycott British courts, schools and cloth and to spin their own. He called it off after violence at Chauri Chaura in 1922. After the Simon Commission (1927), which had no Indian members, Congress declared *purna swaraj*, complete independence, in December 1929.

**The Salt March (1930).** Gandhi walked about 240 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi, from 12 March to 6 April, and broke the salt law. Salt was a necessity that everyone used and the tax weighed on the poor. Millions took part in the civil disobedience that followed and tens of thousands were jailed. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931) ended the campaign for a time.

**Quit India (1942).** After the Cripps Mission failed to offer full independence during the war, Congress demanded that Britain "quit India" in August 1942. The government arrested the leaders. Protests, sabotage and violence spread across many regions and were put down with force. Quit India showed deep feeling but the movement had no central leadership.

**Political negotiation.** Elections under the 1935 Act (1937) gave Congress control of most provinces. The Cabinet Mission (1946) proposed a loose federation. Talks between Britain, Congress and the Muslim League decided the final settlement.

## Leaders and their importance

- **Gandhi:** turned the Congress into a mass movement through non-violence (*satyagraha*), village symbols such as spinning, and the idea that all communities could unite. Critics say his religious language alienated some Muslims, and that his campaigns often ended in compromise.
- **Nehru:** a Congress socialist and internationalist, he became the party's most important younger leader, the first prime minister and architect of a secular state.
- **Jinnah:** a lawyer who began in Congress and later led the Muslim League; he argued that Muslims were a nation and demanded Pakistan.
- **Subhas Chandra Bose:** left Congress after disputes with Gandhi, sought help from Germany and then Japan, and formed the Indian National Army, which fought in Burma. His military role was small but his image was powerful. He died in 1945.

## Reasons for, and impact of, the Partition

**Muslim separatism.** The League's support grew in the 1930s, and the "Two-Nation theory" held that Muslims and Hindus were separate nations. At Lahore in March 1940 the League passed a resolution calling for independent Muslim-majority states. Congress rule in 1937 to 1939 left many Muslims feeling excluded.

**British actions.** After 1945 Britain was weak and wanted to leave. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 failed. "Direct Action Day" (16 August 1946) set off communal killings in Calcutta. Louis Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, arrived in March 1947 and set an earlier date for transfer of power, 15 August 1947. Historians argue that this speed made the violence worse.

**Impact.** The border was drawn by a commission under Cyril Radcliffe, and Punjab and Bengal were divided. About 10 to 15 million people moved across the new borders, and perhaps a million died, though estimates vary. Many women were abducted and assaulted. Communities such as Sikhs, who lived in the divided Punjab, lost their homeland. Gandhi was assassinated in January 1948 by a Hindu extremist.

## Challenges in post-independence India

**Nehru and nation-building (1947 to 1964).** The Constitution of 1950 created a secular republic with universal adult suffrage. The first general election of 1951 to 1952 was a huge exercise. Five-Year Plans set a mixed economy, with a strong state sector. Language states were created in the 1950s, beginning with Andhra in 1953 after a fast by Potti Sriramulu.

**Ethnic and religious conflict.** Communal violence recurred. In the north-east, Naga groups fought for independence. Tensions over language and region affected Punjab and the south.

**The princely states.** More than 560 states had to join India or Pakistan. Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon persuaded most to accede. **Junagadh**, with a Muslim ruler and Hindu majority, acceded to Pakistan, and India took it by force and a plebiscite. **Hyderabad**, the largest state, was taken in a police action in September 1948. **Kashmir**, with a Hindu ruler and Muslim majority, saw invasion by tribal forces from Pakistan in October 1947; the ruler signed accession to India. War followed, and a United Nations ceasefire in 1949 left it divided.

**Foreign policy and conflict.** Nehru followed non-alignment and promoted the Bandung Conference (1955). India clashed with Pakistan over Kashmir and, in 1962, lost a short war against China in the Himalayas, which damaged Nehru's reputation. He died in May 1964.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Nationalist historians, such as Bipan Chandra, see a unified mass struggle against colonial exploitation.
- The "Cambridge school" (such as Anil Seal) argued that Indian politics was driven by local interests and rivalry for office, with nationalism as a convenient language.
- Subaltern Studies (Ranajit Guha) looked at the actions of peasants and workers as independent of elite leaders.
- Ayesha Jalal argued in *The Sole Spokesman* that Jinnah used the demand for Pakistan to bargain for a stronger Muslim share in a united India, and was surprised by the outcome.
- Historians of Partition such as Urvashi Butalia and Yasmin Khan use oral history to recover the experience of ordinary people and women.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent was Gandhi the most important factor in achieving Indian independence?"** Rank factors: Gandhi's mass mobilisation (1920 to 1922, 1930 and 1942); the Congress organisation and other leaders; the Second World War, which weakened Britain; the pressure of constitutional reform from 1919 and 1935; and the League's separate demand. Judge that Gandhi turned nationalism into a mass movement, but independence in 1947 was decided by wider forces.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Gandhi as the only leader and ignoring Nehru, Jinnah and Bose.
- Describing Partition as inevitable.
- Writing a list of dates for the Raj without answering the question.
- Giving casualty figures as exact.
