> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the six concepts of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. The study summary is constructed for practice. Question 10 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; the others use practice tariffs.

Related: [Concepts study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define "bias" as the term is used in psychology. **[2]**

**2.** State two of the six concepts of the course, other than bias. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by "bidirectional ambiguity". **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by the operationalisation of a variable, using one example. **[3]**

**5.** Outline the 3Rs principle for the use of animals in research. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** Read the summary (constructed for practice).

> A researcher wanted to know whether a school mindfulness programme reduces stress. She invited all students in Year 12 to volunteer. Seventy students joined and completed a stress questionnaire before the programme and again after eight weeks. Their mean stress score fell from 31 to 24. She concluded that mindfulness reduces stress in teenagers. She did not collect data from a comparison group. The programme took place in the weeks after the mock examinations had ended.

**(a)** Explain one way in which **causality** is a problem for the researcher's conclusion. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way in which **bias** may have affected the sample. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one way in which **change** was measured and one limitation of that measure. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how an ethical standard can affect the results of research. Use one named standard. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the difference between an etic and an emic approach, with one example of when each would be used. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why reductionism can be both a strength and a limitation in explaining behaviour. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Discuss the extent to which psychologists can be confident about the causes of behaviour. In your answer refer to at least **two** concepts and to one context you have studied. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** A limitation in objective thinking **[1]**, a tendency to see information through a filter of experience and preference **[1]**.

**2.** Any two of: causality, change, measurement, perspective, responsibility **[1 each]**.

**3.** In a correlation, it is unclear whether variable A causes B or B causes A **[1]**, because the study shows a relationship but not the direction of the effect **[1]**.

**4.** Operationalisation means defining a variable in terms that can be observed and measured **[1]**. For example, "aggression" could be operationalised as the number of times a child hits another child in a ten-minute observation **[1]**. This allows the variable to be measured reliably and replicated by others **[1]**.

**5.** Reduce the number of animals used **[1]**; refine procedures to minimise harm and distress **[1]**; replace animals with alternatives (such as computer models or human volunteers) wherever possible **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Any one, three marks: there is no comparison group **[1]**, so the fall in stress could be due to something else, such as the end of the mock examinations (an extraneous variable) **[1]**, which means the researcher cannot claim that mindfulness caused the change **[1]**. Also creditable: a before-and-after design with no random allocation cannot rule out maturation or practice effects on the questionnaire.

(b) Any one, three marks: students volunteered, so the sample is self-selected **[1]**; volunteers may be more interested in mindfulness or more motivated to feel better **[1]**, so they may not represent all Year 12 students and may report lower stress after the programme to please the researcher **[1]**.

(c) Change was measured by repeated measures, with the same stress questionnaire given before and after the programme **[1]**. Limitation, any one: the questionnaire is self-reported, so participants may respond in a socially desirable way, or the interval is short so lasting change is not shown **[1]**. A second mark is for linking the limitation to the claim that mindfulness caused the fall **[1]**.

**7.** Any one standard, for example: informed consent **[1]**. Telling participants the aim may change how they behave (demand characteristics), so consent can introduce participant variables **[1]**. This affects the validity of the results **[1]**. Alternative: the right to withdraw can bias the sample because those who leave may differ from those who stay; anonymity can raise the honesty of self-reports.

**8.** An etic approach studies behaviour from outside a culture, using measures that are applied across cultures so that they can be compared **[1]**; for example, comparing the prevalence of a disorder in several countries using the same questionnaire **[1]**. An emic approach studies a group from the inside, in its own terms **[1]**; for example, interviewing members of a community about what well-being means to them **[1]**.

**9.** Strength: reductionism breaks behaviour into simple, testable parts, for example one neurotransmitter, which allows controlled research and clear causal claims **[2]**. Limitation: it can ignore other factors such as culture and thinking, so it may give an incomplete or biased explanation **[2]**. Credit developed examples.

**10.** Markband guide (see the 13 to 15 band in the guide): well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts explained throughout, accurate terminology and a reasoned conclusion. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Causality: true experiments with controls, random allocation and double-blind procedures give the strongest evidence of cause **[2]**; correlational and quasi-experimental studies cannot rule out third variables or show direction **[2]**.
- Complexity: most behaviour results from several interacting factors, so a single cause is rarely enough; reductionist explanations risk oversimplifying **[2]**.
- Bias: researcher, sampling or participant bias can create false causal impressions; publication bias hides null findings **[2]**.
- Measurement: results depend on how a variable is operationalised and whether data are self-reported or direct **[2]**.
- Responsibility: ethics limits the strongest designs, for example it would be unethical to cause harm to test a cause, so some causes are only inferred **[2]**.
- A context example used well, such as health and well-being (treatment trials with placebo and double-blind controls) or learning and cognition (controlled experiments on memory) **[2]**.
- A conclusion that is consistent with the argument, for example that confidence is greater for narrow questions in controlled conditions than for complex, real-world behaviour **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 6:** writing about "validity" in general rather than the missing comparison group.
- **Question 7:** describing the standard but not saying how it changes the results.
- **Question 10:** discussing causality only, with no second concept and no link between concepts, or writing a conclusion that does not follow from the argument.
