These notes condense section 7.3, *Globalisation, power and politics*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 4 Section A. Syllabus strands: the spread of liberal democracy and human rights; global social movements and attempts to oppose globalisation; and debates about the role of the nation state in tackling global social and environmental problems.

Full explanations: [globalisation, power and politics study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-power-and-politics/).

## Core argument

Globalisation has helped spread democratic and rights ideals, but unevenly and with reversals. Global social movements use global networks to oppose corporate globalisation, with more success in shaping debate than changing structures. Nation states have not disappeared; they increasingly act through cooperation.

## Liberal democracy

**Definition:** competitive elections plus civil liberties, rule of law and limits on state power.

| Evidence of spread | Evidence against |
|---|---|
| Huntington, *The Third Wave* (1991): wave from Portugal 1974 through southern Europe, Latin America, parts of Asia, central and eastern Europe after 1989 | Most Arab Spring uprisings did not produce lasting democracy |
| Arab Spring from late 2010: media and networks spread demands | Diamond (2015): "democratic recession" |
| Fukuyama (1989): liberal democracy defeated rivals | Growth can coexist with authoritarian rule |
| Aid and membership conditions | "Democracy promotion" may serve Western interests |

**Tension:** global markets constrain elected governments. **Held (1995): cosmopolitan democracy** — extend accountability to international institutions.

## Human rights

- Spread through declarations, courts, global civil society and media.
- **Universalism versus relativism:** the 1990s "Asian values" debate; critics saw it as justifying authoritarian rule.
- **Selective enforcement:** powerful states escape scrutiny (Marxist and post-colonial critique).
- **Agency:** local movements use rights language for their own claims.

## Global social movements

- **Kaldor (2003): global civil society** — movements, NGOs and networks beyond the state.
- **Castells:** networked movements gain power from communication.

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Anti-corporate | Anti-sweatshop campaigns; Klein, *No Logo* (1999) |
| Alter-globalisation | World Social Forum (from 2001) |
| Local resistance, global reach | Zapatista uprising, Chiapas, 1 January 1994 (NAFTA came into force) |
| Global protest | Seattle 1999; Occupy 2011; climate strikes from 2018 |

**Evaluation:** strong at publicity and coordination; weak at lasting policy change; often dominated by richer-country activists; paradox of using global networks; nationalist movements also oppose globalisation.

## Nation state and global problems

| Weakened state | State still central | Transformationalist |
|---|---|---|
| Strange, *The Retreat of the State* (1996): power shifts to markets and non-state actors | States control borders, armies, law, tax, citizenship | States pool sovereignty |
| Climate change crosses borders | Border closures in the coronavirus pandemic from 2020 | WTO (1995), G20 leaders' summits since 2008 |
| Capital mobility avoids tax and regulation | Paris Agreement (2015) relies on national commitments | Held: states change role |

**Marxist view:** states serve global capital, so weak regulation of corporations reflects class interest, not incapacity.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Liberal democracy | Elections plus civil liberties and limits on state power |
| Democratisation wave | Cluster of transitions to democracy in a period (Huntington) |
| Democratic recession | Stalling or reversal of democratic gains (Diamond) |
| Cosmopolitan democracy | Democratic accountability extended beyond the state (Held) |
| Human rights | Rights held by every person as a human being |
| Cultural relativism | View that values should be judged within their own culture |
| Global social movement | Cross-border network acting on a shared issue |
| Global civil society | Movements and NGOs operating beyond states (Kaldor) |
| Alter-globalisation | Seeking a different globalisation, not none |
| Sovereignty | Supreme authority of a state over its territory |
| Pooled sovereignty | States sharing decision-making in international bodies |

## Worked essay plan

*"The nation state is no longer able to tackle global environmental problems." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define the nation state and sovereignty; name the problem (climate change, pollution).
2. For: Strange on power shifting to markets; emissions cross borders; capital avoids regulation.
3. Against: states still control law, tax and borders; the Paris Agreement relies on national commitments.
4. Transformationalist view: pooled sovereignty and cooperation (Held).
5. Alternative actors: global social movements and civil society (Kaldor); Marxist view that states serve capital.
6. Judgement: states remain essential but cannot succeed alone.

## Exam traps

- Assuming democracy spreads steadily; use waves and reversals.
- Treating all opposition to globalisation as progressive.
- Equating human rights with Westernisation without considering local agency.
- Forgetting environmental problems in nation-state essays.
- Listing movements without judging effectiveness.

## Self-test

1. Define liberal democracy.
2. When and where did Huntington's third wave begin?
3. What did Diamond mean by a democratic recession?
4. What is cosmopolitan democracy?
5. Summarise the "Asian values" debate.
6. Define global civil society and name who used the term in 2003.
7. Why is the Zapatista uprising a useful example?
8. Give two weaknesses of global social movements.
9. What did Strange argue in 1996?
10. Give one piece of evidence that states remain powerful.
11. How does the Paris Agreement support the transformationalist view?
12. How do Marxists interpret states' weak regulation of corporations?

### Answers

1. A political system combining free, competitive elections with civil liberties, the rule of law and limits on state power.
2. With Portugal's revolution in 1974.
3. Democratic gains stalled and in places reversed from around the mid-2000s.
4. Held's argument that democratic accountability should extend to international institutions.
5. Some Asian governments argued that community and order came before individual rights; critics said it justified authoritarian rule.
6. Movements, NGOs and networks operating beyond the state; Mary Kaldor.
7. It was a local rebellion timed to the day NAFTA came into force, which used global communication to gain international support.
8. Any two: limited lasting policy change; domination by richer-country activists; reliance on the global networks they criticise; fragmentation of aims.
9. Power has shifted from states to markets and non-state actors.
10. Border closures and national public health measures during the coronavirus pandemic.
11. Global action is delivered through each state setting its own commitments, so states act through cooperation.
12. As a reflection of states serving the interests of global capital rather than lacking power.
