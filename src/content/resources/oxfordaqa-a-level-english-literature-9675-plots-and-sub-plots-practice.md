## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain the difference between a parallel and a parody in a tragic sub-plot. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how a sub-plot can help a Section A answer show the significance of a printed passage,
given that Unit 1 is closed book. **[3]**

**3.** The specification lists "how the behaviour of the heroes affects the world around them" as an
aspect of tragedy. Explain how a sub-plot can develop this aspect. **[2]**

**4.** Choose one Section A play. Identify one moment where its sub-plot converges with the main
plot and explain why the timing of that convergence matters. **[4]**

**5.** A student writes: "The comic scenes are there to give the audience a rest from the tragedy."
Give two reasons why this view is incomplete. **[2]**

## Section B: original extracts and planning

**Extract 1 (original).** In this invented tragedy, Duke Alonso has banished his honest adviser
for criticising a war. In a sub-plot, a merchant, Pietro, has disinherited his elder son for the
same kind of frankness. This scene comes from the sub-plot, in Act 3.

> **PIETRO:** Gone? Then let him go. He spoke to me
> As if my house were his to set in order.
> **YOUNGER SON:** He spoke too much, sir. I speak only love.
> **PIETRO:** *(aside)* And yet the accounts were truer in his hand.
> The figures in this boy's book are all fair weather.
> **YOUNGER SON:** Shall I bring the keys, father?
> **PIETRO:** Bring them. Bring all of them.
> *(Exit YOUNGER SON. PIETRO sits.)* The Duke did this today.
> I laughed at him at dinner. Now I am him.

**6.** Analyse how Extract 1 presents Pietro's story as a mirror of the Duke's. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the aside and the final stage direction shape the audience's response. **[4]**

**Extract 2 (original).** In a comic scene from another invented tragedy, two servants imitate
their master, a scholar who has sold his soul for knowledge.

> **NICK:** Stand back. I'll raise a devil with this book.
> **TOM:** You cannot read.
> **NICK:** I need not read. I'll shout.
> *(He shouts. Nothing happens.)*
> **TOM:** Your devil's deaf.
> **NICK:** Or busy with my master.
> They say he has the whole of hell to wait on him.
> **TOM:** Then hell is like a tavern. Pay, and pay again.

**8.** Explain how Extract 2 parodies the main plot and what this parody suggests about the scholar.
**[5]**

**9.** A student plans to use Extract 2 as the only evidence in an answer about the scholar's damnation.
Explain one strength and one weakness of this plan. **[2]**

**10.** For a passage from your own set play in which the protagonist is at the height of his or her
power, name a sub-plot moment you would link to and explain what the link would show. **[3]**

**11.** Read this student sentence on *King Lear*: "Gloucester is exactly like Lear because both have
bad children." Explain two weaknesses and write an improved sentence. **[4]**

**12.** Plan a 25-mark answer on a passage from your own set play for the question: "Explore the
significance of the passage in relation to the tragedy as a whole." List five elements of the plan,
including at least one use of a sub-plot. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A parallel repeats the main plot's pattern in another character or family, so the error seems
part of a wider human pattern [1]. A parody imitates the main action in a comic or debased form, so it
deflates or exposes the main action [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The passage method requires links to the rest of the play [1]. A sub-plot supplies memorable
moments that mirror or contrast with the passage, which can be recalled as short quotations [1]. These
links show the passage's place in the tragedy as a whole, which is what significance requires [1].
**[3]**

**3.** A sub-plot shows people beyond the hero suffering from similar errors or from the hero's
choices [1], so the tragedy appears to damage a family, household or state rather than one person [1].
**[2]**

**4.** Moment identified accurately [1], with a precise reference [1]; timing explained [2]. For example:
in *King Lear*, Edmund's alliance with Goneril and Regan joins the two plots [1]; his order for the
deaths of Lear and Cordelia [1] comes so late that Edmund's attempt to cancel it fails, so the
convergence turns the sub-plot villain into the immediate cause of the catastrophe [1] and denies the
audience the rescue it has begun to hope for [1]. Credit equivalent answers on *Hamlet* (Laertes and
Claudius's plot with the poisoned foil and cup), *Othello* (the handkerchief passing to Bianca),
*Doctor Faustus* or *The Duchess of Malfi* (Julia's death as Bosola turns against the Cardinal). **[4]**

**5.** Any two, [1] each: comic scenes often parody the main action and so comment on it; the
contrast can sharpen the shock of the next tragic moment; low-status characters may see the truth the
hero misses; comedy can be bitter and itself reveal corruption. **[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- Pietro's "Then let him go" echoes a ruler's dismissal, placing the merchant in the Duke's role.
- "As if my house were his to set in order" treats frank advice as a threat to authority, the same
  error as the Duke's.
- The younger son's "I speak only love" is flattery that offers comfort instead of truth.
- The financial imagery ("accounts", "figures", "book") turns honesty into a matter of accurate
  records, so the elder son's frankness is shown to have been useful.
- "Fair weather" suggests the flatterer's figures hide future storms, foreshadowing loss.
- Handing over "all" the keys shows Pietro surrendering control of his house, a domestic version of a
  state falling into the wrong hands.
- "Now I am him" states the mirror outright, so the sub-plot comments on the main plot.
- The change from public to domestic scale suggests the error belongs to fathers and rulers alike.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the aside reveals that Pietro already doubts his choice, creating
dramatic irony because the younger son cannot hear it; his private recognition makes the audience
judge him more gently than the Duke; the stage direction "PIETRO sits" makes his deflation visible,
since he lowers himself as he loses control; the silence after the son's exit isolates him on stage;
his self-recognition comes too late, which creates pity. **[4]**

**8.** Any five, [1] each: Nick's attempt to "raise a devil" imitates the scholar's conjuring;
"You cannot read" mocks the idea that power comes from books, since the servant has none of the
scholar's learning; the failed shout shows magic reduced to noise; "busy with my master" implies the
scholar's devils serve him only for now; "Pay, and pay again" presents hell as a business of endless
debt, suggesting the scholar's bargain will cost more than he believes; the comedy makes the
scholar's grand ambition look like the same trick on a larger scale. **[5]**

**9.** Strength [1]: the parody's image of hell as endless payment does point towards damnation.
Weakness [1]: one comic scene cannot support an argument about the whole tragedy; the answer needs the
scholar's own scenes, especially the bargain and the ending. **[2]**

**10.** A relevant sub-plot moment named [1]; explanation of what the link shows [2], for example that
the sub-plot's earlier fall foreshadows the hero's, or that a foil's different choice exposes the
hero's flaw at the moment of greatest confidence. Example: a passage of Hamlet planning the play
within the play could link to Fortinbras marching to war over a small piece of land [1], which
contrasts Hamlet's thought with Fortinbras's action [1] and raises the question of whether Hamlet's
intelligence is strength or weakness [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Weakness one [1]: "exactly like" ignores the differences that give the parallel meaning.
Weakness two [1]: "bad children" is vague and reduces both plots to blaming the children, with no
reference to the fathers' own errors of judgement. Improved sentence [2] for a precise, argued
comparison, [1] for a partial improvement. Example: "Gloucester mirrors Lear in believing a child's
words over a child's actions, but where Lear's error drives him to rage, Gloucester's drives him to
despair, so the double plot shows two ways of breaking under the same mistake." **[4]**

**12.** Any five, [1] each, in a sensible order:

- a one-sentence argument about the passage's significance;
- where the passage sits in the structure (complication, crisis or catastrophe) and what the
  audience knows;
- two or three methods in the passage to analyse, each with a short quotation;
- a sub-plot moment that mirrors or contrasts with the passage, and what it adds;
- one earlier or later main-plot moment linked to the passage;
- one context that changes the reading;
- an alternative interpretation to weigh;
- a conclusion on the audience's response. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the sub-plot rather than arguing what it shows about the main tragedy.
- Treating parallels as identical, when the difference carries the meaning.
- Letting a sub-plot digression push the printed passage out of the answer.
- Misremembering quotations: in a closed-book exam a short accurate phrase is worth more than a long
  inaccurate one.
