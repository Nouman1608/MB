## The sources in one table

| Source | Type | Key benefit | Key drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal funds | Internal | No interest; full control | Limited savings; sole trader risks personal assets |
| Retained profit | Internal | No interest; no outside claims | Not available to start-ups; smaller dividends |
| Sale of assets | Internal | No debt | Few spare assets; may cut capacity |
| Share capital | External | Never repaid | Ownership and control diluted; companies only |
| Loan capital | External | Control kept; suits long-lived assets | Interest and repayment whatever the profit; may need collateral |
| Overdraft | External | Flexible; interest only on amount used | Short-term; high rate; can be withdrawn |
| Trade credit | External | Delay payment for stock | Lost early payment discounts; supplier relations |
| Crowdfunding | External | Tests demand; builds customer base | Campaign effort; target may not be met |
| Leasing | External | No large upfront payment; servicing often included | May cost more than buying; asset not owned |
| Microfinance provider | External | Lends without collateral or history | Small sums; rates can be high |
| Business angel | External | Money plus expertise and contacts | Gives up ownership and some control |

## Short term versus long term

* **Short term** (usually repaid within about a year): overdraft, trade credit, some microfinance loans.
* **Long term**: share capital, loan capital, retained profit, personal funds, business angels, crowdfunding, leasing (often medium term).
* **Matching principle:** finance a long-lived asset with long-term finance; finance a temporary cash gap with short-term finance.

## Access check: rule out before you judge

* Shares only for companies.
* Retained profit only after profitable years.
* Loans easier with collateral and a trading record.
* Sale of assets only if spare assets exist.
* Crowdfunding and angels suit appealing or high-growth ideas.

## Costing formulas used in cases

These are standard calculations, not formulas from the guide.

* Lost discount = discount rate × invoice value.
* Simple interest = amount borrowed × annual rate × years.
* Interest for part of a year = amount × annual rate × days ÷ 365.
* Total lease cost = payment × number of payments.
* Retained profit = profit after tax − dividends.

## Worked mini-example

Calderbrook Joinery (invented) needs 24,000 for a machine lasting eight years and 6,000 to cover a two-month gap before a large customer pays.

* Machine: long-term need, so loan capital or leasing. A four-year loan at 7% simple interest costs 24,000 × 0.07 × 4 = 6,720 in interest.
* Two-month gap: short-term need, so an overdraft. At 15% a year, 6,000 × 0.15 × 60 ÷ 365 = 148 (to the nearest dollar).
* Using the overdraft for the machine would be risky, because the bank could demand repayment while the machine still has years of use left.

## AO3 judgement checklist

1. Purpose and life of the asset or need.
2. Amount needed.
3. Cost of each option, using case figures.
4. Effect on control and risk.
5. Access: which sources are realistically open.
6. Recommendation plus the condition that would change it.

## Exam traps

* Share capital recommended for a sole trader.
* Overdraft recommended for long-term assets.
* Trade credit described as costless.
* Retained profit described as a pile of cash.
* Crowdfunding described only as donations; some models give rewards or shares.
* Leasing described as owning the asset.
* Lists of pros and cons with no recommendation.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three internal sources in the guide.
2. Which external sources are usually short term?
3. Why can a sole trader not use share capital?
4. Give one advantage of a business angel over a bank loan.
5. What is the main risk of using an overdraft to buy machinery?
6. A supplier offers 3% off a 15,000 invoice for early payment. What does taking full credit cost?
7. Calculate simple interest on a 20,000 loan at 6% for three years.
8. Profit after tax is 60,000 and dividends are 22,000. What is retained profit?
9. Who are microfinance providers designed to help?
10. Give one drawback of leasing.
11. Why might a business choose share capital over loan capital even though it dilutes control?
12. A lease costs 600 a month for three years. What is the total cost?

### Answers

1. Personal funds (for sole traders), retained profit, sale of assets.
2. Overdrafts and trade credit (and some microfinance loans).
3. Shares exist only in companies; a sole trader's business is not a company.
4. Any one: no repayments or interest; brings expertise and contacts.
5. The bank can demand repayment at short notice while the machine still has years of use.
6. The lost discount: 3% × 15,000 = 450.
7. 20,000 × 0.06 × 3 = 3,600.
8. 60,000 − 22,000 = 38,000.
9. Entrepreneurs who cannot get ordinary bank credit, often because they lack collateral or a credit history.
10. Any one: total payments may exceed the purchase price; the asset is not owned at the end.
11. Share capital never has to be repaid and has no fixed interest, which suits a firm with uncertain profits or heavy existing debt.
12. 600 × 36 = 21,600.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between short-term and long-term finance?**
Short-term finance, such as an overdraft or trade credit, is usually repaid within about a year and suits temporary cash gaps. Long-term finance, such as loan or share capital, suits assets that will be used for many years.

**Is trade credit free?**
There is no interest if the business pays on time, but it may lose a discount for paying early, and late payment can damage supplier relationships.
