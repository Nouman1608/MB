This study guide goes deeper into subtopic **5.1 Units of measure**, the first subtopic of Topic 5 Mensuration in the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The Core (C5.1) and Extended (E5.1) statements are identical, so everything here is for both tiers. The basic conversion facts and first examples are in the [units of measure, area and perimeter study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-0580-units-of-measure-area-and-perimeter/); this page concentrates on two-step conversions, standard form, comparing quantities and practical problems.

Consolidate with the [units of measure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-units-of-measure-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [units of measure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-units-of-measure-practice/).

## What this subtopic covers

The syllabus statement is: use metric units of mass, length, area, volume and capacity in practical situations and convert quantities into larger or smaller units. The notes list exactly which units are included.

| Quantity | Units in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| Length | mm, cm, m, km |
| Area | mm², cm², m², km² |
| Volume | mm³, cm³, m³ |
| Capacity | ml, l |
| Mass | g, kg |

The notes also say conversion includes **between different units of area** (for example cm² ↔ m²) and **between units of volume and capacity** (for example m³ ↔ litres). Those two types cause most of the lost marks.

## Why the factors are what they are

The prefixes carry the meaning. **Kilo** means a thousand, **centi** a hundredth and **milli** a thousandth. So a kilometre is 1000 metres, a centimetre is one hundredth of a metre and a millilitre is one thousandth of a litre.

Area and volume units are built from lengths, so their factors are powers of the length factor:

| Length factor | Area factor (square it) | Volume factor (cube it) |
|---|---|---|
| mm ↔ cm: 10 | mm² ↔ cm²: 100 | mm³ ↔ cm³: 1000 |
| cm ↔ m: 100 | cm² ↔ m²: 10 000 | cm³ ↔ m³: 1 000 000 |
| m ↔ km: 1000 | m² ↔ km²: 1 000 000 | (km³ is not in the syllabus) |
| mm ↔ m: 1000 | mm² ↔ m²: 1 000 000 | mm³ ↔ m³: 1 000 000 000 |

The last row shows why you should not chain area or volume conversions in your head without care: going from mm³ to m³ divides by 10⁹.

The capacity link is **1 cm³ = 1 ml**. From it: 1 litre = 1000 cm³, and 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³ = 1000 litres.

## Two-step conversions

When two units are not next to each other, convert in steps through a unit you are sure of, or use the combined factor from the table.

### Worked example 1 (non-calculator)

Convert (a) 3.2 m² to mm², giving your answer in standard form; (b) 450 000 mm³ to litres; (c) 0.0075 km² to cm².

```
(a) m -> mm: length factor 1000, area factor 1000² = 1 000 000
    smaller unit, so multiply: 3.2 × 1 000 000 = 3 200 000 = 3.2 × 10⁶ mm²

(b) mm³ -> cm³: divide by 1000:  450 000 ÷ 1000 = 450 cm³
    cm³ -> ml: same number:        450 ml
    ml -> l: divide by 1000:       0.45 litres

(c) km² -> m²: × 1 000 000:        0.0075 × 1 000 000 = 7500 m²
    m² -> cm²: × 10 000:           7500 × 10 000 = 75 000 000 cm²
                                   = 7.5 × 10⁷ cm²
```

Standard form is the clearest way to write very large or very small answers, and on Extended papers it is often what the question asks for.

## Practical situations

The syllabus wants units used "in practical situations". In practice that means three habits.

1. **Make every quantity match** before you multiply or divide. A length in metres times a length in centimetres gives a meaningless number.
2. **Pick the working unit** that keeps numbers simple, then convert the final answer to the unit the question asks for.
3. **Round to fit the context**: "how many are needed" rounds up, "how many can be made" rounds down.

### Worked example 2 (non-calculator): rainfall on a roof

During a storm, 12 mm of rain falls on a flat roof measuring 8 m by 15 m. All the water drains into a tank. How many litres of water go into the tank?

```
Work in metres: 12 mm = 1.2 cm = 0.012 m
volume = 8 × 15 × 0.012 = 120 × 0.012 = 1.44 m³
1 m³ = 1000 litres, so 1.44 m³ = 1440 litres
```

The rain forms a very thin cuboid on the roof. Converting 12 mm to metres first is the step that matters; using 12 directly would give an answer a thousand times too big.

### Worked example 3 (calculator): a fish tank

A fish tank is a cuboid with internal measurements 60 cm long, 35 cm wide and 40 cm high. It is filled with water to 5 cm below the top, using a jug that holds 1.5 litres. How many full jugs are needed?

```
depth of water = 40 − 5 = 35 cm
volume = 60 × 35 × 35 = 73 500 cm³ = 73 500 ml = 73.5 litres
number of jugs = 73.5 ÷ 1.5 = 49
```

The division is exact here. If it had come to 48.3, you would need 49 jugs, because 48 would leave the tank short.

### Worked example 4 (non-calculator): rounding with mass

A 2 kg bag of rice is divided into portions of 75 g. How many full portions can be made, and how much rice is left over?

```
2 kg = 2000 g
2000 ÷ 75 = 26.66...
full portions = 26  (round down: a 27th portion is not complete)
left over = 2000 − 26 × 75 = 2000 − 1950 = 50 g
```

## Comparing quantities in different units

To order or compare, convert everything into **one** unit first.

### Worked example 5 (non-calculator)

Write these areas in order of size, smallest first: 0.05 m², 480 cm², 52 000 mm².

```
0.05 m²    = 0.05 × 10 000  = 500 cm²
480 cm²    = 480 cm²
52 000 mm² = 52 000 ÷ 100   = 520 cm²
Order: 480 cm², 0.05 m², 52 000 mm²
```

Give the answer in the **original** units, as the question lists them. Writing only the converted values does not answer the question.

## Choosing a sensible unit

Some questions ask for a suitable unit, or check that an answer is reasonable. Rough sizes help.

- About 1 mm: the thickness of a bank card. About 1 cm: the width of a fingernail. About 1 km: a 10 to 15 minute walk.
- About 1 ml: a few drops. About 1 litre: a large bottle of water.
- About 1 g: a paper clip. About 1 kg: a bag of sugar.

If a classroom floor comes out as 0.006 m² or 6 000 000 m², a conversion has gone wrong.

## Exam technique

- **"Convert"**, **"change"** and **"write … in …"** all want the number **and** the new unit.
- When a question mixes units, say which unit you are working in on the first line of your working.
- On Papers 1 and 2 (no calculator), multiply or divide by powers of 10 by moving digits, not by long multiplication.
- On Papers 3 and 4, give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise; unit conversions themselves are usually exact.

## Common mistakes

- Using the length factor for an area or volume: 1 m² is not 100 cm², and 1 m³ is not 100 cm³.
- Going the wrong way: converting to a smaller unit must give a bigger number.
- Treating 1 litre as 100 cm³ or 1 m³ as 100 litres.
- Multiplying measurements in different units without converting.
- Rounding a "how many are needed" answer down.
- Giving an ordered list in converted units instead of the original ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many litres are in a cubic metre?**
There are 1000 litres in 1 m³. This comes from 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³ and 1 litre = 1000 cm³.

**How do you convert cm² to m²?**
Divide by 10 000, because there are 100 cm in a metre and 100² = 10 000. Dividing by 100 uses the length factor, which is wrong for area.

**Are imperial units in 0580 subtopic 5.1?**
No. The syllabus notes list only metric units: mm, cm, m and km; their square and cube units; ml and l; and g and kg.
