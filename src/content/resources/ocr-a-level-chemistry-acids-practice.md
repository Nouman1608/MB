## Questions

**1.** (a) Give the formulae of ethanoic acid and of potassium hydroxide. **[2]**
**(b)** State the difference between a base and an alkali, and give the formula of one base that is not an alkali. **[2]**

**2.** Hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid solutions have the same concentration. Explain, in terms of dissociation, why hydrochloric acid is strong and ethanoic acid is weak. Then explain why 25.0 cm³ of each solution needs the same volume of one sodium hydroxide solution for neutralisation. **[4]**

**3.** Write full equations for these reactions.
**(a)** Copper(II) oxide with sulfuric acid. **[1]**
**(b)** Potassium carbonate with nitric acid. **[2]**
**(c)** Aqueous ammonia with sulfuric acid, forming a salt only. **[1]**
**(d)** The ionic equation common to every acid and alkali neutralisation. **[1]**

**4.** Calculate the minimum volume of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid needed to react completely with 1.50 g of calcium carbonate. Give your answer in cm³ to 3 significant figures. **[4]**

**5.** A technician makes 100.0 cm³ of a 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ standard solution of ethanedioic acid from the solid hydrate, H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O.
**(a)** Calculate the mass of the hydrate required. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how the solution is made, from the weighed solid to the finished solution. **[3]**

**6.** A student titrates 25.0 cm³ samples of an alkali with an acid in a burette. For each fault, state the effect on the titre (too high, too low, or no effect) and explain it.
**(a)** The pipette was rinsed with distilled water, not with the alkali, before use. **[2]**
**(b)** An air bubble in the burette jet at the start left the jet during the titration. **[2]**
**(c)** The conical flask was rinsed with distilled water before the alkali was added. **[1]**

**7.** 25.0 cm³ portions of sulfuric acid of unknown concentration were titrated with 0.150 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. The titres were 26.80 (rough), 24.60, 24.50 and 24.55 cm³.
**(a)** State the mean titre you would use and why. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the concentration of the sulfuric acid in mol dm⁻³, to 3 significant figures. **[3]**

**8.** Acid X is a solid that releases two H⁺ ions per molecule, written H₂X. A sample of 0.590 g of X was dissolved in water to give 250.0 cm³ of solution. One 25.0 cm³ portion needed 25.00 cm³ of 0.0400 mol dm⁻³ potassium hydroxide for neutralisation. Calculate the relative molecular mass of X. **[6]**

**9.** An antacid tablet of mass 2.00 g contains calcium carbonate as its only base. The tablet was crushed and 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid, an excess, was added. The mixture needed 20.00 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide to neutralise the acid left over. Calculate the percentage by mass of calcium carbonate in the tablet. **[7]**

**10.** A 25.0 cm³ sample of a cleaning solution containing ammonia was diluted to 250.0 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion of the diluted solution needed 21.30 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid. Calculate the concentration of ammonia in the cleaning solution in g dm⁻³, to 3 significant figures. **[6]**

**11.** A student compares 25.0 cm³ of 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid with 25.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid. Each is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide.
**(a)** Calculate the volume of sodium hydroxide needed for each acid. **[3]**
**(b)** The student concludes that ethanoic acid is the stronger acid because it needs more alkali. Explain why this conclusion is wrong. **[3]**
**(c)** Write the equation for ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide. **[1]**
**(d)** State how many times more ethanoic acid than hydrochloric acid is present in the two samples. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) CH₃COOH [1]; KOH [1]
**(b)** An alkali is a base that dissolves in water (a soluble base) [1]; a base such as CuO (or MgO or CaCO₃) is not an alkali because it is insoluble [1]
*Examiner insight:* Define an alkali as a soluble base. Saying "an alkali is a base" without the solubility point does not separate the two terms.

**2.** Hydrochloric acid is almost completely dissociated into H⁺ and Cl⁻ in water [1]; ethanoic acid is only partially dissociated, so most of it stays as CH₃COOH molecules in equilibrium with a little H⁺ [1]. Equal volumes of equal concentrations hold the same amount of acid, and each has one H⁺ to give per molecule [1]. As NaOH removes H⁺, more ethanoic acid dissociates, so all of it is eventually neutralised and the volume of NaOH is the same [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong and weak are about extent of dissociation; "higher pH" or "less reactive" does not explain it in those terms.

**3.** (a) CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O [1]
**(b)** K₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2KNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ [1] for correct formulae and products; [1] for balancing
**(c)** 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ [1]
**(d)** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check the charge balance in the salt formula before balancing; a wrong formula cannot be rescued by balancing.

**4.** CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ [1]
n(CaCO₃) = 1.50 / 100.1 = 0.01499 mol [1]
n(HCl) = 2 × 0.01499 = 0.02997 mol [1]
V = 0.02997 / 1.00 = 0.02997 dm³ = **30.0 cm³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the 1 : 2 ratio line; it is the step easiest to drop.

**5.** (a) M(hydrate) = 24.0 + 2.0 + 64.0 + 36.0 = 126.0 g mol⁻¹, including the two waters [1]; n = 0.1000 dm³ × 0.0800 = 0.00800 mol [1]; m = 0.00800 × 126.0 = **1.01 g** [1]
**(b)** Weigh the solid accurately, dissolve it in a little distilled water in a beaker, and transfer it to a 100.0 cm³ volumetric flask, washing the beaker and funnel into the flask with distilled water [1]. Fill until the lowest point of the meniscus sits on the graduation mark, adding the last drops with a dropping pipette [1]. Stopper and invert several times to mix [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use the molar mass of the hydrate, not of the anhydrous acid. Include the rinsing of the beaker and the meniscus on the mark.

**6.** (a) Titre too low [1]; water dilutes the alkali in the pipette, so the aliquot contains fewer moles of alkali and needs less acid [1]
**(b)** Titre too high [1]; the volume of the bubble is counted as delivered acid, but no acid was delivered by it [1]
**(c)** No effect [1], because the moles of alkali added to the flask are unchanged by water already in it
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the error and then the reason. A direction on its own, or a reason with no direction, is incomplete.

**7.** (a) Mean of 24.60, 24.50 and 24.55 = **24.55 cm³** [1]; the rough titre is excluded because it is not accurate, and the other three agree closely [1]
**(b)** H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O [1]
**(c)** n(NaOH) = 0.02455 × 0.150 = 3.68 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(H₂SO₄) = 3.6825 × 10⁻³ / 2 = 1.84 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; c = 1.84125 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = **0.0737 mol dm⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Keep extra figures until the last line and round once.

**8.** n(KOH) = 0.02500 × 0.0400 = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
H₂X + 2KOH → K₂X + 2H₂O, so the ratio is 1 : 2 [1]
n(H₂X) in 25.0 cm³ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1]
In 250.0 cm³: 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ × 10 = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
M = 0.590 / 5.00 × 10⁻³ [1] = **118 g mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For an unfamiliar acid, write the equation first, and show the scale-up from aliquot to flask as a separate step.

**9.** n(HCl added) = 0.0500 × 0.500 = 0.0250 mol [1]
n(NaOH) = 0.02000 × 0.500 = 0.0100 mol, equal to the HCl left over (1 : 1) [1]
n(HCl reacted with tablet) = 0.0250 − 0.0100 = 0.0150 mol [1]
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ [1]
n(CaCO₃) = 0.0150 / 2 = 0.00750 mol [1]
m = 0.00750 × 100.1 = 0.751 g [1]
Percentage = 0.751 / 2.00 × 100 = **37.5%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In a back titration, subtract the leftover from the total before using the ratio; treating the titre as the acid that reacted with the sample loses credit.

**10.** 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ [1]
n(H₂SO₄) = 0.02130 × 0.0500 = 1.065 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
n(NH₃) = 2 × 1.065 × 10⁻³ = 2.13 × 10⁻³ mol [1]
c(diluted) = 2.13 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.0852 mol dm⁻³ [1]
c(original) = 0.0852 × 10 = 0.852 mol dm⁻³ [1]
Mass concentration = 0.852 × 17.0 = **14.5 g dm⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the dilution factor as a ratio of volumes, 250.0 / 25.0, and say which solution each concentration refers to.

**11.** (a) n(HCl) = 0.0250 × 0.0100 = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol, so V(NaOH) = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.100 = **2.50 cm³** **[1] [1]**; n(CH₃COOH) = 0.0250 × 0.150 = 3.75 × 10⁻³ mol, so V(NaOH) = **37.5 cm³** [1]
**(b)** The volume of alkali depends on the amount of acid present, which is concentration × volume, and not on strength [1]. Ethanoic acid is the weak acid: it is only partially dissociated [1]. The hydrochloric acid is strong, completely dissociated, and needs less alkali only because it is much more dilute [1]
**(c)** CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O [1]
**(d)** 3.75 × 10⁻³ / 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ = **15 times** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link "needs more alkali" to moles of acid, then say strength is a separate property.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a weak acid as "low concentration" or "less acidic" rather than "partially dissociated".
- Leaving out CO₂ in a carbonate equation, or giving water as a product of ammonia with an acid.
- Missing the 1 : 2 ratio for sulfuric acid with a monohydroxide, or for calcium carbonate with hydrochloric acid.
- Averaging the rough titre in with the accurate ones.
- Forgetting to scale from the 25.0 cm³ aliquot to the full volume of solution made up.
- Using the anhydrous molar mass for a hydrate.
- Subtracting the wrong way in a back titration, or using titre moles directly.
