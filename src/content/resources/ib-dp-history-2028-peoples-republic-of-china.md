This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The People's Republic of China (1949 to 2020) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers how Mao Zedong consolidated and kept power, the economic and social changes of his rule, his foreign policy, and then political, economic and foreign developments from 1976 to 2020.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-peoples-republic-of-china-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-peoples-republic-of-china-practice/).

## Mao's consolidation and maintenance of power (1949 to 1976)

**Control of the CCP and the PLA.** The People's Republic was proclaimed on 1 October 1949. The Communist Party controlled the state at every level, and the People's Liberation Army (PLA) was an instrument of the Party, not of the government. Mao's prestige as the victor of the revolution made him the dominant figure. Rivals such as Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai held high office but depended on his approval.

**Early campaigns.** Campaigns were used to remove enemies and teach loyalty. The campaign against counter-revolutionaries (1950 to 1951) killed many former Nationalists and others. The **Three-Antis** campaign (against corruption, waste and bureaucracy) and the **Five-Antis** campaign (against bribery, tax evasion and fraud among business people) of 1951 to 1952 brought private business under state pressure.

**The Hundred Flowers Campaign (1956).** Mao invited intellectuals to speak: "Let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend". In spring 1957 criticism of the Party poured out. The leadership reacted with the **Anti-Rightist Campaign**, which labelled about half a million people as "rightists", costing them their jobs and often their freedom. Historians debate whether Mao set a trap or was shocked by the criticism.

**The Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution (1966 to 1976).** After the failure of the Great Leap Forward, Mao lost some influence to pragmatists such as Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping. In 1966 he called on young people, organised as **Red Guards**, to attack "bourgeois" elements and "the Four Olds" (old customs, culture, habits and ideas). Teachers, officials and intellectuals were humiliated, imprisoned or killed. Liu Shaoqi was purged and died in custody in 1969. Mao's **cult of personality** reached its height: his sayings in the "Little Red Book" were studied by everyone. By 1968 the violence had become so great that the army was used to restore order, and millions of urban youth were sent to the countryside. Defence Minister Lin Biao, named as Mao's successor, died in a plane crash in 1971 after an alleged plot. The radical "Gang of Four", including Mao's wife Jiang Qing, remained influential until his death in September 1976.

## Economic and social transformations under Mao (1949 to 1976)

**Land reform and the First Five-Year Plan.** The Land Reform Movement (1949 to 1952) redistributed land from landlords to peasants. Millions were classified by class, and many landlords were killed, with estimates in the range of one to two million. This won peasant support. The **First Five-Year Plan** (1953 to 1957), with Soviet help, stressed heavy industry and led to strong growth. Farming was collectivised in 1955 to 1956.

**The Great Leap Forward (1958 to 1962).** Mao aimed to overtake Britain in steel and grain production within years. Farms were merged into huge communes, backyard furnaces made poor-quality iron, and local officials reported inflated harvests to please the centre. The state took grain based on these false figures. The result was the worst famine in history, in which, depending on method, estimates of deaths run from about 15 million to over 40 million. Critics such as Peng Dehuai at the Lushan Conference (1959) were purged. Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping later reduced the communes' role, and recovery began in 1962.

**Changing role of women.** The **Marriage Law of 1950** banned arranged and child marriages and concubinage, and allowed divorce. Women were urged to enter the workforce, and the slogan said that "women hold up half the sky". Women's organisations spread, though most leaders were men and household duties stayed with women.

**Education, healthcare and religion.** Literacy rose sharply through mass campaigns. "Barefoot doctors" brought basic care to villages, and life expectancy rose markedly between 1949 and 1976. Religion was placed under state control: foreign missionaries were expelled, Tibet came under Chinese control from 1950 with revolt in 1959, and temples and churches were attacked during the Cultural Revolution.

## Foreign policy successes and failures under Mao (1949 to 1976)

- **Korean War (1950 to 1953).** China entered in October 1950, pushed UN forces back, and fought to a stalemate. This raised its prestige, but cost heavy casualties and left it dependent on the Soviet Union and shut out by the USA.
- **Sino-Soviet split.** Relations were close in the 1950s (the 1950 treaty of alliance). Differences grew after Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin in 1956, Soviet reluctance to share nuclear technology and disagreements over the Great Leap. Soviet advisers were withdrawn in 1960, and the split became open by 1961. In 1969 there were border clashes on the Ussuri River. China tested its own atomic bomb in 1964.
- **Non-aligned movement.** Zhou Enlai presented China at Bandung (1955) as a leader of the newly independent nations, with the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence.
- **Sino-Indian War (1962).** After a dispute over the border and Tibet, Chinese forces defeated Indian forces in October to November 1962, then withdrew in part. The war damaged relations for decades.
- **Relations with the USA.** After 1969, fear of the USSR led China towards the USA. "Ping-pong diplomacy" in April 1971, when an American table tennis team visited, opened the way. Kissinger visited secretly in July 1971, China took the UN seat in October 1971, and President Nixon visited in February 1972.

## Impact of political developments (1976 to 2020)

**Succession after Mao.** Hua Guofeng, Mao's chosen successor, arrested the Gang of Four in October 1976. But **Deng Xiaoping**, who had been purged twice, returned and by December 1978 led the Party. He rejected Mao's class struggle for economic growth.

**Tiananmen Square (1989).** The death of the reformer Hu Yaobang in April 1989 sparked student demonstrations demanding action on corruption and political reform. They were joined by workers and citizens. Martial law was declared in May, and on 3 to 4 June 1989 the army cleared the square, killing many people (estimates range from hundreds to thousands). Party chief Zhao Ziyang was removed. The leadership chose repression and continued economic reform.

**Jiang Zemin and the Three Represents.** Jiang became General Secretary in 1989. His theory of the **Three Represents** (around 2000) said that the Party should represent advanced productive forces, advanced culture and the interests of the majority, which allowed business people to join.

**Xi Jinping.** Xi became General Secretary in 2012 and President in 2013. He launched a large anti-corruption campaign, promoted the "Chinese Dream", strengthened Party control over society, the media and the internet, and in 2018 abolished the limit on presidential terms.

## Economic and social transformations after 1976

- **Four Modernizations and Opening Up.** The policy aimed to modernise agriculture, industry, defence, and science and technology. From 1979 communes were replaced by the household responsibility system, which let farmers keep surplus crops. Special Economic Zones such as Shenzhen (1980) attracted foreign investment.
- **Emergence as an economic power.** Growth averaged near 10 per cent a year for decades. China joined the World Trade Organization in December 2001 after years of negotiation, and became the world's second-largest economy around 2010. Hundreds of millions escaped extreme poverty.
- **Rural–urban divide and migration.** Cities and coastal regions boomed, while inland and rural areas lagged. The household registration (*hukou*) system limited access to urban services, so over 200 million migrant workers lived in cities without full rights.
- **Population control.** The **one-child policy** (from about 1980) cut the birth rate but led to forced abortions, an imbalance between boys and girls, and an ageing population. It was relaxed in 2016 to two children.

## Foreign policy successes and failures (1976 to 2020)

- **Regional relations.** China went to war with Vietnam in 1979, took back Hong Kong in 1997 under "one country, two systems", and had disputes in the South China Sea and with Taiwan.
- **USSR and Russia.** Relations normalised in 1989 when Gorbachev visited Beijing. China and Russia settled their borders and in 2001 signed a friendship treaty.
- **USA.** Full diplomatic relations began in January 1979. Trade grew hugely, but tensions over Taiwan, human rights, technology and trade deepened, especially after 2018.
- **Belt and Road Initiative.** Announced by Xi in 2013, it financed ports, railways and roads across Eurasia, Africa and elsewhere. It raised China's influence but also concerns about debt.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Frank Dikötter and Yang Jisheng hold that Mao's policies caused the famine, and that officials hid it. Jung Chang and Jon Halliday portray Mao as a tyrant, but many historians have criticised the book's use of evidence.
- The Party's own 1981 resolution judged Mao's contributions as mainly positive but the Cultural Revolution a disaster.
- Ezra Vogel's study of Deng praises his pragmatism, while others stress the repression of 1989.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent was Mao's maintenance of power in China between 1949 and 1976 based on terror?"** Weigh campaigns, the Cultural Revolution and censorship against nationalism, land reform, social gains, the cult of personality and the Party's control of the army. Judge that terror was important, but belief and real gains also mattered.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Mao's rule as a single uniform period.
- Using famine figures as exact.
- Forgetting the continuity between Mao and the post-1976 Party's control.
- Ignoring foreign policy.
