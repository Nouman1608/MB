Every figure is invented, set in Country E (currency: the E-unit) and Country F (the F-unit). Prices are quoted as F-units per E-unit. ## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a freely floating exchange rate system. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two reasons why demand for the E-unit might rise. **[4]**

**3.** In the market for the E-unit, demand is D = 220 − 80P and supply is S = 20 + 120P, where P is the price in F-units and quantities are in millions of E-units a week.
**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Foreign demand for E's exports falls, and demand becomes D = 180 − 80P. Calculate the new equilibrium price and the percentage change in the value of the E-unit. **[3]**

**4.** Country E now fixes its rate at the original equilibrium of 1.00, but demand falls as in question 3(b). Its central bank holds reserves of 120 million F-units.
**(a)** Calculate the weekly excess supply of E-units at 1.00. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many weeks the bank can defend the rate by buying E-units. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a rise in interest rates could support the value of the E-unit, and state one cost of doing so. **[4]**

**6.** The rate falls from 1.50 to 1.35 F-units per E-unit. An E export is priced at 600 E-units. An F import is priced at 405 F-units.
**(a)** Calculate the F-unit price of the export before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the E-unit price of the import before and after, and the percentage rise. **[3]**

**7.** Distinguish between a devaluation and a depreciation. **[3]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that a small, open economy should fix its exchange rate. **[9]**

**9.** An exporter in a country about to join a currency union sells 8 million F-units of goods a year. Conversion margins cost 1.5% of this, and hedging costs 0.5%. Annual profit is 2.5 million. Calculate the yearly saving and express it as a percentage of profit. **[3]**

**10.** A currency union sets one interest rate of 2%. Member X has inflation of 4% and Member Y has 0%.
**(a)** Calculate the exact real interest rate in each member. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one problem this creates for the union. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a country with high inflation and a weak currency would gain from joining a currency union. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Market buyers and sellers of the currency alone fix its price [1], with no official target and no routine central bank action [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers earn more when they name both halves: how the price is set and the absence of a target.

**2.** First reason: foreign buyers want more of E's exports [1], so they need more E-units to pay for them, which shifts demand right [1]. Second reason: higher interest rates in E [1] attract foreign savings, which also need E-units [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason usually gains more credit when it is followed by the link to the demand curve, not just named.

**3. (a)** Set D = S: 220 − 80P = 20 + 120P [1], so 200 = 200P. **P = 1.00 F-unit, Q = 140 million** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 180 − 80P = 20 + 120P gives 160 = 200P [1]. **P = 0.80** [1]. Change = (0.80 / 1.00 − 1) × 100 = **−20%**, a depreciation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write out the step where demand is set equal to supply, so that a slip later in the arithmetic still leaves visible working.

**4. (a)** At 1.00: demand = 180 − 80 = 100 [1]; supply = 20 + 120 = 140, so excess supply is **40 million E-units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The bank buys 40 million E-units at 1.00, paying 40 million F-units a week [1]. 120 / 40 = **3 weeks** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the excess supply into the currency that leaves the reserves before dividing.

**5.** Higher interest rates make E-unit deposits more attractive [1], so foreign savers buy E-units and demand shifts right, raising the rate [1]. A cost is that borrowing becomes dearer [1], which reduces consumption and investment and can lower growth and raise unemployment [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A cost explained through to a macro objective tends to earn more than the bare word "inflation" or "unemployment".

**6. (a)** Before: 600 × 1.50 = 900 F-units [1]. After: 600 × 1.35 = **810 F-units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 405 / 1.50 = 270 E-units [1]. After: 405 / 1.35 = 300 E-units [1]. Rise = (300 / 270 − 1) × 100 = **11.1%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide to convert foreign prices into home currency and multiply the other way; mixing them up is a common slip.

**7.** A devaluation is a deliberate cut by the government in the official rate of a fixed system [1]. A depreciation is a fall in the market-determined rate in a floating system [1], caused by changes in demand and supply, not by an official decision [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the system each term belongs to is what separates the two definitions.

**8.** Case for: a peg gives certainty to trade and investment [1], which matters when a small economy relies on trade [1]. It can import low inflation if pegged to a stable partner [1]. Case against: interest rates must follow the peg, not the home economy [1]. Reserves are limited, and a peg thought to be wrong invites speculative attack [1]. Chain: a small economy with few reserves defends the rate by raising interest rates, which can cause a recession [1]. Floating allows depreciation to restore competitiveness, though import prices rise [1]. Evaluation: it depends on how closely trade is concentrated on one partner, and on the credibility and reserves of the central bank [1]. Judgement: pegging suits an economy with concentrated trade and strong reserves; otherwise floating or a managed float is safer [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that reach a conditional judgement, saying when the claim holds, usually do better than lists of advantages.

**9.** Conversion cost: 8,000,000 × 0.015 = 120,000 [1]. Hedging: 8,000,000 × 0.005 = 40,000, so the saving is **160,000 a year** [1]. As a share of profit: 160,000 / 2,500,000 = **6.4%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Add both cost lines before the percentage; an answer that uses one cost only will not match.

**10. (a)** Member X: 1.02 / 1.04 − 1 = **−1.9%** [1]. Member Y: 1.02 / 1.00 − 1 = **+2.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The union rate is below inflation in X, so borrowing is cheap in real terms [1] and may fuel a boom and further inflation [1]. In Y the real rate is positive, which restrains spending [1] and may leave growth weak [1]. One rate cannot suit both, and neither member can set its own rate or adjust its exchange rate. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison of two members makes the one-rate problem concrete, which a general statement does not.

**11.** Gains: the weak currency and high inflation may signal low credibility [1]. Joining a union with a credible central bank can bring lower inflation expectations [1] and lower interest rates [1]. There are no conversion costs or exchange rate risk [1], which can raise trade and FDI [1]. Losses: the country cannot depreciate to regain competitiveness [1]. If its prices stay high it will have to cut costs by internal devaluation [1], with unemployment and lower output [1]. It gives up an independent interest rate [1], and the union rate may not suit it [1]. Evaluation: much depends on whether it can bring inflation down first, and on flexibility of wages [1]. Judgement: if it has failed to control inflation alone, the credibility gain may outweigh the loss, but only if wages and prices can adjust quickly [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* A stronger answer ties the judgement to the country's own starting point, high inflation, rather than giving a general view of unions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the vertical axis for the wrong currency and shifting the wrong curve.
- Writing "the currency appreciates" when the system is fixed and the term needed is revaluation.
- Skipping the line where demand equals supply in a calculation.
- Stating that intervention is free, with no mention of reserves.
- Giving a cost of higher interest rates without linking it to a macroeconomic objective.
- Calculating a percentage change of the wrong currency.
- Listing currency union points without applying them to the country in the question.
- Closing an evaluation with a restatement but no supported judgement.
