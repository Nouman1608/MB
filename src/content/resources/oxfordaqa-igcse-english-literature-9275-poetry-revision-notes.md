## What the marks reward

| AO | In the anthology question | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Understanding of, and engagement with, themes, ideas and contexts | Poems that genuinely fit the question, and a precise view of what each says about it |
| AO2 | How poets create meanings and effects through language, form and structure | Method and effect in both poems within each paragraph |
| AO3 | An informed personal response, appropriate terminology, coherent and accurate writing | An argued comparison, organised point by point, not two essays side by side |

## Thinking in themes

An anthology about people and places invites strands such as identity, belonging and displacement,
memory, the bond between a person and a landscape, and change over time. Most poems fit more than one
strand, which is what makes flexible pairings possible. For each strand, know which poems belong to
it and, more importantly, **how their attitudes differ**: nostalgic or bitter, celebratory or
uneasy, certain or questioning.

Context earns credit when it explains a choice in the poem: a poem written from exile, for example,
may use the present tense for a remembered place to show that it is still vivid. A biography with no
link to the words on the page does not.

## The comparison formula

**Shared idea → difference in view → difference in method → effect on the reader.**

Fix in advance, for each pairing: one real similarity, one real difference and three short
quotations from each poem.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Speaker / persona | The voice of the poem, not necessarily the poet |
| Stanza | A group of lines, like a paragraph in poetry |
| Enjambment | A sentence running over a line or stanza break |
| Caesura | A pause in the middle of a line |
| Volta | A turn in thought or tone |
| Free verse | Poetry without regular rhyme or metre |
| Dramatic monologue | One speaker addressing a silent listener |
| Imagery | Language appealing to the senses, including simile and metaphor |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting ideas or images side by side |
| Tone | The attitude the voice conveys |
| Comparative connectives | "Similarly", "whereas", "in contrast", "both" |

## A pairing card in practice

Write one card per pairing and keep it to five lines:

- **Theme:** the shared idea, phrased as a question (for example, "Does the place make the person?").
- **Similarity:** what both poems agree on.
- **Difference:** where their attitudes split.
- **Method contrast:** one choice of voice, imagery or form in each poem that creates that split.
- **Evidence:** three short quotations per poem, each two to five words long.

Because the anthology is open in the exam, the card is for thinking, not for memorising quotations
word for word: it tells you where to look and what to argue.

## Worked mini-comparison

The two extracts are original lines written for these notes, not anthology poems. Imagine they open
two poems about a remembered place.

> **Poem M:** *Grandmother's yard is smaller now, / the mango tree a stump I could sit on.*
>
> **Poem N:** *This city keeps no promises; / still, I know which stair creaks in the dark.*

**Annotated model paragraph:**

> Both speakers return to a place that has shaped them, but where Poem M measures loss, Poem N
> discovers belonging in spite of itself. **[AO1 and AO3: comparative argument in one sentence]**
> In Poem M the comparative "smaller" shows the adult speaker seeing the yard with new eyes, and the
> image of the mango tree reduced to "a stump" turns a symbol of childhood growth into something
> cut down, perhaps suggesting that the past cannot be recovered. **[AO2: language and imagery in
> Poem M]** Poem N, by contrast, opens with a bitter generalisation, yet the semicolon and the turn
> on "still" introduce an intimate, almost tender detail: the speaker knows the city's sounds by
> heart. **[AO2: punctuation and volta in Poem N]** Whereas Poem M's place has shrunk while the
> speaker grew, Poem N's speaker has absorbed the place into memory, so the first poem mourns a
> distance and the second admits a closeness it did not want. **[AO3: comparative judgement that
> develops the argument]**

Notice that each sentence either compares or prepares a comparison, and that structure (the
semicolon turn) is analysed as well as imagery.

## Organising the answer

- Introduction: one sentence naming both poems and your argument.
- Three paragraphs, **each covering both poems**: for example voice, imagery, form or structure.
- Conclusion: return to the difference that matters most.

## Revision plan

1. Sort the 20 poems into four or five theme groups.
2. Write a profile for each poem: speaker, theme, form, key language, context, links.
3. Prepare three or four flexible pairings with a clear similarity and difference.
4. Practise one 45-minute comparison per week using the open anthology.

## Exam traps

- Two separate mini-essays.
- Theme comparison with no analysis of method.
- Long quotations.
- Biography instead of analysis.
- Route A: overrunning and squeezing the unseen sections.
- Choosing a second poem that is almost identical, leaving nothing to contrast.
- Treating the speaker as the poet in every poem.
- Spending the introduction on background instead of the comparative argument.
- A conclusion that summarises instead of judging which difference matters most and why.

## Self-test

1. How many poems are in *People and Places*?
2. How many marks is the anthology question worth on each route?
3. What does the Route B paper contain besides the anthology question?
4. What does "enjambment" mean?
5. Why should you choose a partner poem that contrasts within the theme?
6. Name two comparative connectives.
7. Which assessment objective is most affected by writing two separate mini-essays, and why?
8. Name three thematic strands that suit an anthology about people and places.
9. When does context earn credit in the anthology answer?
10. In the worked comparison, what does the semicolon and "still" do in Poem N?
11. What five things should a pairing card record?
12. Is the anthology supplied in the exam, and how should that change your revision?

**Answers:** 1. 20. 2. 30 marks on both routes. 3. One unseen poetry question (30 marks). 4. A
sentence running on over a line or stanza break. 5. A contrast in how the theme is treated gives you
more to compare in method and meaning. 6. Any two of: similarly, whereas, in contrast, both, likewise,
however. 7. AO3, because it rewards an organised, argued comparison; separate essays also weaken the
comparison of methods under AO2. 8. Any three of: identity, belonging and displacement, memory, the
bond between a person and a landscape, change over time. 9. When it explains a choice in the poem,
not when it is a separate biography. 10. They create a turn from a bitter general statement to an
intimate detail, showing an unwanted sense of belonging. 11. Theme, similarity, difference, method
contrast and evidence (three short quotations per poem). 12. Yes; revise to find and choose evidence
quickly and to know the pairings, rather than memorising poems word for word.
