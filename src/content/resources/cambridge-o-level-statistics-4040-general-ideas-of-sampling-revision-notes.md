These notes condense section 1.1 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: general ideas of sampling, including the terms population, census, sample and representative sample. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [1.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-general-ideas-of-sampling/). For exam-style questions, use the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-general-ideas-of-sampling-practice/). Sampling methods are in the [types of sampling revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-types-of-sampling-revision-notes/).

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population | The whole set of people or items an investigation is about |
| Member | One person or item in the population |
| Census | Data collected from every member of the population |
| Sample | Data collected from part of the population |
| Representative sample | A sample with the same characteristics as the population, in roughly the same proportions |
| Estimate | A value for the population worked out from a sample; not exact |

## Defining a population

Answer three questions:

- **Who or what?** pupils, households, batteries, fish
- **Where?** one school, one town, one factory, one lake
- **When?** a date, a term, a year

Example: "all households in Northfield on 1 March 2026".

## Census or sample?

| Choose a census when... | Choose a sample when... |
|---|---|
| The population is small | The population is large |
| Exact information about every member is needed | An estimate is good enough |
| Every member can be reached | Some members are hard to reach or the population changes |
| Testing does not destroy items | Testing destroys the items (lifetimes, breaking strength) |
| Time and money are available | Results are needed quickly or cheaply |

## Advantages and disadvantages of a sample

**Advantages:** cheaper; quicker; less data to process; the only option for destructive tests; fewer, well-trained collectors can reduce recording errors.

**Disadvantages:** results are estimates; different samples give different results; may not be representative; small groups in the population may be missed.

## Representative samples

- Same proportions as the population for characteristics that matter to the investigation (age, gender, year group, area).
- Check by comparing percentages in the sample with percentages in the population.
- A large sample is not automatically representative; where it comes from matters.
- Random selection methods (section 1.2) help; sources of bias (section 1.3) work against it.

## Method in steps

### Checking whether a sample is representative

1. Work out the percentage of the population in each group.
2. Work out the percentage of the sample in each group.
3. Compare: name any group that is over- or under-represented.

### Estimating a population figure from a sample

1. Find the proportion in the sample.
2. Multiply by the population size.
3. State that the answer is an estimate.

## Small worked reminders

**Representativeness.** Club of 500: 300 adults (60%), 200 juniors (40%). Sample of 50 has 40 adults (80%), 10 juniors (20%). Adults are over-represented; a representative sample would have about 30 adults and 20 juniors.

**Estimating a total.** In a sample of 80 pupils, 24 walk to school. School has 1,200 pupils. Estimate = 24/80 × 1200 = 360 pupils.

**Estimating from a mean.** A sample of trees in an orchard of 450 trees gives a mean of 3.4 kg of fruit per tree. Estimated total = 3.4 × 450 = 1,530 kg.

## Reading the question

- "Define" or "state": a precise definition, no example needed unless asked.
- "Give a reason": tie it to the situation (size, cost, time, destruction).
- "Explain why the sample may not be representative": name the group and the direction (over or under).
- "Estimate": show the proportion and the multiplication.

## Must-know distinctions

- A population is not always people.
- A census is not always more accurate in practice.
- Sample size and representativeness are different ideas.
- An estimate from a sample is not exact.
- The population is what you want to know about; the sample is what you actually measure.

## Exam traps

- Vague populations ("people", "students") with no place or time.
- "A sample is cheaper" as the reason for every situation; use the context.
- Thinking a bigger sample fixes a badly chosen one.
- Forgetting that destructive testing rules out a census.
- Rounding an estimate to look exact, or not saying it is an estimate.

## Quick self-test

1. Define population.
2. Define census.
3. Give two reasons why a factory testing the strength of ropes uses a sample.
4. Give one situation in which a census is sensible.
5. What is a representative sample?
6. A population is 70% female. A sample of 40 contains 20 females. Is it representative of gender?
7. In a sample of 150 phones, 6 are faulty. Estimate the number faulty in a delivery of 4,000 phones.
8. Why is your answer to question 7 an estimate?
9. Give one advantage of a census over a sample.
10. Write a precise population for an investigation into the reading habits of pupils at your school this year.
11. True or false: doubling the size of a sample guarantees it is representative.
12. Why might a sample be more accurate than a census in practice?

### Answers

1. The whole set of people or items an investigation is about.
2. Collecting data from every member of the population.
3. Testing strength breaks the rope, so testing all of them would leave none to sell; a sample is also quicker and cheaper.
4. A small population where every member can be reached, such as a class of 25 pupils.
5. A sample whose characteristics are in roughly the same proportions as in the population.
6. No: 50% female in the sample compared with 70% in the population, so females are under-represented.
7. 6/150 × 4000 = 160 phones.
8. It is based on part of the population; another sample would probably give a different figure.
9. It gives complete information about every member, not an estimate.
10. For example: "all pupils enrolled at the school during the 2026 academic year".
11. False: size does not fix where the sample came from.
12. Fewer data collectors can be better trained and take more care, so recording errors are reduced.
