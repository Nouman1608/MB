Unit 3 of Pearson Edexcel International A Level English Literature (YET01) has two sections: a compulsory
20-mark essay on an unseen post-1900 poem (AO1, AO2) and a 30-mark comparative essay on two prose texts
from one theme (AO1–AO4). The poem below is an original poem written for this resource, in the manner of
a modern free-verse lyric. Section B gives one comparative question per theme, each with indicative
content.

## Section A: Poetry (20 marks)

**1.** Read the poem below. Explore the poet's presentation of memory and change in this poem. In your
answer you should consider the poet's use of language, form and structure. **[20]**

> **Clearance**
>
> They took the orchard down in March,
> the month my father would have pruned it.
> Men in bright jackets, a yellow machine
> patient as a cow at the gate.
>
> By noon the ground was opened,
> roots lifted like the hands of the drowned
> asking for nothing. I stood where the swing
> had been, and the air was the wrong shape.
>
> They will build forty houses here.
> Children will grow up in kitchens
> standing where the old pear leaned,
> and never know they are inside a tree.
>
> I keep one branch. It does not flower.
> I keep it anyway, the way you keep
> a voice on an answering machine
> long after the number has changed.

## Section B: Prose (30 marks)

Answer **one** question, on your chosen theme. In your answer you must compare your two texts and
consider relevant contextual factors.

**2. Theme 1: Growing Up.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts present the
influence of adults on a child's understanding of the world. **[30]**

**3. Theme 2: Colonisation and After.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
present the experience of being an outsider. **[30]**

**4. Theme 3: Science and Society.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
present the human cost of scientific or social control. **[30]**

**5. Theme 4: Women and Society.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
present the effects of the past on women's lives. **[30]**

## Indicative content

The points below are a guide to the range of relevant material, not a checklist. Credit any
well-supported response that answers the question.

### Question 1 ("Clearance")

**AO1: informed personal response, terminology, expression**
- The speaker watches an orchard, linked to a dead father, being cleared for housing; memory survives
  only in the speaker and in one kept branch.
- A personal reading might see the poem as elegy for both father and place, or as a calm acceptance that
  change erases private meaning.
- Accurate terminology: free verse, quatrains, enjambment, simile, metaphor, volta, elegy.

**AO2: how meanings are shaped**
- *Form:* four unrhymed quatrains give a controlled, almost reportorial shape to grief; free verse keeps
  the tone conversational.
- *Opening:* the plain statement "They took the orchard down in March" is immediately undercut by the
  second line, which brings the absent father into the poem.
- *Imagery:* the machine "patient as a cow at the gate" makes destruction seem gentle, even pastoral;
  the roots "like the hands of the drowned / asking for nothing" give the trees a passive, human grief.
- *Absence made physical:* "the air was the wrong shape" turns a missing tree and swing into a
  distortion of space itself.
- *Structure and time:* stanza 3 moves into the future tense; the image "inside a tree" folds the past into
  the new houses without their inhabitants knowing.
- *Ending:* the final simile of the answering-machine voice moves memory into modern technology, and
  the repeated "I keep" suggests attachment that the speaker knows is irrational.

**What separates stronger answers.** Weaker answers paraphrase stanza by stanza. Stronger answers trace a
shift (from observation to private memory to the future to an act of keeping) and explain how the
calm tone makes the loss more painful. Remember that context and comparison earn nothing in Section A.

**Model paragraph.** "The poem's grief is muted by its form. Four unrhymed quatrains and a level,
observational voice report the clearance as if it were routine, and the similes soften the violence: the
machine is 'patient as a cow'. The one line that breaks this calm is 'the air was the wrong shape', where
the speaker can describe the loss only as a fault in space itself. The poem's restraint makes this
single disturbance feel larger than any open lament."

**What separates stronger Section B answers.** Integrated comparison in every paragraph, context used to
explain a writer's choice, and analysis of narrative method rather than plot. A top response sustains a
single comparative argument across the essay.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should I spend on the unseen poem?**
It is worth 20 of 50 marks, so about 45–50 minutes is proportionate, including reading and planning time.

**Can I compare the unseen poem with poems from Unit 1?**
No. Section A rewards AO1 and AO2 on the printed poem only; comparison is assessed in Section B.

**Should my Section B essay give equal space to both texts?**
Broadly, yes. AO4 is about exploring connections, so a lopsided essay limits how far you can compare.
