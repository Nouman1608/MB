## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between primary and secondary tourism resources, giving one example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why sport tourism draws on both sport and tourism as fields of study. **[2]**

**3.** State two sources of data used to measure leisure participation. **[2]**

**4.** Outline one reason why participation surveys may understate leisure among low-income groups. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by leisure spending being income-elastic. **[2]**

**6.** Suggest two national-scale factors that influence how much leisure time people have. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows the average hours of leisure per week reported in a time-use survey of an invented country.

| Year | Home-based leisure (hours) | Away-from-home leisure (hours) |
|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 18 | 12 |
| 2020 | 24 | 9 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in home-based leisure between 2000 and 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the share of total leisure that was home-based in 2020. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons for the change in the balance between home-based and away-from-home leisure. **[2]**

**8.** In an invented country, 62% of adults in the capital city and 38% of adults in rural districts did sport at least once a week.

**(a)** State the difference in percentage points. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons for this difference. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one limitation of the measure "at least once a week". **[2]**

**9.** Explain how economic development can change the nature of sport participation in a country. **[4]**

**10.** Using examples, explain how investment in secondary resources can change a leisure pattern. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Examine the reasons why participation in sport varies between countries. **[10]**

**12.** "Technology has changed leisure patterns more than any other factor." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary resources are the attractions themselves, such as a coastline, mountains or a heritage site [1]; secondary resources are the facilities that make use of them possible, such as hotels, airports or ski lifts [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The motivation for travel is sport, either playing or watching [1], but the journey and stay create tourism geography: flows, destination facilities and impacts on host places [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two, for example: time-use diaries [1]; participation surveys [1]; club membership or ticket data [1]; arrivals or bed-night data [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**4.** Surveys often record formal, paid activities such as gym membership or club sport [1], while free, informal activities such as walking or street games, more common among lower-income groups, are missed or under-reported [1]. **[2]**

**5.** As incomes rise, spending on leisure rises proportionally faster than income [1], because leisure is not an essential, so it grows quickly in boom periods and falls sharply in recessions [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two, for example: laws on maximum working hours [1]; statutory paid holidays [1]; the number of public holidays [1]; school calendars that fix family holiday periods [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Change = 24 - 18 = 6 hours [1]. 6 / 18 x 100 = **33.3% increase** [1].

**(b)** Total = 24 + 9 = 33 hours; 24 / 33 x 100 = **72.7%** [1].

**(c)** Any two, for example: streaming, gaming and social media have made home entertainment cheaper and more varied [1]; rising costs of travel or venues reduce trips out [1]; an ageing population favours home-based activity [1]; a shock such as a pandemic shifted habits towards the home [1]. Maximum [2]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** 62 - 38 = **24 percentage points** [1].

**(b)** Any two, for example: more facilities such as gyms, pools and clubs within easy reach in the capital [1]; higher average incomes in the capital [1]; a younger population in the capital [1]; longer travel distances and poorer public transport in rural districts [1]. Maximum [2].

**(c)** It counts a single short activity the same as daily training [1], so two places with the same percentage could have very different total amounts of activity [1]. **[5]**

**9.** As incomes rise, more sport moves from informal play into organised clubs, leagues and gyms (formalisation) [1]. More sport is paid for, through membership, equipment and facilities (commercialisation) [1]. Spectatorship, both live and through broadcasting, tends to grow faster than playing [1]. At the same time, sedentary work and car travel may reduce overall physical activity, so participation in organised sport can rise while activity in daily life falls [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Secondary resources make primary resources usable [1]. For example, building ski lifts and resort accommodation converts a snowy mountain into a winter sports destination [1]; opening an airport with low-cost routes can turn a quiet coastline into a mass-tourism resort [1]. The pattern of who visits, from where and in which season changes as a result, even though the primary resource has not changed [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content; credit a structured, evaluative answer:

- **Income and cost:** wealthier countries have more paid facilities and households can afford equipment and membership [1].
- **Climate and physical geography:** snow, mountains and coastlines make some sports possible, such as skiing or surfing [1].
- **Culture and history:** some sports are tied to national identity or were diffused through historical links, such as cricket in former British colonies [1].
- **Government policy:** physical education in schools, public facilities and health campaigns raise participation [1] [1].
- **Demographic structure:** youthful populations favour active team sports; ageing populations favour gentler activity [1].
- **Gender norms and safety:** restrictions on women's use of public space reduce participation in some countries [1].
- **Data issues:** countries measure participation differently, so comparisons are uncertain [1].
- **Interaction:** physical geography sets what is possible, but income, policy and culture decide who takes part [1].
- **Judgement:** a supported conclusion, for example that income and policy explain most of the variation between countries of similar climate [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Technology as a driver:** streaming, gaming and social media have expanded home-based leisure [1]; online booking and comparison sites made independent travel easier [1].
- **Technology in transport:** jet aircraft and low-cost airline models drove time-space compression [1].
- **Social media effects:** images of particular sites concentrate visitors [1].
- **Counter-argument, income:** without rising disposable income, technology would not translate into more travel or paid leisure [1].
- **Counter-argument, time:** laws on working hours and paid holidays created the time for leisure [1].
- **Counter-argument, demographics:** ageing and a growing middle class in emerging economies reshape demand [1].
- **Shocks:** the COVID-19 pandemic changed patterns abruptly, regardless of technology [1].
- **Uneven effects:** access to technology depends on income and connection, so its influence varies between groups [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, technology has been the most visible influence on how leisure is organised, but income and time determine whether people can take part, so it is one factor among several [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* both Section C questions reward factors that interact, a consideration of data quality and a clear final judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a difference in rates as a percentage instead of percentage points.
- Calling a facility a primary resource.
- Listing factors for international comparison without weighing them.
- Accepting survey data without commenting on what it misses.
