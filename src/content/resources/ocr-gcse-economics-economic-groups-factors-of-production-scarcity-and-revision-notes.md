## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Resources are limited relative to wants |
| Unlimited wants | People always want more; satisfying one want creates others |
| Opportunity cost | The gain from the best use you passed up |
| Consumer | Buys goods and services to meet wants |
| Producer | Makes goods or provides services using factors of production |
| Government | Taxes, spends, regulates and provides public services |
| Land | Natural resources, and space (reward: rent) |
| Labour | People's physical and mental work (reward: wages) |
| Capital | Man-made equipment used to produce (reward: interest) |
| Enterprise | Organising factors and taking risks (reward: profit) |
| Labour-intensive | Uses a high proportion of labour |
| Capital-intensive | Uses a high proportion of machinery and equipment |

## 1.1 Economic groups at a glance

| Group | Role | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Buy output; supply labour; pay taxes | Firms for goods and jobs; government for services |
| Producers | Employ factors; make output; pay wages and taxes | Consumers for sales; households for labour; government for infrastructure and law |
| Government | Collects tax; provides services; makes rules; redistributes income | Taxes from firms and households |

### Interdependence in five links

1. Households supply labour.
2. Firms pay wages and other rewards.
3. Households spend on output.
4. Both pay tax.
5. Government spends on services both use.

Use "this leads to" chains. Example: higher business tax, so lower profit, so less investment and fewer jobs, so lower household spending, so lower sales and lower tax receipts. Then evaluate: it depends on how long it lasts and what the tax funds.

## 1.1 Factors of production and how to combine them

Capital is equipment, not money. A van is capital; the cash used to buy it is not.

### Method: compare two ways of producing

1. Output per worker = total output ÷ number of workers.
2. Total cost = wages bill + equipment cost.
3. Cost per unit = total cost ÷ output.
4. Compare, then comment on risk, jobs and demand.

**Reminder.** A firm makes 150 units at a total cost of £900. Cost per unit = £900 ÷ 150 = £6.00. A firm with 8 workers makes 400 units: 400 ÷ 8 = 50 each. After buying a machine, 6 workers make 720: 720 ÷ 6 = 120 each.

Choice of method depends on relative factor prices, the product, scale, available skills and demand.

## 1.2 Scarcity, wants and the economic problem

Scarcity is permanent and applies to all economies. It is not the same as a temporary shortage of one product. The economic problem is the need to choose. It produces four linked questions.

| Question | Examples |
|---|---|
| Allocation | How should limited resources be shared between uses, and who decides: markets, government or a mix? |
| What | Hospitals or roads? Food crops or fuel crops? |
| How | Labour-intensive or capital-intensive? Which materials and energy? |
| For whom | Who receives the output? How is income shared? |

Every answer to one question affects the others. Choosing capital-intensive methods (how) can mean fewer jobs and so a different pattern of incomes (for whom).

## Opportunity cost

### Steps to follow

1. Write down the realistic options.
2. Put them in order of preference.
3. Pick out the top option you rejected.
4. Give it in units: money, time, goods or services.

**Reminder 1.** A student can work a 6-hour shift at £9 an hour and earn 6 × £9 = £54. If she instead spends the time on a trip she values more, her opportunity cost is the £54 she gave up (if the shift was her best alternative).

**Reminder 2.** A hairdresser has an 8-hour day (480 minutes). A haircut takes 30 minutes and a colouring takes 120 minutes. Opportunity cost of one colouring = 120 ÷ 30 = 4 haircuts. A full day of colourings gives 480 ÷ 120 = 4 colourings; a full day of haircuts gives 480 ÷ 30 = 16 haircuts.

**Reminder 3.** In a table of production options, the cost of moving between options is the fall in the other good. If the cost per extra unit rises, resources are not equally suited to both goods.

Applies to consumers, producers and governments. It is not the total of all alternatives.

## Evaluating economic choices

### Framework

1. List costs and benefits (money and non-money).
2. Group them: economic, social, environmental.
3. Say who gains and who loses.
4. Compare size and timing: short term against long term.
5. Reach a judgement and say what it depends on.

### Sustainability types

| Type | Question to ask | Example point |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Can it keep generating income and jobs? | A mine closes when the ore runs out |
| Social | Does it support well-being and fairness over time? | Job losses hurt a whole town |
| Environmental | Does it protect natural resources? | Overfishing reduces future catches |

### Short worked reminder

A council compares a car park earning £108,000 a year for a £360,000 build with a garden costing £60,000. Payback on the car park is £360,000 ÷ £108,000 = 3.3 years. The garden has no income, but brings social and environmental benefits that cannot be priced easily. The judgement depends on which the council values more.

### Model evaluation chain

Point: a firm replaces five workers with a machine. Effect: cost per unit falls, so it can cut prices or raise profit. Cost: five households lose wages and spend less locally. Weighing: the firm gains at once, but the community bears a lasting social cost unless the workers find new jobs. Judgement: it is justified if demand is strong and retraining is available; it is harder to justify if the town depends on those wages. Opportunity cost: the wages and community goodwill given up.

### Linking 1.1 and 1.2

Every group faces scarcity. Consumers have limited income and time. Producers have limited factors. The government has limited tax revenue. Each group therefore chooses, and each choice has an opportunity cost that falls on someone else in the chain.

## Must-know distinctions

- Scarcity versus shortage: scarcity is always present; a shortage is temporary and product-specific.
- Needs versus wants: needs are essentials; wants have no limit.
- Capital versus money.
- Labour-intensive versus capital-intensive.
- Opportunity cost versus total cost of a choice.
- Economic versus social versus environmental sustainability.
- Producers (firms) versus the government as a provider of services.

## Quick self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. Which factor of production is a delivery van, and what is its usual reward?
3. A hairdresser's haircut takes 30 minutes and a colouring takes 120 minutes. What is the opportunity cost of one colouring, in haircuts?
4. A workshop with 8 workers makes 400 units. After buying a machine, 6 workers make 720. By how much does output per worker rise?
5. A firm makes 150 units for a total cost of £900. What is the cost per unit?
6. Name the three main economic groups and give one link between two of them.
7. A student turns down a 6-hour shift at £9 an hour to revise. Give her opportunity cost, assuming the shift was her best alternative.
8. Is "a bakery sells out of bread by noon" an example of scarcity? Explain.
9. A government builds solar plants rather than coal plants. Which of the "what, how, for whom" questions is mainly answered?
10. Overfishing raises catches now but reduces them later. Which type of sustainability is at risk?

### Answers

1. The value of the next best alternative given up.
2. Capital; interest.
3. 120 ÷ 30 = **4 haircuts**.
4. 400 ÷ 8 = 50; 720 ÷ 6 = 120; rise = **70 units per worker**.
5. 900 ÷ 150 = **£6.00**.
6. Consumers, producers, government. Example: producers pay wages to consumers, who spend them on producers' output.
7. 6 × £9 = **£54**.
8. No. That is a shortage of one product on one day. Scarcity means limited resources compared with unlimited wants across the whole economy.
9. "How" (and also "what" is produced).
10. Environmental (and, in time, economic, as the industry's income falls).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "money" when defining capital.
- Naming several alternatives as the opportunity cost instead of the single best one.
- Leaving out units or the working in a calculation.
- Describing a business as scarce rather than its resources.
- Listing costs and benefits with no judgement or no reason for it.
- Writing "sustainable" with no type and no link to the context.
- Treating interdependence as a list of separate roles rather than a chain of cause and effect.
- Copying a definition when the question says "explain" and asks for the context.
