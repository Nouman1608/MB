## Condensed recall

**Approach**
- Reductionist: study parts separately. Systems (holistic): study interactions between parts.
- Systems thinking reveals leverage points and unintended consequences.

**Building a system diagram**
- Storages = boxes. Flows = arrows labelled with the process. Boundary = a line, chosen by the
  modeller.
- Transfer = change of location only (river carrying sediment downstream).
- Transformation = change of form, state or energy type (light → chemical energy; liquid → gas;
  coal → heat).

**Sankey diagrams**
- Arrow width ∝ flow size.
- Outputs must add up to inputs (conservation of energy or matter).
- Efficiency = useful output ÷ input × 100.

**System types**
- Open: energy and matter cross (pond, forest, city).
- Closed: energy crosses, matter does not (Earth, approximately; sealed terrarium).
- Isolated: neither crosses; no natural example on Earth.
- Scale and boundary are choices; change them and the inputs and outputs change.

**Residence time**
- Residence time = storage ÷ flow rate (in steady state).
- Long residence time → pollution persists; depletion is slow to notice.

**Feedback**
- Negative: response reduces the change → stability.
- Positive: response amplifies the change → moves system away from its state.
- Write the full chain; judge the direction of the last step.
- Real systems contain both; outcome depends on which loop dominates.

**Tipping points**
- Threshold → shift to an alternative stable state.
- Little warning; positive feedback drives the shift; the new state reinforces itself; reversal is
  hard.
- Examples: clear lake → turbid algal lake; coral reef → seaweed reef; rainforest → savanna-like
  state.

**Resilience**
- Raised by biodiversity, large storages, fast reproduction, connectivity.
- Lowered by monoculture, erosion, fragmentation.
- A degraded state can also be resilient.

**Emergent properties**
- Arise only from interactions in the whole system (food-web stability).

**Evaluating models**
- Purpose, simplifications, assumptions, data quality, testing, range of use.

## Method in steps: drawing a system diagram

```
1. Decide the boundary and scale (a field, a lake, a city).
2. Draw each storage as a labelled box.
3. Draw each flow as an arrow, labelled with the process.
4. Mark inputs crossing the boundary in and outputs crossing out.
5. If asked, note whether each flow is a transfer or a transformation.
6. Check the budget: inputs − outputs = change in storage.
```

Mini check (invented): a field receives 600 mm of rain a year; 250 mm evaporates or transpires,
300 mm runs off or drains, and the soil store rises by 40 mm. 250 + 300 + 40 = 590 mm, so 10 mm is
unaccounted for: a flow, such as interception loss or deep percolation, is missing from the diagram.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| System | A set of interacting components forming a functional whole |
| Storage | A stock of matter or energy in a system |
| Flow | Movement of matter or energy into, out of or within a system |
| Transfer | A flow that changes location but not form |
| Transformation | A flow that changes form, state or energy type |
| Sankey diagram | Flow diagram with arrow widths proportional to flow size |
| Residence time | Average time matter spends in a storage |
| Steady-state equilibrium | Constant average state despite continuing inputs and outputs |
| Negative feedback | A response that counteracts a change |
| Positive feedback | A response that amplifies a change |
| Tipping point | Threshold beyond which a system shifts to a new state |
| Alternative stable state | A different, self-reinforcing configuration of the same system |
| Resilience | Ability to absorb disturbance and keep structure and function |
| Emergent property | A property of the whole that the parts alone do not show |

## Exam traps

- **Unnamed flows.** "Input" is not a process; write "precipitation", "photosynthesis", "harvest".
- **Evaporation is a transformation.** It is a change of state, even though the water also moves.
- **Positive does not mean good.** Check the direction of the effect.
- **Residence time units.** Storage in m³ divided by m³ per year gives years.
- **Earth is not isolated.** Sunlight enters and heat leaves.
- **Resilience is not always good.** Degraded states can resist restoration.
- **"Simplified" is not an evaluation.** Say what is left out and how it affects the prediction.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish a transfer from a transformation, with one example of each.
2. Why does Earth count as approximately closed?
3. A Sankey diagram shows 500 J in and arrows of 120 J and 300 J out. What is missing?
4. A lake holds 900 million m³ with an outflow of 30 million m³ per year. Calculate the residence time.
5. Give one reason a long residence time matters for pollution.
6. Is "more prey → more predators → fewer prey" negative or positive feedback?
7. Describe one positive feedback in a forest.
8. State three features of a tipping point.
9. Give two factors that increase an ecosystem's resilience.
10. Why might a turbid algal lake stay turbid after nutrient inputs are cut?
11. What is an emergent property? Give an example.
12. Give two questions to ask when evaluating a model.

### Answers

1. Transfer: water flowing downstream (location changes). Transformation: water evaporating (state
   changes).
2. Energy crosses its boundary (sunlight in, heat out) but almost no matter does.
3. A flow of 500 − 420 = 80 J has been missed.
4. 900 ÷ 30 = **30 years**.
5. Pollutants stay in the storage for a long time, so damage persists after the source stops.
6. Negative feedback.
7. Clearing reduces transpiration and rainfall, drying the forest and increasing fires, which clear
   more forest.
8. Any three: a threshold; little warning; driven by positive feedback; leads to a new
   self-reinforcing state; hard to reverse.
9. Any two: high biodiversity; large storages; rapid reproduction; connectivity to other areas.
10. The new state has its own reinforcing feedbacks, such as nutrients released from sediments and
    shading by algae preventing rooted plants from returning.
11. A property of the whole system that arises from interactions, such as the stability of a food web.
12. Any two: What is its purpose? What does it leave out? What does it assume? How good are its data?
    Has it been tested?
