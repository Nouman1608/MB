Condensed recall for **2.4 Ocean management futures** (IB DP Geography, Option B: Oceans and coastal margins). See the [Oceans and coastal margins study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-study-guide/).

## Key ideas

Governance, sustainable use and uncertainty. Oceans lie largely beyond single national control, so international frameworks matter.

The **tragedy of the commons** explains why: when a resource such as a high-seas fish stock is open to all, each user gains from taking more, while the cost of depletion is shared by everyone. Without agreed limits, the rational choice for each user leads to collapse for all.

## Governance tools

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea | Sets rules for ocean use, including exclusive economic zones |
| Exclusive economic zone (EEZ) | Zone in which a coastal state has rights over resources |
| Marine protected areas | Conserve habitats and give ecosystems space to recover |
| Fish quotas, certification schemes | Limit and label sustainable catches |
| Plastic reduction agreements | Target marine plastic |
| Ecosystem-based management | Manages the whole system rather than one species or use |

### UNCLOS zones

The convention was adopted in 1982 and came into force in 1994. It divides the sea into zones measured from a coastal **baseline**:

| Zone | Extent | Rights of the coastal state |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial sea | Up to 12 nautical miles | Sovereignty, though foreign ships have a right of innocent passage |
| Contiguous zone | Up to 24 nautical miles | Limited control, for example over customs and immigration |
| Exclusive economic zone | Up to 200 nautical miles | Rights over fish, oil, gas and minerals; other states keep freedom of navigation |
| Continental shelf | Can extend beyond 200 nautical miles where the shelf continues | Rights over seabed resources, if the claim is supported by scientific evidence |
| High seas | Beyond national jurisdiction | Open to all; the deep seabed is treated as the common heritage of humankind and administered by the International Seabed Authority |

Because small islands can generate large EEZs, control of islands, reefs and rocks matters greatly in disputes. A newer agreement on marine biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction, often called the High Seas Treaty, was agreed in 2023 and creates a legal route to set up protected areas in international waters.

## Pressures on oceans

Overfishing, marine plastic, oil and chemical pollution, shipping, offshore energy, deep-sea mining and tourism, with climate change altering currents, ecosystems and sea level.

- **Overfishing:** catches above the **maximum sustainable yield** (the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock) deplete populations. Subsidies, illegal fishing and bycatch make it worse.
- **Plastic:** floating plastic accumulates in the centres of ocean gyres, such as the area of the North Pacific known as the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, mostly as small fragments rather than a solid mass. Microplastics enter food chains.
- **Deep-sea mining:** interest in metal-rich nodules on the deep sea floor has grown with demand for battery metals; scientists warn that deep-sea ecosystems recover extremely slowly, and the International Seabed Authority has been debating rules for commercial extraction.

## Case studies

### Collapse of the Grand Banks cod fishery, Canada

For centuries the Grand Banks off Newfoundland supported a huge cod fishery. Industrial trawlers greatly increased catches in the second half of the twentieth century, and stock estimates proved too optimistic. In 1992 the Canadian government imposed a moratorium on fishing for northern cod, ending work for tens of thousands of people in fishing and processing. Recovery has been slow. Lessons: scientific uncertainty, political pressure to keep quotas high and the slow response of ecosystems can turn overfishing into collapse.

### The South China Sea

Several states, including China, Vietnam, the Philippines and Malaysia, have overlapping claims to islands, reefs and waters rich in fish and possibly oil and gas, and crossed by major shipping lanes. China has built artificial islands on reefs and asserts claims based on a historic "nine-dash line". In 2016 an arbitral tribunal under UNCLOS, in a case brought by the Philippines, ruled that these historic claims had no legal basis under the convention; China rejected the ruling. It shows the limits of international law where enforcement depends on powerful states choosing to comply.

### Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, Australia

The park is managed by **zoning**: some areas allow general use, while others restrict fishing, and roughly a third of the park has been in no-take "green zones" since a rezoning in 2004. Evaluation: zoning is a strong model of a large, managed marine protected area, but it cannot protect the reef from warming water, which has caused repeated mass bleaching, or from pollution running off farmland on land.

## Evaluation criteria

- Is it **enforceable** (especially beyond national waters)?
- Is it **fair** to developing states?
- Is it **effective across borders**?
- Is it **adaptable**?

**Adaptive management** can change as evidence improves, so it is often presented as more resilient than fixed plans under climate uncertainty.

## Building an answer

1. Name the pressure and the measure.
2. Explain how the measure should work.
3. Weigh strengths against limits (enforcement, fairness, cross-border effect).
4. Conclude with a judgement.

## Model paragraph

*To what extent can international agreements secure the future of ocean resources?* "Agreements such as UNCLOS have created a clear framework of EEZs that gives coastal states rights and responsibilities to manage fish stocks and seabed resources, and new agreements extend protection into the high seas. However, the South China Sea shows that a powerful state can ignore rulings, and the collapse of Grand Banks cod shows that even national control fails if quotas are set too high. Climate change also crosses every boundary. Agreements are therefore necessary but only partly sufficient: their success depends on enforcement, good science and adaptive management."

## Exam traps

- Listing pressures without linking them to a management response.
- Assuming a treaty works because it exists: ask about enforcement.
- Forgetting developing-state fairness.
- Inventing treaty dates or fish-stock figures; state general patterns instead.
- Confusing the territorial sea (sovereignty) with the EEZ (resource rights only).
- Describing ocean garbage patches as solid floating islands of rubbish.
