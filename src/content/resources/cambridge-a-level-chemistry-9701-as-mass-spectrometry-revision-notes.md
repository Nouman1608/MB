**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **AS Level** subtopic 22.2.
The working of the mass spectrometer is **not** required.

## The six things you must be able to do

| Skill | What to use |
|---|---|
| Interpret a spectrum | m/e (mass-to-charge) on the x-axis; relative abundance on the y-axis |
| Calculate Ar | weighted mean: Σ(m/e × abundance) ÷ Σ(abundance) |
| Find Mr | m/e of the molecular ion, M⁺ (highest m/e, ignoring M+1 and M+2) |
| Identify fragments | m/e of fragment ion; M − fragment = mass of radical lost |
| Count carbons | n = (100 × abundance of [M+1]⁺) / (1.1 × abundance of M⁺) |
| Detect Cl or Br | ratio of M⁺ to [M+2]⁺ peak heights |

## Ar calculations: two checks

- If abundances are percentages, divide by 100; otherwise divide by their **sum**.
- Ar should lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses, nearer the more abundant one.

Example: boron, ¹⁰B 19.9 and ¹¹B 80.1: Ar = (10 × 19.9 + 11 × 80.1) / 100 = 10.8.

## Molecular ion and fragments

- M⁺ forms when the molecule loses one electron: M → M⁺ + e⁻.
- Fragmentation: M⁺ → X⁺ + Y•. Only **X⁺** is detected.
- The tallest peak is not necessarily M⁺.

| m/e | Likely fragment |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ |
| 31 | CH₂OH⁺ |
| 43 | CH₃CO⁺ or C₃H₇⁺ |
| 45 | COOH⁺ |
| 57 | C₂H₅CO⁺ or C₄H₉⁺ |
| 77 | C₆H₅⁺ |

Positional isomers can be separated by fragments:

- **propan-1-ol** gives CH₂OH⁺ at m/e 31; **propan-2-ol** gives CH₃CHOH⁺ at m/e 45.
- **pentan-3-one** gives C₂H₅CO⁺ (57) and C₂H₅⁺ (29); **pentan-2-one** gives CH₃CO⁺ (43) and C₃H₇CO⁺ (71).

## Carbon counting

| M⁺ abundance | [M+1]⁺ abundance | n |
|---|---|---|
| 40.0 | 1.76 | (100 × 1.76) / (1.1 × 40.0) = 4 |
| 20.0 | 0.66 | (100 × 0.66) / (1.1 × 20.0) = 3 |
| 60.0 | 3.3 | (100 × 3.3) / (1.1 × 60.0) = 5 |

After finding n, subtract 12n from Mr to find what is left for H, O, N or halogens.

## Halogen patterns

| Atoms | Peaks | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Cl | M : M+2 | 3 : 1 |
| 1 Br | M : M+2 | 1 : 1 |
| 2 Cl | M : M+2 : M+4 | 9 : 6 : 1 |
| 2 Br | M : M+2 : M+4 | 1 : 2 : 1 |

Fragment ions that still contain the halogen show the same pattern, two mass units apart.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Isotopes | atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons |
| Relative atomic mass, Ar | weighted mean mass of an atom of an element relative to 1/12 of the mass of a ¹²C atom |
| m/e | mass-to-charge ratio of an ion |
| Molecular ion, M⁺ | the ion formed when a whole molecule loses one electron |
| Fragment ion | a positive ion formed when the molecular ion breaks apart |
| [M+1]⁺ peak | small peak one unit above M⁺, due to ¹³C |
| [M+2]⁺ peak | peak two units above M⁺, due to ³⁷Cl or ⁸¹Br |

## Exam traps

- Divide by the **sum** of abundances when they do not total 100.
- M⁺ is at the **highest** m/e, not the tallest peak.
- Fragments need a **+ charge**; radicals are not detected.
- [M+1]⁺ formula: **[M+1]⁺ abundance on top**, × 100; M⁺ abundance × 1.1 underneath.
- **3 : 1 = chlorine; 1 : 1 = bromine.**
- No marks for describing how the spectrometer works.

## Self-test

1. What does m/e stand for?
2. Gallium has isotopes ⁶⁹Ga (60.1%) and ⁷¹Ga (39.9%). Calculate Ar.
3. Why is the M⁺ peak not always the tallest peak?
4. Write an equation for the formation of the molecular ion of ethanol.
5. Ethanol (Mr 46) shows a peak at m/e 31. Identify the fragment ion and the species lost.
6. A compound has M⁺ abundance 50.0 and [M+1]⁺ abundance 2.75. How many carbon atoms does it have?
7. A compound shows peaks at m/e 78 and 80 in the ratio 3 : 1. What does this show?
8. A compound shows peaks at m/e 108 and 110 in the ratio 1 : 1. Suggest its identity.
9. Give the peak ratio for a compound with two chlorine atoms.
10. Which fragment distinguishes propan-1-ol from propan-2-ol?
11. Why is the [M+1]⁺ peak larger for hexane than for ethane?
12. Why can a fragment radical never appear as a peak?

**Answers:**

1. Mass-to-charge ratio.
2. (69 × 60.1 + 71 × 39.9) / 100 = 69.8.
3. The molecular ion may fragment easily, so a fragment ion can be more abundant.
4. C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅OH⁺ + e⁻.
5. CH₂OH⁺; a CH₃ radical (46 − 31 = 15) is lost.
6. n = (100 × 2.75) / (1.1 × 50.0) = 5.
7. One chlorine atom (M⁺ at 78, e.g. C₃H₇Cl).
8. One bromine atom: bromoethane, C₂H₅Br (m/e 108 with ⁷⁹Br, 110 with ⁸¹Br).
9. M : M+2 : M+4 = 9 : 6 : 1.
10. m/e 31 (CH₂OH⁺) for propan-1-ol; m/e 45 (CH₃CHOH⁺) for propan-2-ol.
11. Hexane has six carbons, so a molecule is more likely to contain a ¹³C atom than ethane with two.
12. Only charged particles are detected; the radical has no charge.

## Frequently asked questions

**What precision should Ar answers have?**
Follow the data: abundances to three significant figures usually give Ar to one decimal place, as in the
Periodic Table of the Data section.

**Do I need M+4 peaks?**
The syllabus outcome names the [M+2]⁺ peak. Molecules with two chlorine or two bromine atoms also show an
[M+4]⁺ peak, and the ratios in these notes help you recognise them.
