## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a "true and fair" view. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the accounting convention of prudence. **[2]**

**3.** Quayside Ltd pays £4,800 rent on 1 July for twelve months. Its financial year ends on 31 December. Calculate the rent charge for the year and the prepayment. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following conventions says that revenue should be recorded when a sale is made, not when an order is received?

A: Consistency.

B: Realisation.

C: Materiality.

D: Going concern. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business should comply with GAAP. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Tolley Electrics has stock that cost £9,000. A change in fashion means it can now be sold for £7,800, after spending £600 on selling costs. Receivables are £45,000, of which £3,500 is thought unlikely to be paid. Profit before these adjustments is £64,000.

**(a)** Calculate the net realisable value of the stock and the amount of the write-down. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the value of receivables after a prudent provision. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the revised profit and the percentage fall in profit. **[3]**

**(d)** Evaluate the usefulness of the prudence convention to Tolley Electrics and its stakeholders. **[6]**

**7.** Furlong Bakery had sales of £95,000 for the year, of which £12,000 had not been paid by the year end. It paid costs of £70,000 in cash, and owes £3,000 of wages for the last week of the year.

**(a)** Calculate the profit on a cash basis. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit under the accruals (matching) convention. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the two figures differ and which gives the fairer view. **[5]**

**8.** Halden Plastics buys a machine for £30,000 with a five-year life and no residual value. In year 1 it uses reducing balance at 30%. In year 2 the finance director proposes switching to straight line because profit is low.

**(a)** Calculate the depreciation charge in year 1, and the year 2 charge under reducing balance. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain which accounting convention the finance director is proposing to ignore, and why this matters. **[4]**

**9.** A business has a rule that items costing under £500 are treated as expenses immediately. Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of applying the materiality convention in this way. **[4]**

**10.** Marlow Studios is facing serious losses. A lender asks whether the accounts have been prepared on a going concern basis. Evaluate the impact on the accounts, and on the studio's stakeholders, if the going concern assumption no longer applies. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the importance of accounting conventions to a public limited company and its shareholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A true and fair view means the accounts are free from material error and show what happened [1], and are prepared without bias so that users are not misled [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Prudence means not overstating profit or assets [1]; likely losses are recognised as soon as they are expected, but gains are recorded only when certain [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Rent for the year = £4,800 × 6 ÷ 12 [1] = **£2,400** [1]. Prepayment = £4,800 − £2,400 = **£2,400** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** GAAP makes accounts comparable between businesses and years [1], so lenders and investors can trust and compare the figures when deciding where to put their money [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** NRV = £7,800 − £600 = **£7,200** [1]. Stock is shown at the lower of cost (£9,000) and NRV (£7,200), so £7,200 [1]. Write-down = £9,000 − £7,200 = **£1,800** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** £45,000 − £3,500 = **£41,500** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Revised profit = £64,000 − £1,800 − £3,500 = **£58,700** [1] [1]. Fall = £5,300 ÷ £64,000 × 100 = **8.3%** (8.28%) [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** For: prudence stops the stock and receivables being overstated, so owners do not pay tax or dividends on profit that will not arrive [1]; lenders can rely on cautious figures [1]; managers see losses early and can act [1].
Against: it lowers reported profit by £5,300, which may affect bonuses or share price [1]; the £3,500 provision is a judgement, and might prove too cautious [1].
Judgement: prudence is useful because the loss is likely and real, but the estimate should be based on evidence, such as the customer's payment history, to keep the accounts objective [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Cash received = £95,000 − £12,000 = £83,000 [1]. Profit = £83,000 − £70,000 = **£13,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Costs = £70,000 + £3,000 = £73,000 [1]. Profit = £95,000 − £73,000 = **£22,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** The £9,000 difference arises because the cash basis ignores £12,000 of sales earned but not yet paid and £3,000 of wages owed [1] [1]. The accruals convention matches revenue and costs to the period in which they were earned and incurred [1]. This gives the fairer view of the year's trading [1], although the cash figure is useful for judging whether the bakery can pay its bills [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Year 1 = 30% × £30,000 = **£9,000** [1]. Year 2 = 30% × (£30,000 − £9,000) [1] = 30% × £21,000 = **£6,300** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The convention of consistency [1], which requires the same methods from year to year [1]. Changing method only because profit is low would make profit look better without any change in trading [1]. It would also mean that the accounts could not be compared with last year, which could mislead users and breach the true and fair view [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Advantage: it saves time and keeps the accounts simple, because small items are not tracked as assets for years [1] [1]. Disadvantage: many small purchases could together be material, so users might not see a full record of assets [1] [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Effect on the accounts: assets are valued at break-up (sale) value rather than cost less depreciation, which is usually lower [1]. Stock, equipment and studio fittings may fall sharply in value [1]. Some liabilities, such as redundancy costs, may need to be recognised [1].
Stakeholders: lenders may ask for repayment or refuse further credit [1]; suppliers may demand cash on delivery [1]; employees fear for jobs [1]; owners may see equity fall to nothing [1].
Judgement: the change is very significant, because it shows that the business may be unable to continue, so users need to be told promptly [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Importance to the company: conventions make accounts comparable and reliable, which lowers the cost of raising capital [1]; auditors check them [1]; consistent methods allow trends to be seen [1].
Shareholders: they rely on the accounts to value shares and decide on dividends [1]; prudence protects them from over-optimistic profit [1]; accruals shows true trading profit [1].
Others: lenders and tax authorities use the same figures [1].
Evaluation: conventions leave room for judgement, such as the estimate of a doubtful debt, so managers could still manipulate results [1]. Judgement: they are very important, because without agreed rules, shareholders could not rely on any figure, but they need to be applied honestly and checked by auditors [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a convention without using the figures in the question.
- Confusing accruals with prudence.
- Saying prudence means "always lower the profit".
- Forgetting to subtract selling costs when finding net realisable value.
- Giving no judgement in evaluation.
