These notes condense **1.1.1 Planning** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026). The three learning outcomes are (a) experimental design, including problems set in a practical context, with selection of suitable apparatus, equipment and techniques (HSW3); (b) identification of variables that must be controlled, where appropriate; and (c) evaluation that an experimental method is appropriate to meet the expected outcomes (HSW6). Section 1.1 is assessed in the written papers.

Worked examples and an annotated plan are in the [1.1.1 study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1/). Exam-style questions are in the [1.1.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1-practice/). For all four written-paper skills together, use the [practical skills revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-practical-skills-written-examination-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | The variable you deliberately change |
| Dependent variable | The variable you measure |
| Controlled variable | Any other variable that could affect the result, kept constant |
| Range | The lowest and highest values of the independent variable |
| Interval | The step between values of the independent variable |
| Resolution | The smallest change an instrument can show |
| Weighing by difference | Weigh container plus solid, transfer, reweigh container; the difference is the mass transferred |
| Standard solution | A solution of accurately known concentration, made in a volumetric flask |
| Excess | More reagent than needed to react with all of the other |
| Hazard | Something that could cause harm |
| Risk | The chance of harm from the way the work is done |

## The plan checklist

```
aim → equation and data → variables → apparatus (with sizes)
→ quantities (with a calculation) → numbered method with repeats
→ safety → how the result is processed
```

A plan is complete when someone else could follow it and get the answer without asking a question.

## Apparatus choices

| Need | Choose |
|---|---|
| Exact fixed volume | Pipette (with filler) |
| Volume added to an end point | Burette |
| Reagent in excess | Measuring cylinder |
| Standard solution | Volumetric flask, solid weighed by difference |
| Gas volume | Gas syringe; over water only for gases that are not very soluble |
| Small temperature change | Thermometer or probe with fine resolution; insulated cup |
| Weak acid titrated with strong base | Phenolphthalein |
| Strong acid titrated with weak base | Methyl orange |

## Sizing quantities: always calculate

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Estimate moles from the quantity you plan to use.
3. Convert to the reading: titre, gas volume, temperature change or mass.
4. Compare with the apparatus range. Aim for a reading that fills a good part of it.
5. If it does not fit, change a quantity: dilute, use less solid, or use larger apparatus.

**Useful relationships.**

```
n = m ÷ M          n = c × V(dm³)          V(gas, cm³) = n × 24 000 (at RTP)
```

## Variables

- Name each controlled variable precisely and say **how** it is controlled (water bath, same solution, same volume by burette).
- "Where appropriate" matters: a single titration has no independent variable, but temperature and indicator are still kept the same.
- Measure the temperature of the reaction mixture, not the water bath.
- Use at least five values of the independent variable over a wide range, with repeats.

## The four checks for 1.1.1(c)

| Check | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Right quantity | Does the method measure what the aim needs? |
| Right size | Will readings fit the apparatus and give a small percentage uncertainty? |
| Losses or gains | Can gas, heat or product escape, or something extra be counted? |
| Side reactions | Could another reaction alter the reading? |

Give a verdict, a reason and, if the method fails, the change that fixes it.

## Exam traps

- **Apparatus without size:** "burette" alone; write "50 cm³ burette".
- **No calculation of the expected reading.** Without it, an examiner cannot tell the plan works.
- **Vague control:** "same amount" instead of "25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ acid from a burette".
- **Soluble gas collected over water.**
- **Wrong indicator** for a weak acid or weak base titration.
- **Generic safety:** "wear goggles" alone; link a specific hazard to a specific control.
- **No processing step:** the plan must say how readings give the final answer.
- **Too few values** of the independent variable for a trend.

## Quick self-test

1. List the four checks for whether a method is appropriate to meet its expected outcome.
2. Which apparatus measures an exact 25.0 cm³ of solution to be titrated?
3. Why can ammonia not be collected over water?
4. 25.0 cm³ of about 0.0600 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid is to be titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide in a 50 cm³ burette. Calculate the expected titre and say whether it is suitable.
5. A student plans to react 0.120 g of magnesium with excess acid and collect the hydrogen in a 100 cm³ gas syringe. Use Ar(Mg) = 24.3 and 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. Is this appropriate? If not, what is the largest mass to use?
6. Why does a titration to find one unknown concentration have no independent variable?
7. Give three controlled variables for an experiment on how the concentration of hydrochloric acid affects the rate of its reaction with magnesium ribbon.
8. How many values of the independent variable should a plan include, and why?
9. Why is a solid weighed by difference?
10. A student plans to measure a temperature rise of about 6.5 °C with a thermometer reading to 1 °C, in a glass beaker. Suggest two improvements.
11. Which indicator suits a titration of hydrochloric acid against aqueous ammonia?
12. What should the processing part of a plan state?

### Answers

1. Right quantity measured; readings of the right size for the apparatus; no losses or gains; no side reactions.
2. A **25.0 cm³ pipette** (with a pipette filler).
3. Ammonia is **very soluble in water**, so most of it would dissolve and not be collected.
4. H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. n(H₂SO₄) = 0.0600 × 0.0250 = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol; n(NaOH) = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol; titre = **30.0 cm³**, which fits a 50 cm³ burette well.
5. n(H₂) = 0.120 ÷ 24.3 = 4.94 × 10⁻³ mol, giving **118.5 cm³**: too much for the syringe. Largest mass: (100 ÷ 24 000) × 24.3 = **0.101 g**; use about 0.08 g to stay within range.
6. Nothing is deliberately changed: the aim is a single value, found by repeating the same titration until the titres are concordant.
7. Any three: **temperature**, **length (or mass) of magnesium ribbon**, **volume of acid**, surface area of the ribbon (cut from the same roll and cleaned the same way).
8. **At least five**, over a wide range, so a graph can show the trend and anomalies can be spotted.
9. The mass that matters is what reaches the flask; reweighing the container accounts for any solid left behind.
10. Use a thermometer or probe with **finer resolution** (for example 0.1 °C), and an **insulated container** such as a polystyrene cup with a lid. A larger temperature change, from more concentrated solutions, also reduces the percentage uncertainty.
11. **Methyl orange**: strong acid with weak base.
12. How the readings are turned into the final answer: the calculation steps, or the graph to be plotted and what is read from it.
