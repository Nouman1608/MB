These questions cover section 14.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: the four economic sectors and their roles in development, the nature, causes and distribution of global inequalities in wellbeing, and the evaluation of measures of inequality. All countries and data are fictional. Revise first with the [14.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development/) and the [14.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development-revision-notes/). Give calculated answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "quaternary sector" and give one example. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the share of a country's workers in farming may be much larger than farming's share of national output. **[3]**

**3.** Outline two physical causes of global inequalities in wellbeing. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows employment structure in the fictional country of Taravia.

| Year | Primary (%) | Secondary (%) | Tertiary and quaternary (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 55 | 20 | 25 |
| 2020 | 30 | 28 | 42 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in the tertiary and quaternary share between 1990 and 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in Taravia's employment structure. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons for the fall in primary employment. **[4]**

**5.** The fictional country of Ulmar has a health index of 0.85, an education index of 0.55 and an income index of 0.75.

**(a)** Calculate Ulmar's HDI, using the geometric mean of the three indices. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Ulmar's HDI is lower than the arithmetic mean of its three indices. **[2]**

**6.** The share of national income received by each fifth of the population of the fictional country of Varo, from poorest to richest, is 4%, 8%, 13%, 22% and 53%.

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative income shares needed to plot a Lorenz curve. **[2]**

**(b)** How many times larger is the income share of the richest fifth than that of the poorest fifth? **[1]**

**(c)** Varo's Gini coefficient is 0.45. Explain why this value alone does not show how many people in Varo are poor. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how dependence on one primary export can contribute to low levels of wellbeing. **[4]**

**8.** Describe the global distribution of social and economic wellbeing. **[4]**

**9.** Compare GNI per capita and the Multidimensional Poverty Index as measures of inequality. **[4]**

## Section B

**10.** Explain the roles of the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors in economic development. **[8]**

**11.** "The Human Development Index is the most useful measure of inequality between countries." Evaluate this view. **[20]**

**12.** "Human factors are more important than physical factors in causing global inequalities in wellbeing." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Economic activity that produces or handles knowledge and information [1], for example research and development, ICT or financial consultancy [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Output per worker in farming is low [1] because of little mechanisation, small plots or subsistence production [1], so many workers together produce a small share of output, while fewer, more productive workers in other sectors produce more value [1]. **[3]**

**3.** A landlocked location means goods must cross other countries to reach ports [1], raising trade costs and reducing investment and income [1]. A high disease burden, such as malaria in tropical areas, reduces working and school days [1], lowering productivity and income [1]. Also credit climatic hazards such as drought or a lack of resources, each with its effect. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" still needs the effect of each cause, not a bare name.

**4. (a)** (42 - 25) / 25 x 100 [1] = 68.0% increase [1].

**(b)** Primary employment fell by 25 percentage points, from 55% to 30% [1]. Secondary employment rose from 20% to 28% [1]. Tertiary and quaternary became the largest sector, rising from 25% to 42% [1].

**(c)** Mechanisation and new farming methods raised productivity, so fewer workers were needed on farms [1], releasing labour to other sectors [1]. Rural-urban migration to factory and service jobs in cities [1], attracted by higher and more regular wages [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* For (b), quote figures and use "percentage points" for changes in a share.

**5. (a)** 0.85 x 0.55 x 0.75 = 0.3506 [1]; cube root = 0.705 [1].

**(b)** The geometric mean penalises uneven performance [1]; Ulmar's low education index (0.55) pulls the HDI below the arithmetic mean of 0.717, because high health and income scores cannot fully compensate [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 4, 12, 25, 47, 100 [2] (one mark for at least three correct values).

**(b)** 53 / 4 = 13.25, so about 13 times larger [1].

**(c)** The Gini coefficient measures how income is distributed [1], not its absolute level, so a country can have a high Gini with few people below a poverty line, or a low Gini where nearly everyone is poor [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Export earnings depend on the world price of one commodity [1]. When prices fall, government revenue and foreign exchange fall [1], so spending on health and education may be cut and debts become harder to repay [1]. Few linkages from exporting raw materials mean few jobs in processing, so income stays low and concentrated [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Wellbeing is highest in North America, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea and Australasia [1]. It is lowest in much of sub-Saharan Africa [1] and parts of South Asia [1]. There are exceptions to a simple North-South pattern, such as high-income Gulf states and East Asian economies that have grown rapidly [1]. **[4]**

**9.** GNI per capita measures average income per person [1], which is simple to compare, but it hides distribution and non-income aspects of wellbeing [1]. The MPI measures overlapping household deprivations in health, education and living standards [1], so it shows who is poor and how, but its results depend on chosen cut-offs and weights and need detailed survey data [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Levels-based. Credit: primary sector provides food and raw materials [1] and, as farm productivity rises, releases labour to other sectors [1]; secondary sector adds value to raw materials [1] and creates urban jobs and linkages to suppliers [1]; tertiary sector supports the other sectors through transport, finance and trade [1] and grows as rising incomes raise demand for services [1]; quaternary sector generates innovation that raises productivity across the economy [1]; recognition that roles change over time, as in the Clark-Fisher model, with a valid limitation such as countries moving from farming to services [1]. **[8]**

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response. Indicative creditworthy content:

*Introduction:* define the HDI and its three dimensions [1]; note that inequality between countries has economic and social dimensions [1].

*For the view:* wider than income alone, combining health, education and income [1]; the geometric mean penalises uneven development [1]; published for almost every country, allowing comparison over time [1]; widely used by governments and agencies [1].

*Against the view:* national averages hide internal inequality [1]; the IHDI shows how much is lost to inequality [1]; the HDI ignores political freedom, security and environment [1]; the Gini coefficient is better for distribution [1]; the MPI is better for household deprivation [1]; the GII is needed for gender gaps [1]; data quality is weakest where inequality is greatest [1].

*Purpose and context:* the most useful measure depends on the question being asked [1]; for a broad ranking the HDI may be best [1]; for distribution or poverty other measures are better [1].

*Judgement:* a clear view on "most useful" [1]; justified with reference to construction and examples [1]; recognition that measures are complementary [1]; a conclusion that answers the question directly [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* The best answers judge measures against a stated purpose rather than listing strengths and weaknesses.

**12.** Levels-based. Indicative content:

*Introduction:* define wellbeing and physical and human factors [1]; state a line of argument [1].

*Physical factors:* landlocked location and trade costs [1]; disease burden in tropical climates [1]; climatic hazards destroying assets [1]; resource endowment [1].

*Human factors:* colonial history and commodity dependence [1]; conflict [1]; governance and corruption [1]; investment in education and health [1]; trade rules and debt [1].

*Interaction:* the effect of physical factors depends on human responses, such as irrigation, disease control or trade corridors [1]; resource-rich countries can still have low wellbeing because of governance, showing human factors can outweigh physical ones [1]; landlocked countries vary widely in wellbeing [1].

*Scale and time:* physical factors may matter more in the poorest, most hazard-exposed countries [1]; human factors explain change over time, such as rapid growth in East Asia [1].

*Judgement:* a supported view on "how far" [1]; justified with named examples [1]; recognition that factors combine [1]; a conclusion that answers the question [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Arguing that physical factors set limits and human factors decide outcomes is a strong evaluative line, but it needs evidence.
