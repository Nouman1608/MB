## Section A

**1.** State the two contexts this component asks students to consider for every text. **[2]**

**2.** Name one non-literary text type this component's questions could apply to. **[1]**

**3.** State why "context" should not be treated as background information mentioned only once. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** A 1980s advertisement for a household product uses gender roles that a modern audience would consider outdated.

**(a)** Identify the production context assumption this advertisement relies on. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a modern reader's reception of this advertisement differs from its original audience's likely reception, and why this shift itself is the analytical point. **[3]**

**5.** A political speech from the 1960s uses formal, elevated rhetoric that would seem unusually formal if delivered today.

**(a)** Explain one reason a modern reader might respond differently to this register than the speech's original audience. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how a student could use this text to demonstrate the "shift in reception" skill this component rewards, rather than simply describing the speech's style. **[2]**

**6.** A novel written in the 19th century contains an assumption about social class that its original readers would have accepted without comment, but which a contemporary reader might question or misread.

**(a)** Explain why identifying this gap between original and contemporary understanding is more valuable analytically than simply summarising the novel's plot. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how this same analytical approach could be adapted for a non-literary text studied in the same course. **[2]**

## Section C

**7.** Choosing one text you have studied (literary or non-literary), analyse both its production context and how its reception has shifted for a contemporary reader, and explain what this shift reveals about the text's meaning. **[8]**

## Worked answers

**1.** Production context (when/where/by whom a text was made) and reception context (when/where/by whom it is now being read). **[2]**

**2.** Any valid example, e.g. journalism, advertising, a speech, a report, or digital/social media. **[1]**

**3.** Because context actively shapes how meaning is made and received throughout an analysis, not just a fact to mention once before moving on to other observations. **[2]**

**4. (a)** The advertisement assumes its original audience would find the depicted gender roles unremarkable or normal, reflecting the social attitudes and expectations of its production period rather than being intended as provocative or ironic. **[2]**
**(b)** A modern reader is likely to find the gender roles dated, stereotypical or even offensive, where the original audience would likely have received them as an unremarkable, even reassuring, reflection of everyday domestic life; this shift -- from unremarkable to noticeably dated -- is itself evidence of how social attitudes embedded in a text's production context can become visible only once reception context has moved on, which is exactly the kind of active, shift-tracing analysis this component rewards over static description of either moment alone. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Public rhetorical register has generally become less formal over time, so a modern reader accustomed to more conversational public speech may perceive the same elevated, formal language as distant, artificial or "old-fashioned," where the original audience would likely have received it as appropriately serious and authoritative for the occasion. **[2]**
**(b)** Rather than only describing the speech's formal features, a student should explicitly compare how the register would have been received by its original audience against how a contemporary audience is likely to receive it, naming the specific shift (e.g. authoritative-then vs distant-now) and explaining what that shift suggests about changing expectations of public communication. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Because the novel's plot exists independently of when it is read, but the gap between what its original readers accepted unquestioned and what a contemporary reader notices or challenges reveals something specifically about how meaning is historically situated -- summarising plot treats the text as fixed and timeless, while identifying the reception gap treats meaning as actively shaped by the reader's own context, which is the governing idea this component is built around. **[3]**
**(b)** The same approach applies by asking, for a non-literary text (e.g. a historical newspaper article or advertisement), what assumptions its original audience would have accepted without comment, and then identifying what a contemporary reader is likely to notice, question or find dated in those same assumptions -- the underlying analytical move (identifying a reception gap, not just describing content) transfers directly regardless of whether the text is literary or non-literary. **[2]**

**7.** A strong answer selects a specific studied text and addresses both contexts explicitly rather than favouring one: production context (who made the text, when, for what original audience, and what assumptions or conventions that audience would have shared) and reception context (how a reader today is likely to understand, question or reinterpret the same text, and specifically what has changed). The strongest answers identify a precise shift -- not just "attitudes have changed in general" but a specific instance where an assumption, reference or stylistic choice would have functioned differently for its original audience than it does now -- and explain what that specific shift reveals about the text's meaning, such as showing that a text's apparent meaning is not fixed but depends partly on when and by whom it is read. Answers that discuss only production context, or only note that "things are different now" without specifying how, fall short of the component's central analytical demand. **[8]** (Marks for accurate, specific production-context detail; a precisely identified reception shift rather than a general claim; and a clear explanation of what the shift reveals about meaning.)

## Why every answer names a specific shift

Every worked answer in this set deliberately names a precise reception shift rather than a general
observation that "things have changed," because the revision notes identify this exact distinction --
between describing two contexts side by side and actively tracing how reception has shifted -- as
what separates a strong Time and Space answer from an adequate one. Question 6 in particular is
written to show that this skill applies identically to literary and non-literary texts, matching the
revision notes' point that the course's dual text-type structure expects the same analytical questions
to apply to both equally.
