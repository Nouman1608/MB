Section A concentrates on case study 2, German and Italian expansion. Section B is a one-hour source paper on case study 1, Japanese expansion in East Asia, with the Paper 1 tariffs of 3, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks.

**Source A** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a statement issued by a Japanese government spokesman to foreign journalists, October 1931.

> Japan has no territorial ambitions in Manchuria. Our troops acted only to protect the railway and the lives of Japanese subjects against Chinese bandits and lawless soldiers. Manchuria has been plunged into chaos by Chinese misrule, and Japan, which has invested so much in its development, cannot stand by. Order will be restored, and our forces will withdraw when it is safe.

**Source B** -- *Invented for practice:* description of a cartoon published in a European newspaper, 1933. A Japanese soldier stands on a map of Manchuria, holding a rolled-up paper labelled "Report". Behind him, a group of elderly men in formal dress, labelled "League of Nations", wag their fingers. The soldier is walking towards a door marked "Exit". The caption reads: "Thank you for your opinion."

**Source C** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from the diary of a Japanese diplomat posted in Europe, February 1933.

> The Assembly has voted against us, and our delegation will leave. I fear the army has led us into isolation from which we cannot easily return. The officers in Manchuria acted first and asked Tokyo afterwards, and now the government must defend what it never decided. Yet I cannot deny that our people cheer every victory, and no cabinet could order a retreat and survive.

**Source D** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a modern historian's study of Japanese foreign policy, published in 2008.

> Manchuria was not the work of a few hot-headed officers alone. The army's seizure was popular because the Depression had made the promise of land and resources seem a matter of survival, and because Chinese nationalism appeared to threaten Japan's position on the mainland. The League's failure to act did not cause Japanese expansion, but it showed Tokyo that expansion would carry little cost.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** ways in which Germany challenged the Treaty of Versailles between 1933 and 1936. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** ideological and **one** economic reason for Italy's invasion of Abyssinia. **[4]**

**3.** Explain why League sanctions failed to stop Italy in Abyssinia. **[3]**

**4.** Outline the significance of the Anglo-German Naval Agreement (1935) for the Stresa Front. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **two** reasons why Britain followed a policy of appeasement towards Germany in 1938. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why the occupation of Bohemia and Moravia in March 1939 changed British policy. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the importance of the Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939) for the outbreak of war. **[3]**

## Section B

**8.** What, according to Source A, were the reasons for Japanese military action in Manchuria? **[3]**

**9.** What is the message conveyed by Source B? **[2]**

**10.** With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source C for a historian studying the role of the army in Japanese foreign policy. **[4]**

**11.** Compare and contrast what Sources C and D reveal about the causes of Japanese expansion in Manchuria. **[6]**

**12.** Using the sources and your own knowledge, examine the reasons for Japanese expansion in East Asia between 1931 and 1941. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: leaving the Disarmament Conference and the League (1933) (1); rearmament, including the announcement of conscription (1935) (1); open creation of an air force (1935) (1); remilitarisation of the Rhineland (March 1936) (1). **[2]**

**2.** 2 marks for each developed reason. Ideological: Fascism glorified war and Mussolini wanted a new Roman empire, which included avenging the Italian defeat at Adwa in 1896 (2). Economic: Mussolini hoped colonies would provide land for settlers, raw materials and markets, and a victory would distract from economic difficulties at home (2). **[4]**

**3.** Sanctions excluded oil, the resource Italy most needed for its war (1). Britain and France did not close the Suez Canal, so Italian troops and supplies reached East Africa (1). Some states, including non-members such as the United States, continued to trade with Italy, and Britain and France wanted to keep Italy as an ally against Germany, so they were unwilling to go further (1). **[3]**

**4.** The Stresa Front of April 1935 joined Britain, France and Italy against German breaches of Versailles (1). Two months later Britain agreed to German naval expansion without consulting its partners, which accepted a breach of Versailles and weakened trust within the Front (1). **[2]**

**5.** 2 marks for each developed reason: Britain was not ready militarily, and rearmament needed time, so Chamberlain sought to delay or avoid war (2); many in Britain believed Versailles had been unfair and that German claims to German-speaking areas such as the Sudetenland were reasonable (2); public opinion feared another war on the scale of 1914-1918 and the bombing of cities (2); Britain lacked reliable allies: France was divided, the USSR distrusted and the United States isolationist (2). **[4]**

**6.** Bohemia and Moravia were not German-speaking, so Hitler could no longer claim to be uniting Germans (1). The occupation broke the Munich Agreement and showed that his promises could not be trusted (1). Britain therefore turned to deterrence, guaranteeing Poland at the end of March 1939 and speeding rearmament (1). **[3]**

**7.** It removed the danger of a Soviet response to an attack on Poland, so Germany faced no two-front war (1). A secret protocol divided Poland and eastern Europe between Germany and the USSR (1). It ended British and French hopes of an alliance with the USSR and made Hitler confident that the Western powers would not fight, so he invaded Poland on 1 September (1). **[3]**

**8.** 1 mark per point, up to 3: to protect the railway (1); to protect Japanese subjects (1); because Manchuria had fallen into chaos under Chinese rule (1); to safeguard Japanese investment (1); to restore order (1). **[3]**

**9.** 1 mark per point, up to 2: Japan ignored the League's report and simply left the League (1); the League was powerless and could only disapprove (1); Japan kept Manchuria regardless (1). **[2]**

**10.** Markbands (summarised from the guide's Question 2 descriptors): **3-4 marks**, explicit discussion of both value and limitations, linked to origin, purpose and content; **1-2 marks**, limited, one-sided, or not linked to the topic.
- *Value:* origin, a Japanese diplomat writing privately at the time of the League vote; purpose, a personal diary, so candid views he could not express publicly; content shows the army acting without authority and the government's inability to reverse its actions because of public support.
- *Limitations:* a diplomat may resent the army's influence over foreign policy and exaggerate it; written in Europe, he may not know Tokyo's decisions at first hand; one individual's view at one moment. **[4]**

**11.** Markbands: **5-6 marks**, detailed comparison and contrast with explicit links throughout; **3-4 marks**, some similarities and differences, possibly unbalanced; **1-2 marks**, superficial or sources described separately.
- *Comparisons:* both agree the army took the lead in Manchuria; both see popular support as important.
- *Contrasts:* C stresses the army acting independently of the government, D argues it was "not the work of a few hot-headed officers alone"; D gives economic and Chinese nationalist causes that C does not mention; D links the League's failure to later expansion, while C sees the League vote only as a source of isolation; C regrets expansion, D explains it. **[6]**

**12.** Markbands: **7-9 marks**, focused response using the sources and accurate own knowledge, with effective synthesis; **4-6 marks**, mostly focused, uses sources and some own knowledge; **1-3 marks**, limited focus, sources described or own knowledge only.
- *From the sources:* A (official justification: order, investment, protection of subjects); B (League powerless); C (army independence and popular support); D (Depression, Chinese nationalism, low cost of expansion).
- *Own knowledge, domestic causes:* the Kwantung Army and the Mukden Incident; assassination of Inukai (1932) and the February 26 Incident (1936); the collapse of silk exports in the Depression; the search for resources for total war.
- *Own knowledge, international causes:* Chinese disunity and the Nationalist-Communist conflict; League inaction after Lytton; Western weakness in the Depression; German successes in Europe in 1940 opening French and Dutch colonies; US embargoes in 1940-1941 that pushed Japan south.
- *Judgement:* for example, domestic militarism and economic crisis drove expansion, while the weakness of international responses decided its pace and direction. **[9]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Source A useless because it is propaganda?**
No. It is valuable as evidence of how Japan justified the occupation to foreign opinion, even though it is unreliable on the army's real aims.

**Should Question 12 go beyond Manchuria?**
Yes. The question covers 1931 to 1941, so include the war in China from 1937 and the southward advance as well.
