Condensed notes on section **3.9.3 Assessing globalisation and internationalisation** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is A-level only. The weighted scoring and Marchmont off-shoring examples are in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-globalisation-and-internationalisation/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-globalisation-and-internationalisation-practice/).

## Specification content

- Reasons for greater globalisation; its importance, and the importance of emerging economies, for business.
- Reasons for targeting, operating in and trading with international markets; entry methods: export, licensing, alliances, direct investment.
- Factors influencing the attractiveness of international markets.
- Reasons for producing and sourcing more abroad: off-shoring and re-shoring.
- Ways of entering international markets and their value; being a multinational.
- Influences on buying, selling and producing abroad.
- Managing international business: pressures for local responsiveness and for cost reduction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Globalisation | Growing integration of the world's economies |
| Internationalisation | A business's move to sell, source or produce abroad |
| Multinational | A business with operations in more than one country |
| Emerging economy | Fast-growing economy moving towards industrialisation |
| Export | Selling goods made at home to overseas customers |
| Licensing | Granting a foreign firm rights to use brand or technology for a fee |
| Alliance | Co-operation with a partner, sharing costs and risk |
| Direct investment | Buying or building operations abroad |
| Off-shoring | Moving production or services abroad |
| Re-shoring | Bringing production or services back home |

## Why globalisation has grown

Trade liberalisation; cheaper transport and communication; technology and the internet; financial liberalisation; growth of multinationals; rising incomes and converging tastes.

## Opportunities and threats

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| Bigger markets; economies of scale | More foreign competition at home |
| Cheaper inputs | Supply chain disruption |
| Risk spread across markets | Exchange rate risk |
| Skills, technology, finance | Price and margin pressure |

## Emerging economies

Offer rising middle classes, large populations and low-cost production. Risks: instability, corruption, weak IP protection, poor infrastructure, currency swings, different tastes. May also produce new competitors.

## Attractiveness of a market

Size and growth; incomes; competition; political and legal stability; trade barriers; culture and language; infrastructure; exchange rates; cost of entry; labour (for production).

Weighted score = sum of (weight × score). Example: weights 0.5 and 0.5, scores 4 and 2 give 3.0.

## Entry methods

| Method | Value | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Export | Low cost and risk; flexible | Transport costs, tariffs; less control |
| Licensing | Low investment; quick; local knowledge | Less control of quality; may train a rival |
| Alliance | Shares risk and cost; local knowledge | Conflicts; profit sharing; know-how leaks |
| Direct investment | Full control; close to customers; avoids tariffs | High cost and risk; political risk |

Choice depends on resources, risk appetite, control needed, speed, local rules and experience. Many firms move from exporting to direct investment as a market grows.

## Off-shoring and re-shoring

| Off-shore because | Re-shore because |
|---|---|
| Lower labour or material costs | Rising wages abroad |
| Skills or resources | Shipping costs and lead times |
| Proximity to customers | Quality problems and disruption |
| Avoiding tariffs | Faster response; reputation; incentives |

Always count all costs: shipping, tariffs, inventory, quality control, redundancy and set-up.

Mini-example: UK cost £30 a unit. Overseas: £20 production + £4 shipping + £1 tariff + £1.50 other = £26.50. Saving £3.50 a unit; on 100,000 units £350,000 a year.

## Local responsiveness against cost reduction

| Pressure | Meaning | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Cost reduction | Standardise globally | Economies of scale; may not suit local tastes |
| Local responsiveness | Adapt to local tastes, laws and language | More sales; higher cost; less control |

Middle path: a global core with local variations.

## Model analysis paragraph

"Licensing could be a good first step into an overseas market. The brand owner earns a royalty, for example 6% of £10m is £600,000 a year, without building factories. The partner brings local knowledge. But the owner has less control over quality, and a poor product could damage the global brand. The partner may also learn the business and become a rival. The choice depends on how much the brand owner values control against speed and low risk."

## Command words

- **Assess a method of entry:** compare cost, risk, control, speed and local knowledge.
- **Evaluate a market:** use factors such as size, growth, stability, competition and ease of entry.

## Exam traps

- Globalisation is the trend; internationalisation is the firm's decision.
- Off-shoring is not always cheaper when all costs are counted.
- Licensing grants rights; it is not direct investment.
- Weighted scores are subjective; test the weights.
- Always consider political and cultural risk.

## Self-test

1. Define "globalisation".
2. Give two reasons for the growth of globalisation.
3. Give two reasons a firm might target an emerging economy.
4. Name the four entry methods in the specification.
5. Give one limit of exporting.
6. Give one risk of licensing.
7. A licensee sells £5m and pays an 8% royalty. Calculate the royalty.
8. Define "re-shoring" and give one reason for it.
9. A market has weight 0.6 for size (score 4) and 0.4 for stability (score 3). Calculate the weighted score.
10. Explain "pressure for local responsiveness".
11. Why might a multinational prefer standardised products?
12. Give two risks of operating in an emerging economy.

### Answers

1. The growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment and information flows.
2. Any two: trade liberalisation; cheaper transport; the internet; financial liberalisation; growth of multinationals.
3. Any two: rising middle class; large population; fast growth; lower costs.
4. Export, licensing, alliances, direct investment.
5. Any one: transport costs; tariffs; exchange rate risk; less control over marketing.
6. Any one: loss of control over quality and brand; the licensee may become a rival.
7. 8% × £5m = **£400,000**.
8. Bringing production back to the home country; for example rising wages abroad or long lead times.
9. 0.6 × 4 + 0.4 × 3 = 2.4 + 1.2 = **3.6**.
10. The need to adapt products, marketing and management to local tastes, laws and culture.
11. It gives economies of scale and lower costs through common design, production and purchasing.
12. Any two: political instability; corruption; weak protection of intellectual property; poor infrastructure; currency swings.
