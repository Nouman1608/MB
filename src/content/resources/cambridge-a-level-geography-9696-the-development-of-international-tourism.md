This guide covers section 13.3 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists "reasons for, and trends in, the growth of international tourism"; "the impacts of tourism on the environments, societies and economies (local and national) of tourist destinations"; "carrying capacity and the tourism multiplier effect"; "recent developments in different types of tourism (including ecotourism)"; and states that candidates "should be able to critically evaluate the life cycle model of tourism". It is part of Topic 13, Global interdependence, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 13 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-global-interdependence/) defines the key ideas and uses Venice for the 13.4 case study. This page separates demand and supply reasons for growth, shows how to calculate a multiplier, adds two models that support evaluation (Doxey's irritation index and Plog's traveller types), and gives a fuller critique of Butler's model. Condense it with the [13.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-development-of-international-tourism-revision-notes/) and test it with the [13.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-development-of-international-tourism-practice/).

## Reasons for growth

**Demand factors (why people want to travel and can afford to):**

- rising real incomes and a growing middle class in MICs;
- paid holidays and more leisure time;
- longer, healthier retirements in HICs;
- education, curiosity and the influence of film, television and social media;
- family links created by migration.

**Supply factors (why travel has become easier and cheaper):**

- jet aircraft, then wide-bodied jets, which cut the cost per seat;
- low-cost airlines and the opening up of air routes;
- package holidays and, later, online booking and price comparison;
- easier visa rules and the opening of countries to visitors;
- government marketing and investment in airports, resorts and attractions.

## Trends

- **Long-term growth** in international arrivals, interrupted by shocks such as terrorist attacks, financial crises and disease outbreaks; the sharpest fall was during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.
- **Changing destinations:** Europe and North America still receive many visitors, but the share going to Asia and the Pacific has grown.
- **New source markets:** outbound tourism from China, India and other MICs has grown rapidly; China became the largest source of international tourism spending in the 2010s.
- **Changing products:** more short breaks, long-haul trips, cruises, city breaks and independent travel; fewer traditional two-week beach packages in some markets.

## Impacts at local and national scale

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Economic (national) | Foreign exchange; tax revenue; investment in airports and roads | Leakage to foreign-owned firms; dependence on a volatile industry |
| Economic (local) | Jobs; demand for local farms and crafts; new small businesses | Seasonal, low-paid jobs; rising land and house prices |
| Social | Funding for heritage and culture; pride in local identity; better services | Displacement of residents; crime; loss of cultural authenticity; overcrowding |
| Environmental | Entry fees and visitor spending fund conservation; protected areas | Erosion of paths and dunes; water shortages; waste and sewage; habitat loss; aviation emissions |

The same impact can look different at different scales: a resort may add to national foreign earnings while local residents lose access to the beach.

## Carrying capacity

Carrying capacity is the level of use a destination can sustain without unacceptable damage. Its three forms (physical, ecological and perceptual or social) often have different limits; the lowest one usually sets the practical limit.

A related approach, **limits of acceptable change**, sets indicators (such as path width or crowding) and acceptable thresholds, then manages to keep within them, rather than fixing a single number of visitors.

**Doxey's irritation index (1975)** describes how residents' attitudes can change as tourism grows: **euphoria** (visitors welcomed), **apathy** (taken for granted), **annoyance** (concern at crowding and prices) and **antagonism** (open hostility). It is a way of judging social carrying capacity.

## The tourism multiplier effect

Visitor spending is **direct** income for hotels and attractions. Those businesses buy supplies and pay wages (**indirect** income), and workers spend their wages locally (**induced** income). At each round, some money **leaks** out of the local economy: imported food and fuel, profits to foreign owners, savings and taxes.

### Worked example

*Tourists spend 10 million currency units in a fictional resort. At each round of spending, 40% leaks out of the local economy.*

- Round 1: 10 million.
- Round 2: 60% of 10 = 6 million re-spent locally.
- Round 3: 60% of 6 = 3.6 million.
- Round 4: 2.16 million, and so on.
- Total local income approaches 10 ÷ 0.40 = **25 million**, a multiplier of **2.5**.

If leakage rises to 80%, total income is 10 ÷ 0.80 = 12.5 million, a multiplier of only 1.25. Destinations with local supply chains and local ownership therefore gain far more from each tourist.

## Recent developments in types of tourism

- **Ecotourism:** responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, supports local people and involves education. Costa Rica is widely known for ecotourism based on its protected areas. Critics point to greenwashing, the emissions of long-haul flights and lodges that are not locally owned.
- **Community-based tourism:** tourism owned and managed by local communities, so more income stays local.
- **Cruise tourism:** large numbers arrive for a few hours, spend little ashore and crowd small ports.
- **Medical tourism:** travel for cheaper or faster treatment, for example to Thailand or India.
- **Dark tourism:** visits to sites of death or disaster.
- **Volunteer tourism:** combining travel with volunteering, criticised when it serves visitors more than hosts.
- **Adventure tourism** and **wellness tourism**, often in remote or mountain areas.
- **Overtourism:** the term now used for destinations where visitor numbers exceed social or ecological carrying capacity.

## Critically evaluating Butler's life cycle model

Butler's tourism area life cycle (1980) has the stages exploration, involvement, development, consolidation and stagnation, followed by a range of possible outcomes from rejuvenation to decline.

**Indicators of each stage:**

| Stage | Visitors | Facilities and control | Resident attitudes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exploration | Few, adventurous | Local, informal | Curious, welcoming |
| Involvement | Rising | Local businesses provide services | Positive |
| Development | Rapid growth | External investment; large hotels | Growing concern |
| Consolidation | Slowing growth | Destination dominated by tourism | Some opposition |
| Stagnation | Peak; capacity reached | Ageing facilities | Frequent tension |

Plog's traveller types (1974) fit alongside: **allocentric** travellers seek new places and arrive early; **psychocentric** travellers prefer familiar, safe places and arrive at the mass-tourism stage.

**Strengths:** simple and widely applicable; links growth to carrying capacity; warns planners that unmanaged growth leads to stagnation; offers several outcomes rather than inevitable decline.

**Weaknesses:**

- Stages can usually be identified only in hindsight, so it is weak as a forecasting tool.
- The length of each stage varies hugely; some destinations jump stages after large-scale investment.
- A destination may serve several markets at different stages at once.
- External shocks (conflict, pandemics) can cause sudden decline unrelated to the cycle.
- Government and private intervention can change the path at any stage.
- It is hard to define the boundary of the "area" being studied.

**Judgement to aim for:** the model is a useful descriptive framework and planning warning, but a poor predictor, and works best when combined with carrying capacity and resident attitudes.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why the tourism multiplier effect is smaller in some destinations than others. [5]*

> The multiplier depends on how much tourist spending is re-spent locally (**principle**). Where hotels are foreign-owned, profits leave the country (**leakage of profits**). Where food, drink and fuel are imported, money flows to overseas suppliers (**import leakage**). All-inclusive resorts keep tourists' spending inside the hotel, so little reaches local businesses (**enclave tourism**). Destinations with local ownership and supply chains therefore have larger multipliers (**conclusion**).

## Common mistakes

- Listing reasons for growth without separating demand and supply.
- Describing impacts without saying local or national.
- Treating carrying capacity as a single fixed number.
- Calculating a multiplier without accounting for leakage.
- Describing Butler's stages without critical evaluation, which the syllabus requires.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** trends: direction, rate, regional shifts and shocks, with figures from the resource.
- **Explain** impacts: give the mechanism and the scale.
- **Critically evaluate** the life cycle model: apply it to data or a named place, then test it against the weaknesses and conclude.
