This guide covers section 11.2 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. It is a case study: candidates "must study the need for, and some of the difficulties in, the management of agricultural change in one country, at the local scale (the farm, holding or producer) and at the national scale, and evaluate the attempted solutions". It is part of Topic 11, Production, location and change, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 11 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-production-location-and-change/) sets out India as a full case study. This page shows how to build any 11.2 case study so that it answers the syllabus wording, how to evaluate it, and gives China as a second worked example. Condense it with the [11.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-agricultural-change-revision-notes/) and practise with the [11.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-agricultural-change-practice/).

## Unpacking the syllabus wording

| Phrase | What your case study must contain |
|---|---|
| "the need for" | Why change was necessary: food shortage, low incomes, degradation, rural poverty, export ambitions |
| "some of the difficulties" | Physical, economic, social and political obstacles to change |
| "management of agricultural change" | Deliberate action by governments, agencies, co-operatives or farmers |
| "one country" | A single named country; examples from elsewhere only for comparison |
| "local scale (the farm, holding or producer)" | At least one named farm, village, co-operative or type of producer |
| "national scale" | Laws, prices, subsidies, land reform, research and extension services |
| "evaluate the attempted solutions" | Judge what worked, for whom, at what cost and for how long |

A common weakness is a case study that is all national policy. The local scale needs real detail: what changed on the holding, who made the decision, and what it meant for the household.

## Why agricultural change needs managing

Change happens without management, through markets, migration and technology. Management is needed when unmanaged change produces outcomes a society does not accept:

- **food insecurity**, when output does not keep up with population or demand;
- **rural poverty and inequality**, when gains go to larger farmers;
- **land degradation**, such as soil erosion, salinisation or falling water tables;
- **labour loss**, when young people leave and holdings are left to older farmers;
- **trade pressure**, when cheaper imports undercut domestic producers.

## Difficulties: a checklist

- **Physical:** drought, poor soils, limited water, climate change.
- **Economic:** cost of inputs and machinery, limited credit, volatile prices, the cost of subsidies to the state.
- **Social:** small and fragmented holdings, insecure tenure, ageing farm populations, low literacy, resistance to new methods.
- **Political:** competing interest groups, the scale of reform needed, weak enforcement, protest when support is withdrawn.
- **Scale mismatch:** a national policy may suit some regions and harm others.

## Worked case study: China

### The need for change

From 1958 China organised farming in large **people's communes**, where land was collectively worked and output shared. Incentives to work harder were weak, and the famine of 1959–61 showed the dangers of centrally planned farming. By the late 1970s output per person was still low and many rural households were poor.

### Local scale: Xiaogang village, Anhui province

In 1978 eighteen farming households in Xiaogang secretly agreed to divide the collective land into household plots. Each household would deliver a quota to the state and keep or sell anything above it. The next harvest was much larger than before. This is the producer-scale origin of the **Household Responsibility System**.

### National scale: from communes to household farming

- The Household Responsibility System spread across the country in the early 1980s. Land stayed collectively owned, but households held contracts to farm it.
- Grain output rose sharply in the early 1980s as households responded to the incentive to sell surplus.
- Contracts were later lengthened to give farmers more security; in 2017 the government announced a further 30-year extension.
- From the mid-2010s, policy separated three rights: collective **ownership**, household **contract** rights, and **management** rights that can be rented to others. This lets households that have moved to cities rent out their plots, so that larger family farms and co-operatives can form.
- The national agricultural tax was abolished in 2006.
- To protect food security, the state set a minimum area of farmland (the "red line" of 1.8 billion mu, about 120 million hectares) that must not be lost to building.

### Difficulties that remain

- Holdings are small, well under a hectare on average, and often split into scattered strips, which limits mechanisation.
- Young adults migrate to cities, so much farm work falls to older people and women.
- Heavy fertiliser use raised yields but polluted water and acidified soils; national plans from 2015 aimed to stop the growth in fertiliser and pesticide use.
- Farmers cannot sell collectively owned land, so they cannot use it as collateral or capital when they leave.

### Evaluation

- **Success at national scale:** decollectivisation gave rapid output growth and reduced rural poverty, showing the power of incentives.
- **Success at local scale:** the Xiaogang model worked because it matched the decision-maker (the household) with the reward.
- **Limits:** the small, equal plots that made the reform fair now hold back mechanisation and incomes; renting management rights is a partial solution, but tenure insecurity slows it.
- **Sustainability:** yield gains came partly from agrochemicals, creating degradation that later policy has had to correct.
- **Judgement:** the solutions worked best when they changed incentives for individual producers, and less well where they depended on rural people staying on the land.

## How to evaluate any 11.2 case study

Use clear criteria rather than "it was good":

| Criterion | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Did output, yields or incomes actually rise? By how much? |
| Equity | Who gained: large or small farmers, owners or tenants, which regions? |
| Sustainability | Were soils, water and ecosystems protected over time? |
| Cost | What did it cost the state or the farmer, and was it affordable? |
| Durability | Did benefits last after subsidies or projects ended? |
| Scale | Did a national solution work locally, and did local success scale up? |

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question: Evaluate the attempts to manage agricultural change at the local scale in one country you have studied.*

> In China, the Xiaogang households' 1978 agreement shows that local change succeeds when producers keep the reward for extra effort (**criterion: effectiveness, linked to a mechanism**). Their first harvest under household contracts was much larger than under the collective (**evidence**). However, the same equal division of land that made the reform acceptable left each family with small, scattered plots (**consequence**), which now limits machinery and incomes as younger members leave for cities (**later difficulty**). The local solution was therefore highly successful in the short term but has needed national changes to land rights to remain workable (**judgement across time and scale**).

## Common mistakes

- Writing only about national policy; the syllabus names both scales.
- Describing a solution without saying whether it worked.
- Using two countries; 11.2 asks for one, though you may compare briefly.
- Quoting figures you are unsure of; an accurate general point is safer.
- Forgetting the "need for" change, so the essay has no starting problem.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the need for**: start with the problem and its cause, then the consequence if nothing is done.
- **Assess the difficulties**: rank them, and say which are hardest to overcome and why.
- **Evaluate** or **How successful**: apply at least three criteria and vary your judgement by scale and group.
- **To what extent**: decide early, then test your view against counter-evidence in each paragraph.
