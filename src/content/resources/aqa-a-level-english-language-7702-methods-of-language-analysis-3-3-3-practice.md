These tasks practise 3.3.3 Methods of language analysis for the AQA A-level English Language (7702) non-exam
assessment. The transcript and figures are **original** and hypothetical, written for this page.

**Data P**: part of a student council meeting. Key: (.) micropause; = latching (no gap between turns); ↑ rising
pitch; line numbers on the left.

> 1 CHAIR: right (.) next item is the canteen (.) Leah  
> 2 LEAH: yeah so (.) basically people want more vegetarian options  
> 3 TOM: I mean (.) there's already the pasta=  
> 4 LEAH: =but that's like (.) every day  
> 5 CHAIR: okay (.) could we maybe do a survey  
> 6 TOM: ↑really (.) another survey  
> 7 CHAIR: I know (.) I know (.) but it'd give us something to show the head

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain what the symbol "=" between lines 3 and 4 shows, and what it suggests about the interaction. **[2]**

**2.** Why should an NEA transcript include line numbers and a key? Give **two** reasons. **[2]**

**3.** Write an operational definition of "hedge" that a student could use to code Data P consistently, and identify
**two** hedges in the data. **[3]**

**4.** As a hypothetical exercise, Group 1's transcript has 12 interruptions in 300 turns and Group 2's has 10 in 200
turns. Express each per 100 turns, and say which group interrupts more often. **[3]**

**5.** A student plans to investigate the language of three supermarket websites. Name **three** relevant language
levels and say what each would examine. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a student investigating attitudes to a local accent might analyse the wording of their own
questionnaire. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Analyse how the Chair manages the discussion in Data P, using discourse features. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse the pragmatics of lines 5 to 7, using politeness or face. **[5]**

**9.** Analyse the discourse markers in Data P and their functions. **[4]**

**10.** A student finds that Leah uses "like" four times in a longer recording. Explain how they should move from this
count to a qualitative analysis. **[4]**

**11.** Write a commentary sentence analysing a choice in your own persuasive speech at the level of grammar, and one
at the level of pragmatics. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate this student's analysis: *"Tom is rude because he interrupts and says 'really'. This proves boys are
more aggressive in meetings."* Rewrite it as a stronger analytical point. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Latching: Leah's turn begins with no gap after Tom's [1], suggesting she is keen to challenge his point and
that the discussion is lively and informal [1].

**2.** Line numbers let the analysis cite evidence precisely and let the moderator find it [1]; a key makes the
conventions clear so features such as pauses and overlaps are interpreted correctly [1].

**3.** Example definition: "a word or phrase that reduces the speaker's commitment to a claim or softens its force,
such as 'maybe', 'sort of', 'I think', 'I mean' when used to qualify" [1]. Hedges in the data, 1 mark each: "maybe"
(line 5) [1]; "I mean" (line 3) or "basically" if used to soften [1].

**4.** Group 1: 12 ÷ 300 × 100 = 4 per 100 turns [1]. Group 2: 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5 per 100 turns [1]. Group 2
interrupts more often relative to turns, although its raw count is lower [1].

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each [1] [1] [1]: graphology (layout, images, colour, navigation); lexis and semantics
(evaluative adjectives, semantic fields of freshness or value); grammar (imperatives, second-person address, noun
phrase modification); discourse (organisation of the page, calls to action); pragmatics (presupposition and implied
value).

**6.** The wording of questions shapes the responses, so a loaded question can bias the data [1]; analysing it allows
the student to evaluate the validity of their findings in the methodology [1].

**7.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: the discourse marker "right" (line 1) opens a new phase and signals the Chair's
control [1]; the Chair names the agenda item ("next item is the canteen") [1]; nominating the next speaker by name
("Leah") allocates the turn [1]; "okay" (line 5) closes the exchange between Leah and Tom and moves on [1]; the
Chair proposes the next step ("do a survey"), controlling the topic [1]; in line 7 the Chair responds to Tom's
challenge and justifies the proposal, maintaining authority while acknowledging dissent [1].

**8.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: the Chair's proposal is softened by the modal "could", inclusive "we" and the hedge
"maybe", reducing the imposition on others' negative face [1]; Tom's rising "↑really" and "another survey" challenge
the proposal, a face-threatening act towards the Chair [1]; the implicature is that surveys are pointless or
overused [1]; the repeated "I know (.) I know" concedes Tom's point, protecting Tom's positive face by showing
agreement [1]; "but it'd give us something to show the head" offers a reason, a politeness strategy that preserves
the Chair's proposal while appearing reasonable [1].

**9.** Up to 4 points, 1 mark each: "right" marks a transition to a new topic [1]; "so" introduces Leah's report and
frames it as the main point [1]; "I mean" introduces Tom's qualification or mild disagreement [1]; "but" (line 4 and
line 7) introduces counter-points, structuring the exchange as argument and response [1].

**10.** Credit, 1 mark each: locate each instance in context, citing line numbers [1]; classify each by function, for
example quotative, hedge, filler or focus marker [1]; relate functions to context, such as informality or
uncertainty [1]; compare with other speakers or settings before drawing cautious conclusions [1].

**11.** Credit, 1 mark each: grammar sentence names a feature accurately [1] and explains its effect for the audience
[1], for example: "I used parallel declaratives ('We built this library. We fund this library.') to create a rhythm
that councillors would remember." Pragmatics sentence names a feature [1] and explains its effect [1], for example:
"The question 'Who will tell the children?' implies blame without stating it, which avoided an open face threat to
councillors I needed to persuade."

**12.** Evaluation, 1 mark each: "rude" is an impressionistic judgement, not linguistic analysis [1]; the latching
in lines 3 and 4 is Leah's, not Tom's, so the evidence is misread [1]; one speaker cannot support a claim about all
boys, and roles may matter more than gender [1]. Rewrite (3 marks), for example: *"Tom's rising '↑really' (line 6)
challenges the Chair's proposal, implying that surveys are overused; the Chair's concession 'I know (.) I know'
suggests Tom's view is shared. In this short sample, challenge seems linked to the topic and roles rather than to
any single speaker's personality."* Credit precise feature [1], function in context [1] and cautious claim [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Misreading who latches or overlaps, by not checking the key and line numbers.
- Counting features without defining them.
- Comparing raw counts across data sets of different sizes.
- Personal judgements ("rude", "nice") instead of linguistic description.
