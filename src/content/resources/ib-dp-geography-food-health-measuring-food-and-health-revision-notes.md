## Food security

Exists when all people at all times have physical and economic access to enough safe and nutritious food for an active, healthy life. Four dimensions: **availability, access, utilisation, stability**.

| Dimension | Question it asks | Example of failure |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Is enough food produced or imported? | Harvest failure after drought |
| Access | Can households afford or obtain it? | Food in the market but prices too high for poor families |
| Utilisation | Can the body use it well? | Diarrhoeal disease from unsafe water stops nutrients being absorbed |
| Stability | Are the other three secure over time? | Seasonal hunger before harvest, or a sudden price spike |

Food insecurity can be **chronic** (long-lasting, linked to poverty) or **transitory** (short-term, after a shock such as conflict or flood).

Malnutrition covers too little food, too much, or poor-quality diets, so undernutrition and obesity both count.

### Terms that are easily confused

- **Hunger:** the discomfort of not having enough to eat; often used loosely for chronic undernourishment.
- **Undernourishment:** dietary energy intake below what is needed for a healthy, active life.
- **Undernutrition:** the physical result of too little food or poor absorption, shown by stunting, wasting or underweight.
- **Hidden hunger:** deficiency of micronutrients such as iron, iodine or vitamin A, which can occur even when calorie intake is adequate. Iron deficiency causes anaemia.
- **Double burden of malnutrition:** undernutrition and overweight or obesity in the same country, community or even household.

## Measures

| Type | Measures |
|---|---|
| Food | Calorie supply per person per day; prevalence of undernourishment |
| Anthropometric | Stunting (low height for age), wasting (low weight for height), BMI |
| Composite | Hunger indices combining several indicators |
| Health | Life expectancy, infant and under-five mortality, maternal mortality, disease incidence and prevalence, DALYs |

- **BMI = weight (kg) / height (m) squared.** Example: 52 / 2.56 = 20.3. A commonly used healthy adult range is 18.5 to 24.9.
- **DALY** = years of life lost to early death + years lived with disability.
- **Prevalence** example: 14.4 million of 120 million = 12 per cent.

### What each measure tells you

- **Stunting** reflects long-term undernutrition in early childhood; **wasting** reflects recent, acute food shortage or illness. A region with high wasting but moderate stunting suggests a recent crisis.
- Using the World Health Organization adult categories, a BMI below 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 overweight and 30 or above obese. BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat, so it is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.
- The **Global Hunger Index** combines four indicators: undernourishment, child stunting, child wasting and child mortality. Combining them gives a broader picture than calories alone.
- **Incidence** is the number of new cases in a period; **prevalence** is the total number of existing cases at a point in time. A long-lasting disease can have low incidence but high prevalence.
- **Infant mortality** is deaths under age one per 1,000 live births; **under-five mortality** uses the same base for deaths before age five; **maternal mortality ratio** is usually given per 100,000 live births.
- **Healthy life expectancy** estimates the years lived in good health, so it adds quality to the quantity measured by life expectancy.

## Worked examples

- **Infant mortality:** 45 infant deaths among 3,000 live births = 45 / 3,000 x 1,000 = 15 per 1,000.
- **DALYs:** an illustrative disease causes 200 early deaths, each losing 15 years (YLL = 3,000), and 1,000 people live with it for 4 years at a disability weight of 0.1 (YLD = 1,000 x 4 x 0.1 = 400). Total = 3,400 DALYs.
- **BMI category:** 95 kg and 1.78 m gives 95 / 3.1684 = 30.0, on the boundary of the obese category.

## Limits of data

National averages hide inequality between regions, income groups and genders. Data quality varies and definitions differ, so compare cautiously.

Further limits worth naming:

- Calorie supply per person is calculated from national food balances, so it says nothing about how food is shared within households.
- Surveys may miss people in informal settlements, conflict zones or nomadic groups.
- Data are often several years old by the time they are published, which matters after a sudden shock.
- Survey-based tools such as the Food Insecurity Experience Scale ask people about their experiences, capturing access problems that production data miss, but depend on honest recall.

## Global patterns

Reports by the Food and Agriculture Organization have consistently shown that the **largest number** of undernourished people live in Asia, while the **highest share** of the population affected is in sub-Saharan Africa. This difference between numbers and rates is a common data question. Overweight and obesity have risen in almost every world region, including in many lower- and middle-income countries.

## Nutrition transition

As incomes and urbanisation rise, diets shift to more processed foods, fats and sugar, increasing obesity and diet-related non-communicable disease, sometimes alongside continuing undernutrition.

The stages are often summarised as: traditional diets high in starchy staples -> rising consumption of meat, oils, sugar and processed food -> rising obesity and non-communicable disease -> in some wealthier groups, a later shift towards healthier eating. Urban lifestyles also reduce physical activity, reinforcing the trend.

## Command words

- **Describe** a map of undernutrition: give the overall pattern, quote values with units, name regions, then note exceptions.
- **Distinguish** two measures: give one clear difference for each, such as stunting (long-term) versus wasting (short-term).
- **Evaluate** a measure: give what it shows well, what it hides, and conclude on its usefulness for a stated purpose.

## Model paragraph

*Evaluate calorie supply per person as a measure of food security.* "Calorie supply per person is widely available and allows comparison over time and between countries, so it is useful for judging availability. However, it is a national average calculated from production and trade, so it hides unequal access between rich and poor households, and it ignores dietary quality, missing hidden hunger. It also says nothing about utilisation. It is therefore a useful starting point but should be combined with anthropometric indicators such as stunting."

## Exam traps

- Treating hunger and malnutrition as identical.
- Using national averages as if everyone in a country is the same.
- Forgetting that obesity is also malnutrition.
- Quoting country statistics from memory.
- Confusing incidence with prevalence.
- Mixing up the largest number of undernourished people with the highest rate.
