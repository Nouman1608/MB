These questions practise the three skills of a Historical Investigation in Section A of Pearson
Edexcel International GCSE History 4HI1 Paper 2: describing features (6), cross-referencing two
sources (8) and evaluating an interpretation using sources and own knowledge (16). Two enquiries
cover the five key topics of Investigation A1.

## Enquiry 1 — the road to war, 1905–14

**1(a)** Describe **two** features of the naval race between Britain and Germany, 1906–14. **[6]**

**Source A** (genuine). Sir Edward Grey, British Foreign Secretary, recalling a remark he made on the
evening of 3 August 1914, in his memoirs *Twenty-Five Years* (1925).

> "The lamps are going out all over Europe; we shall not see them lit again in our life-time."

**Source B** (invented for this exercise). From a letter by a British journalist in Berlin, July 1914.

> "Here they speak of war as a storm that must break sooner or later. Many officers seem almost
> relieved that the waiting is over, and the crowds on the Unter den Linden cheer the soldiers as if
> victory were already certain."

**1(b)** How far does Source A support the evidence of Source B about attitudes to the coming of war
in 1914? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**Extract C** (invented interpretation, written for this exercise).

> "The war of 1914 was not an accident. It came because the great powers had spent a decade
> building alliances and weapons that made any local crisis a European one."

**1(c)** How far do you agree with the interpretation in Extract C about the causes of the First
World War? Use Extract C, Sources A and B and your own knowledge to explain your answer. **[16]**

## Enquiry 2 — the course of the war, 1914–18

**2(a)** Describe **two** features of the Gallipoli campaign (1915–16). **[6]**

**Source D** (invented). From a British staff officer's report, July 1916.

> "The enemy's wire was found uncut in many places. Where the guns had done their work our men
> advanced; elsewhere the attack was held up with severe loss."

**Source E** (invented). From the memoir of a British private, published in 1930.

> "We were told the guns would leave nothing alive. We walked forward in lines, as ordered, and the
> machine-guns opened up from trenches that should have been empty."

**2(b)** How far does Source D support the evidence of Source E about the first day of the Battle
of the Somme? **[8]**

**Extract F** (invented interpretation, written for this exercise).

> "Germany lost the war in 1918 because its own spring offensive destroyed its army."

**2(c)** How far do you agree with the interpretation in Extract F about why Germany was defeated?
Use Extract F, Sources D and E and your own knowledge. **[16]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

Any two developed features, about 3 marks each, for example: Britain's launch of HMS *Dreadnought*
(1906), which made older battleships obsolete and let Germany compete on equal terms; German Navy
Laws and Tirpitz's expansion of the fleet, prompting British fears and the 1909 public campaign for
eight new dreadnoughts; Britain keeping a clear lead in numbers by 1914.

### 1(b) [8]

**Support:** both suggest that war was seen as a great, unavoidable turning point — Grey's "lamps
going out", B's "storm that must break".
**Challenge:** the tone is opposite — Grey expresses gloom and long-term loss, while B describes
relief and cheering crowds expecting victory.
**Context and provenance:** Grey, as Foreign Secretary, knew the scale of what was coming and wrote
with hindsight in 1925; B reports public mood in Berlin, where enthusiasm was widely recorded in the
first days. **Judgement:** limited support — they agree war was momentous but disagree on how it was
felt. Top answers make specific comparisons and reach a clear judgement.

### 1(c) [16]

**Supporting the interpretation:** the Triple Alliance and Triple Entente; the naval race and the
growth of conscript armies; war plans such as the Schlieffen Plan, which turned a war with Russia into
a war with France and Belgium; Source B's sense of inevitability.
**Challenging it:** the alliances did not oblige Britain or Italy to fight; previous crises (Morocco
1905 and 1911, Bosnia 1908–09) were settled peacefully; the decisions of July 1914 — the blank cheque,
the ultimatum to Serbia, Russian mobilisation — were choices; the invasion of Belgium decided British
entry; Grey's words suggest leaders who did not want war.
**Judgement:** the long-term system made a general war possible; July 1914 decisions made it
happen. Reward balanced use of extract, sources and own knowledge.

### 2(a) [6]

Two features, for example: the failed naval attempt to force the Dardanelles in March 1915; the
April 1915 landings at Helles and ANZAC Cove, where troops were pinned to narrow beaches; the
successful evacuation in December 1915–January 1916.

### 2(b) [8]

**Support:** both state that enemy defences survived the bombardment and that attacks were held up
with heavy loss ("wire… uncut"; machine-guns from trenches "that should have been empty").
**Difference:** D reports partial success where guns had worked; E describes general failure and
blames orders to walk forward.
**Provenance:** D is an official report written at the time in cautious language; E was written in
1930, when disillusionment with the war's leadership was common. **Judgement:** strong support on the
causes of failure, less on its extent.

### 2(c) [16]

**Supporting:** the spring offensive (March 1918) used Germany's best stormtroop units and left them
exhausted and holding long, exposed lines; the Allies then broke through from August.
**Other factors:** the British blockade and hunger at home; American troops and resources from 1917;
improved Allied tactics combining tanks, aircraft and artillery (Amiens, August 1918); the collapse of
Bulgaria, the Ottoman Empire and Austria-Hungary; mutiny and revolution in Germany; attrition from
the Somme and Passchendaele (relevant to Sources D and E).
**Judgement:** the offensive's failure was the immediate military cause, but long-term attrition,
blockade and American strength explain why Germany could not recover.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Summarising each source separately instead of comparing them point by point.
- Ignoring provenance (who, when, why) in the cross-reference question.
- In (c), using only own knowledge and forgetting to evaluate the extract.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the Paper 2 historical investigation question structured?**
Part (a) asks you to describe two features (6 marks), part (b) asks how far one source supports another (8 marks) and part (c) is a 16-mark judgement that uses the sources and your own knowledge.

**Why was HMS Dreadnought important?**
Launched in 1906, it made older battleships obsolete and intensified the Anglo-German naval race.

**What loses marks in the cross-reference question?**
Summarising each source separately instead of comparing them point by point, and ignoring provenance.
