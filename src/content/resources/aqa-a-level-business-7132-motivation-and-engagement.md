## Motivation and engagement

**Motivation** is the desire or drive to work towards a goal. **Engagement** is a deeper, longer-lasting commitment to the business, where employees care about its success and give extra effort. Motivated and engaged employees:

- are more **productive** and produce better quality work
- give better **customer service**
- are **absent less** and **leave less often**, cutting recruitment and training costs
- come up with **ideas** and accept change more readily
- improve the business's **reputation** as an employer, which helps recruitment
- reduce the need for close supervision.

The benefits show up as higher output per employee, lower labour cost per unit and lower turnover (see [analysing HR performance](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance/)). Poor motivation shows as absence, errors, complaints, high turnover and disputes.

## Theories of motivation

### F. W. Taylor: scientific management

Taylor (early 1900s) believed workers are motivated mainly by **money**. Managers should:

- break jobs into simple, repeated tasks and find the most efficient method (using time and motion study)
- select and train workers for those methods
- supervise closely
- pay by results (such as **piece rate**) so that higher output earns higher pay.

*Strengths:* highlights the importance of pay and clear methods; suits routine, repetitive work. *Weaknesses:* ignores social and psychological needs; repetitive jobs can be boring; heavy supervision demotivates; modern jobs are more varied and skilled. Taylor's ideas are behind piece rate pay and strict performance targets.

### Maslow: hierarchy of needs

Maslow (1940s to 1950s) argued that people have five levels of need, and that a lower need must be broadly satisfied before the next one motivates.

| Level | Need | Workplace examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Physiological | Pay enough for food and housing; breaks; comfortable temperature |
| 2 | Safety | Job security; safe conditions; pension |
| 3 | Social | Team membership; friendly colleagues; communication |
| 4 | Esteem | Recognition; status; promotion; praise |
| 5 | Self-actualisation | Challenging work; creativity; development to full potential |

*Application:* managers should find out which level their staff are at and offer rewards to match. Offering a team-building day (social) to staff who worry about redundancy (safety) will not work. *Strengths:* a wide view of needs; useful for matching rewards to individuals. *Weaknesses:* people do not always move up in order; needs differ by person and culture; hard to measure; self-actualisation is vague.

### Herzberg: two-factor theory

Herzberg (1950s) found that some factors cause **dissatisfaction** if they are bad but do not motivate when they are good, and others actually **motivate**.

| Hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction) | Motivators (create satisfaction and motivation) |
|---|---|
| Pay and benefits | Achievement |
| Working conditions | Recognition |
| Company policy and administration | The work itself (interest, variety) |
| Supervision | Responsibility |
| Job security | Advancement and personal growth |
| Relationships with colleagues | |

*Application:* a business must first get the hygiene factors right (fair pay, safe conditions), then add motivators through job enrichment, responsibility and recognition. A pay rise may stop complaints but will not by itself motivate for long. *Strengths:* explains why raising pay often fails to motivate; supports job enrichment. *Weaknesses:* based on a survey of professional workers, so may not fit all jobs; pay is a motivator for some people; relies on self-reported answers.

## Methods of improving motivation and engagement

### Financial methods

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Piece rate** | Pay per unit produced | Directly links pay to output; easy to understand; encourages effort | Quality may fall; stress; unsuitable where output is hard to measure; unfair if machine breaks down; pay unstable |
| **Commission** | Payment as a percentage of sales (alone or on top of basic salary) | Strong incentive for sales staff; cost rises only when sales rise | Encourages hard selling; staff may ignore service and teamwork; unstable income; pay is low in a downturn |
| **Salary scheme** | Fixed annual pay, possibly with benefits | Secure and predictable; easy to budget; supports teamwork and quality | Weak link to effort; may not motivate extra work; poor performers paid the same |
| **Performance-related pay (PRP)** | Bonuses or pay rises linked to meeting targets | Rewards achievement; focuses on goals | Targets may be unfair or hard to measure; can encourage short-term focus; may harm cooperation; those who miss targets are demotivated |

*Calculations.*

- Piece rate: 520 units at £0.80 earns 520 × £0.80 = **£416** a week.
- Commission: a basic of £1,200 a month plus 5% of sales above £20,000. Sales of £36,000 give 5% × (£36,000 − £20,000) = £800, so total pay is **£2,000**.
- PRP: a bonus of 6% of a £30,000 salary for hitting targets pays £30,000 × 0.06 = **£1,800**.

*Worked comparison.* A packing worker is paid £10 an hour for 40 hours, £400 a week, and packs 500 units, so labour cost is £0.80 per unit. The firm switches to piece rate of £0.80 per unit. Output rises to 600 units: pay is 600 × £0.80 = £480 (£80 more). Each unit has £1.80 of revenue after materials, so 100 extra units add £180, and the net gain per worker per week is £180 − £80 = **£100**. But if quality falls and returns rise, or if workers burn out, the gain may disappear.

### Non-financial methods

| Method | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empowerment** | Giving employees authority to make decisions about their work | Faster decisions; shows trust; develops staff; uses their knowledge | Needs training and trust; decisions may be poor; managers may feel threatened |
| **Team working** | Staff organised in teams with shared goals | Meets social needs; shares skills; better ideas; mutual support | Slower decisions; conflict; some members may rely on others; team needs training |
| **Flexible working** | Choice over hours or place: flexitime, part-time, job share, remote work | Better work-life balance; attracts and keeps staff; cuts absence | Harder to coordinate and supervise; not possible for all jobs; communication problems |
| **Job enrichment** | Giving more responsibility, challenge and variety in the same job (a vertical change) | Uses skills; satisfies esteem and self-actualisation needs; matches Herzberg's motivators | Needs training and may need higher pay; some workers do not want more responsibility |
| **Job rotation** | Moving staff between different jobs at the same level (a horizontal change) | Reduces boredom; broadens skills; cover for absence | Does not add challenge; training costs; staff may prefer a settled role |

Job enlargement (adding more tasks of the same level) is similar to rotation but has a lasting change in tasks.

## Influences on the choice of method

- **Nature of the work**: routine, measurable output (suits piece rate); creative or complex (suits empowerment, enrichment); teamwork-dependent work (avoid individual incentives).
- **Employees' needs and attitudes**: older staff may value security, younger staff development or flexibility (link to Maslow and Herzberg).
- **Management style and culture**: autocratic cultures lean towards financial control; democratic ones towards empowerment.
- **Cost and finance**: bonuses and training cost money; flexible working may be cheap.
- **Labour market**: skills shortages require better packages.
- **Competition**: rivals' pay and conditions.
- **Technology**: automation reduces repetitive work; remote working enables flexibility; monitoring tools can measure output.
- **Business objectives**: quality or cost, short- or long-term.
- **Legal and ethical influences**: minimum wage, working time, fairness.

## Assessing effectiveness

A method is effective if it achieves its purpose at an acceptable cost. Measures:

- **Productivity** and output per employee
- **Quality**: defects and complaints
- **Labour turnover** and absence
- **Employee engagement surveys**
- **Customer satisfaction**
- **Cost against benefit**: extra pay or training cost compared with the value of improvements.

Effectiveness is hard to prove because many things change at once (market, management, technology). The impact can take months, so short-term data can mislead, and what motivates one group may not motivate another. Good assessment uses several measures over time and compares groups where possible.

## Technique for exam questions

- Link the theory to the case: identify which need or factor the evidence refers to.
- Pair each method with the type of work and workforce in the case.
- Weigh benefits and costs, and say what the decision depends on: the type of job, the workforce, the cost and the objective.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that money is the only motivator, or that it never motivates.
- Describing theories without applying them.
- Confusing job rotation with job enrichment.
- Forgetting that piece rate and commission can damage quality and teamwork.
- Treating a pay rise as a Herzberg motivator rather than a hygiene factor.
- Recommending one method for all employees.
