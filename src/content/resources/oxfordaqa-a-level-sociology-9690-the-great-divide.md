Section **3.2.1.1 The Great Divergence** opens Unit 3 People and development in OxfordAQA
International AS/A level Sociology (9690). The specification asks "How a gap developed between
countries that developed quickly and those that have developed more slowly", then lists
imperialism ("the European empires; exploration and trade; enslavement; colonialism;
neocolonialism"); industrialisation, urbanisation and capitalism; reasons why Europe
industrialised ("modernisation theory; the Protestant work ethic (Weber); dependency theory;
enslavement"); legacies of colonialism and enslavement; and factors associated with development,
from life expectancy to democracy. The named sociologists for 3.2.1 are Bello, Braudel, Frank,
Polanyi, Rodney and Sachs. The [Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-people-and-development/)
summarises the main explanations (Rostow, Weber, Frank, Rodney, Williams, Pomeranz); this guide
goes deeper into the history, the named sociologists and how to argue about them.

Unit 3 is assessed by a 1 hour 30 minute exam of 50 marks with short and extended response
questions. Selected extended answers require examples from your own society or one you know.

## What the "gap" is, and why it matters

Historians disagree about how far ahead Europe already was before about 1800: some, following
Pomeranz, see the most advanced regions of Europe and Asia as broadly comparable, while others date
a European lead earlier. Either way, over the nineteenth century industrial societies in
north-western Europe and later North America pulled away in output, income and military power. The
sociological question is not only *when* the gap opened but *why*, and whether the same processes
still keep it open. Two broad families of answers compete:

- **Internal explanations**: something about European values, institutions or culture
  (modernisation theory, Weber).
- **External or relational explanations**: Europe's rise depended on taking resources, labour and
  markets from others (dependency theory, enslavement, imperialism).

Strong answers also use **contingent or material explanations**, such as geography and access to
energy, which fit neither camp neatly.

## Imperialism: from trade to rule

**Exploration and trade.** Portuguese voyages around Africa, Columbus's crossing of the Atlantic in
1492 and Vasco da Gama's arrival in India in 1498 opened sea routes controlled by European states.
Chartered trading companies followed, such as the English East India Company (founded 1600) and the
Dutch East India Company (1602), combining trade with armed force.

**Enslavement.** The Atlantic trade forcibly transported more than twelve million Africans to the
Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, to work on plantations producing sugar,
tobacco and cotton for European markets. **Walter Rodney** (*How Europe Underdeveloped Africa*,
1972) argued that the trade removed young, productive people, encouraged warfare and raiding, and
oriented African economies towards supplying captives rather than developing production.

**Colonialism.** Direct rule extended across Asia and Africa, especially in the nineteenth century.
After 1858 the British Crown ruled India directly, and the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 set rules for
European powers dividing Africa. Colonial economies were typically organised to export raw
materials and import manufactured goods. Railways often ran from mines and plantations to ports
rather than linking local markets. India's handloom textile industry declined in the face of
British machine-made cloth and colonial trade policy, a process historians describe as
**deindustrialisation**.

**Neocolonialism.** After formal independence, mostly after 1945, influence continued through debt,
trade terms, foreign ownership and conditions attached to loans. **Walden Bello**, a named
sociologist, has argued that structural adjustment programmes imposed by the International
Monetary Fund and World Bank from the 1980s opened poorer economies to foreign competition on unequal
terms.

## Industrialisation, urbanisation and capitalism

**Karl Polanyi** (*The Great Transformation*, 1944) argued that nineteenth-century Britain created
something historically new: a **market society**, in which the economy was "disembedded" from
social relationships and land, labour and money were treated as commodities. Polanyi called these
**fictitious commodities**, because they are not produced for sale; treating them as if they were,
through enclosure of common land and the creation of a wage-labour market, caused great social
dislocation, as people were pushed off the land into growing industrial towns. Society responded
with a protective **double movement**, such as factory laws and trade unions.

**Fernand Braudel** (*Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century*) distinguished three layers
of economic life: everyday **material life** (subsistence and self-provisioning), the **market
economy** of ordinary local exchange, and, at the top, **capitalism**, the domain of large merchants
and financiers able to dominate long-distance trade. For Braudel, European capitalism grew over
centuries through control of trade networks centred on a succession of dominant cities, which helps
explain why the divergence had deep roots before factories appeared.

**Urbanisation** accompanied industrialisation: people moved from rural areas to towns for factory
work, creating crowded cities with high death rates before public health reforms. Comparisons with
today's rapidly urbanising societies are useful in exams, but note that many cities in less
developed countries have grown without equivalent industrial employment.

## Why Europe industrialised: deepening the four explanations

| Explanation | Core claim | Extra depth for evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Modernisation theory** | Internal values and institutions (achievement, science, secular rationality, secure property rights) enabled growth | David McClelland's *The Achieving Society* (1961) linked growth to a cultural "need for achievement"; critics call such accounts ethnocentric and blind to exploitation |
| **Protestant work ethic (Weber)** | Ascetic Protestantism encouraged disciplined work and reinvestment, an "elective affinity" with capitalism | Weber did not claim religion alone caused capitalism; his wider thesis is about **rationalisation** of economic, legal and bureaucratic life |
| **Dependency theory** | The core developed by exploiting the periphery | Andre Gunder Frank's phrase "the development of underdevelopment" (1966); Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory adds a semi-periphery and treats the world economy as one system |
| **Enslavement** | Profits from slavery and plantation trade helped fund industry | Seymour Drescher (*Econocide*, 1977) argued Britain abolished the slave trade while it was still profitable, challenging purely economic accounts of abolition; others stress plantation cotton as an input to textile factories |

Other explanations credited when used relevantly: **Jeffrey Sachs** (*The End of Poverty*, 2005)
stresses geography, such as being landlocked or carrying a heavy tropical disease burden, and argues
that poor countries can be caught in a **poverty trap** without outside investment. Daron Acemoglu
and James Robinson (*Why Nations Fail*, 2012) argue that colonial powers built **extractive
institutions** in some colonies, which persisted after independence.

## Legacies of colonialism and enslavement

- **Economic**: dependence on a narrow range of primary exports; infrastructure built for
  extraction; debt.
- **Political**: borders drawn by colonial powers that cut across ethnic and linguistic groups;
  authoritarian state structures inherited from colonial administration.
- **Social and cultural**: racism and racial hierarchies; colonial languages dominating education
  and government; diasporas created by forced and indentured migration.
- **Example**: after the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), France demanded a large indemnity in 1825
  for recognising Haitian independence, a burden that weighed on Haiti's economy for generations.

## Factors and changes associated with development

The specification lists life expectancy; living standards, greater affluence and reduced poverty;
education; health; rights of women and other groups; human rights; and democracy. Amartya Sen's
*Development as Freedom* (1999) argues development should be judged by people's **capabilities**,
their real freedom to live lives they value, not income alone. This lets you question whether rapid
economic growth always counts as development, for example where it occurs without political rights
or with growing inequality.

## Worked example: annotated 12-mark plan

*Evaluate the view that enslavement explains why Europe industrialised first.*

- **For**: Williams' profits argument; Rodney on Africa's losses; plantation cotton feeding textile
  mills; Atlantic trade building shipping, finance and insurance (**knowledge and application**).
- **Against**: Drescher's evidence that abolition came while the trade was profitable; Weber and
  modernisation theory stress values; Polanyi and Braudel stress the long rise of market society
  and merchant capitalism; Sachs and others stress geography and resources (**evaluation**).
- **Judgement**: enslavement was a significant contributor to Britain's lead but works best
  combined with institutional and material explanations (**conclusion**).

## Common mistakes

- Treating colonialism and neocolonialism as the same thing.
- Calling Weber a dependency theorist.
- Naming Polanyi or Braudel without their concepts (fictitious commodities, double movement; layers
  of economic life).
- Measuring development by income alone when the specification lists social and political factors.
- Giving only one explanation in a question that invites comparison.

## Exam technique

Short questions reward precise definitions with a historical example. Extended answers reward
setting internal against external explanations, using at least one named sociologist from the
specification list, and applying to a named society's colonial history or development path.
