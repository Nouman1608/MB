This study guide teaches the method for **Paper 1: guided literary analysis** in IB Diploma
Programme Language A: Literature, at SL and HL. It follows the current IB subject brief (first
assessments for SL and HL 2021). It draws on two areas of exploration, **Readers, writers and
texts** and **Time and space**, and teaches you to annotate an unseen poem or prose passage, then
plan, structure and time an analysis of its form and style.

Use it with the [Paper 1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-paper-1-guided-literary-analysis-revision-notes/)
and the [Paper 1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-paper-1-guided-literary-analysis-practice/).

The brief says "passage/passages". Your teacher will tell you how many you write about at your
level and show you specimen material. On this page, each passage comes with a guiding question pointing to
one aspect of the text. Your teacher will share the assessment criteria; this page does not quote
them.

## How Paper 1 uses the two areas of exploration

- **Readers, writers and texts**: how a writer's choices of form and style shape what you, the
  reader, notice and feel. See the
  [Readers, writers and texts study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-readers-writers-texts/).
- **Time and space**: the world inside the passage (setting, period, social detail). On an unseen
  passage you work from what the text shows you. Never invent a biography for the writer.

## Step 1: read the guiding question first

Read the guiding question before the passage. Underline its key words: the aspect it names (a
character, a mood, a change) and the verb ("present", "explore", "convey"). Your first reading is
then already aimed. Your thesis must answer the question, and every paragraph must return to it.

## Step 2: annotate in three readings

| Reading | Your job | What you write |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Literal | What happens? Who speaks? Where and when? | Two or three lines of plain summary at the top |
| 2. Form and style | Find features that answer the guiding question | Underlining, arrows, margin notes on effect |
| 3. Argument | Decide what the features add up to | One sentence: your thesis |

On the second reading, check four layers. The questions differ by form.

| Layer | Prose | Poetry | Drama |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice | Who narrates? First or third person? Whose thoughts do we hear? | Who is the speaker? Who is addressed? | Who speaks most, and who stays silent? |
| Structure | Paragraph length, where the passage turns, how it ends | Stanzas, line breaks, the turn or shift | Entrances, pauses, how the scene ends |
| Language | Diction, imagery, simile and metaphor, sentence length | Imagery, sound (alliteration, assonance), rhyme and metre | What is said, what is avoided (subtext) |
| Context in the text | Setting, period detail, social world | Occasion, place, time of day or year | Stage directions: set, props, movement |

Annotate for **effect**: "short sentence" is a label; "short sentence: hope ends in three words"
starts a paragraph.

## Worked example 1: prose fiction

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- prose fiction extract
>
> The last bus had gone at ten past eleven, and Mara had watched it go. She had been standing under
> the shelter the whole time, her hand half raised, as if the driver might read her mind through
> the rain. He had not. Now the street was hers: the shut bakery, the chemist's green cross
> blinking on and off, the puddle at the kerb that held the whole sky and kept shaking it.
>
> She could ring her father. He would come. He would come in his dressing gown with his good coat
> over it, and he would say nothing for the first mile, and that nothing would be worse than
> anything he could say.
>
> The green cross blinked. On. Off. On.
>
> Three miles was not far. People walked three miles all the time; people in films walked across
> whole countries. She pulled her hood up, and her phone stayed in her pocket, warm and heavy as a
> stone she had decided not to throw.

**Guiding question:** How does the writer present Mara's decision?

### Reading 1: literal notes

Mara misses the last bus, thinks of ringing her father, and walks home instead. Third person, but
we hear her thoughts.

### Reading 2: form and style notes

- "her hand half raised" shows hesitation; "He had not." ends the hope in three words.
- The puddle "held the whole sky and kept shaking it": an unsteady reflection of an unsteady mind.
- "He would come. He would come" repeats a certainty that the dressing-gown detail and the
  paradox of a silence "worse than anything he could say" turn into dread.
- "The green cross blinked. On. Off. On." Fragments slow time into the see-saw of her choice, and
  the rhythm stops on "On".
- "Three miles was not far" is free indirect style: her own voice persuading herself. The jump to
  "whole countries" is exaggeration that shows doubt.
- The phone is "a stone she had decided not to throw": not calling becomes restraint, even pride.

### Reading 3: thesis

> The writer presents Mara's decision as a slow argument with herself, in which the fear of her
> father's silence outweighs the fear of the dark road, so that walking home becomes an act of
> pride as much as of courage.

### Plan

Plan by **idea**, not by device and not line by line.

```
Thesis: an argument with herself; pride outweighs fear.
P1  Hesitation: "hand half raised", "He had not.", the shaking
    puddle -> an unsteady, lonely setting.
P2  The case against calling: repeated "He would come", the
    silence "worse than anything he could say".
P3  The choice: "On. Off. On." rhythm; free indirect
    self-persuasion; "a stone she had decided not to throw".
```

### Model introduction

> In this extract Mara misses the last bus home and must choose between ringing her father
> and walking three miles alone at night. The third-person narration stays close to Mara's
> thoughts, so the reader watches the decision form rather than being told it. The writer presents
> that decision as a slow argument with herself, in which the fear of her father's silence
> outweighs the fear of the dark road.

### Model body paragraph

> The case against calling her father is made through what he would not say. The repeated
> sentence "He would come" first sounds reassuring, but the writer then adds the dressing gown under
> "his good coat", a detail that shows how much trouble the rescue would cause him. The paragraph
> ends in a paradox: "that nothing would be worse than anything he could say". Silence, usually
> the absence of blame, becomes the harshest blame of all. By the end of the paragraph the reader
> understands that Mara is choosing between two discomforts, and that the one she fears more is
> being judged.

The paragraph follows **claim → evidence → effect → link**. Each quotation is short and sits
inside a sentence of analysis. A conclusion then takes two or three sentences, adds no new
evidence, and says how the features work together to answer the question.

## Worked example 2: form and style in a poem

In poetry, lines, breaks, stanzas and sound all carry meaning.

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- poem
>
> *Inventory*
>
> My mother kept the buttons in a tin
> that once held biscuits for a Christmas long
> before my time. She never threw one out.
> Brass ones from a coat my grandfather wore
> to work, and wore to church, and wore to wait
> outside the hospital. Small pearl ones, cracked.
> A red one no one could explain.
>
> I sort them now the way she did: by size,
> then colour, then by nothing I can name.
> The tin is lighter than I thought it was.
> I shake it once. It answers, like a voice
> that knows the house is empty, and speaks up.

**Guiding question:** How does the poem present memory through ordinary objects?

Form notes from the second reading:

- **Line length.** The poem is unrhymed, and eleven of its twelve lines have ten syllables. The
  short line "A red one no one could explain" stops early, like the missing explanation.
- **Enjambment.** "a Christmas long / before my time" stretches across the line break, so the
  memory reaches back further than the speaker's own life.
- **Repetition.** "wore to work, and wore to church, and wore to wait" builds a life in three
  phrases; the last runs over the line to "the hospital" and breaks the pattern with grief.
- **Stanza break.** Stanza 1 is the mother's past; stanza 2 is the present ("I sort them now").
- **Closing simile.** The rattle of the tin "answers, like a voice / that knows the house is
  empty". Sound replaces the absent person.

A model paragraph on form:

> The stanza break carries the poem's loss. The first stanza belongs to the mother, who "kept" the
> buttons and "never threw one out"; the second begins in the present tense, "I sort them now the
> way she did". The poem never says that the mother has died, but the white space between the
> stanzas, and the speaker's copying of her method, make the absence clear. Memory is presented as
> something held in habits as well as objects: the speaker keeps the mother present by repeating
> what her hands once did.

## Step 3: manage your time

The brief sets **1 hour 15 minutes at SL** and **2 hours 15 minutes at HL**. A workable split for
one analysis in 75 minutes:

| Stage | Time |
|---|---|
| Guiding question and three readings | 15 min |
| Thesis and plan | 10 min |
| Introduction | 5 min |
| Three body paragraphs | 35 min |
| Conclusion | 5 min |
| Check quotations and links to the question | 5 min |
| **Total** | **75 min** |

If your paper asks you to write about more than one passage in the HL time, divide the 135 minutes
evenly: 65 minutes per analysis, with 5 minutes spare. Finish the first analysis, conclusion
included, before you start the second.

## Common errors

- **Retelling the story.** One sentence of context is enough; the rest is analysis.
- **Feature-spotting.** Devices with no thesis about the guiding question read as a checklist.
- **Confusing the speaker with the poet.** Write "the speaker", not the poet's name, unless the
  poem makes them the same.
- **Ignoring form.** In a poem, line breaks, stanza breaks and line length are evidence. In drama,
  stage directions are evidence.
- **Vague effects.** "This makes the reader want to read on" fits any text. Say what this feature
  makes you think about this character or idea.
- **Long quotations.** Quote a few words inside your own sentence.
- **Running out of time.** A missing conclusion is a planning failure.
