These notes condense section 2.2, Demand, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. Worked examples with full tables are in the [2.2 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-demand/); then try the [2.2 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-demand-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand | The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a period |
| Individual demand | One consumer's demand |
| Market demand | The total of all individual demands at each price |
| Extension in demand | More demanded because the good's price fell (movement along) |
| Contraction in demand | Less demanded because the good's price rose (movement along) |
| Increase in demand | The curve shifts right: more demanded at every price |
| Decrease in demand | The curve shifts left: less demanded at every price |
| Substitute | A good that can be used instead of another |
| Complement | A good used together with another |
| Price elasticity of demand (PED) | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price |

## Demand curves

- Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis, both with units.
- Downward sloping: a lower price means more is demanded.
- Market demand: **add quantities** at each price, never prices.

**Worked reminder.** At £5, three buyers want 4, 2 and 7 units. Market demand at £5 = 4 + 2 + 7 = **13**.

## Movement or shift?

```
Has the good's OWN price changed?
  YES → movement along the curve (extension or contraction)
  NO  → shift of the whole curve (increase right, decrease left)
```

**Shift factors:** income; price of substitutes; price of complements; tastes and fashion; advertising; population; seasons and weather.

- Substitute's price rises → demand for this good shifts right.
- Complement's price rises → demand for this good shifts left.

## Analysis chain for a shift

```
1. Cause (e.g. a heatwave)
2. Curve and direction (demand for fans shifts right)
3. Old price now gives a shortage
4. Price rises; quantity traded rises
5. Producers: higher revenue. Consumers: pay more; some priced out
```

## PED: formula and values

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
% change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100
```

| Size, ignoring the sign | Type |
|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic |
| 0 to 1 | Inelastic |
| 1 | Unitary |
| Above 1 | Elastic |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic |

**Worked reminder.** Price rises from £8 to £10 (+25%); quantity falls from 400 to 300 (−25%). PED = −25 ÷ 25 = **−1**: unitary.

## PED and total revenue

| Demand | Price rise | Price cut |
|---|---|---|
| Inelastic | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |
| Elastic | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |

**Worked reminder.** A theatre ticket rises from £20 to £22 (+10%); sales fall from 600 to 570 (−5%). PED = −0.5. Revenue: 12,000 → 12,540, a rise of **£540**.

## What decides PED

- Number and closeness of substitutes.
- Necessity or luxury.
- Share of income spent on the good.
- Habit.
- Time to adjust.

## Drawing different elasticities

- Perfectly inelastic: vertical.
- Perfectly elastic: horizontal.
- Relatively inelastic: steep.
- Relatively elastic: shallow.
- Draw on the same axes and scale, and mark the same price change on each.

## Importance of PED

- **Producers:** predict the revenue effect of a price change; inelastic demand gives pricing power; branding aims to reduce elasticity.
- **Consumers:** with inelastic necessities, consumers bear price rises; with elastic demand, they can switch.
- **Limits:** estimates come from past data and change over time; other factors may change at the same time.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Extension versus increase:** an extension is a movement along the curve after a price fall; an increase is a shift right.
- **Individual versus market demand:** one consumer versus the sum of all consumers.
- **Elastic versus inelastic:** judged by the size of PED, not its sign.
- **Steep versus vertical:** a steep curve is relatively inelastic; only a vertical line is perfectly inelastic.

## Exam traps

- Calling a price change a shift.
- Adding prices for market demand.
- Turning the PED formula upside down.
- Treating the minus sign as meaning "elastic".
- Saying a price rise always increases revenue.
- Unlabelled axes or curves.

## Quick self-test

1. Define demand.
2. Three consumers demand 2, 5 and 3 units at £4. What is market demand at £4?
3. The price of a good falls. Is this a shift or a movement? Name it.
4. Tea becomes more expensive. What happens to demand for coffee?
5. Printers become cheaper. What happens to demand for printer ink?
6. Price rises from £5.00 to £5.50 and quantity falls from 1,000 to 950. Calculate PED.
7. Is the answer to question 6 elastic or inelastic?
8. Using question 6, calculate the change in total revenue.
9. Draw in words a perfectly inelastic demand curve.
10. Give two factors that make demand more elastic.
11. Why might a firm advertise its brand heavily?
12. Give one reason a PED estimate may be unreliable.

### Answers

1. The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a period of time.
2. 2 + 5 + 3 = **10**.
3. Movement along the curve: an extension in demand.
4. Demand for coffee shifts right (a substitute became dearer).
5. Demand for ink shifts right (a complement became cheaper).
6. Price +10%; quantity −5%; PED = −5 ÷ 10 = **−0.5**.
7. Inelastic.
8. Before 5.00 × 1,000 = £5,000; after 5.50 × 950 = £5,225; revenue rises by **£225**.
9. A vertical line: the same quantity at every price.
10. Many close substitutes; a large share of income; a long time to adjust; a luxury (any two).
11. To build loyalty, so demand becomes less price elastic and price rises lose fewer sales.
12. It is based on past data; tastes change; other factors may have changed at the same time.
