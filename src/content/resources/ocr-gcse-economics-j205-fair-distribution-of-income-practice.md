These questions cover section 3.3 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Fair distribution of income. All people, countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [fair distribution of income study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fair-distribution-of-income/) and the [fair distribution of income revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-fair-distribution-of-income-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the difference between income and wealth. **[2]**

**2.** Give an example of a transfer payment. **[1]**

**3.** In one year Daniel receives a salary of £2,150 a month, a dividend of £90 every three months, and £110 a week rent from a lodger. Calculate his total income for the year. **[3]**

**4.** A household owns a house worth £265,000, savings of £18,000 and a car worth £9,000. It owes £172,000 on its mortgage, £1,100 on an overdraft and £6,400 on car finance. Calculate its net wealth. **[2]**

**5.** Which one of the following is a liability? **[1]**

- A. Shares in a company
- B. A pension fund
- C. A mortgage
- D. A savings account

**6.** Explain why a "fair" distribution of income need not be an equal one. **[2]**

**7.** Mia owns £75,000 of shares paying a 2.8% dividend and has £12,000 in savings earning 3.5% interest. Calculate the income her wealth produces in a year. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Country G has 12 million households, 1.2 million in each tenth, and total household income of £400bn in both years shown.

| | Richest tenth's share of income | Poorest tenth's share of income |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 26% | 2.6% |
| Year 8 | 31% | 2.2% |

**(a)** Calculate the average income of a household in the richest tenth in Year 8, to the nearest pound. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times as much a richest-tenth household receives as a poorest-tenth household in Year 1 and in Year 8. **[2]**
**(c)** State what your answers to (b) show. **[1]**

**9.** Priya is 52, a senior engineer earning £68,000 a year, and owns her house outright. Tom is 23, an apprentice earning £19,500 a year, and rents a room.

**(a)** Explain two reasons why Priya's income is higher than Tom's. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the gap in their wealth is likely to be even larger than the gap in their income. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how the growing use of new technology at work may widen differences in income. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences for an economy of a widening gap between high-income and low-income households. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether inheritance is the main cause of differences in wealth. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Income is a flow of money received over a period, such as a monthly salary [1]. Wealth is a stock, the value of assets owned at a point in time, such as a house or savings, less any debts [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any one: unemployment-related benefit; state pension; child-related benefit [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Salary: 2,150 × 12 = £25,800; dividends: 90 × 4 = £360 [1]. Rent: 110 × 52 = £5,720 [1]. Total = **£31,880** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert each source to a yearly figure before adding. Mixing monthly, quarterly and weekly amounts is the usual slip.

**4.** Assets = 265,000 + 18,000 + 9,000 = £292,000; liabilities = 172,000 + 1,100 + 6,400 = £179,500 [1]. Net wealth = **£112,500** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **C** [1]. A mortgage is money owed; the others are assets. **[1]**

**6.** Fairness is a value judgement, and many people think differences are fair if they reflect differences in effort, skills or risk taken [1]. An equal distribution would give the same to someone who works long hours as to someone who chooses not to work, which many would see as unfair [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Dividends: 75,000 × 0.028 = £2,100; interest: 12,000 × 0.035 = £420 [1]. Total = **£2,520** [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** 31% × £400bn = £124bn [1]; £124bn ÷ 1.2 million = **£103,333** [1].
**(b)** Year 1: 26 ÷ 2.6 = **10 times** [1]. Year 8: 31 ÷ 2.2 = **14.1 times** [1].
**(c)** The gap between the richest and poorest tenths widened, so income became more unequally distributed [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Priya has higher qualifications and scarce engineering skills, which are in high demand, so employers pay more for them [1]; her productivity is higher, so her wage is higher [1]. She is 52 with many years' experience, while Tom is at the start of his career [1]; pay usually rises with experience and seniority [1].
**(b)** Priya owns a house outright, an asset worth a large sum, while Tom owns no property [1]. She has had decades to save and her house has no mortgage, so her wealth may be many times Tom's, even though her income is about 3.5 times his [1]. **[6]**

**10.** New technology increases demand for workers who can design and use it, so their wages rise [1]. It replaces some routine jobs, which reduces demand for those workers and holds down their wages or causes unemployment [1]. So the gap between high-skilled and low-skilled workers' incomes widens [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Lower spending: poorer households spend most of any extra income while richer ones save more, so as income shifts to the top, consumer spending may grow more slowly [1], reducing sales and jobs [1]. Lost potential: children in low-income households may have less access to education and training, wasting talent and lowering future growth [1]. Government: more households need benefits while those on low incomes pay little tax, raising costs, with an opportunity cost for other services [1]. Social cohesion: large gaps may reduce trust and create tension [1]. On the other hand, higher rewards may encourage people to train and start businesses, raising output [1]. Evaluation: the effect depends on whether the gap reflects effort and choice or barriers to opportunity, and how fast it is widening [1]. Judgement: a modestly widening gap may do little harm, but if it reflects blocked opportunities its consequences for growth and government finances are likely to outweigh any incentive benefit [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For: inherited property, savings and shares give some people a large stock of wealth they did not build themselves [1], and that wealth earns rent, interest and dividends that can be reinvested, so the gap grows across generations [1]. Against: most people build wealth gradually from income, buying a home and saving into a pension, so differences in earnings caused by skills and occupation matter a great deal [1]. Age also matters, because older people have had more time to save and repay mortgages [1], and region affects how much property values have risen [1]. Judgement: inheritance is a major cause at the top of the distribution, but for most households income and age explain more of the difference in wealth, so it is an important but not the main cause overall [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding weekly, monthly and quarterly amounts without converting to a year.
- Treating a mortgage as an asset.
- Dividing a tenth's income by all households instead of the households in that tenth.
- Giving a ratio without saying what it shows.
- Arguing only one side when asked to evaluate.
