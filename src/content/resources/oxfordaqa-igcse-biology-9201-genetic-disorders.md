This guide goes deeper into section **3.5.4 Genetic disorders** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and any part of the
specification may be assessed on either paper.

The specification draws attention to the **potential sensitivity needed in teaching about inherited disorders**. Many
people live with these conditions or have family members who do. Questions treat the science factually, and this page
does the same.

The existing [Genetic variation, monohybrid inheritance, DNA and genetic disorders study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-genetic-variation-monohybrid-inheritance-dna-and/)
covers 3.5.3 and 3.5.4 together. This page concentrates on genetic disorders and adds new worked examples: family
probability calculations, an inheritance pattern from a pedigree table and chromosome counts. Afterwards, use the
[Genetic disorders revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-genetic-disorders-revision-notes/) and the
[Genetic disorders practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-genetic-disorders-practice/).

## What 3.5.4 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Some disorders are inherited. You should be able to interpret data relating to genetic disorders such as polydactyly, cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anaemia |
| b | Some inherited conditions are caused by the inheritance of abnormal numbers of chromosomes, for example Down's syndrome is caused by the presence of an extra chromosome |

## Inherited disorders caused by alleles

An **inherited** disorder is passed from parents to children in the genetic material. Some are caused by an
**allele** (a form of a gene) that differs from the usual allele. Whether a person is affected depends on whether
the allele is **dominant** or **recessive** (the ideas are developed in 3.5.3).

| Disorder | Description | Type of allele |
|---|---|---|
| Polydactyly | Extra fingers or toes | Dominant allele |
| Cystic fibrosis | A disorder of cell membranes that affects the lungs and digestive system | Recessive allele |
| Sickle cell anaemia | A disorder of red blood cells | Recessive allele |

- A **dominant** allele has an effect when one copy is present. An affected person has at least one copy.
- A **recessive** allele has an effect only when two copies are present. A person with one copy is a **carrier**:
  not affected but able to pass the allele on.

Use letters that make the idea clear: a capital letter for the dominant allele and the same letter in lower case for
the recessive allele.

### Worked example 1: polydactyly, one affected parent

Invented family. Polydactyly is caused by a dominant allele, D. Normal is d. One parent has the genotype Dd and has
polydactyly. The other parent has the genotype dd.

```
            Parent 2: d     d
Parent 1: D           Dd    Dd
          d           dd    dd

probability of a child with polydactyly = 2/4 = 1/2
```

Interpretation: each child has a 1 in 2 chance of polydactyly. This is a probability for each child separately. A
family with four children does not have to contain exactly two affected children. If the first child is affected, the
chance for the second child is still 1/2. The chance that two children are both unaffected is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.

### Worked example 2: cystic fibrosis, two carriers

Invented family. The cystic fibrosis allele is recessive, c. Normal is C. Both parents are unaffected but both are carriers
(Cc).

```
            Parent 2: C     c
Parent 1: C           CC    Cc
          c           Cc    cc

CC = unaffected, Cc = unaffected carrier, cc = affected
probability of an affected child (cc) = 1/4
```

Now find the chance that in a family of three children **none** is affected.

```
chance that one child is not affected = 3/4
chance that all three are not affected = (3/4)³ = 27/64 = 0.42  (2 s.f.)
chance that at least one is affected = 1 − 27/64 = 37/64 = 0.58  (2 s.f.)
```

Because the three births are independent, we multiply the probabilities. The answer shows that even when the chance for
one child is only 1/4, it is more likely than not that a family of three contains an affected child (0.58).

### Worked example 3: working out the inheritance pattern

Invented data from family trees. In a large set of families, children were recorded as affected or unaffected.

| Parents | Children |
|---|---|
| Both unaffected | Some affected children occur |
| One affected, one unaffected | Some affected, some unaffected |
| Both affected | All children affected |

Reasoning: if two unaffected parents have an affected child, the disorder must be caused by a **recessive** allele,
because the child inherited the allele from each parent, who were therefore carriers. If two affected parents
have an unaffected child, the allele must be dominant. In this table, affected children appear from unaffected
parents, so the disorder is caused by a recessive allele. If both parents were affected by a recessive disorder (cc and cc),
all children would be cc and affected, as the final row shows.

### Worked example 4: the sickle cell disorder, a carrier and an affected parent

Invented family. The sickle cell allele is recessive, h. Normal is H. One parent is a carrier (Hh) and the other has the
disorder (hh).

```
            Parent 2: h     h
Parent 1: H           Hh    Hh
          h           hh    hh

probability of an affected child = 2/4 = 1/2
probability of a child who is a carrier = 2/4 = 1/2
```

Interpretation: each child has a 1/2 chance of having sickle cell anaemia and a 1/2 chance of being a carrier. There is
no chance that a child will have two copies of the normal allele (HH), because one parent has no H allele.

### Worked example 5: using a frequency to predict numbers

Invented frequency. In a region, cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2 000 babies. In one year there are 36 000 births.

```
expected affected babies = 36 000 ÷ 2 000 = 18
```

This is an expected number from a frequency, and the actual number will differ in any year. Data of this type can be
used by health services to plan resources.

## Inherited conditions caused by chromosome numbers

Some inherited conditions are caused by the inheritance of **abnormal numbers of chromosomes**. The specification's
example is **Down's syndrome**, caused by the presence of an **extra chromosome**.

Human body cells normally have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. A gamete (egg or sperm) normally has 23 chromosomes. If a
gamete has one extra chromosome, then after fertilisation the zygote has an extra chromosome in every cell.

### Worked example 6: counting chromosomes

```
normal gamete = 23 chromosomes
gamete with an extra chromosome = 23 + 1 = 24 chromosomes
fertilisation by a normal gamete = 24 + 23 = 47 chromosomes in the body cells of the child
normal body cell = 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes
```

A child with Down's syndrome therefore has 47 chromosomes in the body cells. The condition is not caused by a faulty
allele on a normal chromosome, but by having a whole extra chromosome. This is why it is described differently from
cystic fibrosis.

### Worked example 7: reading a table of cases

Invented illustration. The number of babies with Down's syndrome per 10 000 births for four groups of mothers.

| Age of mother | 20-24 | 30-34 | 35-39 | 40-44 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cases per 10 000 births | 7 | 11 | 40 | 120 |

```
40-44 compared with 20-24 = 120 ÷ 7 = 17.1 times as many (3 s.f.)
35-39 compared with 20-24 = 40 ÷ 7 = 5.7 times as many (2 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: the number of cases per 10 000 births increases with the age of the mother, particularly after 35. The
data show a pattern across age groups but do not on their own explain the cause. Describe the trend, quote values and
avoid over-interpreting.

## Comparing the two types

| | Allele disorders | Chromosome number disorders |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Polydactyly, cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia | Down's syndrome |
| Cause | Dominant or recessive allele of a gene | An extra chromosome |
| Predicting outcomes | Genetic diagrams give the probability of each genotype | Depends on whether a gamete carries an extra chromosome |

## Common mistakes

- Writing that a recessive disorder "skips a generation" as a fixed rule. It can appear in any generation where two
  carriers have children.
- Saying that one in four children will definitely be affected. It is a probability for each child.
- Describing a carrier as "has the disease" or as "affected".
- Using the wrong allele symbols: the dominant allele is the capital letter.
- Saying that Down's syndrome is caused by a gene. It is an extra chromosome.
- Forgetting to multiply for independent events, or adding instead.
- Treating a table of cases as proof of a cause.

## Exam technique

- For a genetic diagram, state the key, parent genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes and the probability as a
  fraction, ratio or percentage.
- For family tree questions, work out which allele is dominant by finding two unaffected parents with an affected
  child.
- For data questions, quote figures, compare groups and say what can and cannot be concluded.
- Use neutral, factual language when describing people with a disorder.
