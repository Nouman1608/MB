## Questions

**1.** A copper wire is connected to a cell and lamp in a closed circuit.

**(a)** Explain why the copper conducts but a strip of plastic in its place would not. **[2]**
**(b)** State the direction in which the electrons move relative to the arrows drawn for conventional current. **[1]**

**2.** Calculate each quantity.

**(a)** The charge transferred when 0.60 A flows for 25 minutes. **[2]**
**(b)** The current when 54 C flows in 36 s. **[2]**
**(c)** The time, in minutes, for a current of 150 mA to deliver 330 C. **[3]**

**3.** A student rubs a polythene rod with a dry cloth.

**(a)** Explain why the rod becomes charged. **[2]**
**(b)** The student rubs a metal rod held in the hand in the same way, and it does not stay charged. Explain why. **[2]**

**4.** A storm cloud discharges to the ground through a lightning flash.

**(a)** Explain how a lightning flash happens, in terms of charge. **[4]**
**(b)** The flash transfers 24 C in 0.60 ms. Calculate the current. **[2]**
**(c)** The potential difference between cloud and ground is 1.5 × 10⁸ V. Calculate the energy transferred. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate each quantity.

**(a)** The energy supplied by a 9.0 V battery that drives 40 C round a circuit. **[2]**
**(b)** The potential difference across a lamp that transfers 84 J from 24 C. **[2]**
**(c)** The charge that passes through a motor that transfers 150 J with 6.0 V across it. **[2]**

**6.** A 12 V battery drives a steady current of 0.50 A for 3.0 minutes round a circuit containing a lamp and another component.

**(a)** State the difference between the voltage of the battery and the potential difference across the lamp. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge that flows. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy supplied by the battery. **[1]**
**(d)** The potential difference across the lamp is 8.0 V. Calculate the energy transferred by the lamp, and the potential difference across the other component. **[3]**

**7.** Name the component shown by each description of a circuit symbol.

**(a)** A circle containing a cross. **[1]**
**(b)** A rectangle with a sloping arrow through it. **[1]**
**(c)** A rectangle inside a circle, with arrows pointing towards it. **[1]**
**(d)** A triangle pointing at a bar, inside a circle, with two arrows pointing away. **[1]**
**(e)** A rectangle with a sloping line through it, ending in a bar. **[1]**

**8.** Draw a circuit diagram for a cell, a closed switch and a lamp in series. Add an ammeter to measure the current through the lamp and a voltmeter to measure the potential difference across it. **[4]**

State which meter measures the current through the lamp. **[1]**

**9.** A thermistor is connected to a 6.0 V cell and an ammeter. The current is 20 mA at 10 °C, 45 mA at 30 °C, 90 mA at 50 °C and 160 mA at 70 °C.

**(a)** State the trend in the current as the temperature rises. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what this shows about the resistance of the thermistor. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge that flows in 2.0 minutes at 50 °C. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe how a thermistor can be used in a room thermostat. **[2]**

**10.** An LDR is connected in series with a cell and an ammeter. The current is 14 mA in bright light, 6.2 mA at dusk and 1.2 mA in the dark.

**(a)** Describe how the resistance of the LDR changes as the light level falls, and explain your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge that flows in 5.0 minutes in the dark. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how an LDR can be used so that a street lamp comes on when it gets dark. **[3]**

**11.** A torch has a 4.5 V battery. The lamp carries 0.28 A for 1.5 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the charge that flows. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy supplied by the battery. **[2]**
**(c)** The potential difference across the lamp is 4.2 V. Calculate the energy transferred by the lamp, and the energy transferred elsewhere in the circuit. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the charge passing through the battery equals the charge passing through the lamp. **[2]**

**12.** Correct each statement.

**(a)** "The lamp uses up the current, so less current comes out of it." **[2]**
**(b)** "The voltage flows through the wire." **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Copper contains many electrons that are free to move through it [1]; plastic has almost no free charges, so nothing can flow through it [1].
**(b)** The electrons move in the **opposite direction** to the arrows [1].
*Examiner insight:* name the free electrons in the metal. "Plastic has no electrons" is wrong, because it has electrons that cannot move freely.

**2. (a)** t = 25 × 60 = 1500 s [1]; Q = 0.60 × 1500 = **900 C** [1].
**(b)** I = Q / t = 54 / 36 [1] = **1.5 A** [1].
**(c)** I = 0.150 A [1]; t = Q / I = 330 / 0.150 = 2200 s [1]; 2200 / 60 = **37 minutes** (36.7 minutes) [1].
*Examiner insight:* the conversions are where marks go. Convert minutes to seconds and milliamperes to amperes before substituting, and write each step.

**3. (a)** Rubbing transfers electrons from the cloth to the rod [1]; the rod gains an excess of electrons and is negatively charged, and the cloth is left positive [1].
**(b)** The metal is a conductor, so charge flows along it and through the student's body to the ground [1]; there is a conducting route, so the charge does not stay on the rod [1].
*Examiner insight:* say that electrons move. Writing that "charge is made by rubbing" does not explain the imbalance.

**4. (a)** Charge is separated inside the cloud [1]; the ground below becomes charged oppositely to the base of the cloud [1]; the potential difference between them becomes very large [1]; the air stops acting as an insulator and charge flows through it as a discharge [1].
**(b)** I = Q / t = 24 / 0.00060 [1] = **4.0 × 10⁴ A** [1].
**(c)** E = Q V = 24 × 1.5 × 10⁸ [1] = **3.6 × 10⁹ J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* in the explanation, a build-up of charge plus a conducting path is the core. Convert 0.60 ms to 0.00060 s before you divide.

**5. (a)** E = Q V = 40 × 9.0 [1] = **360 J** [1].
**(b)** V = E / Q = 84 / 24 [1] = **3.5 V** [1].
**(c)** Q = E / V = 150 / 6.0 [1] = **25 C** [1].
*Examiner insight:* write the rearranged equation before the numbers. A correct substitution with no unit leaves the answer incomplete.

**6. (a)** The voltage of the battery is the energy supplied to each coulomb driven round the circuit [1]; the potential difference across the lamp is the energy each coulomb transfers in the lamp [1].
**(b)** t = 3.0 × 60 = 180 s [1]; Q = 0.50 × 180 = **90 C** [1].
**(c)** E = 90 × 12 = **1080 J** [1].
**(d)** Lamp: E = 90 × 8.0 = **720 J** [1]; remaining energy = 1080 − 720 = 360 J [1]; V = 360 / 90 = **4.0 V** [1].
*Examiner insight:* use your answer to part (b) in the later parts and show it. Clear working lets a reader see how each later figure was obtained.

**7. (a)** **Lamp** [1]. **(b)** **Variable resistor** [1]. **(c)** **LDR** (light-dependent resistor) [1]. **(d)** **LED** (light-emitting diode) [1]. **(e)** **Thermistor** [1].
*Examiner insight:* give the full name or the standard abbreviation. An LED and an LDR both have arrows, so check which way the arrows point.

**8.** Cell, closed switch and lamp drawn in a single loop [1]; ammeter drawn in the loop in series with the lamp [1]; voltmeter drawn with its two connections on either side of the lamp, outside the loop [1]; all symbols correct and the long line of the cell shown as the positive terminal, drawn with straight lines [1]. The **ammeter** measures the current through the lamp [1].
*Examiner insight:* a voltmeter placed in the loop, or an ammeter placed across the lamp, is a common reason a diagram does not gain full credit.

**9. (a)** The current **increases** as the temperature rises [1].
**(b)** The potential difference is constant at 6.0 V [1]; a larger current means the resistance has **decreased** as the temperature rises [1].
**(c)** I = 0.090 A and t = 120 s [1]; Q = 0.090 × 120 = **10.8 C** [1].
**(d)** As the room warms, the thermistor's resistance falls and the current in the sensing circuit rises [1]; at a set value the circuit switches the heater off, and it switches it on again as the room cools [1].
*Examiner insight:* link the current to the resistance explicitly, with the constant potential difference stated. "It conducts better" alone is vague.

**10. (a)** The resistance of the LDR **increases** as the light level falls [1]; the current falls while the potential difference stays the same [1].
**(b)** I = 1.2 mA = 0.0012 A and t = 5.0 × 60 = 300 s [1]; Q = 0.0012 × 300 = **0.36 C** [1].
**(c)** As it gets dark, the resistance of the LDR increases [1]; the current in the sensing circuit falls [1]; this change operates a switch that turns the street lamp on [1].
*Examiner insight:* a bare "light dependent" is not an answer. State the direction of change and what the circuit then does.

**11. (a)** t = 1.5 × 3600 = 5400 s [1]; Q = 0.28 × 5400 = **1512 C** (1500 C to 2 significant figures) [1].
**(b)** E = Q V = 1512 × 4.5 [1] = **6800 J** (6804 J) [1].
**(c)** Lamp: 1512 × 4.2 = **6350 J** (6350.4 J) [1]; elsewhere: 6804 − 6350.4 = **454 J** (453.6 J) [1].
**(d)** The torch is a single loop with one path [1]; charge is not used up or stored, so the same charge passes each point in the same time [1].
*Examiner insight:* in a long multi-part calculation, keep unrounded values until the final line and round at the end.

**12. (a)** Current is the rate of flow of charge, and it is the same at the lamp's input and output in a single loop [1]; the lamp transfers the energy that the charge carries, but the charge is not used up [1].
**(b)** Charge flows through the wire, and this flow is the current [1]; potential difference is measured across a component, as energy transferred per coulomb [1].
*Examiner insight:* "flows through" for current and "across" for potential difference is a simple way to show you have the ideas right.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting a time in minutes or hours into Q = It.
- Using milliamperes without converting to amperes.
- Giving a discharge answer with no conducting route and no mention of charge building up.
- Saying charge is "made" by rubbing instead of transferred.
- Mixing up the energy supplied by a source and the energy transferred by a component.
- Quoting the thermistor or LDR rule the wrong way round.
- Drawing a voltmeter in the loop or an ammeter across a component.
