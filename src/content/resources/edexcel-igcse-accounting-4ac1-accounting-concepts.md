This guide covers section 1.2, Accounting concepts, in Topic 1 (The accounting environment) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. The specification asks you to "understand the significance of" six concepts: **consistency, prudence, accruals, materiality, money measurement and business entity**. All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign.

The [types of business organisation and accounting concepts overview](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-types-of-business-organisation-and-accounting-concepts/) defines each concept with a first example, and the [application of accounting concepts guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-application-of-accounting-concepts/) applies them to year-end adjustments in Topic 5. This page sits between the two: it explains why each concept is **significant**, shows how each one shapes the everyday bookkeeping of Topic 2, and deals with cases where concepts pull in different directions. Condensed notes are in the [accounting concepts revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-accounting-concepts-revision-notes/) and you can practise with the [accounting concepts practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-accounting-concepts-practice/).

## What "significance" means here

A concept is significant because of what would go wrong without it. For each one, be ready to say three things: what it requires, which record or figure it affects, and how a user of the statements would be misled if it were ignored. That three-part answer is what "understand the significance" is testing.

| Concept | What it requires | Without it, users would be misled because... |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency | The same methods and classifications from one period to the next | Profit could change because the method changed, not because performance did |
| Prudence | Profits and assets are not overstated; expected losses are recognised promptly | Owners might draw, and lenders lend, against profit that will never turn into cash |
| Accruals | Income and expenses recorded in the period they belong to, not when cash moves | Profit would depend on when bills happened to be paid |
| Materiality | Detailed treatment only for items large enough to affect users' decisions | Statements would be cluttered and costly to prepare, with no benefit |
| Money measurement | Only items that can be measured in money are recorded | Users might assume the statements show everything of value in the business |
| Business entity | The business's transactions are recorded separately from the owner's | Private spending would distort profit and the owner's capital |

## Consistency: comparing like with like

Consistency applies to **classification** as well as to methods such as depreciation.

**Worked example.** Kestrel Café's revenue was 80,000 in both Year 1 and Year 2, and its purchases and carriage inwards were also identical each year: 46,000 and 2,000. In Year 1 carriage inwards was included in cost of sales. In Year 2 the bookkeeper put it with expenses.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of sales | 48,000 | 46,000 |
| Gross profit | 32,000 | 34,000 |
| Gross profit as % of revenue | 40.0% | 42.5% |

The café's trading did not change, but gross profit appears to rise by 2,000 and the margin by 2.5 percentage points. Profit for the year is the same, because the 2,000 is still deducted lower down. An owner comparing the two years would wrongly think buying had become more efficient. Consistency matters because comparisons between years are one of the main uses of financial statements. A method may change only when the new one gives a fairer picture, and the change should then be made clear to users.

## Prudence: not overstating

Prudence means profit and assets are not overstated: likely losses are recorded as soon as they are foreseen, while gains are recorded only when they are realised. It is not a licence to understate deliberately; it guards against optimism.

**Worked example.** Kestrel Café sells to local offices on credit. At the year end trade receivables are 24,000. One customer owing 1,500 has ceased trading and will not pay. The owner also expects, from experience, that 3% of the remaining receivables will not be collected.

- Irrecoverable debt written off: **1,500**
- Remaining receivables: 24,000 − 1,500 = 22,500
- Provision for irrecoverable debts: 22,500 × 3% = **675**
- Receivables shown in the statement of financial position: 22,500 − 675 = **21,825**
- Profit reduced by 1,500 + 675 = **2,175** (assuming no provision existed before)

Without prudence, the statements would show 24,000 of receivables that the café knows it will not fully collect. The bookkeeping for irrecoverable debts and provisions in Topic 2 exists because of this concept.

## Accruals: matching to the period

Under the accruals concept, income and expenses belong to the period in which they are earned or incurred.

**Worked example (income side).** Kestrel Café earns commission from a local bakery for selling its bread. The December commission of 450 is received in January. Under accruals, the 450 is income of this year, and at the year end the bakery owes it: an **other receivable** of 450. Recording it only when the cash arrives would understate this year's profit by 450 and overstate next year's.

This is why the specification later asks you to record other receivables and payables (Topic 2, section 7): those entries are the accruals concept in action.

## Materiality: a matter of size and judgement

An item is material if leaving it out or misstating it could affect the decisions of users. Businesses often set a policy, such as treating any non-current asset costing under 500 as an expense.

**Worked example.** Under a 500 policy, Kestrel Café treats a 180 kettle as an expense but records a 2,400 coffee machine as a non-current asset and depreciates it. A national café chain might set its limit far higher, because 2,400 is tiny compared with its profit. Materiality is relative: the same amount can be material to one business and immaterial to another.

Materiality also excuses minor inaccuracies in small items, such as not counting the paper clips in the stationery cupboard as closing inventory, because the cost of precision would outweigh any benefit to users.

## Money measurement: what the statements leave out

Only items with a reliable money value are recorded. Kestrel Café's skilled baristas, loyal customers and good reviews are real strengths, but none appears in its statement of financial position. The concept also has a second limit: the value of money changes over time. A van bought ten years ago for 12,000 and an identical van bought this year for 19,000 are recorded at different amounts, so the totals of different years are not exactly comparable.

The significance is that users must not treat the statement of financial position as a complete valuation of the business.

## Business entity: the business is separate

The records show only the business's transactions, even for a sole trader who is legally the same person as the business. This concept explains the double entry for **capital** and **drawings**.

**Worked example.** During the year, the café's owner, Femi:

- paid a business supplier's invoice of 600 from her **personal** bank account: debit trade payables 600, credit capital 600, because she has put more of her own money into the business;
- took food costing 250 home for her family: debit drawings 250, credit purchases 250, because the goods left the business for private use;
- paid her home electricity bill of 140 from the **business** bank account: debit drawings 140, credit bank 140, not electricity expense.

Without the business entity concept, profit would be reduced by private bills, and the owner's capital would not show what she has really invested.

## When concepts pull in different directions

Concepts sometimes conflict, and recognising this is a sign of real understanding.

- **Prudence and accruals.** Accruals matches revenue and costs to the period, but prudence records an expected loss now, before it happens, as with the 675 provision.
- **Consistency and prudence.** Consistency says keep the same method; prudence may suggest a method that values assets more cautiously. A change is justified only if it gives a fairer view, not to manage profit.
- **Materiality and accuracy.** Exact treatment of every item would be most accurate, but materiality accepts small simplifications.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a concept with no link to a record or figure.
- Treating prudence as "always show the lowest possible profit".
- Confusing accruals (timing) with cash paid or received.
- Saying materiality depends only on the amount, not on the size of the business.
- Saying money measurement means assets are recorded at current market value.
- Putting an owner's private bill through expenses rather than drawings.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **name** a concept: one word is enough, but choose the best fit.
- **Explain** the significance: what the concept requires, the figure it affects, and how users would be misled without it.
- **Apply** to a case: give the double entry or the amount, as in the business entity entries above.
- In longer answers, using the case's figures (AO2) and judging between concepts (AO3) are what move an answer beyond knowledge marks.
