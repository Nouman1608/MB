Cambridge IGCSE Economics (0455) is an introduction to how economies work, from the basic problem of scarce resources to the role of markets, firms, workers and governments, and on to development and international trade. It suits students meeting economics for the first time and gives a firm base for further study. The syllabus edition for examination in 2026 is assessed by two compulsory papers: Paper 1 Multiple Choice (45 minutes, 30 marks, 30% of the qualification) and Paper 2 Structured Questions (135 minutes, 90 marks, 70%).

## How the course is organised

The content is arranged in six topics that move outwards from individual choices to the global economy.

**Foundations**

- **Topic 1, The basic economic problem**: the nature of the economic problem, the factors of production, opportunity cost and production possibility curve (PPC) diagrams. This short topic sets up the vocabulary for everything that follows.

**Markets and the people in them**

- **Topic 2, The allocation of resources**: microeconomics and macroeconomics; the role of markets; demand, supply, price determination and price changes; price elasticity of demand (PED) and price elasticity of supply (PES); market and mixed economic systems; and market failure. It has more sections than any other topic.
- **Topic 3, Microeconomic decision makers**: money and banking, households, workers, trade unions, firms, firms' production, firms' costs, revenue and objectives, and market structure.

**The economy as a whole**

- **Topic 4, Government and the macroeconomy**: the role of government and its macroeconomic aims; fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy; economic growth; employment and unemployment; and inflation and deflation.
- **Topic 5, Economic development**: living standards, poverty, population and differences in economic development between countries.

**The international economy**

- **Topic 6, International trade and globalisation**: international specialisation; globalisation, free trade and protection; foreign exchange rates; and the current account of the balance of payments.

## How it's assessed

| Paper | Title | Duration | Marks | Weighting | Format |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | Multiple Choice | 45 minutes | 30 | 30% | All 30 questions compulsory |
| Paper 2 | Structured Questions | 135 minutes | 90 | 70% | One compulsory question, then three from a choice of four |

Key features of the assessment:

- **One route for everyone.** The course is not tiered, both papers are compulsory, and both are externally assessed. Grades run from A* to G.
- **Linear.** Both papers are taken in the same examination series at the end of the course.
- **Unequal weighting.** Paper 2 carries more than twice the weight of Paper 1, so written explanation, analysis and evaluation matter most to the final grade.
- **Choice in Paper 2.** After the compulsory question you choose three questions from four, which gives some room to play to your strengths, but only if every topic has been studied.

This syllabus is a single-year edition: 2026 is both its first and its final assessment year. If you will sit the examination in a later year, check the syllabus Cambridge publishes for that year.

## How the parts fit together

Demand and supply analysis from Topic 2 is the engine of the course. It explains price changes in a single market, but it also reappears when you look at wages and the workers and trade unions in Topic 3, and again with foreign exchange rates in Topic 6, where a currency's value is set by demand and supply in the same way.

Topic 3 and Topic 4 mirror each other: one looks at decisions made by households, workers and firms, the other at decisions made by government for the whole economy. Policies in Topic 4, such as fiscal and monetary measures, are judged by their effects on the people and firms from Topic 3. Development in Topic 5 draws on living standards and population, while Topic 6 links back to growth and employment because trade affects both.

Throughout the course you will need to:

- define terms precisely;
- draw and label diagrams, such as PPC and demand and supply diagrams;
- explain a chain of cause and effect step by step;
- weigh up arguments and reach a justified conclusion.

## Planning your study

Follow the syllabus order for the first pass through the content; it is designed so each topic uses the ones before it.

- **Start of the course.** Topic 1, then Topic 2. Take Topic 2 slowly, since elasticity and market failure cause difficulty later if they are rushed.
- **Middle of the course.** Topic 3, followed by Topic 4. Keep a running glossary of key terms, as the macroeconomic vocabulary builds quickly.
- **Later in the course.** Topics 5 and 6, which apply earlier ideas to development and trade.
- **Final stage.** Revisit every topic, alternating between multiple-choice practice for breadth and structured questions for depth.

Short multiple-choice quizzes are useful from the first topic onwards. Move on to full papers once all six topics have been taught, and turn to the course's exam-preparation page for paper-by-paper planning.

## Frequently asked questions

**What papers make up Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455?**
Paper 1 Multiple Choice (45 minutes, 30 marks, 30%) and Paper 2 Structured Questions (135 minutes, 90 marks, 70%). Both are compulsory.

**Is there any choice of questions?**
Paper 1 has no choice: all 30 questions are compulsory. In Paper 2 you answer one compulsory question and then three questions from a choice of four.

**Is IGCSE Economics 0455 tiered?**
No. All candidates take the same papers, and grades run from A* to G.

**How many topics are in the syllabus?**
Six: the basic economic problem; the allocation of resources; microeconomic decision makers; government and the macroeconomy; economic development; and international trade and globalisation.

**Which exam year does this syllabus cover?**
This edition is for examination in 2026 only.
