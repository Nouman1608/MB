## Formulas for this section

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost | fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output) |
| Unit cost | total cost ÷ output |
| Capacity utilisation (%) | actual output ÷ maximum possible output × 100 |
| PED | percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price |
| Four firm concentration ratio | sum of the four largest firms' market shares (%) |
| YED | percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in income |
| Market share (%) | firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Market growth (%) | (this year's market − last year's) ÷ last year's × 100 |
| Foreign price | home price × exchange rate (foreign units per home unit) |
| Home value of a foreign amount | foreign amount ÷ exchange rate |
| Gross / operating / net margin (%) | that profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| ROCE (%) | operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| Labour turnover (%) | number leaving in the year ÷ average number employed × 100 |

**Method in steps: interpreting a cost and capacity change**

1. Find total cost before and after (fixed + variable × output).
2. Divide each by actual output for unit cost.
3. Find capacity utilisation before and after.
4. Explain which economy (or diseconomy) moved the unit cost.
5. Judge: is the extra capacity likely to be filled?

**Worked reminder.** Fixed costs £250,000, variable cost £12 a unit, output 40,000, capacity 50,000. Total cost = 250,000 + 480,000 = £730,000; unit cost = £18.25; utilisation = 80%.

## Business growth

**SME.** Small or medium sized enterprise. Pros: speed, flexibility, customer closeness, low overheads. Cons: higher unit costs, harder finance, few key people, weak buying power.

**Reasons to grow.** Lower unit costs; market share and power; spread risk; higher owner returns; survival.

| Economies of scale (unit cost falls) | Diseconomies of scale (unit cost rises) |
|---|---|
| **Purchasing:** bulk discounts | **Control:** hard to monitor every part |
| **Technical:** bigger, more efficient equipment | **Communication:** long chains distort messages |
| **Financial:** cheaper, wider borrowing | **Coordination:** sites and departments diverge |

**Growth methods in one line each**

- **Merger:** two firms agree to combine into one.
- **Acquisition (takeover):** one firm buys a controlling stake in another.
- **Franchising:** franchisees pay fees to trade under the brand; fast and low capital, but less control.
- **Joint venture:** two firms set up a jointly owned project; share cost and risk, risk of dispute.

**Integration**

- **Backward vertical:** buy a supplier (earlier stage). Secures inputs.
- **Forward vertical:** buy a customer or outlet (later stage). Controls distribution.
- **Horizontal:** buy a rival at the same stage. Market share, economies of scale, possible competition-law issues.
- **Conglomerate:** buy into an unrelated industry. Spreads risk, little synergy.

**Method in steps: assessing a growth method**

1. State the method precisely (merger, acquisition, franchise, joint venture, organic).
2. Link one advantage to the case firm's objective, such as speed or market share.
3. Link one drawback to its resources, culture or control.
4. Weigh them, and judge which matters more for this firm now.

**Retrenchment.** Shrinking deliberately. Reasons: falling demand, losses, overcapacity, diseconomies, need for cash, refocus. Problems: redundancy costs, morale, lost skills, reputation, no capacity for a recovery.

**Functional areas.** Growth: more finance, staff, structure, capacity, wider marketing. Retrenchment: redundancies, closures, narrower range, cash released.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Organic vs external growth:** own resources and slow versus joining another firm and fast.
- **Merger vs acquisition:** agreement between equals versus purchase of control.
- **Economies vs diseconomies:** both are about **unit cost**, not total cost.
- **Capacity vs output:** capacity is the maximum; output is what was actually made.

## Innovation

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Product innovation | New or improved good or service |
| Process innovation | New or improved method of production or delivery |
| Kaizen | Continuous small improvements from staff |
| R&D | Technical work to create and test new products and processes |
| Intrapreneurship | Staff act as entrepreneurs inside the firm |
| Benchmarking | Comparing with the best and adopting what works |
| Cross-functional team | Staff from several functions on one project |
| Patent | Legal right to block others from making or selling an invention for a limited time |
| Copyright | Protects original creative work (software, text, designs) |
| Trademark | Protects brand names and logos |

**Purpose and value:** competitive advantage, sales growth, premium prices, lower costs.
**Pressures:** rivals, tastes, short life cycles, technology, law, shareholders.
**Barriers:** cost, risk, short-termism, resistance, skills gaps, risk-averse culture, copying.
**Functional impact:** marketing gets a USP to promote; operations retools; finance funds spending before revenue; HR recruits and trains.

## Globalisation

**Why trade globally:** saturated home market, scale, cheaper inputs, spread risk. **Why become multinational:** nearness to customers, lower transport cost, avoid tariffs, cheaper resources.

**Factors for choosing a market:** size, growth, incomes, competition, stability, barriers, culture, currency.
**Adapt or standardise:** standard gives scale; adapting fits local tastes and laws at extra cost.
**Where to produce:** cost, skills, infrastructure, nearness to market, tariffs.

- **Offshoring:** moving production abroad, mainly for lower costs.
- **Re-shoring:** bringing it back, for example after foreign wages or shipping costs rise or supply chains fail.

**Trading blocs.** EU: customs union with no internal customs duties and a common external tariff. ASEAN: tariffs cut within the bloc; members set their own on outside goods. USMCA: free trade agreement of the United States, Mexico and Canada. Insiders gain access; outsiders face barriers.

**Protectionism.** Tariff = tax on imports. Quota = limit on import quantity.

**Economic data in a target market.** Inflation raises local wages, rents and prices: at 5% inflation a monthly wage bill of 40,000 local units becomes 42,000. High unemployment weakens spending but eases recruitment. High interest rates make local borrowing dear and cut spending by customers who buy on credit.

**Entry methods ranked by control, cost and risk:** export (lowest) → licensing/franchising → joint venture → FDI (highest).

**Method in steps: comparing two international markets**

1. Calculate what the data allows: concentration ratio, market growth, the firm's likely share.
2. Use YED with the GDP growth forecast to predict demand growth in each market.
3. Use PED to judge whether to enter at a low or a premium price.
4. Convert prices or costs at the current exchange rate, then at the expected one.
5. Add the non-numerical factors: trading bloc membership, tariffs or quotas, culture, stability.
6. Judge which market fits the firm's resources and objectives, and say what would change your view.

## Sustainability

- **Environmental:** less waste and emissions, renewables, energy efficiency.
- **Social:** fair treatment of workers, community support, ethical sourcing.
- **Economic:** long-term profit without exploiting resources or people.

**Challenges:** cost, measurement, profit trade-offs, supplier resistance, greenwashing.
**Triple Bottom Line:** people, planet, profit. Value: fuller picture for stakeholders. Limits: hard to measure, selective reporting.

## Quick self-test

1. Fixed costs £60,000, variable cost £8 a unit, output 5,000. Find total cost and unit cost.
2. Output 7,200 units, capacity 9,000. Find capacity utilisation.
3. The four largest firms hold 22%, 18%, 15% and 9%. Find the four firm concentration ratio.
4. PED is −2.0. Price rises 5%. What happens to quantity demanded?
5. YED is 1.2. Incomes rise 4%. What happens to quantity demanded?
6. A part costs €30. Find its cost in pounds at £1 = €1.20 and at £1 = €1.25.
7. Gross profit £180,000 on revenue £600,000. Find the gross margin.
8. Operating profit £90,000, capital employed £750,000. Find ROCE.
9. 14 staff left; average employment was 175. Find labour turnover.
10. A firm sells £6m in a £48m market, which grows to £50.4m next year. Find its share now and the market growth rate.
11. A clothing retailer buys its main garment factory. Name the type of integration.
12. Name the three diseconomies of scale in the specification.

### Answers

1. 60,000 + (8 × 5,000) = **£100,000**; 100,000 ÷ 5,000 = **£20**.
2. 7,200 ÷ 9,000 × 100 = **80%**.
3. 22 + 18 + 15 + 9 = **64%**.
4. −2.0 × 5 = **falls by 10%**.
5. 1.2 × 4 = **rises by 4.8%**.
6. 30 ÷ 1.20 = **£25**; 30 ÷ 1.25 = **£24**.
7. 180,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **30%**.
8. 90,000 ÷ 750,000 × 100 = **12%**.
9. 14 ÷ 175 × 100 = **8%**.
10. 6 ÷ 48 × 100 = **12.5%**; (50.4 − 48) ÷ 48 × 100 = **5%**.
11. **Backward vertical** (the factory is an earlier stage).
12. **Control, communication, coordination.**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using capacity rather than actual output as the divisor for unit cost.
- Giving an economy of scale that lowers total cost, not unit cost.
- Calling a takeover a merger, or forward integration backward.
- Listing advantages of franchising with no link to the case business's capital, brand or control.
- Forgetting the minus sign on PED when predicting the direction of a demand change.
- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting a foreign-currency amount into the home currency.
- Using the year-end headcount instead of the average in labour turnover.
- Using net profit in ROCE; the formula uses operating profit.
- Treating TBL as only an environmental report.
- Ending an "assess" or "evaluate" answer with no judgement about which strategy suits this business.
