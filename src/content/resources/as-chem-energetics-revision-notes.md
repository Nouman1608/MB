## Standard conditions and definitions

**Standard conditions:** 298 K, 100 kPa, all substances in their standard states, solutions at 1 mol dm⁻³.

| Term | Definition — quantity is critical |
|---|---|
| **Formation ΔH_f** | **One mole** of a compound formed from its elements in standard states |
| **Combustion ΔH_c** | **One mole** of a substance burned completely in excess oxygen |
| **Neutralisation** | **One mole of water** formed from acid and alkali |
| **Atomisation** | **One mole of gaseous atoms** formed from the element |

The examiner is checking the "one mole of **what**" in each case. ΔH_f of an element in its standard state is **zero** by definition.

## Hess's law

> The total enthalpy change is **independent of the route taken**, provided initial and final conditions are the same.

Two cycle types:

```
FROM FORMATION DATA
dH = sum(dHf PRODUCTS) - sum(dHf REACTANTS)
     (arrows point UP from elements)

FROM COMBUSTION DATA
dH = sum(dHc REACTANTS) - sum(dHc PRODUCTS)
     (arrows point DOWN to combustion products)
```

The two are **reversed** — which one applies depends on the data given, and mixing them up is the most common error.

Remember to multiply each ΔH by the number of moles in the balanced equation.

## Bond energies

```
dH = sum(bonds BROKEN) - sum(bonds FORMED)
```

**Breaking is endothermic** (energy in, positive). **Making is exothermic** (energy out, negative).

Bond energy values are **averages** across many compounds, so results are approximate — a standard evaluation point when a calculated value differs from an experimental one.

## Calorimetry

```
q = m c delta-T          m = mass of SOLUTION (not the solid), in g
                         c = 4.18 J g^-1 K^-1 for aqueous solutions

dH = -q / n              n = moles of the LIMITING reactant
```

Convert J to kJ. The sign: temperature **rise** means exothermic, so ΔH is **negative**.

**Why experimental values are less exothermic than data-book values:** heat loss to the surroundings, incomplete combustion, evaporation of fuel, non-standard conditions.

## Exam traps

- Omitting "one mole" from a definition.
- Using the mass of the solid instead of the mass of solution in q = mcΔT.
- Forgetting the minus sign in ΔH = −q/n.
- Using the wrong Hess arrangement for the data type.
- Not multiplying by stoichiometric coefficients.
- Quoting the answer without units (kJ mol⁻¹) or sign.

## Self-test

1. Define standard enthalpy of formation.
2. Give the Hess expression when combustion data is supplied.
3. 50 cm³ of solution rises 8.0 °C; 0.010 mol reacted. Find ΔH.
4. Is bond breaking endothermic or exothermic?
5. Give two reasons an experimental ΔH_c is less exothermic than the data-book value.

**Answers:** 1. The enthalpy change when **one mole** of a compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states under standard conditions. 2. ΔH = Σ ΔH_c(reactants) − Σ ΔH_c(products). 3. q = 50 × 4.18 × 8.0 = 1672 J = 1.672 kJ; ΔH = −1.672/0.010 = **−167 kJ mol⁻¹**. 4. Endothermic — energy must be supplied. 5. Any two: heat loss to the surroundings, incomplete combustion, evaporation of the fuel, non-standard conditions.
