## Section A

**1.** Define compensatory habitat in the context of coastal management. **[1]**

**2.** Outline why a port authority and a fishing community might disagree over dredging in an estuary. **[2]**

**3.** State the three time periods (epochs) used in shoreline management plans in England and Wales. **[1]**

**4.** Outline two ways in which mangroves reduce coastal risk. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one reason why a sea wall could protect Male but not every inhabited island in the Maldives. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A council compares two options for protecting assets worth 15.0 million over 40 years (invented figures).

- Option X: rock armour costing 5.0 million, with maintenance of 0.15 million a year.
- Option Y: beach nourishment costing 0.8 million every 4 years.

**(a)** Calculate the whole-life cost of each option over 40 years. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the benefit-cost ratio of each option, to two decimal places. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one reason why Option Y's real cost could be higher than calculated. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why a shoreline management plan might set "hold the line" for the first 20 years and "managed realignment" after that. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why some mangrove restoration projects fail. **[3]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why benefit-cost ratios alone should not decide coastal management. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** With reference to examples, evaluate the role of stakeholders in the management of a coastal margin. **[10]**

**11.** "Small island states can only adapt to rising sea level by relocating their people." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** New intertidal habitat created, for example by managed realignment, to replace habitat lost elsewhere through coastal squeeze or development [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The port wants deeper channels for larger ships, so it supports dredging [1]; fishers fear that dredging destroys seabed habitat and nursery grounds and stirs up sediment, reducing catches [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Up to 20 years; 20 to 50 years; 50 to 100 years [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Dense roots slow water and absorb wave energy, reducing wave height reaching the shore [1]; they trap sediment, building up and stabilising the land surface [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Male is a single, dense, high-value island where the cost per person protected is affordable [1]; the country has many small, scattered inhabited islands, and walls around all of them would be far too costly to build and maintain [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Option X: 5.0 + (0.15 x 40) = 5.0 + 6.0 = **11.0 million** [1]. Option Y: 40 / 4 = 10 campaigns, 10 x 0.8 = **8.0 million** [1].

**(b)** Option X: 15.0 / 11.0 = **1.36** [1]. Option Y: 15.0 / 8.0 = **1.88** [1].

**(c)** Sea-level rise or stronger storms may remove sand faster, so nourishment is needed more often than every 4 years [1], increasing the number of campaigns and the whole-life cost [1]. (Accept: the price of suitable sand or dredging may rise.) **[6]**

**7.** Holding the line in the short term protects existing homes and infrastructure while people adapt [1]. Over longer periods, rising sea level makes defences increasingly expensive to maintain and less likely to be justified [1], so realignment later allows the coast to move inland and natural habitat to form, with communities given time to relocate [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Seedlings are planted in the wrong place, such as too low in the tidal frame, too exposed to waves or in water that is too saline [1]; the original causes of loss, such as clearing for aquaculture ponds, continue [1]; water flow and sediment supply are not restored, so seedlings cannot establish [1]. **[3]**

**9.** They leave out values that are hard to price, such as habitats, heritage and community ties [1], so a strategy with a high ratio may still cause serious unpriced damage [1]. They do not show distribution [1], so a scheme may benefit wealthy property owners while costs or risks fall on others, such as places downdrift [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Who they are and what they want:** residents, councils, national agencies, ports, tourism businesses, fishers, conservation groups and insurers, each with different priorities [1] [1].
- **How stakeholders shape decisions:** consultation within shoreline management plans and integrated coastal zone management brings views together [1]; powerful stakeholders, such as port authorities or property owners, may have more influence than fishers or future residents [1].
- **Examples:** at Mappleton, protecting the village satisfied residents but starved beaches to the south, angering downdrift landowners [1]; at Medmerry, landowners and conservationists accepted realignment that created habitat and protected homes inland [1].
- **Benefits of involvement:** local knowledge, greater acceptance of difficult decisions, and long-term support for schemes [1].
- **Problems:** conflicting aims can delay decisions; consultation may not change outcomes; future generations have no voice [1].
- **Examination:** the most successful schemes combine stakeholder agreement with a whole-system view of the sediment cell [1].
- **Conclusion:** stakeholders are central to whether management works, but their influence is unequal, so effective management needs fair processes as well as good engineering [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Case for the statement:** most land on atolls lies very low, so long-term sea-level rise threatens habitability [1]; defending every island is unaffordable [1]; saltwater intrusion into freshwater lenses and coral damage add to the pressure [1].
- **Case against:** hard protection can defend critical centres, as the sea wall around Male shows [1]; land raising and reclamation, as at Hulhumale, create higher ground within the country [1]; healthy reefs supply sand and break waves, so reef protection helps islands persist [1]; consolidating population onto fewer, better-protected islands is an option short of leaving the country [1].
- **Further factors:** the rate of future sea-level rise is uncertain and depends on global emissions [1]; small states depend on international finance for adaptation [1].
- **Conclusion:** relocation may become necessary in the long term for some islands, but in the coming decades a mix of protection, land raising and ecosystem management allows adaptation in place, so the statement is too absolute [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" rewards a balanced range of arguments and a conclusion that weighs them. Use time scale (decades versus a century) to structure the judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating nourishment cost per year instead of per campaign.
- Ignoring maintenance in whole-life costs.
- Naming stakeholders without their interests.
- Writing absolute conclusions ("only", "always") that the evidence does not support.
