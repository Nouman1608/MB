These notes condense section 11.3, Manufacturing and related service industry, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 11 option). Full explanations and worked examples are in the [11.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry/); afterwards, try the [11.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry-practice/).

## The three syllabus statements

1. Location factors: land, labour, capital, markets, materials, technology, economies and diseconomies of scale, inertia, transport, government policies.
2. Industrial agglomeration, functional linkages, the industrial estate and the export processing zone (EPZ).
3. The informal sector of manufacturing and services: causes, characteristics, location, impact.

## Location factors: what each one really does

| Factor | Pulls industry towards | Weakening or strengthening? |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Cheap, flat, large sites; urban edge | Still matters for large plants |
| Labour | Low cost or high skill | Strong for assembly and research |
| Capital | Financial centres, state funding | Capital is mobile; weak direct pull |
| Markets | Large, accessible consumer areas | Strong for bulk-gaining and perishable goods |
| Materials | Sources of bulky raw materials | Weakening as inputs get lighter |
| Technology | Places with infrastructure and connectivity | Frees many firms from fixed sites |
| Economies of scale | Large plants and big clusters | Strong in heavy and car industries |
| Diseconomies | Away from congested centres | Push factor at large scale |
| Inertia | Existing industrial regions | Holds old clusters together |
| Transport | Ports, airports, junctions, break-of-bulk points | Container ports now key |
| Government | Zones, grants, tax relief | Often decisive between similar sites |

## Industry types

- **Material-oriented** (bulk-reducing): smelting, sugar refining, cement.
- **Market-oriented** (bulk-gaining, perishable, fragile): bottling, bakeries, furniture.
- **Labour-oriented**: garments (cheap labour); design and research (skilled labour).
- **Footloose**: electronics assembly, software, many services.

## Two calculations to know

**Material index** = weight of localised materials ÷ weight of product.
- MI above 1 → locate near materials.
- MI below 1 → locate near the market.

**Location quotient** = (% of region's jobs in an industry) ÷ (% of national jobs in that industry).
- LQ above 1 → industry concentrated in the region.
- LQ below 1 → under-represented.

## Agglomeration and linkages

- **Localisation economies:** benefits from firms in the same industry nearby.
- **Urbanisation economies:** benefits from a large city's infrastructure, services and market.
- **Cumulative causation:** growth attracts more growth until diseconomies set in.
- **Linkages:** backward (suppliers), forward (customers), horizontal (parallel component makers), service (logistics, finance, repair).
- **Related service industry:** logistics, warehousing, maintenance, testing, design, business services that depend on manufacturing.

## Planned industrial space

| | Industrial estate | EPZ | SEZ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scale | A planned site | A fenced zone | A wider region or city area |
| Rules | Normal | Duty-free inputs; exports | Special tax, trade and investment rules |
| Example | Any serviced estate | Mexico's maquiladoras (from 1965) | Shenzhen, China (1980) |

## Informal sector

- **Causes:** urban labour supply growing faster than formal jobs; lack of skills, capital or documents; costly registration.
- **Characteristics:** small scale, family labour, low capital, easy entry, irregular income, no legal protection, untaxed.
- **Location:** markets, transport terminals, roadsides, homes in informal settlements, waste sites.
- **Impact:** jobs, cheap goods and services, recycling and skills, but low pay, unsafe work, lost tax revenue.
- **Links:** formal firms subcontract to home workers; traders sell factory goods. Sectors are connected.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bulk-reducing industry | Product weighs less than its inputs |
| Bulk-gaining industry | Product weighs more than, or is bulkier than, its localised inputs |
| Localised material | Raw material found only in some places |
| Ubiquitous material | Material available almost everywhere, such as water |
| Break-of-bulk point | Place where goods change transport mode, such as a port |
| Footloose industry | Not tied to materials or markets; free to choose among locations |
| Just-in-time | Parts delivered as needed, pulling suppliers close to assemblers |
| Greenfield site | Undeveloped land, often on the urban edge |
| Brownfield site | Previously developed land reused for new building |
| Internal economies of scale | Cost savings inside one growing firm |
| External economies | Cost savings from location near other firms |
| Subcontracting | Formal firms passing work to smaller or informal firms |

## Exam traps

- Ten factors are not equally important: rank two or three for the industry asked about.
- The MI uses weight, not value.
- An EPZ is defined by its trade and tax rules, not its fence.
- "Related service industry" is in the syllabus wording; include services such as logistics.
- The informal sector is linked to formal firms; it is not a separate world.
- Diseconomies are a push factor; don't treat scale as only an advantage.

## Self-test

1. List the ten location factors named in 11.3.
2. Define a bulk-reducing industry and give one example.
3. A process uses 2.5 t of localised materials to make 1 t of product. Calculate the MI and state the likely location.
4. Why is capital described as a weak direct pull?
5. Give one localisation economy and one urbanisation economy.
6. Name the four types of functional linkage.
7. A region has 9% of its jobs in textiles; the national figure is 3%. Calculate the LQ.
8. State two differences between an industrial estate and an EPZ.
9. Give two causes of the informal sector.
10. Give two places where informal enterprises locate, and why.
11. Give one positive and one negative impact of the informal sector.
12. What is a break-of-bulk point, and why can it attract industry?

### Answers

1. Land, labour, capital, markets, materials, technology, economies of scale, diseconomies of scale, inertia, transport, government policies (economies and diseconomies are one syllabus item).
2. A process where the product weighs less than its inputs, such as ore smelting or sugar refining.
3. MI = 2.5 ÷ 1 = 2.5; near the materials.
4. Money moves easily between places, so it rarely fixes a location.
5. Localisation: shared specialist suppliers or labour. Urbanisation: city transport, banks or universities.
6. Backward, forward, horizontal, service.
7. 9 ÷ 3 = 3.0.
8. EPZ inputs are imported duty-free; EPZ output is mainly exported; EPZs offer tax holidays (any two contrasts with normal rules on estates).
9. Any two: urban labour growth faster than formal jobs, lack of skills or capital, costly registration, lack of documents.
10. Markets or transport terminals, for customers; homes, for low cost; waste sites, for materials.
11. Positive: jobs or cheap goods. Negative: low, insecure pay, unsafe work or lost tax.
12. Where goods change transport mode; unloading costs are paid anyway, so processing there avoids a second transfer.
