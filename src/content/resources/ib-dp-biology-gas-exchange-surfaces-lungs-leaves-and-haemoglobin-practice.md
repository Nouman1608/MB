## Questions

**1.** State two properties that all gas-exchange surfaces share. **[2]**

**2.** A student models two organisms as cubes of side 5 mm and 15 mm.

**(a)** Calculate the surface area-to-volume ratio of each cube. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the larger organism is more likely to need a specialised gas-exchange system. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how four features of mammalian alveolar lungs make gas exchange efficient. **[4]**

**4.** Describe how muscles and ribs produce inhalation, and how forced exhalation differs from quiet exhalation. **[6]**

**5.** A resting student has a tidal volume of 0.55 L, an inspiratory reserve volume of 3.05 L and a vital capacity of 4.70 L. The student breathes 15 times per minute.

**(a)** Calculate the expiratory reserve volume. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of air breathed per minute at rest. **[1]**
**(c)** During exercise, tidal volume is 1.8 L at 24 breaths per minute. Calculate how many times greater the volume breathed per minute is than at rest. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a spirometer cannot give a value for total lung volume. **[2]**

**6.** A student draws a plan diagram of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf.

**(a)** State two features of a plan diagram. **[2]**
**(b)** Outline how three leaf structures help gas exchange while limiting water loss. **[3]**

**7.** A potometer has a capillary of radius 0.60 mm. In still air a bubble moves 20 mm in 4 minutes. With a fan blowing it moves 33 mm in 4 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of water taken up per minute in still air. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in rate with the fan. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the effect of the fan. **[2]**

**8.** A student makes leaf casts from both surfaces of a leaf and counts stomata in five fields of view, each of diameter 0.45 mm.

| Field | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower surface | 21 | 25 | 23 | 24 | 22 |
| Upper surface | 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 |

**(a)** Calculate the stomatal density of the lower surface in stomata per mm2. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the stomatal density of the upper surface. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why five fields were counted rather than one. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why the upper surface has fewer stomata. **[1]**

**9.** **(HL only)** Explain why the oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin is S-shaped. **[5]**

**10.** **(HL only)** The table shows percentage saturation of adult and foetal haemoglobin.

| Oxygen (kPa) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adult | 18 | 40 | 59 | 72 | 81 |
| Foetal | 35 | 62 | 78 | 87 | 91 |

**(a)** Calculate the difference in saturation at 4 kPa. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how the foetal curve compares with the adult curve. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how this difference benefits the foetus. **[3]**
**(d)** Predict how foetal and adult saturation compare at 1 kPa. **[1]**

**11.** **(HL only)** Exercising muscle has an oxygen concentration of 3 kPa. The lungs have 13 kPa. Saturation at 3 kPa is 40% normally and 25% with raised carbon dioxide. At 13 kPa it is 97% and 95%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage points of saturation lost between lungs and muscle in each condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how carbon dioxide causes this change. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the benefit to the muscle. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why loading in the lungs is almost unchanged. **[2]**

**12.** Compare how concentration gradients are maintained at fish gills and mammalian alveoli. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: permeable to the gases; thin, often one cell thick; moist; large surface area. [1][1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit when each property is a separate, named point; "efficient" alone is too vague to be credited.

**2. (a)** 5 mm: 6 x 25 = 150 mm2, volume 125 mm3, ratio **1.2 per mm** [1]. 15 mm: 6 x 225 = 1350 mm2, volume 3375 mm3, ratio **0.4 per mm** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Ratio is lower in the larger organism, so less surface per unit volume [1]. Diffusion distance to the centre is greater, so diffusion alone cannot supply every cell [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the ratio values from part (a) in part (b); comparing them directly makes the explanation clear.

**3.** Any four, each with a reason: high surface area from many alveoli gives more area for diffusion; branched bronchioles carry air close to every alveolus; extensive capillary beds keep blood flowing and the gradient steep; surfactant reduces surface tension so alveoli stay open; thin walls give a short diffusion path. [1][1][1][1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A feature without its function is a weaker answer; use "so that" or "because" every time.

**4.** Inhalation: diaphragm contracts and flattens [1]; external intercostals contract, moving ribs up and out [1]; thoracic volume increases and pressure falls below atmospheric, so air flows in [1]. Quiet exhalation is passive: muscles relax and the diaphragm domes [1]. Forced exhalation: internal intercostals contract, pulling ribs down and in [1]; abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain credit when they link muscle action to volume and pressure change, not just to rib movement.

**5. (a)** ERV = 4.70 - 0.55 - 3.05 [1] = **1.10 L** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 0.55 x 15 = **8.25 L per minute** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 1.8 x 24 = 43.2 L per minute [1]; 43.2 / 8.25 = **5.24 times** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Some air stays in the lungs after the strongest exhalation [1]; this residual volume cannot be breathed out into the spirometer [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the working line and include units; a correct value with no units is a common reason for lost credit.

**6. (a)** Any two of: shows tissue outlines only, no individual cells; clear continuous lines; labelled with non-crossing label lines; a title. [1][1] **[2]**
**(b)** Any three: waxy cuticle reduces water loss; stomata with guard cells open to allow gases in and close to reduce water loss; air spaces in spongy mesophyll let gases diffuse to cells; transparent epidermis lets light through. [1][1][1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links each structure to both aims, gas exchange and water saving.

**7. (a)** Volume = pi x 0.60 x 0.60 x 20 [1] = 22.6 mm3 [1]; rate = 22.6 / 4 = **5.65 mm3 per minute** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Fan rate = pi x 0.36 x 33 / 4 = 9.33 mm3 per minute [1]; increase = (9.33 - 5.65) / 5.65 x 100 = **65%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Wind removes humid air next to the leaf [1]; this steepens the water vapour gradient, so transpiration increases [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage change is relative to the original value, so divide by the still-air rate.

**8. (a)** Mean = 115 / 5 = 23.0 [1]; area = pi x 0.225 x 0.225 = 0.159 mm2 [1]; density = 23.0 / 0.159 = **145 per mm2** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Mean = 6 / 5 = 1.2 [1]; density = 1.2 / 0.159 = **7.5 per mm2** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Counts vary from field to field [1]; a mean of repeats reduces the effect of chance and increases reliability [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The upper surface is more exposed to sun and heat, so fewer stomata reduce water loss. [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The field of view diameter must be halved before finding the area; using the diameter as a radius is a common slip.

**9.** Haemoglobin has four haem groups, each binding one oxygen [1]. At low oxygen the first molecule binds slowly, so the curve rises gently [1]. Binding changes the shape of the molecule [1]. This makes the other haem groups bind oxygen more easily (cooperative binding), so the curve steepens [1]. At high oxygen most sites are full, so the curve flattens [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Name cooperative binding and describe all three regions of the curve.

**10. (a)** 78 - 59 = **19 percentage points** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The foetal curve lies to the left [1]; saturation is higher at every oxygen level shown [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Foetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen [1]. In the placenta, maternal blood gives up oxygen at low oxygen levels [1]; foetal haemoglobin picks it up, keeping the gradient from mother to foetus [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Foetal saturation is higher, because its curve lies to the left throughout. [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Higher affinity" is more precise than "better at" and should be used in explanations.

**11. (a)** Normal: 97 - 40 = **57** [1]; raised carbon dioxide: 95 - 25 = **70** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Carbon dioxide binds to haemoglobin at a site other than the haem group (allosteric) [1]; shape changes, lowering affinity for oxygen [1]; curve shifts right, so more oxygen is released at the same oxygen level (Bohr shift) [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Respiring muscle makes more carbon dioxide [1]; it receives more oxygen where demand is highest [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Carbon dioxide is low in the lungs as it is breathed out [1]; haemoglobin loads almost fully at high oxygen, so the shift hardly changes loading [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the shift and tie it to the tissue, not to the curve alone.

**12.** Gills: water flows continuously over the gills, renewing oxygen at the surface [1]; blood flows through capillaries in the gills, carrying oxygen away [1]. Alveoli: ventilation renews air [1]; extensive capillary beds with continuous blood flow remove oxygen from the alveoli [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparison questions are best answered with a point for each, noting the medium (water or air).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that gases are "pumped" or "sucked" instead of diffusing or flowing down a pressure gradient.
- Giving a feature of the lung with no function.
- Forgetting units on volumes, rates and densities.
- Using the field-of-view diameter as the radius.
- Calculating percentage change from the new value, not the original.
- Saying "residual volume" is measured by the spirometer.
- Describing a plan diagram with cells drawn.
- Mixing up left and right when comparing curves.
- Not naming cooperative binding in the S-shape answer.
