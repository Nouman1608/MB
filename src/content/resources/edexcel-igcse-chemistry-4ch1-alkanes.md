This guide teaches sub-topic 4(c) Alkanes from Topic 4, Organic chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 4.19 to 4.22. None of them carries a C reference, so all can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2.

The [organic chemistry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-organic-chemistry/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on alkanes, with worked formula, yield and substitution examples and a clear way to explain "saturated". ## What the sub-topic asks

| Point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 4.19 | Know the general formula for alkanes |
| 4.20 | Explain why alkanes are classified as saturated hydrocarbons |
| 4.21 | Draw structural and displayed formulae for alkanes with up to five carbons and name the unbranched isomers |
| 4.22 | Describe the reactions of alkanes with halogens in ultraviolet radiation (mono-substitution only; no mechanisms) |

## The general formula (4.19)

The alkanes are a homologous series of hydrocarbons with the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**, where n is the number of carbon atoms. For n carbon atoms there are 2n + 2 hydrogen atoms.

| n | Name | Molecular formula | Mr | State at 20 °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Methane | CH₄ | 16 | Gas | -162 |
| 2 | Ethane | C₂H₆ | 30 | Gas | -89 |
| 3 | Propane | C₃H₈ | 44 | Gas | -42 |
| 4 | Butane | C₄H₁₀ | 58 | Gas | -1 |
| 5 | Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 72 | Liquid | 36 |

Each member differs from the next by CH₂ (Mr 14). The boiling point rises with chain length, because longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed to separate them. That is why methane to butane are gases at room temperature and pentane is a liquid.

**Using the formula.** An alkane with 12 carbons has 2 x 12 + 2 = 26 hydrogens: C₁₂H₂₆. An alkane with 20 hydrogens has n such that 2n + 2 = 20, so n = 9: C₉H₂₀. If an alkane has a relative molecular mass of 142, then 14n + 2 = 142, so n = 10 and the formula is C₁₀H₂₂.

## Saturated hydrocarbons (4.20)

A **saturated** hydrocarbon contains only **single bonds** between carbon atoms. Every carbon atom is bonded to the **maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms** (and other atoms), and no more atoms can be added without breaking the carbon skeleton. Alkanes are saturated because every C-C bond is a single bond.

A good explanation: "Alkanes are saturated because all the carbon-carbon bonds are single bonds, so each carbon atom is bonded to as many other atoms as possible." The word "saturated" is not an adjective for "has no double bonds" alone; the idea of the maximum number of hydrogen atoms completes the answer.

Because they have no double bond, alkanes do not take part in addition reactions and are generally **unreactive** towards many reagents, such as acids and bromine water in the dark. They do burn, and they react with halogens in ultraviolet radiation.

## Drawing and naming alkanes (4.21)

You must draw alkanes with up to five carbon atoms and name the **unbranched** ones.

**Structural and displayed formulae.**

| Name | Structural formula | Displayed formula |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | One C with four H, each shown as a bond |
| Ethane | CH₃CH₃ | Two C joined, each with three H |
| Propane | CH₃CH₂CH₃ | Three C in a chain |
| Butane | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ | Four C in a chain |
| Pentane | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ | Five C in a chain |

Displayed formula of propane:

```
    H   H   H
    |   |   |
H - C - C - C - H
    |   |   |
    H   H   H
```

**Branched isomers.** Butane has an isomer, 2-methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, with a carbon bonded to three CH₃ groups and one H. Pentane has two branched isomers: 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane. You should be able to **draw** the branched isomers, but the specification asks you to name only the unbranched chains in this point. Both of the isomers of butane have the formula C₄H₁₀ and Mr = 58.

Checking a drawing: each carbon must have four bonds, each hydrogen one. Count the hydrogens against 2n + 2.

## Reactions with halogens (4.22)

Alkanes react with chlorine and bromine in the presence of **ultraviolet (UV) radiation**, such as sunlight. The UV radiation provides the energy to start the reaction. In the dark, there is no reaction. The reaction is a **substitution**: a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom. Only **mono-substitution** is needed, replacing one hydrogen.

```
CH4 + Cl2  →  CH3Cl + HCl      (UV light)
chloromethane + hydrogen chloride

CH4 + Br2  →  CH3Br + HBr      (UV light)
bromomethane + hydrogen bromide

C2H6 + Cl2 →  C2H5Cl + HCl     (UV light)
chloroethane + hydrogen chloride
```

Two products form: a halogenoalkane and a hydrogen halide. This feature (two products) distinguishes substitution from addition. Reaction mechanisms are not required.

The halogen's colour fades as it reacts. With bromine, the red-brown colour disappears in UV light but stays in the dark. In real reactions, further substitution can happen (dichloromethane and so on), which is why the yield of the mono-substituted product is not 100%.

**Worked example 1: mass of product.** 3.2 g of methane reacts with excess chlorine in UV light to make chloromethane. Calculate the mass of chloromethane (Mr = 50.5) if the reaction is 100% efficient, and if the yield is 60%.

1. Moles of methane = 3.2 / 16 = **0.20 mol**.
2. The equation is 1 : 1, so moles of CH₃Cl = **0.20 mol**.
3. Theoretical mass = 0.20 x 50.5 = **10.1 g**.
4. Mass at 60% yield = 0.60 x 10.1 = **6.06 g**.

**Worked example 2: naming the product.** What is the organic product when ethane reacts with chlorine in UV light? Replace one H with Cl on an ethane skeleton: CH₃CH₂Cl, named **chloroethane**. The other product is hydrogen chloride, HCl.

## Combustion and other points

Alkanes burn completely in plenty of air to give carbon dioxide and water, releasing heat: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. In limited oxygen, carbon monoxide or soot may form. This is why alkanes are used as fuels (see the [crude oil study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-crude-oil/)).

## More worked examples

**Worked example 3: finding the formula from combustion data.** When 0.10 mol of an alkane burns completely, 0.50 mol of carbon dioxide and 0.60 mol of water form.

1. Moles of CO₂ per mole of alkane = 0.50 / 0.10 = **5**, so the alkane has 5 carbon atoms.
2. Moles of H₂O per mole of alkane = 0.60 / 0.10 = **6**, so the alkane has 12 hydrogen atoms (2 per water).
3. The formula is **C₅H₁₂**, which fits 2n + 2 = 12 for n = 5. It is pentane or one of its isomers.

**Worked example 4: the percentage of carbon.** The percentage of carbon by mass in an alkane is 12n / (14n + 2) x 100.

| Alkane | Calculation | % carbon |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | 12 / 16 | 75.0% |
| Ethane | 24 / 30 | 80.0% |
| Propane | 36 / 44 | 81.8% |
| Butane | 48 / 58 | 82.8% |
| Pentane | 60 / 72 | 83.3% |

The percentage rises as the chain gets longer and approaches 85.7% (the value for CH₂ units, as in alkenes). This explains why longer-chain alkanes burn with smokier flames when air is limited: they contain a higher proportion of carbon, so incomplete combustion produces more soot. Methane, with the lowest proportion, burns with a clean blue flame.

## Properties at a glance

| Property | Alkanes |
|---|---|
| Bonding | All carbon-carbon bonds single; saturated |
| Solubility in water | Insoluble (non-polar molecules) |
| Reaction with bromine water | None; the colour stays |
| Combustion | Burn to carbon dioxide and water (clean flame for short chains) |
| Reaction with halogens | Substitution in ultraviolet radiation |
| Trend in boiling point | Increases with chain length |

## Common mistakes

- Writing the general formula as CₙH₂ₙ (that is the alkenes).
- Explaining "saturated" as "has no oxygen" or "full of hydrogen" without mentioning single bonds.
- Drawing a carbon with five bonds, or leaving hydrogens off the end carbons.
- Listing the same isomer twice by bending the chain.
- Saying that the reaction with chlorine is addition. It is substitution, and gives two products.
- Forgetting the UV light, or writing that the reaction needs heat or a catalyst.
- Writing the products as C₂H₅Cl only; hydrogen chloride is also formed.

## Exam technique

Quote the general formula and use it: show the working "2n + 2 = ..." when finding a formula. For a drawing, count bonds on each atom. For the halogen reaction, write the balanced equation, name both products, state the condition (UV light) and state the type of reaction (substitution). A good one-sentence answer for "why are alkanes saturated" uses the words "single bonds" and "maximum number of hydrogen atoms".
