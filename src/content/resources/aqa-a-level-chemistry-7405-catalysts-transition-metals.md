## What a catalyst does

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative reaction route with a **lower activation energy**. It is not used up: it is regenerated at the end. It does not change the position of equilibrium or the enthalpy change of the reaction. A transition metal makes a good catalyst because it has **variable oxidation states** and can form intermediate species by moving between them.

## Heterogeneous catalysis

A heterogeneous catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants. The usual case is a solid catalyst with gaseous or liquid reactants.

How it works:

1. Reactant molecules are **adsorbed** (weakly bonded) onto **active sites** on the surface of the catalyst.
2. The bonds in the reactants are weakened, and the molecules are held close together in a suitable orientation.
3. The reaction occurs on the surface, forming the product.
4. The product molecules are **desorbed** and leave, freeing the active site.

### Examples

| Process | Catalyst | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Contact process | Vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅ | 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ |
| Haber process | Iron, Fe | N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ |

### Support medium

A solid catalyst works only at its surface, so a large surface area is important. Many industrial catalysts, especially expensive metals such as platinum or rhodium, are spread as a thin layer on a **support medium** such as a ceramic honeycomb. This **maximises the surface area** exposed to the reactants and **minimises the cost**, because less of the expensive catalyst is needed. A catalytic converter in a car uses this design.

### Poisoning

A catalyst is **poisoned** when an impurity in the reactants is adsorbed strongly onto the active sites and blocks them. The reactant molecules cannot be adsorbed, so the catalyst has reduced efficiency, the reaction becomes slower, and the catalyst has to be replaced or regenerated.

| Process | Poison | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Haber process | Sulfur compounds in the hydrogen feed | Block the iron surface |
| Catalytic converter | Lead (from leaded petrol) | Coats the surface |

The **cost implication** is that the feedstock must be purified (for example, by removing sulfur compounds), or the catalyst replaced, both of which add to the cost of production.

### The Contact process with vanadium(V) oxide

Vanadium(V) oxide is the catalyst for the oxidation of sulfur dioxide. It acts by changing its oxidation state:

```
SO₂ + V₂O₅ → SO₃ + V₂O₄          V changes from +5 to +4 (reduced)
2V₂O₄ + O₂ → 2V₂O₅               V changes from +4 to +5 (oxidised)
```

Adding the two steps (the first multiplied by 2) gives 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃, and the catalyst is regenerated.

```
2SO₂ + 2V₂O₅ → 2SO₃ + 2V₂O₄
2V₂O₄ + O₂ → 2V₂O₅
overall: 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃
```

## Homogeneous catalysis

A homogeneous catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants, usually all in aqueous solution. The reaction proceeds through an **intermediate species**: the catalyst reacts with one reactant, forming an intermediate, which then reacts with the other reactant and regenerates the catalyst. Both steps have lower activation energies than the uncatalysed reaction.

### Fe²⁺ catalysing S₂O₈²⁻ and I⁻

The overall reaction is:

```
S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂
```

Without a catalyst it is slow. Both reactants are **negatively charged**, so the ions repel each other and collide with difficulty. Iron(II) or iron(III) ions provide a route that avoids a collision between two negative ions. Each step involves oppositely charged ions:

```
S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺         Fe oxidised from +2 to +3
2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂                 Fe reduced from +3 to +2
```

The two steps add to give the overall equation, with Fe²⁺ regenerated. Fe³⁺ would work equally well as the starting catalyst, with the steps in the opposite order. The ability of iron to change between +2 and +3 is the key.

### Mn²⁺ autocatalysis in the MnO₄⁻ and C₂O₄²⁻ reaction

The overall reaction between manganate(VII) and ethanedioate is:

```
2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 10CO₂
```

This reaction is slow at the start and then speeds up, because **one of the products, Mn²⁺, is a catalyst**. This is **autocatalysis**. Both reactants are negatively charged, so the same repulsion argument applies.

The catalytic steps involve Mn²⁺ and Mn³⁺:

```
4Mn²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Mn³⁺ + 4H₂O
2Mn³⁺ + C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 2CO₂
```

Mn²⁺ is oxidised to Mn³⁺ by the manganate(VII), and Mn³⁺ is reduced back to Mn²⁺ by ethanedioate. The rate is slow at first because there is no Mn²⁺, then it accelerates as Mn²⁺ forms, and finally slows as the reactants are used up. A graph of concentration of MnO₄⁻ against time shows a slow start, a steep fall, then levelling off. If a little Mn²⁺ is added at the start, the initial slow stage disappears.

## Variable oxidation states and catalysis

Variable oxidation states matter for three reasons:

- They allow the metal to form **intermediates** by giving or accepting electrons.
- They let the catalyst be **regenerated** at the end by returning to its original oxidation state.
- They give the reaction a new route with a **lower activation energy**.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: show that Fe²⁺ is a catalyst

Add the equations for the two steps: S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺ and 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂. The 2Fe²⁺ and 2Fe³⁺ appear on both sides and cancel. What is left is S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂, the overall reaction. Fe²⁺ is used then re-formed, so it is a catalyst.

### Worked example 2: oxidation states in the Contact process

State the oxidation state of vanadium in V₂O₅ and V₂O₄. Oxygen is –2. In V₂O₅: 2V + 5(–2) = 0, so V = +5. In V₂O₄: 2V + 4(–2) = 0, so V = +4.

### Worked example 3: explain a slow start

A mixture of acidified potassium manganate(VII) and ethanedioic acid decolourises slowly at first and then quickly. Explain.

The reaction produces Mn²⁺ ions, which catalyse it. Initially there is no Mn²⁺ so the reaction is slow (reactants repel). As Mn²⁺ forms, the rate increases. This is autocatalysis.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a catalyst "takes part in the reaction and is unchanged" without stating that it is regenerated.
- Saying a catalyst changes the position of equilibrium or the enthalpy change.
- Confusing homogeneous and heterogeneous: homogeneous means same phase as the reactants.
- Forgetting that the poison blocks active sites.
- Writing the catalyst equations without balancing, or without cancelling to give the overall equation.
- Missing the reason the uncatalysed S₂O₈²⁻ and I⁻ reaction is slow: ions of the same charge repel.
- Giving Mn³⁺ as a product in the overall equation. It is an intermediate.
- Saying a support makes the catalyst "stronger". It increases the surface area and reduces cost.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain with the aid of equations**: write each step, with oxidation states of the metal, then show that the steps add up to the overall equation.
- **Explain why poisoning increases cost**: the catalyst must be replaced or regenerated, or the feedstock purified.
- **Distinguish between**: state the phase of the catalyst relative to the reactants, then give an example of each.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the support reduce cost?**
Only a thin layer of the expensive metal is needed, because the support provides the large surface area.

**Why can iron(III) also act as the catalyst for the S₂O₈²⁻ and I⁻ reaction?**
Iron(III) reacts first with iodide, forming iron(II) and iodine, and iron(II) then reacts with persulfate, regenerating iron(III).

**What is the difference between a catalyst and a reactant?**
A catalyst is regenerated and appears in the mechanism but not in the overall equation.
