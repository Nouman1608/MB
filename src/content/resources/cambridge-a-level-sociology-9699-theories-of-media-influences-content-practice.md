This set covers section 9.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: pluralist,
Marxist, neo-Marxist and postmodernist theories, influences on news, censorship and the media's
influence on politics. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations and
essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *hegemony*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what pluralists mean by *consumer sovereignty*. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the political economy approach differs from the manipulative Marxist approach.
**[3]**

**4.** Identify **three** news values. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what Hall and colleagues meant by *primary definers*. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish between agenda setting and framing. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by *self-censorship*, with an example. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **one** way in which opinion polls may influence the political process. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** factors other than owners' views that influence the selection of news.
**[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** contributions postmodernism makes to understanding the media. **[8]**

**11.** "Media content is best understood through the concept of hegemony rather than mass
manipulation." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Pluralist theory provides an outdated account of influences on media content." Evaluate
this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** The dominance of ruling-class ideas [1] achieved through consent, so that they seem like
common sense, rather than through force (Gramsci) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The idea that audiences' choices determine what is produced [1], because outlets that fail
to give audiences what they want lose readers, viewers and advertising [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The manipulative approach says owners deliberately shape content to serve their interests
[1]. The political economy approach (Golding and Murdock) says economic pressures, such as
dependence on advertising and the need to cut costs, narrow content [1] without owners needing to
give any orders [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any three: negativity; elite persons; unexpectedness; proximity; personalisation; conflict;
celebrity; entertainment; shareability; magnitude [1 each]. **[3]**

**5.** Powerful, accredited sources such as police, politicians and officials [1] whom journalists
rely on for information [1]; their interpretation of an event becomes the starting point for
coverage, so later voices must respond to their framing [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Agenda setting is influencing which issues people think about [1] by choosing what to cover
and how prominently [1]. Framing is influencing how people think about an issue [1] by emphasising
certain causes, consequences or solutions [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Journalists or editors avoid content because they expect it to cause trouble with owners,
advertisers, the state or the law [1], so no formal ban is needed [1]; for example, a reporter
dropping a story that criticises a major advertiser or that risks a defamation claim [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Identify [1], explain [1], develop [1]: a **bandwagon effect** [1], where polls showing one
party ahead encourage undecided voters to support it [1]; coverage may also focus on who is
winning rather than on policies, narrowing debate [1]. Also creditable: the underdog effect;
polls affecting party strategy or turnout. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per factor: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study or concept [1],
example or consequence [1].
- **News values** [1]: events with negativity, elite persons or celebrity are more likely to be
  reported [1]; Galtung and Ruge (1965), updated by Harcup and O'Neill [1]; so coverage
  over-represents dramatic events and under-represents slow-moving issues [1].
- **Time and budget pressure** [1]: newsrooms with fewer staff rely on press releases and agency
  copy [1]; Davies (2008) called this churnalism [1]; organisations with strong public-relations
  teams gain more coverage [1].
- Also creditable: reliance on primary definers; gatekeeping by editors (White, 1950); advertiser
  pressure; practical constraints such as location of reporters. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per contribution: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evaluate or
example [1].
- **Hyperreality** [1]: Baudrillard argued media images become more real to audiences than
  reality itself [1]; his *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1991) argued that for Western viewers
  the war existed mainly as televised spectacle [1]; critics say this underplays real suffering
  and economic power [1].
- **Fragmentation of audiences and meanings** [1]: media-saturated societies offer so many
  sources that no single ideology dominates [1]; audiences construct their own meanings and
  identities [1]; this challenges both pluralist and Marxist models of a single audience [1].
- Also creditable: blurring of high and popular culture; the media as a source of identity
  rather than information. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level defines both concepts precisely, applies evidence on news
production and reaches a reasoned judgement. Indicative content:
- **Hegemony:** Gramsci; Glasgow University Media Group (*Bad News*, 1976, and later studies) on
  industrial disputes; Hall et al. (1978) on primary definers; journalists' shared backgrounds and
  assumptions; hegemony must be maintained, allowing room for resistance.
- **Mass manipulation:** Miliband; Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model (1988); Harold Evans's
  resignation from *The Times* (1982) after disputes with Murdoch.
- **Political economy as a middle position:** Golding and Murdock; advertising and cost pressures.
- **Pluralist and postmodernist challenges:** audience choice, professional independence,
  fragmentation; both reject a single dominant ideology.
- **Evaluation:** hegemony explains routine bias without proof of orders, but is hard to test;
  manipulation explains particular cases but not everyday newsroom practice.
- **Judgement:** for example, hegemony explains most content, manipulation explains some
  high-profile interventions. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level sets pluralism against Marxist, political economy and
postmodern criticisms and considers whether new media revive or weaken it. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** concentration of ownership; dependence on advertising; churnalism (Davies,
  2008); primary definers; algorithms ranking content for profit; audiences choose only from what
  is offered.
- **Against the view:** public-service broadcasting (Reith's aim to "inform, educate and
  entertain"); professional norms and editorial independence; regulation; new media and
  user-generated content increase diversity of voices; audience choice drives many decisions.
- **Postmodernist angle:** fragmentation supports pluralist claims of diversity but rejects the
  idea of a mirror of reality.
- **Evaluation:** pluralism may describe some sectors and countries better than others; new media
  increase producers but attention remains concentrated.
- **Judgement:** for example, pluralism is incomplete rather than outdated, because it identifies
  real limits on owners' power but ignores structural pressure. **[35]**
