## Questions

**1.** Define the term "corporate objective". **[2]**

**2.** Pelhurst Joinery (figures in £000) has operating profit of 96, total equity of 420 and non-current liabilities of 180. Last year its ROCE was 14.4% and its gearing was 20.0%.

**(a)** Calculate this year's ROCE. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate this year's gearing. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse what the changes in ROCE and gearing suggest about Pelhurst's position. **[4]**

**3.** Pelhurst's revenue is 840, cost of sales 510, receivables 84, inventories 102 and payables 62 (all £000).

**(a)** Calculate receivables days. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate payables days, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate inventory turnover. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one limitation of using ratios to assess a business. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how a strategy of selling in overseas markets could affect two functional areas of a business. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse one advantage and one disadvantage of using Kaplan and Norton's Balanced Scorecard instead of financial ratios alone. **[4]**

**6.** Merrion Foods makes chilled ready meals. Two suppliers provide 70% of its chicken. One supermarket buys 60% of its output and has threatened to switch. A new rival has just opened a factory nearby. Using Porter's five forces, analyse the pressures on Merrion Foods' profits. **[6]**

**7.** Wilbrook Hotels is a UK chain. 40% of its guests come from the euro area. The pound has weakened against the euro, UK interest rates have risen and UK inflation is 4%. Analyse how these changes may affect Wilbrook. **[6]**

**8.** Darnell plc is considering cutting its use of plastic packaging at extra cost to its shareholders. Evaluate whether a plc should put shareholders' interests before those of other stakeholders. **[6]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which tougher environmental legislation could affect a manufacturer. **[4]**

**10.** Vexhall Cycles will buy a machine for £250,000. Expected net cash flows are £50,000, £90,000, £120,000 and £100,000 in years 1 to 4. The discount rate is 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the average rate of return. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the net present value. **[4]**

**11.** Vexhall's finance manager repeats the calculation with every cash flow 15% lower.

**(a)** State what this technique is called and what the new NPV is. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify whether Vexhall should buy the machine. **[6]**

**12.** Explain one reason for greater globalisation and one way in which technological change could affect a retailer's marketing. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A corporate objective is a goal [1] set for the whole business, which should be measurable (for example a growth or return target) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns more with a qualifier such as "whole business" or "measurable" than with a bare "aim"; an example alone rarely defines.

**2. (a)** Capital employed = 420 + 180 = 600 [1]. ROCE = 96 / 600 x 100 [1] = **16.0%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Gearing = 180 / 600 x 100 [1] = **30.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** ROCE rose from 14.4% to 16.0%, so profitability improved [1]. Gearing rose from 20.0% to 30.0%, so more of the funding is long-term debt [1]. The rise in profit may have been helped by borrowing to invest [1]. Higher gearing means more interest and risk if profits fall, so the improvement may not be secure; more data such as cash flow would help [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs both movements linked to each other with a "so" or "because"; quoting the numbers without a consequence usually stays at description.

**3. (a)** 84 / 840 x 365 [1] = **36.5 days** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 62 / 510 x 365 [1] = **44.4 days** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 510 / 102 [1] = **5.0 times** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Ratios use past data, so they may not predict the future [1]; or they ignore non-financial factors or depend on accounting choices; or they need a benchmark, because a figure means little alone (any one, developed) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Payables days use cost of sales, not revenue; using the wrong base is a common slip. A limitation needs a reason it matters, not just a label.

**4.** Marketing would need research and promotion suited to overseas customers [1], for example language and local tastes [1]. Operations might need extra capacity or distribution to meet overseas orders [1], raising costs or requiring investment [1]. (Other functions, such as finance raising funds or HR recruiting language skills, are acceptable.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming two functions is not enough; each needs a specific effect that comes from the overseas strategy rather than a general statement of what the function does.

**5.** Advantage: it balances financial results with customer, internal process and learning and growth measures [1], so it shows drivers of future performance that profit ratios miss [1]. Disadvantage: it is harder and slower to design and keep up to date, and deciding which measures to weight is subjective [1], so managers may be unsure which matters most when measures conflict [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Instead of financial ratios alone" asks for a comparison, so tie each point back to what ratios cannot show or do.

**6.** Supplier power is high because two suppliers provide 70% of chicken [1], so they can raise prices and cut Merrion's margin [1]. Buyer power is high because one supermarket takes 60% of output and could switch [1], so it can push prices down [1]. Rivalry or entry threat: the new factory adds capacity [1], which may lead to price cutting or promotion spending that reduces profit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use the evidence in the stem for each force and finish with the effect on profit; listing all five forces with no context gains little.

**7.** A weaker pound makes the UK cheaper for euro-area guests [1], which may raise demand and revenue from the 40% of guests [1]. Imported supplies may cost more [1], and inflation of 4% raises other costs [1]. Higher interest rates raise borrowing costs, especially if the chain is highly geared [1], and may cut UK customers' spending [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer shows both an opportunity (the exchange rate) and threats; saying which effect is likely to be bigger earns credit for judgement.

**8.** For prioritising shareholders: they own the business and bear risk, and returns attract investment [1]; extra packaging costs cut profit and dividends [1]. Against: the stakeholder concept says employees, customers and the community are also affected [1], and better environmental practice can build reputation and long-term sales [1]. Judgement: a plc should weigh both, as long-term profit often depends on stakeholders, so it should go ahead if the cost is manageable and customer response is likely to be positive [1]. Justification refers to the stem, for example the cost to shareholders against likely reputation gains [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a decision with a reason; stating both views and stopping leaves the final mark out of reach.

**9.** Costs: it may have to buy cleaner equipment or pay for waste treatment [1], raising costs and reducing profit unless prices rise [1]. Strategy and operations: it may redesign products or processes to use less material and cut emissions [1], which could create an environmental selling point in marketing [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs an effect on the business; describing what the law says earns little.

**10. (a)** After year 3 cumulative cash flow is 260,000, so the cost is recovered in year 3 [1]. After year 2 it is 140,000, leaving 110,000 [1]. Payback = 2 + 110/120 = **2.92 years** (about 2 years 11 months) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Total returns 360,000, so net return 110,000 [1]. Average annual return 110,000 / 4 = 27,500 [1]. ARR = 27,500 / 250,000 x 100 = **11.0%** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Present values: 50,000/1.1 = 45,450; 90,000/1.21 = 74,380 [1]; 120,000/1.331 = 90,160; 100,000/1.4641 = 68,300 [1]. Total = 278,290 [1]. NPV = 278,290 - 250,000 = **+£28,290** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the discounting line by line; the cost must be subtracted, and giving the present value total as the NPV is a frequent mistake.

**11. (a)** Sensitivity analysis [1]. New NPV: 0.85 x 278,290 = 236,550, less 250,000 = **-£13,450** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For: NPV is positive (+£28,290) at the forecast [1]; payback of 2.92 years is within the four-year life [1]; ARR of 11.0% may exceed a target return [1]. Against: the result is sensitive, since a fall of just over 10% in cash flows makes NPV negative [1]; payback is long, so risk is high [1]. Judgement: buy only if the manager believes the forecasts are reliable and non-financial factors, such as product quality, support it; otherwise gather more information first [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A justification must reach a decision and give a reason using the calculated figures; quoting results without a view is not enough.

**12.** Globalisation: lower transport and communication costs (or freer trade) make it easier to sell and source across borders [1], so firms can reach larger markets [1]. Technology: online channels and customer data let the retailer target advertising at specific groups [1], for example by sending offers based on past purchases [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each reason with an explained consequence for the business; naming "the internet" or "trade" alone is not an explanation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a ratio with no comparison, so there is nothing to analyse.
- Using revenue instead of cost of sales for payables days and inventory turnover.
- Forgetting that capital employed is equity plus non-current liabilities.
- Applying Porter's forces as a list rather than to the data in the stem.
- Writing about an economic change without saying who it helps or hurts.
- Not subtracting the initial cost in NPV, or discounting the wrong year.
- Stopping an evaluation at "it depends" with no decision.
