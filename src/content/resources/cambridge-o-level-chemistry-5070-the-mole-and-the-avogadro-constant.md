Subtopic 3.3 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 is the calculation engine of the whole course. It has eight learning outcomes, starting with the statement that "the mole, mol, is the unit of amount of substance and that one mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles", and ending with percentage yield, percentage composition by mass and percentage purity. Because 5070 has no Core/Extended split, every one of these calculations is expected of every candidate.

This guide works through each outcome with fresh worked examples. For the formulae and relative masses that feed into these calculations, see the [Formulae, Equations and the Mole study guide](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/). For quick recall, use the [mole revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-the-mole-and-the-avogadro-constant-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [mole practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-the-mole-and-the-avogadro-constant-practice/).

## What the subtopic asks you to do

The eight outcomes fall into four groups:

- **Counting particles (outcome 1):** the mole as the unit of amount, and the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 10²³.
- **The core relationship (outcome 2):** amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol), used to find amount, mass, molar mass, relative atomic or molecular/formula mass, and number of particles.
- **Gases and solutions (outcomes 3 to 6):** the molar gas volume of 24 dm³ at r.t.p., concentration in g/dm³ and mol/dm³, reacting quantities, limiting reactants and titration results.
- **Formulae and percentages (outcomes 7 and 8):** empirical and molecular formulae from data, then percentage yield, composition and purity.

## The mole and what it counts

A mole is a fixed number of particles, in the same way a dozen is twelve of anything. The number is the **Avogadro constant**, 6.02 × 10²³. The particles can be atoms, ions or molecules, so a question must make clear which it means, and so must your answer.

Take 0.500 mol of ammonia, NH₃:

- molecules = 0.500 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **3.01 × 10²³ molecules**
- each molecule has four atoms, so atoms = 4 × 3.01 × 10²³ = **1.20 × 10²⁴ atoms**

The same care applies to ionic compounds. One mole of calcium chloride, CaCl₂, contains one mole of Ca²⁺ ions and **two** moles of Cl⁻ ions, so three moles of ions in total.

## One relationship, five uses

Outcome 2 lists five things you can calculate from one equation. The **molar mass** of a substance is its Ar or Mr expressed in g/mol, so Mr of CO₂ = 44 means its molar mass is 44 g/mol.

| To find | Rearrangement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| (a) amount | mol = mass ÷ molar mass | 11.0 g CO₂ ÷ 44 g/mol = 0.250 mol |
| (b) mass | mass = mol × molar mass | 0.150 mol NaOH × 40 g/mol = 6.00 g |
| (c) molar mass | molar mass = mass ÷ mol | 9.80 g ÷ 0.100 mol = 98 g/mol |
| (d) Ar or Mr | same as (c), then drop the units | 3.20 g of a metal is 0.0500 mol, so Ar = 64 (copper) |
| (e) particles | particles = mol × 6.02 × 10²³ | 0.250 mol CO₂ = 1.51 × 10²³ molecules |

Part (d) is the one candidates forget exists: if a question gives you the mass and the amount of an unknown element, dividing gives its Ar, and the Periodic Table identifies it.

## Gases: the molar gas volume

One mole of **any** gas occupies 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.). This does not depend on which gas it is, so 0.100 mol of hydrogen and 0.100 mol of carbon dioxide both occupy 2.40 dm³, even though their masses are very different.

- volume (dm³) = mol × 24
- volume (cm³) = mol × 24 000

A useful consequence: for reacting gases at the same temperature and pressure, **volume ratios equal mole ratios**. In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, 30 cm³ of nitrogen reacts with 90 cm³ of hydrogen to give 60 cm³ of ammonia, with no conversion to moles needed.

## Solutions: two units of concentration

Outcome 4 says concentration can be measured in **g/dm³** or **mol/dm³**. The two are linked by the molar mass:

- g/dm³ = mol/dm³ × molar mass
- mol/dm³ = g/dm³ ÷ molar mass

So 0.250 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide (Mr 40) contains 0.250 × 40 = 10.0 g/dm³. Volumes must be in dm³ before you use them: divide cm³ by 1000.

## Worked example 1: a limiting reactant

4.80 g of magnesium is added to 100 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid.

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Find the limiting reactant, the volume of hydrogen at r.t.p. and the mass of the reactant left over.

1. Moles of Mg = 4.80 ÷ 24 = 0.200 mol.
2. Moles of HCl = 1.00 × (100 ÷ 1000) = 0.100 mol.
3. Compare with the equation: 0.200 mol Mg would need 0.400 mol HCl. Only 0.100 mol is present, so **hydrochloric acid is limiting** and magnesium is in excess.
4. Base every product calculation on the limiting reactant: 0.100 mol HCl gives 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500 mol H₂.
5. Volume of H₂ = 0.0500 × 24 = **1.20 dm³** (1200 cm³).
6. Mg used = 0.0500 mol = 0.0500 × 24 = 1.20 g, so Mg left over = 4.80 − 1.20 = **3.60 g**.

The trap is step 3. Comparing 0.200 with 0.100 directly and declaring HCl limiting gets the right answer by luck; you must compare each amount with what the **equation** requires.

## Worked example 2: a titration result

Outcome 6 asks you to use experimental data. A student titrates 25.0 cm³ of sodium carbonate solution with 0.150 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid and records titres of 22.10 cm³ (rough), 21.65 cm³ and 21.55 cm³.

Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂

1. Use concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other), not the rough one: mean = (21.65 + 21.55) ÷ 2 = 21.60 cm³.
2. Moles of HCl = 0.150 × (21.60 ÷ 1000) = 3.24 × 10⁻³ mol.
3. Ratio Na₂CO₃ : HCl is 1 : 2, so moles of Na₂CO₃ = 1.62 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Concentration = 1.62 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0648 mol/dm³**.
5. In g/dm³ (Mr of Na₂CO₃ = 106): 0.0648 × 106 = **6.87 g/dm³**.

## Empirical and molecular formulae

An empirical formula comes from the **mole ratio** of elements, never the mass ratio. A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass, and its Mr is 56.

| | C | H |
|---|---|---|
| Mass in 100 g | 85.7 | 14.3 |
| ÷ Ar | 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14 | 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 2.00 |

Empirical formula = CH₂, with formula mass 14. Then 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is **C₄H₈**. If a ratio comes out as 1.5 or 1.33, multiply every value by 2 or 3; do not round 1.5 to 2.

## The three percentages

**Percentage composition by mass** uses only the formula. Nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (Mr 80): there are two N atoms, so % N = (2 × 14) ÷ 80 × 100 = **35.0%**. Forgetting the second nitrogen atom gives 17.5%, a common wrong answer.

**Percentage yield** compares what you made with what the equation predicts:

percentage yield = actual mass ÷ theoretical mass × 100

8.00 g of copper(II) oxide (Mr 80) reacts with excess sulfuric acid: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O. 0.100 mol CuO gives 0.100 mol CuSO₄ (Mr 160), a theoretical 16.0 g. If 13.6 g of dry anhydrous salt is obtained, yield = 13.6 ÷ 16.0 × 100 = **85.0%**.

**Percentage purity** compares the mass of the pure substance in a sample with the mass of the whole sample. 2.50 g of impure calcium carbonate reacts with excess acid and gives 288 cm³ of CO₂ at r.t.p. Moles of CO₂ = 288 ÷ 24 000 = 0.0120 mol, so 0.0120 mol CaCO₃ (Mr 100) = 1.20 g were present. Purity = 1.20 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **48.0%**.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the coefficient as a multiplier on mass.** Equation coefficients are mole ratios. Convert to moles first, apply the ratio, then convert back.
- **Leaving volumes in cm³.** Concentration and gas calculations need dm³, or 24 000 cm³ per mole.
- **Stating particles without saying which.** "3.01 × 10²³" scores only if you say molecules, atoms or ions as the question requires.
- **Using the rough titre** in the mean, or averaging titres that are not concordant.
- **Basing the product on the excess reactant** in a limiting-reactant question.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep at least four significant figures in working and give the answer to three, matching the data.

## Exam technique for 5070 command words

- **Calculate:** show each step with units. Method marks are often awarded even if the final figure is wrong, but only if the working is visible.
- **Deduce:** you are expected to reach a conclusion from data, such as the identity of a metal from its Ar or a molecular formula from Mr; state the conclusion clearly at the end.
- **Determine:** usually several linked steps; set them out as numbered lines as in the worked examples above.
- **State:** a single value or fact, such as "24 dm³ at r.t.p.", with no working needed.
