This guide covers **section 3.1.2 Education**, Section B of Paper 1 (Introducing Sociology) in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). Section B is worth **22 of the paper's 60 marks**, through short and extended response questions. The [Introducing Sociology guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/) outlines all of Paper 1 and summarises global education; this page goes deeper into the rest of education, with the studies and examples you need.

## What section 3.1.2 covers

- **3.1.2.1 Education and schooling**: formal and informal education; official and hidden curriculum; levels of education; types of school; online learning.
- **3.1.2.2 Perspectives**: functionalism (role allocation, shared values, human capital, the bridge from particularistic to universalistic values); Marxism (schooling for different classes, reproducing and legitimising class, preparation for work, the myth of meritocracy); feminisms (shaping gender roles and expectations).
- **3.1.2.3 Relationships and processes in schools**: school ethos and curricula (ethnocentrism, vocational education, unequal access to knowledge); interactionism; behaviour and sanctions; labelling, the self-fulfilling prophecy, stereotyping, ideal pupils and the halo effect; pupil subcultures; racism and sexism.
- **3.1.2.4 Educational attainment**: measuring attainment through examinations and IQ tests; credentialism; differences by class, gender and ethnicity and their intersections; material, cultural, home and in-school explanations.
- **3.1.2.5 Global education**: literacy rates, differences in provision, poverty, gender and other inequalities in access, and digital inequality.

The named sociologists are **Becker, Bourdieu, Davis and Moore, Freire, Illich and Samson**; other sociologists are credited where used appropriately.

## Education and schooling

**Formal education** takes place in institutions with a planned curriculum and qualifications; **informal education** happens through everyday life, from families, peers and the media. Levels run from **nursery or pre-school** through **primary** and **secondary** to **tertiary** (colleges and universities).

| School type | Key feature | Sociological issue |
|---|---|---|
| State or public | Funded by government, usually free | Quality may depend on area and funding |
| Private | Fee-paying | Buys advantage for wealthier families |
| Selective | Admits by ability test | May favour pupils with more cultural capital |
| Faith | Organised around a religion | Shapes values; may separate communities |
| International | Often teaches in English with an international curriculum | Serves globally mobile and wealthier families |
| Boarding | Pupils live at school | Intensive socialisation |
| Progressive | Pupil-led, fewer formal rules | Challenges traditional hidden curriculum |
| Vocational | Trains for specific jobs | Often lower status than academic routes |

**Online learning** widens access for some, but depends on devices and connection, and reduces the social side of schooling.

## Perspectives on education

**Functionalism.** **Durkheim** saw schools as passing on **shared values** that bind society together. **Parsons** saw school as a **bridge** between the family, where a child is judged by **particularistic** standards (as a unique individual), and wider society, where people are judged by **universalistic** standards that apply to everyone. **Davis and Moore** argued that education performs **role allocation**, sorting the most able into the most important jobs. Education also develops **human capital**, the skills an economy needs.

**Marxism.** **Bowles and Gintis** argued that schooling **prepares pupils for work** under capitalism through a correspondence between school and workplace: obedience to teachers mirrors obedience to bosses. They called the idea that success depends only on ability the **myth of meritocracy**. **Bourdieu** argued that middle-class pupils bring **cultural capital** (knowledge, language and tastes valued by schools), so schools **reproduce** class inequality and **legitimise** it by making it look like the result of individual merit.

**Radical critics.** **Illich** argued that schools teach people to depend on institutions and confuse being taught with learning, and called for **deschooling**. **Freire** criticised **"banking" education**, where teachers deposit knowledge in passive pupils, and called for education that develops critical consciousness.

**Feminisms.** Schools can reinforce gender roles through subject choices, careers advice, textbooks and teacher expectations. In the 1970s **Sue Sharpe** found that girls' priorities centred on marriage and family; when she repeated the study in the 1990s their priorities had shifted towards careers.

## Relationships and processes in schools

**Interactionism** studies the **micro level**: everyday interaction between teachers and pupils.

- **Labelling**: a teacher judges a pupil, such as "bright" or "troublemaker". **Becker** found that teachers judged pupils against an image of the **ideal pupil**, which middle-class pupils fitted most closely.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy**: the label shapes how the pupil is treated, the pupil internalises it, and it comes true. **Rosenthal and Jacobson**'s field experiment, in which teachers were told that randomly chosen pupils were about to "spurt", found many of those pupils made greater gains.
- **Stereotyping**: judging a pupil by assumptions about their group. **Halo effect**: one positive impression colours how the teacher sees the pupil in general.
- **Behaviour and sanctions**: **positive** sanctions (praise, merits, prizes) and **negative** sanctions (detentions, exclusion).

**Pupil subcultures**: **pro-school** subcultures value achievement; **anti-school** subcultures reject the school's values. **Willis** studied a group of working-class "lads" who rejected school and saw schoolwork as pointless, which led them into manual jobs.

**School ethos and curricula**: an **ethnocentric** curriculum centres one culture's history, language and literature; vocational tracks can steer some groups away from academic knowledge, creating **unequal access to knowledge**. **Racism and sexism** in schools can work through labelling, low expectations and harsher sanctions.

## Educational attainment

**Measuring attainment.** Examinations reward particular skills on particular days; **IQ tests** have been criticised for cultural bias. **Credentialism** is the growing demand for qualifications for jobs that did not previously need them, which can push people to stay longer in education.

**Explaining differences:**

| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Material** | Poverty means less money for books, devices, tuition and quiet study space |
| **Cultural** | Cultural capital, language and attitudes to education differ (Bourdieu) |
| **Home and community** | Parents' education, expectations and support; local job opportunities |
| **In-school** | Labelling, setting and streaming, subcultures, ethnocentric curricula |

Differences by **social class**, **gender** and **ethnicity** **intersect**: for example, a working-class boy from a minority ethnic group may face several disadvantages at once.

## Global education

The specification asks you to compare **literacy rates**, **provision** (free and compulsory years, class sizes, trained teachers), the **effects of poverty**, **gender and other inequalities in access** and the **digital divide**. **Malala Yousafzai**, shot by the Taliban in Pakistan in 2012 for campaigning for girls' education, is a widely known example of barriers to girls' schooling.

## Worked example: applying labelling

*Explain how teacher expectations can affect a pupil's attainment. [4 marks]*

> A teacher may label a pupil as less able because of their accent or background (1). The teacher may then give them easier work and less attention (1). The pupil may come to see themselves as less able and stop trying (1), so their low attainment fulfils the original expectation: a self-fulfilling prophecy (1).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the hidden curriculum with informal education.
- Describing Bourdieu's cultural capital as money.
- Saying labelling always works; pupils can reject labels.
- Explaining attainment gaps only by in-school factors, without material or cultural ones.
- Forgetting to apply points to a society you know when asked.
