## Section A

**1.** Which change to a gene is most likely to alter every amino acid coded for after the point of the mutation?

A: Substitution of the third base of a codon

B: Deletion of one base

C: Deletion of three adjacent bases

D: Substitution that creates a codon for the same amino acid

**[1]**

**2.** Define single-nucleotide polymorphism and explain why a SNP in a coding region may have no effect on the polypeptide. **[3]**

**3.** State one chemical mutagen and one mutagenic form of radiation, and outline how the radiation damages DNA. **[3]**

**4.** A student writes: "Bacteria mutate in order to become resistant when they are exposed to an antibiotic." Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why only mutation, and not meiosis, can produce a new allele. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** An mRNA reads 5' AUG GCU UGG AAA CAG UAA 3'. Codons you need: AUG Met; GCU and GCC Ala; UGG Trp; AAA Lys; GAA Glu; CAG Gln; AGC Ser; UUG Leu; ACA Thr; GUA Val; UAA and UAG stop.

**(a)** Give the amino acid sequence of the polypeptide. **[1]**

**(b)** Classify each substitution in the mRNA and state its effect: GCU to GCC; UGG to UAG; AAA to GAA. **[3]**

**(c)** An extra A is inserted immediately after the start codon. Give the new amino acid sequence for the next five codons and name this type of effect. **[2]**

**7.** The sixth codon of the beta-globin gene is GAG on the coding strand in most people and GTG in the sickle cell allele. GAG and GAA code for glutamic acid; GUG codes for valine.

**(a)** Explain how this single base change leads to the symptoms of sickle cell anaemia. **[4]**

**(b)** Predict, with a reason, the effect of a mutation that changed this codon from GAG to GAA on the coding strand. **[2]**

**8.** Bacteria were spread on a plate with no antibiotic and grew into about 200 colonies. A sterile velvet pad was pressed onto the plate and then onto three fresh plates containing antibiotic. On each antibiotic plate, four colonies grew, in the same four positions on all three plates.

**(a)** Explain what the matching positions show about when the resistance mutations occurred. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how this supports the idea that mutation is random. **[2]**

**9.** Samples of yeast were exposed to different doses of ultraviolet light and the number of mutant colonies per million surviving cells was counted.

| UV dose / J m⁻² | 0 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mutants per 10⁶ surviving cells | 2 | 9 | 21 | 46 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in mutants between 0 and 40 J m⁻². **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the relationship shown. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain the relationship in terms of the effect of ultraviolet light on DNA. **[2]**

**(d)** Suggest why mutants still appear at a dose of 0. **[1]**

**10.** A woman has a skin tumour caused by mutations in her skin cells after years of sun exposure. Her brother carries an inherited mutation in a gene that controls the cell cycle.

**(a)** Explain why the woman's mutations will not be passed to her children. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the brother's mutation is present in every cell of his body. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why the brother has a higher risk of cancer than a person without the inherited mutation. **[2]**

**11.** (HL only) *Arabidopsis* plants with gene K knocked out flowered after a mean of 38 days; normal plants flowered after a mean of 24 days. Both groups were grown in the same conditions.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in time to flowering, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**(b)** State what the result suggests about gene K and give one reason for caution. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how CRISPR-Cas9 could be used to knock out gene K. **[3]**

**12.** (HL only) A guide RNA must pair with a DNA target strand that reads 3' TACGGATCCA 5'.

**(a)** Deduce the base sequence of the guide RNA, giving it 5' to 3'. **[2]**

**(b)** Distinguish between editing body cells of a patient and editing a human embryo, in terms of ethics. **[3]**

**13.** (HL only) Two genes, P and Q, were compared between two distantly related species. Each gene's coding sequence is about 300 bases.

| Gene | Amino acid differences | Silent base differences |
|---|---|---|
| P | 1 | 31 |
| Q | 1 | 2 |

**(a)** State what is meant by a highly conserved sequence. **[1]**

**(b)** Deduce which hypothesis for conservation each gene supports. Explain your reasoning. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. A one-base deletion shifts the reading frame, so every later codon changes. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**2.** A SNP is a position in the genome where a single base differs between individuals in a population [1]. It arises from a base substitution [1]. The genetic code is degenerate, so the new codon may code for the same amino acid, leaving the polypeptide unchanged [1]. *(Total: 3 marks)*

**3.** Chemical: benzo[a]pyrene from tobacco smoke, or nitrous acid [1]. Radiation: ultraviolet, X-rays or gamma rays [1]. Ultraviolet links adjacent thymine bases on the same strand (thymine dimers), or X-rays and gamma rays break the sugar-phosphate backbone [1]. *(Total: 3 marks)*

**4.** Mutations are random and are not caused by the need for resistance [1]. Resistant mutants were already present before exposure; the antibiotic kills the others, so it selects resistant bacteria rather than producing them [1]. *(Total: 2 marks)*

**5.** A new allele requires a change in base sequence, which is what a mutation is [1]. Meiosis (crossing over and independent assortment) only produces new combinations of alleles that already exist [1]. *(Total: 2 marks)*

**6. (a)** Met-Ala-Trp-Lys-Gln [1] (then stop).

**(b)** GCU to GCC: still Ala, a silent substitution with no change to the polypeptide [1]. UGG to UAG: a stop codon, a nonsense substitution giving a shortened polypeptide (Met-Ala only) [1]. AAA to GAA: Lys replaced by Glu, a missense substitution that may alter the protein's structure [1].

**(c)** AUG AGC UUG GAA ACA GUA: Met-Ser-Leu-Glu-Thr-Val [1]; a frameshift [1]. *(Total: 6 marks)*
*Examiner insight:* rewrite the sequence in threes after the insertion before translating; reading the old codon boundaries is the usual slip.

**7. (a)** The mRNA codon changes from GAG to GUG, so glutamic acid is replaced by valine [1]. Valine is non-polar and hydrophobic whereas glutamic acid is charged [1]. At low oxygen concentration the haemoglobin molecules stick together and form long fibres [1]. Red blood cells become rigid and sickle-shaped, block capillaries and are destroyed early, causing anaemia [1].

**(b)** No effect on the polypeptide [1], because GAA also codes for glutamic acid, so this would be a silent mutation [1]. *(Total: 6 marks)*

**8. (a)** The velvet transfers cells from the same positions on the original plate to each new plate [1]. Resistant colonies at the same positions on all three plates must have come from the same original colonies [1], so the resistant cells were present on the original plate, before any exposure to antibiotic [1].

**(b)** The mutations arose without the antibiotic being present, so they were not caused by or directed towards the need for resistance [1]. The antibiotic only selected mutants that had already arisen by chance [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

**9. (a)** (46 − 2) ÷ 2 × 100 [1] = 2200 % [1].

**(b)** Mutant numbers increase as UV dose increases, a positive correlation [1]; data quoted, such as 9 at 10 J m⁻² rising to 46 at 40 J m⁻², an average of about 1.1 extra mutants per 10⁶ cells for each J m⁻² [1].

**(c)** Ultraviolet light causes thymine dimers, covalently linking adjacent thymine bases [1]. A higher dose produces more dimers, so more errors occur when the DNA is copied or repaired [1].

**(d)** Mutations also occur from uncorrected errors in replication or repair, or from other mutagens [1]. *(Total: 7 marks)*

**10. (a)** Skin cells are somatic cells [1]; they do not give rise to gametes, so the mutations are passed only to descendant skin cells by mitosis, not to offspring [1].

**(b)** The mutation was present in a gamete from one of his parents [1], so it was in the zygote and was copied into every cell made from it by mitosis [1].

**(c)** Cancer usually requires several mutations in cell cycle genes in one cell [1]. Every cell already carries one, so fewer further somatic mutations are needed for uncontrolled division to begin [1]. *(Total: 6 marks)*

**11. (a)** (38 − 24) ÷ 24 × 100 [1] = 58.3 % [1].

**(b)** Gene K is involved in promoting flowering, or its product speeds up flowering [1]. Caution: means alone without spread of data or a statistical test, or other genes may compensate, or the gene may have other roles [1].

**(c)** A guide RNA is designed with a base sequence complementary to part of gene K [1]. It leads Cas9 to that sequence, and Cas9 cuts both strands of the DNA [1]. When the cell rejoins the ends, small insertions or deletions often occur, causing a frameshift so the gene no longer codes for a working product [1]. *(Total: 7 marks)*

**12. (a)** Complementary bases with U in place of T: AUGCCUAGGU [1], written 5' AUGCCUAGGU 3' [1].

**(b)** In body cells, changes affect only the consenting patient [1]. Embryo edits would be present in germ cells and could be inherited by future generations, who cannot consent [1]. Unintended changes in an embryo could therefore spread through a family, so most scientists and regulators treat heritable editing as far less acceptable [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

**13. (a)** A sequence that has stayed identical or very similar over a long period of evolution [1].

**(b)** Gene P: amino acid changes are rare but silent changes are common [1], so mutations do occur but those altering the protein are removed by natural selection, supporting the functional requirement hypothesis [1]. Gene Q: both kinds of difference are rare [1], so few mutations happen at all, supporting the slower mutation rate hypothesis [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Translating codons without re-dividing the sequence into threes after an insertion.
- Explaining sickle cell without linking the amino acid change to a change in haemoglobin properties.
- Saying the antibiotic "caused" resistance.
- Describing relationships without quoting data from the table.
- Forgetting that inherited mutations are in every cell because they were in the zygote.
- (HL) Giving the guide RNA with T instead of U, or without 5' and 3' labels.
- (HL) Discussing conservation with only one hypothesis.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you work out a guide RNA sequence from a DNA strand?**
Write the complementary bases to the target strand, using U instead of T, and keep the 5' and 3' labels antiparallel to the DNA strand.

**Why does replica plating show that mutation is random?**
Resistant colonies appear in the same positions on every replica plate, so the resistant cells were already on the original plate before contact with the antibiotic. The antibiotic selected them; it did not cause the mutations.

**Which parts of D1.3 are HL only?**
Gene knockout, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing and hypotheses for conserved sequences (D1.3.8 to D1.3.10).
