This guide covers section 5.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus wording is a single case study: "candidates must study one international migration stream: its causes, character, scale, pattern and impacts on source areas and receiving/destination areas." It is the final part of Topic 5, Migration, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [5.4 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-management-of-international-migration-revision-notes/) summarise policy tools and outline the Mexico to United States stream. This guide shows how to build any stream into a case study that answers the five headings, and works through a second stream, Turkey to Germany, in full. Test yourself with the [5.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-international-migration-practice/).

## What the case study must contain

The syllabus names five elements. Each one is a different kind of knowledge.

| Element | Question it answers | What to learn |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | Why did the stream start and continue? | Push, pull and enabling factors, including policy |
| Character | Who moves? | Age, sex, skills, legal status, temporary or permanent |
| Scale | How many? | Numbers and how they changed over time |
| Pattern | From where to where, and when? | Source regions, destination regions and cities, phases |
| Impacts | What difference did it make, and to whom? | Effects on both source and destination areas |

The section is titled "management", so a strong case study also covers **how the stream was managed** by governments at each end, and **how well** that management worked. Questions on Paper 2 can ask you to describe, explain or evaluate any of these.

## Choosing a stream

A good stream for the exam:

- is a single, named flow between two countries (or one country and a clear region);
- has data you can remember for at least two dates;
- shows change over time, so you can explain phases;
- has impacts on both ends that you can evaluate.

Common choices include Mexico to the United States, South Asia to the Gulf states, Poland to the UK after 2004, and Turkey to Germany. Your teacher will confirm the stream your class uses. Do not try to learn four streams thinly; learn one well and know one other in outline for comparison.

## Worked case study: Turkey to (West) Germany

### Causes

**Destination pull and policy.** West Germany's rapid post-war growth created a labour shortage, made worse when the Berlin Wall (1961) cut the flow of workers from East Germany. The government signed recruitment agreements with several Mediterranean countries. The agreement with Turkey was signed in October 1961.

**Source push.** Turkey had a fast-growing rural population, under-employment in farming and low wages. The Turkish government saw labour export as a way to reduce unemployment and earn foreign currency through remittances.

**Enabling factors.** Recruitment offices in Turkey selected workers, arranged medical checks and organised transport, so the cost and risk of moving were low for those chosen.

### Character

- **Early phase (1961-1973):** mostly young adults of working age, many of them men from rural Anatolia and other regions, recruited for factory, mining and construction work. Women were also recruited, especially for textile and electronics work.
- **Intended as temporary:** workers were known as *Gastarbeiter* ("guest workers") and were expected to return after a few years.
- **Later phases:** after 1973, family reunification changed the stream to one of wives, husbands and children, and the community became permanent.

### Scale

Recruitment was stopped in November 1973, during the oil crisis and recession. By then the stream had brought several hundred thousand Turkish workers. Numbers did not fall afterwards: family reunification and births in Germany meant the Turkish-origin population kept growing, and today it is commonly estimated at about three million people, the largest group of foreign origin in the country.

### Pattern

- **Source:** recruits came from across Turkey, and as chain migration spread news and contacts, many villages and districts in rural Anatolia sent a large share of their young adults.
- **Destination:** industrial regions and cities with jobs in heavy industry and car manufacturing, such as the Ruhr, Berlin, Cologne, Frankfurt, Stuttgart and Munich. Within cities, migrants clustered in older inner districts with cheap rented housing, such as Kreuzberg in Berlin.
- **Phases:** labour recruitment (1961-1973); family reunification (1970s-1980s); a settled, German-born second and third generation with some return migration.

### Impacts on the source area

- Remittances were an important source of foreign currency for Turkey in the 1960s and 1970s.
- Unemployment pressure in some rural regions was reduced.
- Return migrants invested in houses, shops, transport and small businesses in their home towns, though many investments were in housing rather than productive activity.
- Villages lost young adults, and some households were split for years.

### Impacts on the destination area

- Migrant labour supported the growth of German manufacturing and mining in the 1960s and early 1970s.
- When heavy industry and mining declined, Turkish workers were among the hardest hit by unemployment.
- Residential concentration in inner districts produced lively neighbourhoods and businesses, but also segregation and limited contact with other groups.
- The community faced discrimination and, at times, racist violence. In 1993 an arson attack on a Turkish family's home in Solingen killed five people.
- Turkish-German entrepreneurs created many businesses, and the community changed German food, culture and sport.

### Management and its evaluation

| Policy | Aim | How well did it work? |
|---|---|---|
| Recruitment agreement (1961) | Fill labour gaps with temporary workers | Achieved its labour aim quickly, but the "temporary" assumption proved wrong |
| Recruitment stop (1973) | End new labour migration in recession | Stopped new workers, but encouraged those already in Germany to stay and bring families |
| Return incentives (1983) | Pay workers to leave | Some returned, but most did not; the community was already settled |
| Citizenship reform (in force 2000) | Allow children born in Germany to gain citizenship, under conditions | Improved the status of the second and third generations |
| Integration courses (2005) | Language and orientation courses for newcomers | Recognised that migrants were permanent, decades after settlement began |
| Dual citizenship (2024) | Let people hold German citizenship without giving up another | Removed a long-standing barrier for older Turkish citizens in Germany |

**Overall judgement.** Germany managed the *flow* effectively in the short term but did not manage the *settlement* for many years, because policy was built on the idea that migrants would go home. The recruitment stop shows how a policy can have the opposite effect to its aim. Turkey gained remittances and relief from unemployment, but did not turn returning capital into lasting regional development on a large scale.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: With reference to one international migration stream, evaluate the success of attempts to manage it. [10]*

> West Germany's 1961 agreement with Turkey met its aim of filling labour shortages quickly (**policy and success**). However, it assumed workers would return (**flaw in design**). When recruitment stopped in 1973, many workers stayed and brought their families, so the policy increased permanent settlement (**unintended consequence**). Return incentives in 1983 persuaded only some to leave (**limited success**). Only with citizenship reform in 2000 and integration courses from 2005 did policy accept that the community was permanent (**change of approach**). Management of the flow was therefore successful, but management of its long-term impacts was slow and only partly successful (**judgement that separates flow from settlement**).

## Common mistakes

- Writing a general account of migration with no single named stream.
- Covering only impacts. Causes, character, scale and pattern are equally examinable.
- Giving scale with no numbers or no dates.
- Describing the destination only, when the syllabus asks for both ends.
- Listing policies without saying what each aimed to do and whether it worked.
- Treating a stream as unchanging. Most streams have phases; explain why each phase began.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the character of a stream: who moves, by age, sex, skill and legal status, with numbers where possible.
- **Explain** causes: separate push, pull and enabling factors, and link policy to timing.
- **Evaluate** management: aim, outcome, unintended effects, and a judgement on success for each end of the stream.
- In longer answers, keep the case study to the point of the question. A question on impacts on the source area should not spend half its length on causes.
