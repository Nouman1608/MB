These notes condense content item 1.4.1, The options for start-up and small businesses, from Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Read the [study guide on start-up options](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-options-for-start-up-and-small-businesses/) for worked examples, then try the [start-up options practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-options-for-start-up-and-small-businesses-practice/). The wider [limited liability and location revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-limited-liability-and-business-location-revision-notes/) also cover 1.4.2.

## The three parts of 1.4.1

1. The concept of limited liability: limited and unlimited liability, and the implications for the owner or owners.
2. Types of business ownership for start-ups: sole trader, partnership, private limited company, with advantages and disadvantages.
3. Franchising: the advantages and disadvantages of starting up and running a franchise.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Liability | Responsibility for paying the debts of the business |
| Unlimited liability | The owner is personally responsible for all business debts, so personal possessions can be used to pay them |
| Limited liability | The owner's loss is limited to the amount invested in the business, usually through shares |
| Sole trader | A business owned by one person, who has unlimited liability |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who share profit, decisions and (in an ordinary partnership) unlimited liability |
| Partnership agreement | A written agreement setting out how partners share profit and what happens if one leaves |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | A business owned by shareholders, separate in law from them, whose shares cannot be offered to the general public |
| Shareholder | A part-owner of a company who holds shares in it |
| Franchise | An arrangement allowing one business to trade using another's brand and business format |
| Franchisor | The business that owns the brand and sells franchises |
| Franchisee | The person or business that buys the right to run an outlet under the brand |
| Royalty | An ongoing payment from franchisee to franchisor, often a percentage of sales revenue |

## Implications of liability for the owner

| Unlimited liability | Limited liability |
|---|---|
| Personal savings and possessions at risk | Personal loss capped at the amount invested |
| Owner may avoid borrowing or expanding | Owner more willing to take sensible risks |
| Harder to persuade others to invest | Easier to attract shareholders |
| Simple to set up as a sole trader or partnership | Requires forming a company, with more paperwork |

## Ownership compared

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main advantage | Full control and all the profit | More capital and skills; shared workload | Limited liability; capital from chosen shareholders |
| Main disadvantage | Unlimited liability; limited capital | Unlimited liability; disagreements; shared profit | Paperwork; profit and control shared; shares not sold to the public |
| Best suited to | Low-risk, small-scale start-ups | Owners who need a partner's skills or money | Start-ups with large borrowing or several investors |

## Franchising in one table

| Side | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Franchisee | Known brand; tested format; training and support; national advertising | Initial fee and royalties; little freedom; tied to brand's reputation |
| Franchisor | Fast growth using franchisees' capital; fee and royalty income; motivated owners | Less control over quality; one poor outlet can damage the brand |

## Decision chain for "which option?" questions

1. How much could the owner lose? Large borrowing points to limited liability.
2. How much control does the owner want? A strong wish for independence points to a sole trader.
3. What is missing? A gap in skills or capital points to a partner or shareholders.
4. How much risk can the owner bear with a new idea? A franchise offers a proven model at the cost of fees and freedom.

## Exam traps

- An ordinary partnership does **not** have limited liability.
- Plc is a Theme 2 growth option (2.1.1), not a start-up option in 1.4.1.
- Limited liability protects the **owner**, not the business. A company can still fail.
- "Franchises are less risky" needs a reason: the brand and format are already tested.
- Royalties are paid on sales revenue in many franchises, so they are due even in a month with little profit.
- In Outline and Analyse questions, a point that never mentions the business in the question earns little.

## Quick calculation reminder

Royalties are a percentage calculation: royalty = percentage × sales revenue. For example, a 5% royalty on £84,000 of sales revenue is 0.05 × £84,000 = £4,200. Profit after royalty = profit before royalty − royalty.

## Self-test

1. Define unlimited liability.
2. Which two forms of start-up ownership normally have unlimited liability?
3. State one implication of limited liability for an owner who wants to borrow.
4. Give two advantages of being a sole trader.
5. Give one disadvantage of a partnership that is not about liability.
6. Why might a private limited company find it easier to raise capital than a sole trader?
7. Give one reason a founder might be reluctant to sell shares in her private limited company.
8. Who pays a royalty, and to whom?
9. Give one advantage of franchising for the franchisor.
10. A franchisee's sales revenue is £96,000 and the royalty is 4%. Calculate the royalty.
11. Why is a plc not one of the 1.4.1 start-up options?
12. Explain why a partnership agreement can reduce the risks of a partnership.

### Answers

1. The owner is personally responsible for all the debts of the business, so personal possessions can be used to repay them.
2. Sole trader and (ordinary) partnership.
3. If the business fails, the owner's loss is limited to what they invested, so borrowing is less of a personal risk.
4. Any two: keeps all the profit; full control; quick decisions; simple to set up; close contact with customers.
5. Any one: profit is shared; partners may disagree; decisions take longer; one partner's poor decision affects the others.
6. Investors know they cannot lose more than they paid for their shares, so they are more willing to invest, and the company can sell shares to several chosen people.
7. She would share profit and control with the new shareholder, so she may lose the final say over decisions.
8. The franchisee pays it to the franchisor.
9. Any one: the brand grows using franchisees' money; it earns fees and royalties; franchisees are motivated to succeed.
10. 4% × £96,000 = £3,840.
11. The specification places plcs in Theme 2 as an option for growing businesses; a plc sells shares to the public, which suits large, established firms.
12. It sets out in advance how profit is shared and what happens if a partner leaves, so disagreements are less likely to end the business.
