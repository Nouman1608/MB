## Fiscal policy (3.2.5.1)

- **Definition:** manipulation of government spending, taxation and the budget balance.
- **Macro functions:** influence AD (and so output, jobs, inflation) and AS (infrastructure, skills, incentives).
- **Micro functions:** change the pattern of activity: taxes on demerit goods, subsidies to particular industries,
  redistribution.
- **Expansionary:** higher spending or lower taxes → AD rises. **Contractionary:** the reverse.

## Public expenditure

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Current | Public sector wages, medicines, running costs |
| Capital | Roads, schools, hospitals, broadband networks |
| Transfer payments | Pensions, unemployment and child benefits |

**Reasons:** public goods, merit goods, redistribution, infrastructure, demand management.

## Taxation

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Direct tax | On income or wealth, paid directly to the government (income tax, corporation tax) |
| Indirect tax | On spending, collected via sellers (VAT, excise duties) |
| Progressive | Average rate rises as income rises |
| Proportional | Average rate is the same at every income |
| Regressive | Average rate falls as income rises |
| Average tax rate | Total tax ÷ income × 100 |
| Marginal tax rate | Tax paid on an extra unit of income |

**Why governments tax:** revenue, redistribution, correcting market failure, managing AD.

**Principles of taxation:** equity (ability to pay), certainty, convenience, economy of collection; also
efficiency (least distortion) and flexibility.

**UK taxes to know the role of:** income tax, National Insurance contributions, VAT, corporation tax, excise duties,
council tax. Judge their merits against the principles above.

## Budget balance and debt

- Deficit (flow) adds to the **national debt** (stock); a surplus reduces it.
- **Cyclical** deficit: from the cycle via automatic stabilisers. **Structural** deficit: persists at potential output.
- **Consequences of deficits:** higher AD and output if there is spare capacity; possible crowding out; higher debt
  interest; may fund investment that raises AS.
- **Consequences of surpluses:** lower AD; reduced debt and interest payments; scope for later tax cuts.
- **Significance of the debt:** depends on its size relative to GDP, interest rates relative to growth, and who holds it.
- **Office for Budget Responsibility:** independent forecasts and assessment of fiscal targets; it does not set policy.

## Supply-side policies (3.2.5.2)

- **Supply-side policies** are government measures; **supply-side improvements** are increases in capacity from any
  source, often private-sector innovation and investment.
- Aim: raise **potential output** and the **trend rate of growth**.

| Free market | Interventionist |
|---|---|
| Tax cuts to raise incentives | Education and training spending |
| Privatisation | Industrial policy |
| Deregulation | Subsidies for research and development |
| Labour market reforms | Infrastructure investment |

**Effects:**

- Lower **natural rate of unemployment** (skills, mobility, incentives).
- Less **inflationary pressure**, because capacity grows with demand.
- Better **current account**, through lower costs and higher quality exports.
- Micro and macro effects; can correct market failures such as **short-termism**.

## Worked reminders (invented)

- MPC 0.8, multiplier 5: spending +6 billion → income up to **+30 billion**; tax cut of 6 billion → first-round
  spending 4.8 billion → income up to **+24 billion**.
- Tax 0% to 12,000, 25% above: income 20,000 → tax 2,000, average rate **10%**; income 52,000 → tax 10,000,
  average rate **19.2%**: progressive.

## Must-know distinctions

- Fiscal policy versus monetary policy (interest rates, set separately).
- Direct versus indirect tax.
- Average versus marginal rate.
- Progressive versus regressive.
- Deficit versus debt; cyclical versus structural.
- Supply-side policy versus supply-side improvement; free market versus interventionist.

## Evaluation toolkit

- **Effectiveness:** size of the multiplier; how strongly people respond to incentives; spare capacity.
- **Time lags:** fiscal changes need legislation; supply-side gains from education take years.
- **Opportunity cost:** what else the money could fund; the effect on the deficit and debt.
- **Equity:** who gains and who loses, especially from tax cuts and benefit changes.
- **Side effects:** crowding out, extra imports, inflation near capacity.
- **Uncertainty:** forecasts of potential output and of behaviour can be wrong.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the top marginal rate to the whole of an income.
- Calling an indirect tax progressive because richer people pay more in total.
- Treating every rise in public spending as a supply-side policy.

## Quick self-test

1. Define fiscal policy.
2. Give one macroeconomic and one microeconomic function of fiscal policy.
3. Distinguish current spending from transfer payments.
4. Is VAT a direct or an indirect tax?
5. Income of 40,000 pays 6,000 tax; income of 80,000 pays 10,000. Is the tax progressive, proportional or
   regressive?
6. Name four principles of taxation.
7. Why does a tax cut raise AD by less than an equal rise in government spending?
8. Distinguish a cyclical deficit from a structural deficit.
9. What does the Office for Budget Responsibility do?
10. Give two free market and two interventionist supply-side policies.
11. How can supply-side policies help the current account?
12. Why might supply-side policies be slow to work?

### Answers

1. The manipulation of government spending, taxation and the budget balance.
2. Macro: influencing AD and so output and inflation. Micro: changing the pattern of activity, for example taxing
   tobacco.
3. Current spending buys goods and services for immediate use, such as nurses' wages; transfer payments move income
   without buying output, such as pensions.
4. **Indirect.**
5. Average rates are 15% and 12.5%, falling as income rises, so it is **regressive**.
6. Equity, certainty, convenience and economy (also efficiency and flexibility).
7. Part of the extra disposable income is saved or spent on imports, so the first-round injection is smaller.
8. A cyclical deficit results from the state of the cycle and disappears in recovery; a structural deficit remains
   when the economy is at potential output.
9. Provides independent economic and fiscal forecasts and assesses whether the government is on course to meet its
   fiscal targets.
10. Free market: tax cuts, privatisation, deregulation, labour market reform (any two). Interventionist: education
    and training, industrial policy, R&D subsidies, infrastructure (any two).
11. Lower costs and better quality make exports more competitive and domestic goods better substitutes for imports.
12. Training, infrastructure and research take years to raise capacity, and people and firms respond gradually to
    new incentives.
