## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State **two** reasons why it is important for a business to identify and satisfy customer needs. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** types of customer need, other than price. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a customer and a consumer, using an example. **[2]**

**4.** Pentrow Florist sold 640 bouquets in May at £25 each. After adding an online ordering service, it sold 720 bouquets in June at the same price. Calculate the increase in revenue. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which understanding its customers could help a gym to be competitive. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs to keep checking what its customers need. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Tremorna Toys is planning a new range of wooden toys. The plan involves £22,000 of design costs, £14,000 for the first production run and £8,000 for promotion.

**(a)** Calculate the total amount Tremorna Toys will spend before any toys are sold. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how identifying customer needs before launch could help Tremorna Toys avoid a costly mistake. **[3]**

**8.** Larkmoor Cinema has seen ticket sales fall. Most of its customers are families, but its film times start at 20:30.

**(a)** Identify the customer need that Larkmoor Cinema appears to be failing to meet. **[1]**
**(b)** Analyse how meeting this need could increase Larkmoor Cinema's sales. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how identifying customer needs helps a business select the correct marketing mix. Use the example of a new sports drink aimed at teenagers. **[4]**

**10.** Corvell Coffee sells 3,000 cups a week at £2.80. A survey shows customers want plant-based milk options. After adding them, weekly sales rise by 12%.

**(a)** Calculate the new number of cups sold each week. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the increase in weekly revenue. **[2]**

**11.** Trevanna Bags makes school bags. It has always sold one black design. A rival has started selling bags in many colours with padded laptop pockets. Analyse why Trevanna Bags needs to understand its customers' needs. **[6]**

**12.** Pendrell Meals is a small catering business. The owner wants to launch a lunch delivery service for offices. She is unsure whether to spend £1,800 finding out what office workers want, or to launch straight away using her own ideas to save time and money. Evaluate whether she should spend the money identifying customer needs first. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: to provide a product or service that customers will buy; to increase sales; to select the correct marketing mix; to avoid costly mistakes; to be competitive [1] [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: quality, design or features, convenience, choice, service [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A customer buys the product and a consumer uses it [1]. For example, a parent (customer) buys a toy for a child (consumer) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Revenue in May 640 × £25 = £16,000; June 720 × £25 = £18,000 [1]. Increase **£2,000** [1]. Also accept 80 × £25 = £2,000. **[2]**

**5.** By finding out that many members want early-morning classes before work [1], the gym can offer them when rivals do not [1], so customers choose this gym over competitors, raising membership [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Customer needs change, for example with new technology or tastes [1], so a product that once sold well may stop meeting needs and sales could fall [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** £22,000 + £14,000 + £8,000 = **£44,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Research could show what parents want, for example toys that are safe for under-threes or a particular price range [1]. Tremorna can design the range around this before spending [1], reducing the risk of losing the £44,000 on toys that do not sell [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Convenience: film times that suit families with young children [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Earlier showings, such as 17:00, fit around children's bedtimes [1], so more families can attend [1]. Families buy several tickets and snacks per visit, so revenue rises [1], and satisfied families may return and recommend the cinema, bringing further sales [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Knowing the needs of teenagers lets the business set each element to suit them [1]. For example, teenagers may have little money, so a low price [1]; they use social media, so promotion there [1]; and they buy in shops near schools or leisure centres, so it is placed there [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** 3,000 × 1.12 [1] = **3,360 cups** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 360 × £2.80 [1] = **£1,008** [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Customers now have a choice of colours and laptop pockets from the rival [1], so Trevanna's single design may no longer meet their needs [1]. If it does not find out what students and parents want, it risks losing sales to the rival [1]. Understanding needs would show which features matter most, such as space for a laptop [1], so Trevanna could provide a product customers will buy [1] and stay competitive, protecting its market position [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Knowledge: identifying needs helps avoid costly mistakes and select the right mix [1].
Application: £1,800 is a lot for a small business [1]; office workers may have particular needs, such as delivery times, healthy options or prices [1].
Analysis: research could show the best delivery window and menu, so the service is designed to sell from the start [1]; without it, the owner may buy vans, packaging and stock for a service that does not match needs, losing much more than £1,800 [1]; knowing needs also helps her stand out from other lunch options [1].
Evaluation: launching straight away saves £1,800 and lets her start earning sooner, and she could adjust after hearing customer feedback [1]; research may be wrong if office workers do not do what they say [1]. Judgement: she should spend the money, because it is small compared with the cost of a failed launch, unless she can find out cheaply, for example by asking a few offices directly [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong judgement compares the £1,800 with what could be lost, rather than treating research as automatically worth it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "to make a profit" instead of one of the five reasons in the specification.
- Naming a reason without applying it to the business in the question.
- Forgetting to finish an explanation with a consequence such as higher sales or avoided costs.
- Calculating a percentage increase on the wrong base figure.
- Assuming research is always worth its cost in an evaluate question.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I make a reason about customer needs specific to the case?**
Use a detail from the stem, such as who the customers are or what a rival offers, then show how meeting that need affects this business's sales or costs.

**Can I use market research in a 3.5.1 answer?**
Yes, as the way the business finds out about needs, but keep the focus on why identifying needs matters.
