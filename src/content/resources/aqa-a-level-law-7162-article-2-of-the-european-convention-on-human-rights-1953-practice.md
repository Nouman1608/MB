## Section A

**1.** Which phrase in Article 2.2 governs the use of force? A: necessary in a democratic society. B: reasonable in the circumstances. C: no more than absolutely necessary. D: proportionate to a legitimate aim. **[1]**

**2.** In which case did the European Court hold that Article 2 does not confer a right to die? A: McCann v UK. B: Osman v UK. C: Pretty v UK. D: Jordan v UK. **[1]**

**3.** Identify the three purposes for which force may be used under Article 2.2. **[3]**

**4.** State two requirements of an effective investigation into a death under Article 2. **[2]**

**5.** State why Article 2 is classified as a limited right. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the operational duty under Article 2, referring to two cases. **[5]**

**7.** Explain the decision in McCann v UK (1995) and its significance for the planning of state operations. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the European Court assesses a mistaken belief by a state agent that lethal force was needed. **[3]**

**9.** *Rosa tells police three times that her former partner has threatened to kill her, giving his name, address and details of a knife he has bought. The police take no action. A week later he kills her.* Suggest whether the state has breached Article 2. **[5]**

**10.** *During a violent riot, police fire live ammunition into the crowd after rioters throw stones, killing Tom, a bystander. The police had no water cannon or other less lethal equipment available. An inquiry is held in private by senior officers of the same force, and Tom's family are not told its findings.* Advise Tom's family on whether Article 2 has been breached. **[8]**

**11.** Explain the framework duty under Article 2 and how it differs from the operational duty. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss whether Article 2 provides adequate protection of the right to life. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: no more than absolutely necessary. **[1]**

**2.** C: Pretty v UK. **[1]**

**3.** **Defence of any person from unlawful violence** [1]; **effecting a lawful arrest or preventing the escape of a person lawfully detained** [1]; **action lawfully taken to quell a riot or insurrection** [1].

**4.** Any two of: **independent** of those involved [1]; **effective**, able to establish the facts and identify those responsible; **prompt**; open to **public scrutiny**; **involving the family** of the deceased [1].

**5.** It can be restricted **only in the situations listed in Article 2.2** [1]; there is **no general balancing test** like the second paragraph of a qualified right [1].

**6.** The state must take **reasonable measures within its powers** to protect a person from a **real and immediate risk** to life [1] from the criminal acts of a third party, where the authorities **knew or ought to have known** of it [1] and the risk was to an **identified individual** (Osman v UK, 1998) [1]. In **Osman** the test was not met because there was no decisive point at which the police knew of the risk [1]. In **Rabone v Pennine Care (2012)** the duty was owed to and breached for a voluntary psychiatric patient at real and immediate risk of suicide (or Edwards v UK, 2002, for a breach) [1].

**7.** Soldiers shot dead three IRA suspects in Gibraltar, honestly believing they could detonate a bomb [1]. The soldiers' **honest belief** meant their own actions did not breach Article 2 [1], but the Court found a **violation** because of the **planning and control** of the operation, including the failure to arrest earlier and to allow for wrong intelligence [1]. The significance is that the state must **plan operations to minimise the risk to life**, not only justify the final use of force [1].

**8.** Force is judged on an **honest belief** that it was needed [1], **perceived for good reasons** to be valid at the time [1], even if it later turns out to be **mistaken** (Da Silva v UK, 2016) [1].

**9.** Rosa's death engages the **operational duty** under Article 2 [1]. The police were told three times, with the man's **name, address and weapon**, so they **knew or ought to have known** [1] of a **real and immediate risk** to an **identified individual** [1]. Unlike **Osman** and **Van Colle**, the information here was specific and repeated [1]. Taking no action means they failed to take **reasonable measures**, so there is likely a breach [1].

**10.** Lethal force by the police engages **Article 2.2** [1]. The aim may be **2.2(c), quelling a riot**, or 2.2(a), defending others [1]. The force must be **no more than absolutely necessary** [1]; firing live rounds into a crowd in response to stones, killing a bystander, is unlikely to be strictly proportionate [1]. As in **Güleç v Turkey (1998)**, the lack of **less lethal equipment** points to a breach in the **planning and control** of the operation (McCann) [1]. Contrast **Stewart v UK (1984)**, where a plastic baton round was within 2.2(c) [1]. The private inquiry by the **same force** is not **independent**, and the family was not involved or informed, breaching the **procedural duty** (**Jordan v UK, 2001**) [1]. Conclusion: likely substantive and procedural breaches of Article 2, giving a claim under the HRA 1998 [1].

**11.** The framework duty requires the state to have an **effective system of criminal law and regulation** to deter and punish threats to life, including regulation of **dangerous activities** [1], as in **Öneryildiz v Turkey (2004)** [1]. The operational duty is narrower: it requires action in a **particular case** where there is a **real and immediate risk to an identified person** [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- As a **limited right** with exhaustive exceptions and **no derogation**, Article 2 gives strong formal protection [1].
- **"Absolutely necessary"** is a strict test that controls lethal force [1].
- **McCann** extends scrutiny to **planning and control**, so the state cannot hide behind individual officers [1].
- **Positive duties** protect against third parties, but the **Osman test** is demanding and few claims succeed (Van Colle) [1].
- **Rabone** shows the operational duty extending to vulnerable people outside detention [1].
- The **procedural duty** (Jordan) ensures accountability, though investigations can be slow [1].
- **Da Silva** allows honestly mistaken beliefs, which some see as too generous to the state [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that protection is strong against state killing but weaker for risks from third parties [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying a balancing test as if Article 2 were qualified.
- Missing the planning and control point.
- Forgetting the procedural duty when the facts mention an inquiry.
- Stating the Osman test inaccurately.
- Not applying the specific facts to "real and immediate".
