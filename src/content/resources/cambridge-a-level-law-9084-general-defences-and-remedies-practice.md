## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Explain the requirements of volenti non fit injuria, with an authority. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why volenti rarely succeeds in employment cases. **[3]**

**3.** Explain how contributory negligence differs from volenti. **[2]**

**4.** A claimant's total damages are £84,000. The court finds her 35 per cent responsible for her injuries. Calculate the award and explain the statutory basis. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the defence of Act of God, contrasting two cases. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the defence of necessity, with an authority. **[2]**

**7.** Distinguish aggravated damages from exemplary damages. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the difference between a mandatory and an interlocutory injunction. **[4]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Yusuf, an experienced climber, joins a guided climb run by Peaks Ltd. He signs a form stating he understands climbing is dangerous. The guide, Erin, fails to check Yusuf's harness, which is faulty, and Yusuf falls. Yusuf had removed his helmet because he was hot; wearing it would have reduced his head injury. Erin is herself injured trying to catch him. Advise Peaks Ltd on any defences available against Yusuf and Erin. **[12]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Nadia, aged 30, is seriously injured by a negligent driver. She cannot work again. Her losses to trial are £45,000 in lost earnings and £12,000 in medical costs. She will need care for life and suffers continuing pain. Doctors say there is a small chance she will later develop epilepsy. Advise Nadia on the damages she may claim and the form the award might take. **[10]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Northfield Motocross Club has held races on farmland next to a village for 15 years. Noise has increased since new houses were built nearby. Residents seek an injunction. The club argues that it brings visitors to the area and that the residents knew of the track when they moved in. Advise the residents on the injunctions or other remedies available. **[8]**

**12.** "The lump-sum system of damages for personal injury is unsatisfactory and should be replaced." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** The claimant must have full knowledge and understanding of the nature and extent of the risk (1), and must freely agree to accept it (1). Knowledge alone is not enough: in Smith v Baker (1891) a worker who knew of stones being swung overhead had not consented to the risk (1). **[3]**

**2.** Employees often have no real choice but to accept risks if they want to keep their jobs (1), so their agreement is not truly free (Bowater v Rowley Regis Corporation, 1944) (1). It may succeed where employees freely and deliberately take a risk against instructions, as in ICI v Shatwell (1965) (1). **[3]**

**3.** Volenti is a complete defence, so the claimant recovers nothing (1), whereas contributory negligence is a partial defence that reduces damages by the claimant's share of responsibility under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 (1). **[2]**

**4.** Under s1(1) of the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, damages are reduced as is just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in responsibility (1). Reduction: 35 per cent of £84,000 = £29,400 (1). Award: £84,000 − £29,400 = £54,600 (1). **[3]**

**5.** An Act of God is a natural event so exceptional that no human foresight could guard against it (1). In Nichols v Marsland (1876) an extraordinary rainfall that burst artificial lakes was an Act of God (1), but in Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway (1917) heavy rain was not exceptional enough to qualify (1). **[3]**

**6.** Necessity is a defence where the defendant acted reasonably to prevent greater harm to others or property (1), as in Cope v Sharpe (1912), where burning a firebreak on another's land to protect against fire was justified (1). **[2]**

**7.** Aggravated damages are compensatory (1): they compensate the claimant for additional injury to feelings caused by the manner in which the tort was committed (1). Exemplary damages are designed to punish the defendant (1) and are available only in the categories set out in Rookes v Barnard (1964), such as conduct calculated to make a profit (1). **[4]**

**8.** A mandatory injunction orders the defendant to take positive action (1), such as removing something, and strict conditions apply because it is onerous (Redland Bricks v Morris, 1970) (1). An interlocutory injunction is a temporary order preserving the position until trial (1), granted where there is a serious question to be tried and the balance of convenience favours it (American Cyanamid v Ethicon, 1975) (1). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Volenti against Yusuf: he knew climbing was dangerous and signed a form (1), but knowledge of general risks is not consent to the risk of negligent equipment checks (Smith v Baker) (1). The defence probably fails (1).
- Under s65 Consumer Rights Act 2015 Peaks cannot exclude liability for negligently caused personal injury to a consumer, and agreeing to or knowing of such a notice is not by itself acceptance of the risk (1).
- Contributory negligence: removing his helmet was a failure to take reasonable care for his own safety (Jones v Livox Quarries) (1); it contributed to the damage, though not to the fall (Froom v Butcher by analogy; O'Connell v Jackson) (1). Damages reduced by a just and equitable proportion (1).
- Erin: the faulty harness is Peaks' equipment, so she may claim against Peaks as her employer (1).
- As a rescuer she is not treated as volenti (Haynes v Harwood; Baker v TE Hopkins) (1), and as an employee she lacks true freedom of choice (Bowater) (1).
- Her own failure to check the harness may amount to contributory negligence, reducing her damages (1).
- Conclusion: Peaks can rely only on contributory negligence against Yusuf, and possibly against Erin (1). **[12]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Purpose: restore Nadia, so far as money can, to her pre-tort position (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal) (1).
- Special damages: £45,000 + £12,000 = £57,000 (1).
- General damages: pain, suffering and loss of amenity (1).
- Future loss of earnings: multiplicand (annual earnings) × multiplier based on her working life (1), adjusted by the discount rate and actuarial tables (1).
- Future care costs, calculated on the same basis (1).
- Lump sum: final, so it must predict her future; risk of over- or under-compensation (1).
- Periodical payment orders under the Damages Act 1996 could pay for care for life (1).
- Provisional damages: because of the small chance of epilepsy, she could receive an award now and return if it develops (1).
- Conclusion: a combination of a lump sum for past losses with periodical payments and provisional damages may suit her best (1). **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- A noisy activity may be a private nuisance; coming to the nuisance is not a defence (Miller v Jackson; Coventry v Lawrence) (1).
- Public benefit is not a defence to liability (1), but it is relevant to the remedy (1).
- Prohibitory injunction: discretionary; can limit days and times rather than ban racing (Kennaway v Thompson) (1).
- Prescription is not available, since 15 years is less than 20 (1).
- Damages in lieu: Shelfer suggested this only where the injury is small and an injunction oppressive (1); Coventry v Lawrence said the test is not applied mechanically and public interest can be weighed (1).
- Conclusion: a limited injunction or damages in lieu is likely (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): purpose of damages; special and general damages; multiplier and multiplicand; discount rate; periodical payment orders and provisional damages; Fatal Accidents Act 1976 and Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934 for death.
- Analysis (up to 3): explain why a lump sum requires the court to predict life expectancy, earnings and care needs once and for all.
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, lump sums risk over- or under-compensation, may be dissipated, and fault-based litigation is slow and costly. Against, a lump sum gives finality and freedom of choice, and periodical payments and provisional damages already address the main problems. Consider reforms such as no-fault schemes or tariffs. A reasoned conclusion, for example that the system needs adjustment rather than replacement. **[15]**
