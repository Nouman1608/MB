Section 3.1.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, has eleven statements, a to k. They cover elements and symbols; the nucleus and electrons, and the fact that "the atomic model has changed over time"; the relative charges and masses of protons, neutrons and electrons; atomic number and mass number, with calculations of the numbers of each sub-atomic particle; isotopes; the notation for an atom; electron energy levels (shells) for the first twenty elements; and relative atomic mass (Ar). The specification states that you will **not** be asked to calculate relative atomic masses from isotopic abundances. The qualification is untiered.

The broader [Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-chemistry-atomic-structure-and-periodic-table/) introduces these ideas. This guide works through each statement in turn, with extra examples on particle counting, isotopes and electronic structures.

## Elements, atoms and symbols (statements a and b)

All substances are made of atoms. An **element** is a substance made of only one sort of atom; there are about 100 elements, all shown in the periodic table. Each element has a **chemical symbol**: one capital letter, sometimes followed by a lower-case letter, such as O for oxygen, Mg for magnesium and Cl for chlorine. You need to know the symbols only for elements named in the specification; others are given in the periodic table on the data sheet.

## Inside the atom (statements c, d, e and j)

An atom has a small central **nucleus**, containing **protons** and **neutrons**, surrounded by **electrons**.

| Particle | Where it is | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | shells around the nucleus | −1 | very small |

In an atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges cancel and **atoms have no overall electrical charge**. Almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because electrons have a very small mass.

### How the model changed

The specification asks you to be aware that the model of the atom has changed as new evidence appeared:

- **Dalton** (early 1800s) pictured atoms as tiny, solid spheres that could not be divided.
- **Thomson** discovered the electron (1897) and proposed the "plum pudding" model: negative electrons in a ball of positive charge.
- **Rutherford**, using the results of alpha-particle scattering experiments by Geiger and Marsden, proposed (1911) that the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
- **Bohr** (1913) suggested that electrons occupy fixed energy levels around the nucleus.
- **Chadwick** discovered the neutron (1932).

Each model was replaced or refined because it could not explain new experimental results.

## Atomic number, mass number and notation (statements f and h)

- **Atomic number** = number of protons.
- **Mass number** = number of protons + number of neutrons.

An atom is written with the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left of the symbol, for example ²³₁₁Na for sodium.

```
protons   = atomic number
electrons = protons            (in an atom)
neutrons  = mass number − atomic number
```

## Worked example 1: counting particles

*Complete the table for the atoms shown.*

| Atom | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¹⁶₈O | 8 | 16 − 8 = 8 | 8 |
| ²⁴₁₂Mg | 12 | 24 − 12 = 12 | 12 |
| ⁴⁰₂₀Ca | 20 | 40 − 20 = 20 | 20 |
| ⁵⁶₂₆Fe | 26 | 56 − 26 = 30 | 26 |

## Worked example 2: working backwards

*An atom has 19 electrons and 20 neutrons. Identify the element and write the atom in the form shown in the specification.*

**Step 1.** Electrons = protons in an atom, so there are 19 protons.

**Step 2.** Atomic number 19 is potassium (K), from the periodic table.

**Step 3.** Mass number = 19 + 20 = 39, so the atom is ³⁹₁₉K.

## Isotopes (statement g)

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Carbon has isotopes ¹²C, ¹³C and ¹⁴C: each has 6 protons and 6 electrons, with 6, 7 and 8 neutrons respectively. Isotopes of an element have the same electronic structure, so they react in the same way.

## Electronic structure (statement i)

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells). Each electron is at a particular energy level, and electrons occupy the **lowest available energy levels** first (the innermost shells). For the first twenty elements:

- first shell: up to 2 electrons
- second shell: up to 8 electrons
- third shell: 8 electrons before the fourth shell starts to fill (for elements 19 and 20)

The structure is written as numbers separated by commas, such as sodium 2,8,1, or drawn as a diagram with the symbol in the centre and electrons as dots or crosses on circles. You may answer in terms of shells or energy levels.

## Worked example 3: electronic structures

| Element | Atomic number | Electronic structure |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 7 | 2,5 |
| Magnesium | 12 | 2,8,2 |
| Sulfur | 16 | 2,8,6 |
| Argon | 18 | 2,8,8 |
| Calcium | 20 | 2,8,8,2 |

*Check:* the numbers always add up to the atomic number. For calcium, 2 + 8 + 8 + 2 = 20.

## Relative atomic mass (statement k)

The **relative atomic mass (Ar)** of an element compares the mass of its atoms with the mass of the ¹²C isotope. Because most elements are mixtures of isotopes, Ar is an **average** value for the isotopes of the element, which is why it is often not a whole number; chlorine's Ar is 35.5. You will not be asked to calculate Ar from isotopic abundances, but you may be asked to explain why a value is not a whole number.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing that isotopes have different numbers of protons or electrons.** Only the number of neutrons differs.
- **Using the Ar from the periodic table as the mass number** of a particular atom. The question's notation gives the mass number.
- **Reversing the numbers**: the larger number in the notation is the mass number.
- **Filling the third shell beyond 8** for potassium or calcium.
- **Saying the electron has "no mass"**. Its relative mass is very small, not zero.
- **Forgetting that atoms are neutral.** Equal protons and electrons apply to atoms, not ions.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out protons, neutrons and electrons on separate lines with the subtraction shown.
- **Define** an isotope precisely: "atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons".
- **Draw** or **give** electronic structures with the correct number in each shell, checking the total.
- **Explain** why Ar is not a whole number: "it is an average of the masses of the isotopes of the element".
