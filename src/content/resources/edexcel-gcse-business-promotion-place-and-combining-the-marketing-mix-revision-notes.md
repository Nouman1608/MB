## 1. Promotion strategies by market segment (2.2.3)

A market segment is a group of customers with shared characteristics, such as age, income or lifestyle (the standard meaning). Promotion is appropriate when it reaches the chosen segment, at an affordable cost, with a message the segment responds to.

| Strategy | Remember it as | Best for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising | Paid message in a medium | Telling many people, or a chosen group, about the product | Paid for whether or not people buy |
| Sponsorship | Name linked to a team, event or person | Segments that follow that team or event | Hard to measure the effect |
| Product trials | Try before you buy | New or unfamiliar products | Free samples cost money |
| Special offers | Temporary price incentive | Price-sensitive customers; first purchases | Lower profit per sale; customers may wait for the next offer |
| Branding | Name, logo, image and reputation | Any segment, long term | Slow to build; one scandal can harm it |

### Method in steps: answering "which promotion strategy suits this segment?"

1. Pick out the segment from the stem: who they are, what they care about and how they spend time.
2. Choose one strategy and say what the business would actually do.
3. Link the strategy to the segment's needs, such as price, trust or interests.
4. Add a limit or cost, and end with a judgement if the command word needs one.

### Small worked reminder

Trial: 500 samples are given out, 12% buy a pack at £4.50.

- Buyers: 12% × 500 = 60.
- Revenue: 60 × £4.50 = **£270**.
- Compare it with the cost of the samples and stand. If those cost £200, revenue is £70 above the cost, before the cost of making the packs.

## 2. Technology in promotion (2.2.3)

| Method | What it is | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Targeted advertising online | Adverts shown to selected groups of internet users, chosen by details such as age, location or interests | Reaches the right segment; results can be counted | Rivals can target the same people; adverts can be ignored or blocked |
| Viral advertising via social media | Content that customers pass on to their own contacts by choice | Low cost for the number reached | Cannot be controlled; may not spread, or may spread badly |
| E-newsletters | Regular emails to people who signed up | Low cost; direct; good for existing customers | Only reaches subscribers; many go unopened |

### Must-know distinctions

- Viral advertising spreads through customers sharing it. Targeted advertising is paid for and aimed at chosen groups. Posting on social media is not automatically viral.
- An e-newsletter goes to people who have chosen to receive it, so it mainly helps keep existing customers, not win new ones.
- Technology makes results easier to count, but it does not guarantee sales.

### Small worked reminder

An online advert costs £360 and produces 30 sales.

- Cost per sale: £360 ÷ 30 = **£12**.
- If each sale earns £9 of revenue, the advert did not pay for itself on first sales. If customers come back, the answer may change.

## 3. Place: retailers and e-tailers (2.2.4)

| | Retailer | E-tailer (e-commerce) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Sells to the public from a shop or physical outlet | Sells to the public online |
| Strengths | Customers see, try and collect at once | Wide reach; open at any hour; business controls its website |
| Weaknesses | Retailer takes a share of the selling price; limited to the shop's customers | Delivery, packaging and returns; customers cannot handle the product |

A producer may sell through retailers, through its own e-tailer site, or both. The choice depends on the product, the target market and the money available.

### Small worked reminder

A retailer buys a jacket for £18 and sells it for £30.

- Amount kept by the retailer: £30 − £18 = £12.
- As a share of the selling price: 12 ÷ 30 × 100 = **40%**.

A producer that sells directly online keeps more per item but pays for delivery and has fewer customers unless it promotes the site.

## 4. How the elements influence each other (2.2.5)

| Change | Likely knock-on |
|---|---|
| Product made higher quality | Higher costs, so a higher price; promotion must show the quality |
| Price cut | Promotion may stress low prices; margins fall; place may shift to outlets for value buyers |
| Move to selling in specialist shops | Price must allow for the retailer's share; promotion should fit the shop's customers |
| New online store | Promotion must send people to the site; delivery becomes part of the offer |
| Heavy promotion creating high demand | Product supply and place must be able to cope |

Method: name the change, name the element it affects, say what the business should do, then give the reason.

## 5. Competitive advantage and the integrated mix (2.2.5)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Competitive advantage | Something a business does better or differently from rivals that makes customers prefer it |
| Integrated marketing mix | A mix where product, price, promotion and place support each other and give the customer one clear offer |
| Source of advantage | A distinctive product, a lower price, a strong brand, easier access, or a combination |

- An advantage must matter to the target market.
- An integrated mix is hard for a rival to copy, because it relies on four connected decisions.
- A mix that sends mixed messages, such as a premium product sold in a discount outlet with a low-price advert, weakens the advantage.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five promotion strategies the specification lists.
2. State what is meant by a market segment.
3. A business's sales rise from 800 to 1,000 units after a campaign. Calculate the percentage increase.
4. A coat costing £45 is offered at 20% off. Calculate the offer price.
5. At the offer price in question 4, 300 coats are sold. Calculate the revenue, and compare it with selling 220 coats at the full price.
6. Explain one reason a product trial might suit a brand-new snack.
7. Give one disadvantage of viral advertising via social media.
8. State the difference between a retailer and an e-tailer.
9. A retailer buys an item for £18 and sells it for £30. Calculate the retailer's share of the selling price as a percentage.
10. Explain one way a decision to raise the price of a product could influence promotion.

### Answers

1. Advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers and branding.
2. A group of customers who share characteristics such as age, income or lifestyle (standard meaning).
3. (1,000 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = **25%** (the standard percentage change calculation).
4. 20% of £45 = £9, so offer price = **£36**.
5. 300 × £36 = **£10,800**. At the full price: 220 × £45 = **£9,900**. Revenue is £900 higher, but profit also depends on the extra cost of the additional units.
6. For example: a new snack is unknown, so customers are unsure whether they will like it. Tasting free removes that risk, and happy customers may then buy a full pack.
7. For example: the business cannot control whether it spreads or how people react to it.
8. A retailer sells to the public from a shop or similar outlet. An e-tailer sells to the public online.
9. 12 ÷ 30 × 100 = **40%**.
10. For example: a higher price signals quality, so promotion should stress quality and good service, aimed at customers who will pay more, instead of low-price offers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing a promotion strategy with no link to the market segment in the stem.
- Describing a method but not saying why it is appropriate for these customers.
- Using "social media" as an answer for viral advertising, with no mention of sharing.
- Mixing up the retailer, who sells on, with the producer who made the goods.
- Showing that e-commerce helps place, but forgetting that delivery and returns are costs.
- Describing the four elements one by one when the question asks how one influences another.
- Calling something a competitive advantage with no reference to what rivals do or what customers prefer.
- Showing only a final figure in a calculation, which leaves nothing to reward if the figure is wrong.
- Finishing an evaluate or justify answer without a supported judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What promotion strategies are in the Edexcel GCSE Business specification?**
The 1BS0 specification lists advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers and branding. The right strategy depends on the market segment being targeted.

**What is the difference between a retailer and an e-tailer?**
A retailer sells to the public from a shop or other physical outlet, so customers can see and try the product. An e-tailer sells online through a website or app, reaching customers anywhere the business can deliver to.

**What is competitive advantage?**
Competitive advantage is something a business does better, or differently, than its rivals, so customers prefer it. It may come from a distinctive product, a lower price, a stronger brand, easier access or a combination.

**What is viral advertising?**
Viral advertising spreads because people choose to share it on social media, such as a funny video or a challenge. The spend can be small for the reach, but the business cannot control whether it spreads.
