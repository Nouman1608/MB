## Writing Kc (13.1)

For a general equilibrium aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant in terms of concentrations is:

Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)

Square brackets mean equilibrium concentration in mol dm⁻³. Products go on top, reactants underneath, and each coefficient in the balanced equation becomes a power.

A **homogeneous** system has every species in the same phase, for example all gases or all in one solution. A **heterogeneous** system has species in more than one phase. The rule for heterogeneous systems is simple: **leave out solids**. A solid's concentration is fixed by its density, so it does not change as the reaction proceeds and is absorbed into the constant.

**Worked example 1.** Silver nitrate solution is mixed with a solution of iron(II) ions:

Fe²⁺(aq) + Ag⁺(aq) ⇌ Fe³⁺(aq) + Ag(s)

Silver is a solid, so it is left out:

Kc = [Fe³⁺] / ([Fe²⁺][Ag⁺])

Units: (mol dm⁻³) / (mol dm⁻³ × mol dm⁻³) = (mol dm⁻³)⁻¹ = **dm³ mol⁻¹**.

To find units, write the expression with mol dm⁻³ in place of each concentration and cancel. If the powers on top and bottom balance, Kc has no units.

## Writing Kp (13.2)

For reactions involving gases you can use partial pressures instead. The **partial pressure** of a gas is the pressure it would exert if it filled the container alone. Two facts make Kp calculations work:

- mole fraction of A, x(A) = moles of A ÷ total moles of gas
- partial pressure of A, p(A) = x(A) × total pressure

The partial pressures add up to the total pressure. The specification asks for partial pressures in atm.

Kp is built in the same way as Kc, with p(A) in place of [A]. Only gases appear in Kp. In a heterogeneous system, solids are left out (and so is anything in solution, because it has no partial pressure).

**Worked example 2.** Steam is passed over hot iron in a closed vessel:

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) ⇌ Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

Kp = p(H₂)⁴ / p(H₂O)⁴. The units are atm⁴ / atm⁴, so Kp has **no units**.

For ammonium chloride heated in a sealed tube, NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g), the solid is left out: Kp = p(NH₃) × p(HCl), with units **atm²**.

## Calculating Kc and Kp from data (13.3)

Use an initial / change / equilibrium table every time. The change row must follow the mole ratio in the equation.

**Worked example 3 (Kc, ester equilibrium).** A sealed flask holds 0.250 mol ethanoic acid and 0.350 mol ethanol with a little acid catalyst. After several days, titration shows that 0.0580 mol ethanoic acid remains. Calculate Kc.

CH₃COOH(l) + C₂H₅OH(l) ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅(l) + H₂O(l)

| | CH₃COOH | C₂H₅OH | CH₃COOC₂H₅ | H₂O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial / mol | 0.250 | 0.350 | 0 | 0 |
| Change / mol | −0.192 | −0.192 | +0.192 | +0.192 |
| Equilibrium / mol | 0.0580 | 0.158 | 0.192 | 0.192 |

All four species are in one liquid phase, so this is homogeneous and water is included. Each concentration is moles ÷ V, and the V terms cancel because there are two species on each side:

Kc = (0.192 × 0.192) / (0.0580 × 0.158) = **4.02** (no units)

**Worked example 4 (Kp from moles).** 0.800 mol NO₂ is sealed in a vessel. At equilibrium, 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), 0.240 mol NO₂ remains and the total pressure is 1.30 atm. Calculate Kp.

1. NO₂ used = 0.800 − 0.240 = 0.560 mol, so N₂O₄ formed = 0.560 ÷ 2 = 0.280 mol.
2. Total moles of gas = 0.240 + 0.280 = 0.520 mol.
3. p(NO₂) = (0.240 ÷ 0.520) × 1.30 = 0.600 atm; p(N₂O₄) = (0.280 ÷ 0.520) × 1.30 = 0.700 atm.
4. Kp = p(N₂O₄) / p(NO₂)² = 0.700 / 0.600² = **1.94 atm⁻¹**.

**Worked example 5 (heterogeneous Kc).** Equal volumes of solutions are mixed so that, just after mixing, [Fe²⁺] = [Ag⁺] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³. At equilibrium a titration of the remaining silver ions gives [Ag⁺] = 0.0820 mol dm⁻³.

The ratio is 1 : 1 : 1, so [Fe²⁺] = 0.0820 mol dm⁻³ and [Fe³⁺] = 0.100 − 0.0820 = 0.0180 mol dm⁻³.

Kc = 0.0180 / (0.0820 × 0.0820) = **2.68 dm³ mol⁻¹**

## What changes the composition, and what changes K (13.4, 13.5)

The key idea at this level is that **only temperature changes the value of K**. Concentration, pressure and catalysts can change the composition of the mixture, or how fast it settles, but the mixture always ends up with the same value of K at that temperature.

**Concentration.** Adding a reactant makes the fraction in the K expression smaller than K. The system responds by converting reactant to product until the fraction equals K again. K itself does not move.

**Pressure.** Take the mixture in worked example 4 and halve its volume at constant temperature. Every partial pressure doubles at that instant:

p(N₂O₄) / p(NO₂)² becomes 1.40 / 1.20² = 0.972 atm⁻¹

That is half of Kp, because the bottom line has the higher power. To restore 1.94 atm⁻¹, more NO₂ turns into N₂O₄: the equilibrium moves to the side with fewer gas moles. If both sides have equal numbers of gas moles, as in the iron and steam system, a pressure change alters every term equally and the composition does not change.

**Catalyst.** A catalyst speeds up the forward and backward reactions by the same factor. Equilibrium is reached sooner, but the composition and K are unchanged.

**Heterogeneous systems.** Apply the same reasoning using only the species in the expression. Adding more solid iron to the iron and steam system has no effect, because Fe does not appear in Kp. Removing hydrogen does shift the position, because p(H₂) does appear.

## Temperature and K (13.6, 13.7)

| Forward reaction | Temperature rises | Temperature falls |
|---|---|---|
| Exothermic (ΔH negative) | K decreases | K increases |
| Endothermic (ΔH positive) | K increases | K decreases |

The specification wants the position change explained **through K**. When the temperature rises for an exothermic reaction, K becomes smaller. The existing mixture now has a fraction that is too big for the new K, so products react back to form reactants until the fraction matches the new, smaller K. The equilibrium has moved to the left because K changed, not the other way round.

For the 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ system, the forward reaction is exothermic. Warming a sealed syringe of the mixture lowers Kp, so more brown NO₂ forms and the colour darkens. Cooling raises Kp and the colour fades.

## Entropy and the equilibrium constant (13.8)

From Topic 12, ΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings, and ΔSsurroundings = −ΔH / T. The specification links ΔStotal to K by:

ΔStotal = R lnK

where R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. The K here is the equilibrium constant without units. A positive ΔStotal gives K greater than 1; a negative ΔStotal gives K less than 1.

**Effect of temperature on ΔStotal.** As T rises, ΔH / T gets smaller, so ΔSsurroundings shrinks in size. ΔSsystem changes very little.

- Exothermic reaction: ΔSsurroundings is positive and gets smaller, so ΔStotal falls and **K falls**.
- Endothermic reaction: ΔSsurroundings is negative and gets less negative, so ΔStotal rises and **K rises**.

**Worked example 6.** Use these values for 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g): ΔH = −57.2 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔSsystem = −175.8 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔStotal and K at 298 K and at 350 K.

At 298 K:
- ΔSsurroundings = −(−57 200) / 298 = +191.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (convert kJ to J first)
- ΔStotal = −175.8 + 191.9 = +16.1 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
- lnK = 16.1 / 8.31 = 1.94, so K = e^1.94 ≈ **7.0**

At 350 K:
- ΔSsurroundings = 57 200 / 350 = +163.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
- ΔStotal = −175.8 + 163.4 = −12.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
- K = e^(−12.4 / 8.31) ≈ **0.23**

Raising the temperature has made ΔStotal negative and K much smaller, which matches the exothermic row of the table above.

## Predicting the extent of reaction (13.9)

The size of K tells you how far a reaction goes before equilibrium is reached:

| Size of K | Approximate ΔStotal / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| Greater than about 10¹⁰ | More positive than about +200 | Reaction goes essentially to completion |
| Between about 10⁻¹⁰ and 10¹⁰ | Between about −200 and +200 | A true equilibrium mixture with noticeable amounts of both sides |
| Less than about 10⁻¹⁰ | More negative than about −200 | Reaction hardly happens at all |

These boundaries are a rule of thumb, not sharp cut-offs. Check them: R lnK with K = 10¹⁰ gives 8.31 × 23.0 ≈ +191 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

K says nothing about **rate**. A reaction with a huge K can still be too slow to observe if its activation energy is high. That is kinetic stability, which you met in outcome 12.11.

## Further suggested practicals

The specification lists these practicals for Topic 13. Know what each one measures.

- **Ethanol and ethanoic acid.** Mixtures are left to reach equilibrium, then titrated with sodium hydroxide. Subtract the titre due to the acid catalyst before finding moles of ethanoic acid.
- **Fe²⁺(aq) + Ag⁺(aq) ⇌ Fe³⁺(aq) + Ag(s).** The silver ions left are found by titration with thiocyanate; the other concentrations follow from the 1 : 1 ratio.
- **Distribution between two immiscible solvents.** Iodine or ammonia is shaken with two solvents; titrating each layer gives the ratio of concentrations, which is an equilibrium constant without units.
- **Thermal decomposition of ammonium chloride.** A heterogeneous gas equilibrium with solid present.
- **2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄.** Colour changes in a sealed syringe show the effects of temperature and pressure.

## Common errors

- Putting a solid into Kc or Kp. Heterogeneous expressions leave solids out.
- Using moles instead of concentrations in Kc when the moles of each side differ. The volume only cancels when the total powers top and bottom are equal.
- Forgetting to square a partial pressure when the coefficient is 2.
- Saying that raising the pressure "increases Kp". Pressure changes the composition, never K.
- Explaining a temperature shift by Le Chatelier alone when the question asks for an explanation in terms of K.
- Leaving ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹ while ΔSsystem is in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- Claiming a large K means a fast reaction.
