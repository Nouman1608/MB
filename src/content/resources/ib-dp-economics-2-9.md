This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **2.9 Market failure: public goods** in Unit 2 of the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. The [guide to public goods and asymmetric information](/resources/ib-dp-economics-public-goods-and-asymmetric-information/) introduces 2.9 alongside HL 2.10; this page concentrates on classification, the free rider problem worked through in numbers, and a fuller evaluation of the two government responses. Consolidate with the [2.9 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-economics-2-9-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.9 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-economics-2-9-practice/).

All places and figures are invented. The main example is Kelmoor, whose currency is the kel.

## What 2.9 asks you to do

- Explain the two characteristics of a public good: **non-rivalry** and **non-excludability**.
- Explain the **free rider problem** and why it leads the market to under-provide or not provide public goods at all.
- Evaluate government responses: **direct provision** and **contracting out to the private sector**.

There is no required diagram for 2.9, so marks come from precise definitions, clear reasoning chains and well-chosen examples.

## The two tests

**Rivalry** asks: does one person's consumption reduce the amount available to others? A sandwich is rivalrous; a radio broadcast is not, because one more listener does not weaken the signal for anyone else.

**Excludability** asks: can people who do not pay be prevented from consuming? A cinema seat is excludable; a fireworks display over a city is not, because anyone can look up.

A **pure public good** is both non-rivalrous and non-excludable. A **private good** is both rivalrous and excludable. Using both tests gives a grid that helps with harder examples.

| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rivalrous** | Private goods: a bicycle, a meal | Common pool resources: an open fishery, groundwater (covered in 2.8) |
| **Non-rivalrous** | Goods with a charge but no crowding: a streaming subscription, an uncongested toll bridge | Public goods: national defence, a flood barrier, a lighthouse beam |

### Applying the tests carefully

- **Street lighting in Kelmoor's old town.** One more pedestrian does not dim the lamps (non-rival), and the council cannot stop non-payers from walking under them (non-excludable). A public good.
- **A city road at 3 a.m.** Practically non-rival and non-excludable. **The same road at rush hour** becomes rivalrous, because each extra car slows everyone else. Many goods are public goods only under some conditions; say so in your answer.
- **A park with a gate and an entry fee.** Excludable, so the market could provide it. The fee changes the classification.

## The free rider problem in numbers

A **free rider** is someone who benefits from a good without paying for it. Because a public good is non-excludable, every user has an incentive to wait for someone else to pay. Firms therefore cannot collect enough revenue to cover costs, and the good is under-provided or not provided at all. This is sometimes called a **missing market**.

**Worked example: a flood warning siren.** Three farms in a Kelmoor valley would all be protected by one siren costing 900 kels a year. Each farm values the protection as follows.

| Farm | Value of the siren (kels per year) |
|---|---|
| Ashby | 450 |
| Brennan | 350 |
| Corrie | 300 |
| **Total** | **1,100** |

1. Because the siren is non-rivalrous, every farm consumes the **same unit**. Society's benefit is the **sum** of the valuations: 1,100 kels.
2. The total benefit of 1,100 exceeds the cost of 900, so the siren is worth providing: net benefit 1,100 - 900 = 200 kels.
3. No farm values it at 900 or more, so no single farm will buy it alone.
4. A private firm could try to charge each farm, but because the siren is non-excludable, each farm can understate its value or refuse to pay, knowing it will be warned anyway.
5. If Brennan and Corrie each pay only 100 and hope Ashby pays the rest, the firm collects too little, and the siren is not built. A worthwhile good goes unprovided: **market failure**.

The same logic explains why markets fail to supply national defence, flood barriers or public health campaigns at the level society would choose.

## Government responses

### Direct provision

The government produces the good itself and funds it through **taxation**, so the free rider problem disappears: everyone pays through taxes whether or not they would have volunteered.

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Solves the free rider problem through compulsory finance | Uses tax revenue with an opportunity cost, such as schools or hospitals forgone |
| Can provide goods no firm would supply | The government does not know true valuations, since people have no reason to reveal them; it may provide too much or too little |
| Allows decisions based on social benefit, including for future generations | State producers may face weak incentives to control costs |
| | Political priorities may shape which goods are provided |

### Contracting out to the private sector

The government **pays for** the good but a private firm **produces** it under contract, usually after competitive bidding. Finance stays public; production becomes private.

**Worked example.** Kelmoor council currently maintains street lighting itself at a cost of 4.2 million kels a year. A private contractor bids 3.6 million. Monitoring the contract (inspections, legal costs) would cost the council 0.3 million.

```
Cost of contracting out = 3.6 + 0.3 = 3.9 million kels
Saving = 4.2 - 3.9 = 0.3 million kels
Saving as a share of current cost = 0.3 / 4.2 x 100 = about 7.1%
```

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Competitive bidding can lower costs | A contractor maximising profit may cut quality where the contract is vague |
| Private firms may bring specialist skills and technology | Monitoring and contract design are costly, as the 0.3 million shows |
| The free rider problem is still solved, because tax pays | Too few bidders weakens competition; one firm may dominate renewals |
| | If the contractor fails, the government must step in to keep the service running |

The saving of about 7.1% only matters if quality holds. A strong evaluation asks how measurable quality is. Lamp repair times are easy to specify and check; the quality of a public health campaign is much harder to write into a contract.

## Building an evaluation

Use these questions to weigh the responses for a particular good:

- **Is quality easy to measure?** If yes, contracting out is more attractive.
- **How competitive is the market of potential contractors?** Few bidders reduce the gains.
- **How large is the opportunity cost of tax funding?** In a country with a narrow tax base, every kel spent on one public good is a large sacrifice elsewhere.
- **How good is government information?** Without knowing valuations, provision decisions risk over- or under-supply whichever method is used.
- **Who bears the risk of failure?** With essential goods such as flood defence, the state carries the final risk under either method.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a public good as "a good provided by the government". Many government-provided goods, such as school places, are rivalrous and excludable; they are merit goods, not public goods.
- Explaining only one characteristic. Both non-rivalry and non-excludability are needed.
- Saying the free rider problem means people are dishonest. It is a rational response to non-excludability.
- Treating contracting out as privatisation. Under contracting out the government still decides and pays.
- Ignoring opportunity cost when praising direct provision.

## Exam technique

- **Define** a public good with both characteristics in one sentence.
- **Explain** the free rider problem as a chain: non-excludable, so users can benefit without paying, so firms cannot earn revenue, so the market under-provides or fails to provide.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss** the responses by setting strengths against limitations for a named good, then reaching a judgement that depends on conditions such as how measurable quality is. In Paper 1 part (b) answers, support the argument with real-world examples you have researched.
- In Paper 2, quote the text: if it describes a service no one can be excluded from, say so explicitly and name the characteristic.
