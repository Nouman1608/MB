This guide covers subtopic **8.2 Group I properties** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). The overview in [Periodic Table: Groups and Trends](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/) summarises the alkali metals in a few lines; here each trend is unpacked with data, observations and the reasoning you need to predict the behaviour of Group I elements beyond potassium.

## What the syllabus asks

The two 5070 outcomes are:

1. Describe the Group I alkali metals, **lithium, sodium and potassium**, as **relatively soft metals** with general trends down the group, limited to:
   - decreasing melting point
   - increasing density
   - increasing reactivity
2. **Predict** the properties of other elements in Group I, given information about the elements.

The phrase "limited to" matters. You are expected to describe exactly three trends for the three named metals, and then to apply them to rubidium, caesium or an unnamed Group I element from data in the question. The reactions of Group I metals with water also appear in subtopic 9.1 (general chemical properties of metals) and 9.4 (reactivity series), so the observations below serve all three subtopics.

## Why they are called alkali metals

All Group I elements react with water to form a **hydroxide**, which dissolves to give an **alkaline** solution, and **hydrogen** gas:

```
metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g)
2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
2K(s)  + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq)  + H₂(g)
```

Adding universal indicator to the water before the metal goes in shows the solution turning blue or purple as the hydroxide forms. Because every atom has one outer electron, every Group I metal forms a 1+ ion, so the hydroxide is always MOH and the chloride MCl.

## "Relatively soft metals"

Lithium, sodium and potassium can be cut with a knife, unlike iron or copper. Freshly cut surfaces are shiny but dull quickly as the metal reacts with oxygen and moisture in the air, which is why the metals are stored under oil. Softness increases down the group: lithium is the hardest of the three, potassium the softest. These metals are still metals in every other sense: they conduct electricity and heat and have metallic bonding, but the attraction holding their lattices together is weak compared with most metals, which also explains their low melting points.

## Trend 1: decreasing melting point

| Metal | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|
| Lithium | 181 |
| Sodium | 98 |
| Potassium | 63 |

All three melt below the boiling point of water at 100 °C except lithium. This is why sodium is seen to **melt into a ball** on water: the heat released by the reaction is enough to melt it. Lithium does not melt on water because its melting point is higher and its reaction slower.

## Trend 2: increasing density

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ |
|---|---|
| Lithium | 0.53 |
| Sodium | 0.97 |
| Potassium | 0.86 |

The general trend down the group is an increase in density, and the syllabus states it that way. Notice, however, that real data show potassium slightly less dense than sodium. If a question gives you a table like this and asks for the trend, describe the **general** increase and, if the question invites it, note that potassium does not fit. Elements further down (rubidium, caesium) are denser again, which confirms the general direction.

All three densities are below 1.0 g cm⁻³, the density of water, so all three **float**. This is an observation examiners credit.

## Trend 3: increasing reactivity

Reactivity is shown by how vigorously each metal reacts with cold water.

| Metal | Observations with cold water |
|---|---|
| Lithium | Floats; fizzes steadily; moves slowly on the surface; gradually disappears |
| Sodium | Floats; melts into a silvery ball; fizzes rapidly; darts about the surface; disappears quickly |
| Potassium | As sodium but more violent; the hydrogen ignites with a **lilac** flame; may spit or crackle |

**Why reactivity increases.** Each atom reacts by losing its single outer electron. Down the group, the atom has more shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more easily lost. The syllabus asks you to describe the trend, but a one-sentence explanation like this shows understanding and connects to 8.1 (similar properties from the same number of outer electrons).

## Predicting other Group I elements

Outcome 2 is where most marks are lost. A prediction must continue every trend in the right direction and keep everything the group has in common.

### Worked example: predicting rubidium

*Rubidium is below potassium in Group I. Predict its melting point, density and reaction with water, and the formula of its hydroxide.*

Model answer, annotated:

- **Melting point lower than 63 °C** (lower than potassium), because melting point decreases down Group I. *[The actual value is about 39 °C, but any value below 63 °C is creditworthy with the reason.]*
- **Density higher than potassium's**, following the general increase down the group. *[Its real density is about 1.53 g cm⁻³, greater than water's, so a careful answer does not claim that rubidium floats.]*
- **Reacts more violently with water than potassium**, possibly explosively, forming rubidium hydroxide and hydrogen, because reactivity increases down the group.
- **Hydroxide formula RbOH**, because rubidium, like all Group I metals, forms a 1+ ion.
- The solution formed is **alkaline**.

Two predictions are often missed: the formula (from the shared group chemistry, not from a trend) and the alkaline solution. Combine "what changes" (the trends) with "what stays the same" (ion charge, products, type of reaction).

### Worked example: predicting from given data

*An unknown Group I element, M, reacts so violently with water that it explodes. Its melting point is 28 °C. Deduce where M is in Group I relative to potassium and rubidium.*

M is below rubidium. Two pieces of evidence agree: its reaction is more violent than potassium's (reactivity increases down the group) and its melting point is lower than rubidium's 39 °C (melting point decreases down the group). Use **both** pieces of information when a question provides two.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "hydrogen and oxygen" or "sodium oxide" as products** with water. The products are the hydroxide and hydrogen.
- **Saying the metals sink.** Lithium, sodium and potassium all float.
- **Describing observations that cannot be seen**, such as "hydrogen is produced" when asked what is *observed*. Write "fizzing" or "bubbles".
- **Reversing the melting point trend.** Group I melting points *decrease* down the group; Group VII boiling points increase. Learn the two groups separately.
- **Giving a lilac flame for sodium.** Only potassium's reaction gives a lilac flame.
- **Predicting a different formula** for an element lower down. All Group I hydroxides are MOH and all chlorides MCl.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a trend: one direction word per property ("decreases", "increases") and the phrase "down the group".
- **Describe** observations: give things you can see or hear (floats, melts, moves, fizzes, flame colour, disappears). Three distinct observations earn three marks; repeating "bubbles" in different words does not.
- **Predict**: state the value or behaviour **and** link it to the trend or the shared group chemistry.
- **State** the products of a reaction with water: name both, and that the solution is alkaline if asked about pH.
