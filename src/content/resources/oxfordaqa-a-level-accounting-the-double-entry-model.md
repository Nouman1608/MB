## What 3.1.3 covers

| Specification statement | What you must be able to do |

| Using the double entry system | Record transactions from source documents in books of prime entry and ledger accounts; transfer accounts to income statements; balance accounts; prepare statements of financial position |
| Record adjustments in ledger accounts and financial statements | Other payables, other receivables, income due, income received in advance, irrecoverable debts (writing off and recovery), provisions for doubtful debts, depreciation charges, disposal of non-current assets, opening and closing inventory |

## Source documents, books of prime entry and ledgers

Every entry starts with a source document. It goes into a book of prime entry, and the totals or individual items are then posted to ledger accounts.

| Source document | Book of prime entry |
|---|---|
| Purchase invoice | Purchases journal |
| Sales invoice | Sales journal |
| Credit note sent to a customer | Sales returns journal |
| Credit note received from a supplier | Purchases returns journal |
| Journal voucher | General journal |
| Cheque counterfoil, till roll, cash receipt, paying-in slip counterfoil | Three column cash book |
| Bank statement (standing orders, direct debits, credit transfers, dishonoured cheques, debit card transactions, direct transfers) | Three column cash book |

The general journal records items with no other book: irrecoverable debts, contra entries, disposals, depreciation, corrections and opening entries.

Ledger accounts are subdivided into the **receivables ledger** (one account per credit customer), the **payables ledger** (one per credit supplier) and **general ledger accounts** (everything else: sales, purchases, expenses, assets, capital).

Every transaction has an equal debit and credit. Debit an increase in an asset, expense or drawings. Credit an increase in a liability, income or capital.

**Balancing an account.** Add both sides. Put the difference on the smaller side as "Balance c/d" so the two totals match. Bring it down on the opposite side as "Balance b/d" on the first day of the next period. Income and expense accounts are not carried down. Instead, the year's figure is transferred to the income statement.

## The three column cash book

The three column cash book has discount, cash and bank columns on each side. Discounts allowed go in the debit-side discount column; discounts received go in the credit-side one. The discount columns are memorandum totals. They are not balanced. At the period end their totals are posted to the Discounts allowed account (debit) and the Discounts received account (credit).

### Worked example 1

Ravelstone Garden Supplies starts May with cash 380 and bank 2,150. In May:

- 4 May: Halvorsen, a credit customer owing 1,200, pays by cheque less 2.5% cash discount.
- 9 May: pays Brightmoor Seeds 840 by cheque less 5% cash discount.
- 15 May: cash sales 610 (till roll).
- 20 May: standing order for insurance 95 (bank statement).
- 28 May: wages 420 paid in cash.

```
Dr                        Disc  Cash   Bank | Cr                        Disc  Cash   Bank
1 May  Balance b/d               380  2,150 | 9 May  Brightmoor Seeds     42          798
4 May  Halvorsen            30        1,170 | 20 May Insurance                         95
15 May Sales                     610        | 28 May Wages                     420
                                            | 31 May Balance c/d               570  2,427
                            30   990  3,320 |                             42   990  3,320
1 Jun  Balance b/d               570  2,427 |
```

Discount allowed: 1,200 × 2.5% = 30, so the bank receives 1,170. Discount received: 840 × 5% = 42, so the cheque is 798. Halvorsen's account is credited with both 1,170 and 30, clearing the full 1,200.

## Trade and cash discounts

A **trade discount** is a reduction from list price for trade customers. It is never entered in a ledger: the invoice shows the net figure, and that is what is recorded. A **cash discount** is given for prompt payment. It is recorded when payment is made.

Ravelstone buys mowers on credit with a list price of 3,200, less 15% trade discount, and 2.5% cash discount for payment within 14 days.

- Invoice value: 3,200 × 85% = 2,720. Purchases journal: debit Purchases 2,720, credit the supplier 2,720.
- Paid within 14 days: discount received 2,720 × 2.5% = 68, cheque 2,652. Debit the supplier 2,720; credit Bank 2,652 and Discounts received 68.

## Capital and revenue items

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Buying or improving non-current assets, including costs of getting them ready for use | Delivery van; legal fees on buying premises; fitting a new machine |
| Revenue expenditure | Day-to-day running costs and maintaining assets | Fuel, repairs, wages, repainting |
| Capital income | Money received that is not from normal trading | Capital introduced, loan received, proceeds of selling a non-current asset |
| Revenue income | Income from normal operations | Sales, rent received, commission received |

Capital expenditure goes to the statement of financial position and is depreciated. Revenue expenditure is charged against profit in the income statement for the year. Getting this wrong misstates both profit and assets.

## Irrecoverable debts, recovery and contra entries

**Write-off.** Customer D. Mensah owes 640 and cannot pay. Debit Irrecoverable debts 640; credit D. Mensah 640. The expense goes to the income statement.

**Recovery.** If Mensah later pays the 640, first reinstate the debt: debit D. Mensah 640; credit Irrecoverable debts recovered 640 (income). Then record the cash: debit Bank 640; credit D. Mensah 640. Reinstating first keeps a record that the customer did pay.

**Contra.** Ravelstone owes Orrin Timber 1,350 and Orrin owes Ravelstone 900. Set off the smaller amount: debit Orrin Timber (payables ledger) 900; credit Orrin Timber (receivables ledger) 900. Ravelstone now owes Orrin 450.

## Depreciation

**Straight line:** (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. A delivery van costing 27,500 with residual value 3,500 and a six-year life: (27,500 − 3,500) ÷ 6 = 4,000 a year.

**Reducing balance:** rate × carrying amount at the start of the year. Equipment costing 16,000 at 25%: year 1 = 4,000; year 2 = 25% × 12,000 = 3,000; carrying amount after two years 9,000.

Double entry: debit Depreciation (income statement expense); credit Accumulated depreciation. In the statement of financial position, show cost less accumulated depreciation equals carrying amount.

## Disposal of non-current assets

The equipment above is sold at the start of year 3 for 8,600 (no depreciation in the year of sale).

```
Disposal account
Dr Equipment (cost)             16,000 | Cr Accumulated depreciation   7,000
                                       | Cr Bank                      8,600
                                       | Cr Income statement (loss)     400
                                16,000 |                             16,000
```

Carrying amount 9,000 − proceeds 8,600 = loss on disposal 400, charged as an expense. A profit on disposal would appear on the debit side and be added to gross profit as income.

## Other payables, other receivables, income due and income received in advance

| Adjustment | Income statement | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Expense owing (other payable) | Add to expense | Current liability |
| Expense paid in advance (other receivable) | Deduct from expense | Current asset |
| Income due (not yet received) | Add to income | Current asset |
| Income received in advance | Deduct from income | Current liability |

### Worked example 2

- Electricity paid 2,310 in the year and 280 owing at the year end: expense 2,590, other payable 280.
- Commission received 1,940 and 160 still due: income 2,100, current asset 160.
- Rent received from a subtenant 3,900, of which 300 is for next year: income 3,600, current liability 300.

In the electricity ledger account, the 280 appears as Balance c/d on the debit side, the 2,590 transfer to the income statement on the credit side, and the 280 comes down as a credit balance b/d.

## Opening and closing inventory

Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − purchases returns − closing inventory. Closing inventory is a current asset at the year end and becomes next year's opening inventory. Service businesses have no cost of sales, so their income statement starts with fees or revenue and deducts expenses.

| Discounts received | | 620 |

Additional information: closing inventory 10,600; rent owing 600; general expenses include 350 of insurance paid in advance; depreciate fittings at 20% reducing balance; write off an irrecoverable debt of 400, then make the provision for doubtful debts 3% of remaining receivables.

## Common errors

- Recording a trade discount in the ledger. Only the net invoice value is recorded.
- Leaving the discount columns of the cash book unposted, or posting them to the wrong side.
- Charging the whole new provision for doubtful debts to the income statement instead of the change in it.
- Calculating reducing balance depreciation on cost instead of the carrying amount.
- Showing income received in advance as a current asset. It is a liability: the business owes the service.
- Forgetting to deduct the irrecoverable debt from receivables before calculating the provision.
- Putting drawings in the income statement. Drawings reduce equity only.
