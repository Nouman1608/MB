## Reactivity 3.2.1 Four ways to describe redox

**Oxidation** is loss of electrons, a rise in oxidation state, gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. **Reduction** is the reverse of each. The **oxidizing agent** is itself reduced; the **reducing agent** is itself oxidized.

### Assigning oxidation states

Write the sign first, as in +3 or −2.

1. An uncombined element is 0; a simple ion equals its charge.
2. Oxygen is −2 (−1 in peroxides); hydrogen is +1 (−1 in metal hydrides).
3. Neutral compound: values total zero. Ion: values total its charge.

**Worked example 1.** Find the oxidation state of the named atom.

- Cr in CrO₄²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so x = **+6**.
- H in CaH₂: calcium is +2, so 2x = −2, x = **−1** (a metal hydride).
- N in N₂O: 2x + (−2) = 0, so x = **+1**.

Transition elements and most main group non-metals have variable oxidation states: nitrogen runs from −3 in NH₄⁺ to +5 in HNO₃, and manganese from +2 in Mn²⁺ to +7 in MnO₄⁻. So names carry Roman numerals: FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride and MnO₂ is manganese(IV) oxide. Common oxyanion names such as sulfate remain acceptable.

Oxidation states treat every bond as ionic, so they are bookkeeping, not real charges. Corrosion is surface oxidation of a metal.

**Worked example 2.** In SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O, sulfur in SO₂ goes from +4 to 0 (reduced) and sulfur in H₂S goes from −2 to 0 (oxidized). So **SO₂ is the oxidizing agent** and **H₂S is the reducing agent**.

## Reactivity 3.2.2 Half-equations

To balance a half-equation in acidic solution:

1. Match up the atom whose oxidation state changes.
2. Put in water molecules to even out O atoms.
3. Put in H⁺ ions to even out H atoms.
4. Put in electrons until the charges agree.

To combine two, multiply so the electrons match, add, and cancel.

**Worked example 3.** Nitrate ions in acid oxidize tin(II) ions to tin(IV), forming nitrogen monoxide.

- Reduction: add 2H₂O on the right, 4H⁺ on the left, then 3e⁻ to balance charge: **NO₃⁻ + 4H⁺ + 3e⁻ → NO + 2H₂O**
- Oxidation: **Sn²⁺ → Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻**
- Electrons: 3 × 2 = 6, so double the first and triple the second.
- Overall: **2NO₃⁻ + 8H⁺ + 3Sn²⁺ → 2NO + 4H₂O + 3Sn⁴⁺**
- Check charge: left −2 + 8 + 6 = +12; right 3 × (+4) = +12.

In **neutral solution**, with no oxygen moving, no H⁺ is needed: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ and Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag give Mg + 2Ag⁺ → Mg²⁺ + 2Ag.

A titration with manganate(VII) is self-indicating: purple MnO₄⁻ is reduced to almost colourless Mn²⁺, so the first lasting pink marks the end point.

## Reactivity 3.2.3 Trends in a group

**Metals** lose electrons when they react. Going down groups 1 and 2, atoms get larger and outer electrons are more shielded, so they are removed more easily: ease of oxidation increases.

**Halogens** gain an electron. Down group 17 the incoming electron is further away and more shielded, so ease of reduction decreases: Cl₂ is the strongest oxidizing agent of the three.

A more easily reduced halogen displaces a less easily reduced one from its halide ions:

- Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq): the colourless solution turns yellow-orange.
- Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq): the solution turns brown.
- I₂ does not react with Cl⁻ or Br⁻.

**Metal/metal ion data.** A metal reduces the ions of any metal that is less easily oxidized. Questions supply the data.

**Worked example 4.** Each metal meets the other ions (✓ = deposit).

| Metal | Cr³⁺(aq) | Ni²⁺(aq) | Pb²⁺(aq) | Ag⁺(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cr | – | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Ni | ✗ | – | ✓ | ✓ |
| Pb | ✗ | ✗ | – | ✓ |
| Ag | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | – |

The metal reacting with most ions is oxidized most easily: **Cr > Ni > Pb > Ag**.

## Reactivity 3.2.4 Reactive metals and dilute acids

Reactive metals release hydrogen from dilute hydrochloric and sulfuric acids: the metal is oxidized and H⁺ is reduced. Bubbles form and the metal dissolves. Copper and silver do not react.

- Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
- Ni(s) + 2HCl(aq) → NiCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
- Ionic form, for any metal M forming M²⁺: M(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → M²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)

**Worked example 5.** 1.308 g of zinc reacts with excess dilute sulfuric acid. Find the volume of hydrogen at STP (use 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ and Ar(Zn) = 65.38).

- Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g), so 1 mol Zn gives 1 mol H₂.
- n(Zn) = 1.308 ÷ 65.38 = 0.02001 mol
- V(H₂) = 0.02001 × 22.7 = **0.454 dm³**

## Reactivity 3.2.5 and 3.2.6 Electrodes and primary cells

In every cell, **oxidation is at the anode, reduction at the cathode**. Only the signs change.

| Cell | Anode | Cathode |
|---|---|---|
| Voltaic (primary or secondary, discharging) | negative | positive |
| Electrolytic | positive | negative |

A voltaic anode pushes electrons into the wire (negative); an electrolytic anode has electrons pulled out by the supply (positive).

A **primary (voltaic) cell** turns the energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. Two **half-cells**, each a metal in a solution of its own ions, are joined by a wire and a **salt bridge**.

**Worked example 6.** A nickel half-cell (Ni in Ni²⁺(aq)) is joined to a silver half-cell (Ag in Ag⁺(aq)). Nickel is oxidized more easily than silver.

- Anode (negative): Ni(s) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
- Cathode (positive): Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Ag(s)
- Overall: Ni(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)
- Electrons flow through the wire **from the nickel anode to the silver cathode**.
- Use KNO₃ in the bridge; chloride would precipitate AgCl.
- **Anions** (NO₃⁻) move toward the anode half-cell, where Ni²⁺ builds up; **cations** (K⁺) move toward the cathode half-cell, where Ag⁺ is removed. This keeps both solutions neutral and completes the circuit. Electrons never pass through the bridge.

## Reactivity 3.2.7 Secondary cells, and comparing cells

A **secondary (rechargeable) cell** has reversible electrode reactions. Charging uses an external supply to drive each discharge half-equation backwards, so you deduce it by reversing that half-equation.

**Lead–acid cell, discharge:**

- Negative electrode: Pb(s) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2e⁻
- Positive electrode: PbO₂(s) + 4H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l)

**Charging** reverses both: PbSO₄(s) + 2e⁻ → Pb(s) + SO₄²⁻(aq) at the negative electrode (now the cathode), and PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l) → PbO₂(s) + 4H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ at the positive electrode (now the anode).

In a **lithium-ion cell**, discharge oxidizes lithium held in graphite (Li → Li⁺ + e⁻) and reduces a metal oxide (Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → LiCoO₂); charging reverses both.

| Cell type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | cheap; light; ready to use; long shelf life | used once; more waste; some contain toxic metals |
| Secondary | recharged many times; lower cost over its life; less waste | higher initial cost; capacity falls with use; lead and cadmium types are toxic and heavy |
| Fuel cell | runs as long as fuel is supplied; efficient; a hydrogen cell gives only water | expensive catalysts; hydrogen is hard to store; hydrogen is often made from fossil fuels |

## Reactivity 3.2.8 Electrolytic cells and molten salts

In an **electrolytic cell**, electrical energy forces a non-spontaneous redox reaction to happen: a DC supply is connected to two electrodes in an **electrolyte** (a molten or dissolved ionic compound). Current is conducted by:

- **electrons** in the external circuit, from the anode to the supply and on to the cathode;
- **ions** in the electrolyte, cations to the cathode and anions to the anode;
- electron transfer at each electrode surface.

Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: their ions are fixed in the lattice.

**Worked example 7.** Molten lithium iodide is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

- Cathode (negative), reduction: Li⁺ + e⁻ → Li
- Anode (positive), oxidation: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
- Overall: 2LiI(l) → 2Li(l) + I₂(g)

So a molten binary salt gives its **metal at the cathode** and its **non-metal element at the anode**.

## Reactivity 3.2.9 Oxidation of alcohols

Write the oxidizing agent as [O]; the reagent is not assessed. Acidified dichromate(VI) is often used, turning from orange to green.

- **Primary alcohols** oxidize in two steps: alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
- **Secondary alcohols** oxidize to ketones.
- **Tertiary alcohols** are not oxidized under the same conditions, as the carbon bearing the OH has no hydrogen atom to lose.

**Worked example 8.**

- Hexan-1-ol to hexanal: C₅H₁₁CH₂OH + [O] → C₅H₁₁CHO + H₂O
- Hexanal to hexanoic acid: C₅H₁₁CHO + [O] → C₅H₁₁COOH
- Hexan-3-ol to hexan-3-one: C₂H₅CH(OH)C₃H₇ + [O] → C₂H₅COC₃H₇ + H₂O
- 3-Methylpentan-3-ol: no reaction.

**Choosing the set-up.**

- For the **aldehyde**, **distil** as it forms. With no hydrogen bonding between its molecules, it boils below the alcohol and leaves before further oxidation.
- For the **carboxylic acid**, heat under **reflux** with excess oxidizing agent. Vapour condenses in the vertical condenser and drips back, so the aldehyde cannot escape before it is oxidized further.

## Reactivity 3.2.10 Reduction of carbonyl compounds

Write the reducing agent as [H]. Reduction runs the oxidation sequence backwards:

- **Carboxylic acid → aldehyde → primary alcohol**
- **Ketone → secondary alcohol**

The reducing agents supply **hydride ions, H⁻**: a hydrogen carrying two electrons, which adds to the slightly positive carbonyl carbon; the oxygen then gains H⁺. Reagent names and mechanisms are not assessed.

**Worked example 9.**

- 2-Methylpropanoic acid to 2-methylpropanal: (CH₃)₂CHCOOH + 2[H] → (CH₃)₂CHCHO + H₂O
- 2-Methylpropanal to 2-methylpropan-1-ol: (CH₃)₂CHCHO + 2[H] → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH
- Pentan-2-one to pentan-2-ol: CH₃COC₃H₇ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)C₃H₇

Carbon is −4 in CH₄, −2 in CH₃OH, 0 in HCHO, +2 in HCOOH and +4 in CO₂: each step along this series is an oxidation.

## Reactivity 3.2.11 Hydrogenation lowers unsaturation

Adding hydrogen across C=C or C≡C is a reduction. A catalyst (usually nickel, heated) is needed. Each H₂ added removes one degree of unsaturation.

- Alkene + H₂ → alkane: CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₃
- Alkyne + H₂ → alkene; alkyne + 2H₂ → alkane: CH₃C≡CCH₃ + 2H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃

**Worked example 10.** How much hydrogen, at STP, fully saturates 2.70 g of but-2-yne, C₄H₆? (Use 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹.)

- M(C₄H₆) = 4(12.01) + 6(1.01) = 54.10 g mol⁻¹
- n = 2.70 ÷ 54.10 = 0.04991 mol
- Two H₂ per molecule: n(H₂) = 0.09982 mol
- V = 0.09982 × 22.7 = **2.27 dm³**

## Common errors

- Leaving electrons, or unequal electron numbers, in an overall equation.
- Giving the anode the same sign in voltaic and electrolytic cells.
- Forgetting the water when going straight from alcohol to acid: RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O.
- Claiming tertiary alcohols oxidize to ketones.
