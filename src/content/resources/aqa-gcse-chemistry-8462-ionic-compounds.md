This guide covers **4.2.1.3 Ionic compounds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016 onwards (version 1.1). It sits in topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of
matter, and is examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. Nothing in it is marked (HT only), and
there is no required practical. The specification lists three skills here: deducing that a compound is
ionic from a diagram, describing the limitations of the diagrams, and working out an empirical formula
from a model.

For quick recall use the [ionic compounds revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [ionic compounds practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds-practice/).
The broader [chemical bonds and ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-bonds-and-ionic-compounds/)
introduces the lattice; this page adds more models, more formula work and a full model answer.

## The four sentences you must be able to write

The specification's description of an ionic compound breaks into four separate claims. Each can be a
marking point on its own.

1. An ionic compound is a **giant structure of ions**.
2. It is held together by **strong electrostatic forces of attraction** between **oppositely charged ions**.
3. These forces act **in all directions** in the lattice.
4. This attraction is what we call **ionic bonding**.

A "describe the structure and bonding" question usually wants points 1 to 3, plus the arrangement: a
**regular, repeating** pattern in which positive and negative ions **alternate**.

### What "giant" and "lattice" tell you

A **lattice** is a regular three-dimensional arrangement that repeats. **Giant** means the repeat goes on
for an enormous number of ions, with no natural stopping point inside the crystal. Two consequences follow,
and both come up in questions:

- **There is no single "molecule" to draw.** The formula only gives the ratio of ions, which is why it is
  called an **empirical formula**.
- **Every ion is attracted by all the oppositely charged ions near it**, not by one partner. That is the
  meaning of "in all directions".

## The sodium chloride models

You are expected to be familiar with sodium chloride, which the specification shows in several forms. You
do not need to learn the structure of any other ionic compound; a question on another compound will give
you its diagram.

| Model | What you see | What it lets you work out |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | One Na⁺ and one Cl⁻ in square brackets, with charges and outer electrons | Which electron moved, and the charge on each ion |
| Ball and stick | Small and large balls at the corners of cubes, joined by sticks | The 3D arrangement: each ion has six neighbours of the opposite charge |
| 2D diagram | A grid of alternating + and − particles | The alternating pattern and the 1 : 1 ratio |
| 3D space-filling | Large chloride spheres packed with smaller sodium spheres between them | Relative ion sizes and how tightly the ions pack |

The chloride ion is drawn larger than the sodium ion in good models; a question may ask which sphere is
which, and size is the clue.

## Skill 1: deducing that a compound is ionic

Look for **two kinds of particle with opposite charges** arranged in a **regular, repeating** pattern.
Strong supporting clues:

- a key that names a **metal ion** and a **non-metal ion** (for example "potassium ion" and "bromide ion")
- charges such as +, −, 2+ or 2− written on the particles
- square brackets around ions in a dot and cross diagram
- the pattern runs off the edge of the drawing, showing a giant structure.

Clues that point **away** from ionic: separate small groups of atoms (small molecules), lines joining one
type of atom into a network with no charges (giant covalent), or one kind of positive particle among
free electrons (metallic).

## Skill 2: limitations of the diagrams

A limitation is a **specific feature of the diagram that could mislead you** about the real lattice. Strong
answers name the feature and say what is actually true.

| If you only had this diagram… | …you might wrongly think | In reality |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | Sodium chloride is made of separate Na⁺Cl⁻ pairs | Each ion is surrounded by many oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice |
| Ball and stick | The sticks are bonds to particular neighbours, and there is empty space between ions | The attraction acts in all directions, and the ions are touching |
| 2D diagram | Each ion has only four neighbours | In 3D each ion in sodium chloride has six oppositely charged neighbours |
| 3D space-filling | The arrangement inside is unknown or irregular | The pattern continues regularly through the whole crystal, but the inner ions are hidden |

All four share one limitation: they show a **tiny fragment** of a structure that contains a vast number of
ions.

## Skill 3: working out the empirical formula

Method:

1. Use the key to identify each ion.
2. Count each kind of ion in the model (or in one repeating unit).
3. Divide by the highest common factor to get the simplest whole-number ratio.
4. Write the metal first, with subscripts only for numbers above 1.
5. If charges are known or given, check that the total charge is zero.

### Worked example 1: a ball and stick model with a key

*A model of part of an ionic lattice contains 5 magnesium ions and 10 chloride ions. Work out the
empirical formula.*

```
Mg : Cl = 5 : 10
Divide by 5:  1 : 2
Empirical formula: MgCl2  (written MgCl₂)
Check: Mg is Group 2 -> Mg2+; Cl is Group 7 -> Cl-
       (+2) + 2 x (-1) = 0, so the ratio is consistent
```

### Worked example 2: charges given in the key

*A diagram of an iron oxide lattice shows 8 iron ions, labelled Fe³⁺, and 12 oxide ions, labelled O²⁻.
Work out the empirical formula.*

```
Fe : O = 8 : 12
Divide by 4:  2 : 3
Empirical formula: Fe2O3  (written Fe₂O₃)
Check: 2 x (+3) + 3 x (-2) = +6 - 6 = 0
```

Iron is not in Groups 1, 2, 6 or 7, so you are not expected to know its ion charge; the key supplies it.
Counting does the real work, and the charges only confirm it.

### Worked example 3: a fragment with an odd count

*A 2D drawing of one layer of magnesium oxide is a 5 × 5 grid of alternating ions, with magnesium ions in
all four corners. A student counts 13 magnesium ions and 12 oxide ions. What is the empirical formula?*

```
13 : 12 does not simplify, but the drawing is a cut-off fragment.
In an odd-sized alternating grid, the corner type appears once more.
Mg2+ and O2- carry equal and opposite charges, so a neutral lattice
needs equal numbers: Mg : O = 1 : 1
Empirical formula: MgO
```

When the count from a small drawing gives an awkward ratio, look at the repeating pattern rather than the
raw totals. In the middle of the grid, every Mg²⁺ alternates with an O²⁻.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Describe the structure and bonding in solid sodium chloride.*

> Sodium chloride has a **giant ionic lattice** *(structure named)*. It contains **positive sodium ions and
> negative chloride ions** *(both ions with charges)* arranged in a **regular pattern** in which the ions
> **alternate** *(arrangement)*. The ions are held together by **strong electrostatic forces of attraction
> between the oppositely charged ions** *(the bond)*, and these forces **act in all directions** through the
> lattice *(the feature most often missed)*.

The answer avoids the word "molecule" entirely, which is the single most common way to lose credit here.

## Exam technique

- **Deduce:** state your conclusion, then the evidence from the diagram, such as "ionic, because it shows
  positive and negative ions in a regular repeating lattice".
- **Describe a limitation:** name the diagram feature and contrast it with reality. "It is not accurate" is
  not a limitation.
- **Work out:** show the counts and the simplified ratio, not just the formula; method marks depend on it.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a formula straight from raw counts (Mg₅Cl₁₀) instead of the simplest ratio.
- Putting the non-metal first (Cl₂Mg).
- Assuming every ionic compound has a 1 : 1 ratio like sodium chloride.
- Saying the ions in a lattice are "far apart" because a ball and stick model shows gaps.
- Describing the bonding as "between a sodium and a chlorine atom". The particles are ions.
