This guide covers **4.3.5 Use of amount of substance in relation to volumes of gases (chemistry only) (HT only)**
of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). The
whole subsection is marked (HT only), so it is **Higher Tier** content on Paper 1. The "chemistry only" label
separates it from combined science; in this qualification it is examined in full. There is no required
practical named in this subsection, although collecting gas volumes appears in practical work elsewhere.

## The molar volume of a gas

At the same temperature and pressure, **one mole of any gas occupies the same volume**. At **room temperature
and pressure (RTP)**, which means 20 °C and 1 atmosphere, that volume is **24 dm³**.

```
volume of gas (dm3) = moles x 24
moles = volume (dm3) / 24
1 dm3 = 1000 cm3, so 24 dm3 = 24 000 cm3
```

This is true for hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide or any other gas, because the particles of a gas are far
apart and the volume of the gas depends on the number of particles, not on their size or mass.

## From mass to volume

To find the volume from a mass, convert the mass to moles first (mass ÷ Mr), then multiply by 24.

### Worked example 1: volume of carbon dioxide

*Calculate the volume of 4.4 g of carbon dioxide at room temperature and pressure.*

```
Mr CO2 = 12 + 32 = 44
moles = 4.4 / 44 = 0.10 mol
volume = 0.10 x 24 = 2.4 dm3
```

### Worked example 2: volume of hydrogen

*Calculate the volume of 1.0 g of hydrogen gas, H₂, at room temperature and pressure.*

```
Mr H2 = 2;  moles = 1.0 / 2 = 0.50 mol
volume = 0.50 x 24 = 12 dm3
```

Hydrogen has a small mass but a large volume, because one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³.

### Worked example 3: from volume to mass

*What is the mass of 4.8 dm³ of oxygen, O₂, at room temperature and pressure?*

```
moles = 4.8 / 24 = 0.20 mol
Mr O2 = 32
mass = 0.20 x 32 = 6.4 g
```

## From a balanced equation

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the ratio of moles. For gases, equal moles occupy equal volumes, so
the coefficients also give the **ratio of volumes** at the same temperature and pressure.

### Worked example 4: volume ratio

*2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g). What volume of oxygen reacts with 10 dm³ of hydrogen, and what volume of steam forms?*

```
Ratio H2 : O2 : H2O = 2 : 1 : 2
Oxygen = 10 / 2 = 5 dm3
Steam  = 10 dm3
```

### Worked example 5: methane burning

*CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g). 3.0 dm³ of methane burns completely. Calculate the volume of oxygen needed
and the volume of carbon dioxide formed (all at the same temperature and pressure).*

```
Ratio CH4 : O2 : CO2 = 1 : 2 : 1
Oxygen = 2 x 3.0 = 6.0 dm3
Carbon dioxide = 3.0 dm3
```

### Worked example 6: Haber process volumes

*N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). 20 dm³ of nitrogen reacts completely with hydrogen. Calculate the volumes of
hydrogen and ammonia.*

```
Ratio N2 : H2 : NH3 = 1 : 3 : 2
Hydrogen = 3 x 20 = 60 dm3
Ammonia  = 2 x 20 = 40 dm3
```

## Volumes from reacting masses

When a solid or solution reacts to give a gas, use the mole method and finish with a volume.

### Worked example 7: carbon dioxide from a carbonate

*5.0 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Calculate the
volume of carbon dioxide produced at room temperature and pressure, in dm³ and cm³.*

```
Mr CaCO3 = 100;  moles = 5.0 / 100 = 0.050 mol
Ratio CaCO3 : CO2 = 1 : 1, so 0.050 mol CO2
volume = 0.050 x 24 = 1.2 dm3 = 1200 cm3
```

### Worked example 8: hydrogen from a metal

*6.0 g of magnesium reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume of hydrogen made.*

```
Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2
moles Mg = 6.0 / 24 = 0.25 mol;  moles H2 = 0.25 mol
volume = 0.25 x 24 = 6.0 dm3
```

### Worked example 9: finding Mr from a gas volume

*0.60 dm³ of a gas at room temperature and pressure has a mass of 1.1 g. Calculate its relative formula mass.*

```
moles = 0.60 / 24 = 0.025 mol
Mr = mass / moles = 1.1 / 0.025 = 44
```

## Changing the subject of the equation

The key rearrangements are:

| Quantity wanted | Equation |
|---|---|
| Volume of gas (dm³) | moles × 24 |
| Moles of gas | volume (dm³) ÷ 24 |
| Moles from mass | mass ÷ Mr |
| Mr | mass ÷ moles |
| Mass | moles × Mr |

## Gas volumes in practical work

In the laboratory, a gas made in a reaction can be collected in a gas syringe or by displacing water from an
upturned measuring cylinder. The volume is read in cm³. To compare with a calculation in dm³, divide by 1000.

### Worked example 10: comparing experiment with theory (HT only)

*0.12 g of magnesium reacts with excess dilute acid and 118 cm³ of hydrogen is collected at room temperature and
pressure. Calculate the theoretical volume and the percentage of it that was collected.*

```
Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2
moles Mg = 0.12 / 24 = 0.0050 mol;  moles H2 = 0.0050 mol
theoretical volume = 0.0050 x 24 = 0.12 dm3 = 120 cm3
collected as a percentage = 118 / 120 x 100 = 98.3% (3 s.f.)
```

The small difference could be gas lost while connecting the apparatus, or measurement uncertainty in the syringe
reading. Gas volumes depend on temperature and pressure, so a value of 24 dm³ per mole is correct only at room
temperature and pressure.

## Why the same volume does not mean the same mass

One mole of hydrogen (2 g) and one mole of carbon dioxide (44 g) both occupy 24 dm³ at room temperature and
pressure. The gas particles are very far apart, so the space taken up depends on how many particles there are, not
on how heavy each one is. That is why **the same volume of different gases contains the same number of molecules**,
but has a different mass.

| Gas | Mr | Mass of 24 dm³ | Mass of 1 dm³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | 2 | 2 g | 0.083 g |
| Nitrogen | 28 | 28 g | 1.17 g |
| Carbon dioxide | 44 | 44 g | 1.83 g |

## Common mistakes

- Using 24 when the volume is in cm³ without converting (24 000 cm³).
- Using the molar volume at the wrong conditions. 24 dm³ applies only at room temperature and pressure.
- Using the mass ratio rather than the mole ratio from the equation.
- Mixing up Mr of the gas, for example using atomic mass for a diatomic gas (H₂ is 2, not 1).
- Applying the volume ratio to a liquid or solid. It works only for gases.
- Leaving out the unit.

## Exam technique

- **Mass to volume:** moles, ratio (if needed), then × 24.
- **Volume to volume:** use the coefficients as a ratio. No Mr needed.
- **State the conditions:** write "at room temperature and pressure" when giving 24 dm³.
- **Check the size:** a gas volume in dm³ should usually be of the order of a few dm³ for a few grams of a light gas.
