Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251 is assessed by two externally assessed papers of equal weight but
unequal length. Paper 1 (Research Methods, Identity and Inequality) runs 2 hours for 80 marks and 50%
of the qualification; Paper 2 (Family, Education and Crime) runs 1 hour 45 minutes, also for 80 marks
and 50%. These notes turn that asymmetry into a preparation plan and complement the site's guides to
[Research Methods, Identity and Inequality](/resources/o-level-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/)
and [Family, Education and Crime](/resources/o-level-sociology-family-education-crime/).

## Equal marks, unequal time -- Paper 2 is the tighter paper

Both papers carry 80 marks, but Paper 1 allows 120 minutes and Paper 2 allows 105. That works out at
1.5 minutes per mark on Paper 1 and about 1.3 on Paper 2 -- a difference of roughly 15 minutes across
the same mark total. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise Paper 2 answers to the tighter budget
specifically. Rehearsing both papers at the same comfortable pace produces a candidate who finishes
Paper 1 calmly and Paper 2 in a rush, which is exactly the wrong way round for two components that
count equally.

## Research methods is not a Paper 1 topic -- it is a tool for both papers

Methods sits in Paper 1's content, but methodological awareness improves answers across the whole
qualification: knowing why a study used interviews rather than a questionnaire, or what a
correlation can and cannot show, is directly usable when evaluating evidence about the family,
education or crime. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise methods early rather than last, and then
deliberately apply methods vocabulary when practising Paper 2 answers. An evaluation that names the
methodological limitation of the evidence it cites reads very differently from one that simply
asserts a counter-view.

## Evaluation questions need a genuine two-sided treatment

The higher-tariff questions on both papers reward weighing perspectives against each other and
reaching a supported judgement, not describing one position at length. **Exam-preparation priority**:
practise the structure rather than accumulating more content. For each major debate in the syllabus,
be able to state the position, one piece of supporting evidence, the strongest objection to it, and
which way you come down and why. Content knowledge is rarely the limiting factor at this level;
structure usually is.

## Concepts must be defined and then used, not just named

Sociological terms -- socialisation, stratification, social control, subculture -- earn marks when
they are defined accurately and then applied to the specific question, and earn very little when
sprinkled through an answer as vocabulary. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each key concept,
practise a one-sentence definition plus one sentence applying it to a concrete example from the
syllabus. That pairing is what an examiner can credit.

## Identity and inequality reward contemporary, specific examples

Paper 1's identity and inequality content is about how real social differences work, and answers
improve markedly when they cite specific patterns -- in gender, ethnicity, class, age or disability --
rather than generalities. **Exam-preparation priority**: build a small bank of concrete examples you
can deploy under pressure, and check they are current enough to be credible. Two well-chosen specific
examples do more work than a paragraph of general claims about inequality.

## Worked structure: an evaluation answer

The structure below is an original planning model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any
official mark scheme, past paper or examiner report.

```
Step 1 - state the position the question puts to you:
Name the perspective or claim precisely, and define its key term.

Step 2 - give the strongest support for it:
One piece of evidence or reasoning, stated specifically -- a named
pattern, study type, or social process, not "some sociologists say".

Step 3 - give the strongest objection to it:
Not a weak objection you can easily dismiss. The marks for balance
depend on the counter-position being taken seriously.

Step 4 - add the methodological note where you can:
What kind of evidence is this, and what are its limits? This is
where Paper 1's methods content pays off in a Paper 2 answer.

Step 5 - judge, and say on what basis:
"On balance X, because..." -- the reason matters more than the side
you take.
```

Step 5 is the step most often missing. An answer that presents both sides fairly and then stops has
done the harder half of the work and declined the mark for it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise Paper 2 answers to a tighter 1.3-minutes-per-mark budget than Paper
  1's 1.5; revise research methods early and then reuse its vocabulary in Paper 2 practice; for each
  major debate, rehearse position, evidence, strongest objection, judgement; build a small bank of
  specific, current examples for identity and inequality.
- **During either paper**: define each key concept you use, then apply it -- never leave a term
  standing unexplained.
- **On every evaluation question**: give the counter-position its strongest form, and finish with an
  explicit judgement and the reason for it.
- **When citing evidence**: note what kind of evidence it is and what that kind can and cannot show.

## Self-test

1. Which paper is the tighter one on time, and by roughly how much per mark?
2. Why should research methods be revised early rather than last?
3. What makes a two-sided answer score well, and what commonly stops it short of full marks?
4. What is the difference between naming a concept and using one?

**Answers:** 1. Paper 2 -- it allows 105 minutes for the same 80 marks that Paper 1 answers in 120,
about 1.3 minutes per mark against 1.5. 2. Because methodological awareness is usable across the whole
qualification: it lets you evaluate the evidence cited in family, education and crime answers, not
just answer Paper 1's methods questions. 3. Taking the counter-position in its strongest form scores
well; what most often stops an answer short is presenting both sides fairly and then omitting an
explicit, reasoned judgement. 4. Naming leaves the term as vocabulary; using it means defining it
accurately and then applying it to the specific case the question raises, which is what can be
credited.
