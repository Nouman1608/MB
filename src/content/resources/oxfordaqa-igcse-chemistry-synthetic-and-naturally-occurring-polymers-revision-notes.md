## Key words

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monomer | A small molecule that can join with many others to make a polymer |
| Polymer | A very large molecule formed from many monomers joined together |
| Polymerisation | The reaction in which monomers join to form a polymer |
| Unsaturated | Has a C=C double bond, as every alkene monomer does |
| Saturated | Only single C–C bonds (the polymer chain) |
| Thermosoftening polymer | Separate tangled chains; softens and melts on heating |
| Thermosetting polymer | Chains joined by cross-links; does not melt on heating |
| Cross-link | A covalent bond (or short bridge of atoms) joining one chain to another |
| Biodegradable | Can be broken down by microbes |
| LD / HD poly(ethene) | Low-density and high-density forms of poly(ethene), made under different conditions |

## 3.10.2 a: Polymerisation of alkenes

What happens: one bond of each C=C opens, and the freed bonds link neighbouring monomers into one long chain. Nothing else is made.

Examples named in the specification: ethene → poly(ethene); propene → poly(propene).

### Method in steps: monomer → polymer

1. Copy the two C=C carbons with all their attached atoms or groups.
2. Make the C=C a C–C.
3. Draw one bond out of each side.
4. Bracket the unit with the outgoing bonds crossing the brackets; write n after it.
5. Put n in front of the monomer in the equation.

### Method in steps: polymer → monomer

1. Pick out the two-carbon repeating unit.
2. Delete the two bonds that cross the brackets, and the n.
3. Put a double bond between the two carbons.
4. Keep every side group where it was.

### Worked reminder

Monomer: CH₂=CH–CN. Chain unit: –( CH₂–CH(CN) )n–. The CN group stays on its carbon as a side group.

Chain unit given as –( CH(CH₃)–CH₂ )n–. Monomer: CH(CH₃)=CH₂, which is propene written the other way round.

### Numbers you can be asked

- Mr of one chain = number of units × Mr of monomer.
- Mass of polymer formed = mass of monomer used (only one product).
- Example: a poly(ethene) chain of 4000 units has Mr = 4000 × 28 = **112 000**.

### Monomer or polymer? Use bromine water

| Sample | Bromine water |
|---|---|
| Alkene monomer | Orange → colourless |
| Polymer chain | Stays orange |

## 3.10.2 b: Same monomer, different polymer

Properties depend on (1) the monomer used and (2) the conditions, including the catalyst.

| | LD poly(ethene) | HD poly(ethene) |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | Ethene | Ethene |
| Conditions | Very high pressure, high temperature, a starter substance | Lower pressure, milder temperature, a special catalyst |
| Chains | Highly branched | Mainly unbranched |
| Packing | Loose | Close |
| Forces between chains | Weaker | Stronger |
| Properties | Lower density, flexible, softens at lower temperature | Higher density, rigid, softens at higher temperature |
| Suits | Thin films, squeezable items | Rigid containers, pipes |

Chain of reasoning to learn: conditions → branching → packing → strength of intermolecular forces → properties.

## 3.10.2 c: Thermosoftening vs thermosetting

| | Thermosoftening | Thermosetting |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Individual, tangled chains | Chains joined by cross-links |
| Holding chains together | Weak intermolecular forces | Strong covalent cross-links |
| On heating | Forces overcome, chains slide, polymer softens and melts | Cross-links hold chains in place; no melting (chars if heated strongly) |
| Reshape by heating? | Yes | No |

### Model sentence for the explanation

"The chains in a thermosoftening polymer are held to each other only by weak intermolecular forces. Little energy is needed to overcome these, so on heating the chains slide past one another and the polymer softens."

What breaks: the forces **between** chains. What does not break: the covalent bonds **within** each chain.

## 3.10.2 d: New materials and uses

You do not need names. Be ready to match a property to a use.

| Area | Property that matters |
|---|---|
| New packaging | Light, tough, keeps food dry and fresh |
| Waterproof fabric coatings | Keep rain out, let sweat vapour escape |
| Dental polymers | Hard, durable, safe in the mouth, set after shaping |
| Wound dressings | Absorb fluid, keep the wound moist, peel off cleanly |
| Hydrogels | Hold large amounts of water without dissolving |
| Smart materials, such as shape memory polymers | Change in response to their surroundings, for example returning to a set shape when warmed |

How new materials are developed: identify the need → choose monomers and conditions → make and test samples → improve → consider cost, safety and disposal.

## 3.10.2 e and f: Waste and cornstarch

**Problem.** Many polymers are not biodegradable, so microbes do not break them down.

- Landfill: waste persists for a long time and sites fill up.
- Litter: lasts for years, looks unsightly, harms wildlife.

**Two responses in the specification.**

| | Polymer + cornstarch bag | Biodegradable plastic from cornstarch |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | An ordinary polymer mixed with cornstarch | A plastic made from cornstarch itself |
| What happens | Microbes break down the starch, so the bag falls apart more easily | The whole material can be broken down by microbes |
| Drawback | Polymer fragments may remain | Needs land to grow the crop; breakdown needs suitable conditions |

Cornstarch comes from plants, so it is renewable; most synthetic polymers come from crude oil.

## Model answers to learn

**Why HD poly(ethene) is more rigid than LD poly(ethene).** "HD poly(ethene) is made with a different catalyst and under different conditions, so its chains have few branches. Unbranched chains pack closely together, so the intermolecular forces between them are stronger and the chains are harder to move."

**Evaluating a polymer–cornstarch bag.** "Microbes digest the starch, so the bag breaks into pieces more quickly than an ordinary plastic bag and causes less long-lasting litter. However, the polymer pieces left behind may still not biodegrade."

## Must-know distinctions

- **Monomer vs polymer:** the monomer has C=C and decolourises bromine water; the polymer does not.
- **Intermolecular forces vs cross-links:** weak forces between separate chains vs strong covalent bonds joining chains.
- **Thermosoftening vs thermosetting:** melts and can be remoulded vs does not melt.
- **LD vs HD poly(ethene):** same monomer, different conditions and catalysts, different branching.
- **Breaks down vs biodegrades:** a polymer–cornstarch bag falls into pieces; a biodegradable plastic is digested by microbes.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the term polymer.
2. Name the polymer made from propene.
3. Draw the chain unit formed from CH₂=CHBr.
4. Give the monomer of the chain unit –( CCl₂–CH₂ )n–.
5. A poly(propene) chain has Mr 105 000. How many propene units does it contain? (Ar: C = 12, H = 1)
6. A factory uses 3.6 tonnes of ethene and all of it polymerises. What mass of poly(ethene) forms?
7. Why do LD and HD poly(ethene) have different densities when both are made from ethene?
8. What holds the chains together in a thermosoftening polymer?
9. Why does a thermosetting polymer not melt when heated?
10. What does biodegradable mean?
11. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of bags made from polymers and cornstarch.
12. State what you would see when bromine water is shaken with a sample of poly(ethene).

### Answers

1. A very large molecule made from many small molecules (monomers) joined together.
2. Poly(propene).
3. –( CH₂–CHBr )n–, with single C–C, bonds through the brackets and n after.
4. CCl₂=CH₂.
5. Mr of C₃H₆ = 42; 105 000 ÷ 42 = **2500** units.
6. **3.6 tonnes**: the polymer is the only product, so mass is conserved.
7. Different conditions and catalysts give chains with different amounts of branching; less branching lets chains pack more closely, so HD is denser.
8. Weak intermolecular forces.
9. Strong covalent cross-links join the chains so they cannot slide past each other.
10. It can be broken down by microbes.
11. Advantage: breaks down more easily, so less long-lasting waste. Disadvantage: the polymer part may remain as fragments (or land is used to grow the crop).
12. It stays orange: the chain is saturated.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the polymer with C=C still in the chain.
- Missing the bonds through the brackets, or missing n.
- Placing a side group, such as Cl or CH₃, in the main chain.
- Saying heating breaks covalent bonds in a thermosoftening polymer.
- Describing cross-links as weak forces, or intermolecular forces as bonds within a chain.
- Writing that HD and LD poly(ethene) use different monomers.
- Listing uses of new materials without linking each to a property.
- Saying a polymer–cornstarch bag disappears completely.
- Defining biodegradable without mentioning microbes.
