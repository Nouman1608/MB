This guide covers subtopic **4.2, Bonding and structure**, from Topic 4, States of matter, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The [States of Matter: Ideal Gases and Structure](/resources/as-states-of-matter-ideal-gases-and-structure/) guide summarises the four structure types in a table; this page describes each named lattice in the detail a "describe" question expects, explains solubility (the property most often under-answered), and works through data-based deductions. Condensed notes are in the [bonding and structure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-bonding-and-structure-revision-notes/), and you can practise with the [bonding and structure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-bonding-and-structure-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. describe, in simple terms, the lattice structure of a crystalline solid which is: **giant ionic**, including sodium chloride and magnesium oxide; **simple molecular**, including iodine, buckminsterfullerene C₆₀ and ice; **giant molecular**, including silicon(IV) oxide, graphite and diamond; **giant metallic**, including copper
2. describe, interpret and predict the effect of different types of structure and bonding on physical properties, including **melting point, boiling point, electrical conductivity and solubility**
3. **deduce** the type of structure and bonding present in a substance from given information

## The key question: what has to be overcome?

Every property explanation in this subtopic starts by identifying **which attraction must be overcome** when the substance melts, boils or dissolves.

| Structure | What is overcome on melting |
|---|---|
| Giant ionic | electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (ionic bonds) |
| Simple molecular | intermolecular forces only; covalent bonds stay intact |
| Giant molecular | many strong covalent bonds |
| Giant metallic | electrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons |

## Describing the named lattices

### Giant ionic: sodium chloride and magnesium oxide

A regular three-dimensional lattice of alternating positive and negative ions. In both NaCl and MgO each ion is surrounded by **six** ions of the opposite charge. The attraction acts in all directions. MgO has the same arrangement but **doubly charged, smaller** ions, so its attraction is stronger (MgO melts at about 2850 °C; NaCl at 801 °C).

### Simple molecular: iodine, C₆₀ and ice

- **Iodine**: I₂ molecules (each a strong covalent I–I bond) arranged in a regular lattice, held together by weak **id-id forces**. Iodine melts at 114 °C and sublimes readily on warming.
- **Buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀**: each molecule is a closed, ball-shaped cage of 60 carbon atoms, with each carbon bonded to three others in a pattern of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons. The balls are held together in the solid by **id-id forces**. Because these act between whole molecules, C₆₀ has a much lower sublimation temperature than graphite or diamond, which are giant structures of carbon.
- **Ice**: H₂O molecules, each **hydrogen-bonded to four others** in a tetrahedral arrangement, giving an open lattice. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest intermolecular force, but it is still much weaker than covalent bonds, so ice melts at 0 °C.

### Giant molecular: silicon(IV) oxide, diamond and graphite

- **Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂**: each silicon atom is covalently bonded to **four** oxygen atoms in a tetrahedral arrangement, and each oxygen atom to **two** silicon atoms. The network extends through the whole crystal. Quartz melts at about 1700 °C.
- **Diamond**: each carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four** others, tetrahedrally, in a rigid three-dimensional network. All four outer electrons are used in localised bonds.
- **Graphite**: carbon atoms form flat **layers of hexagons**, each carbon bonded to **three** others. The fourth outer electron of each carbon is **delocalised** across the layer. The layers are held together by weak **id-id forces**.

### Giant metallic: copper

A regular lattice of **positive copper ions** surrounded by **delocalised electrons**, held by electrostatic attraction in all directions. Copper melts at 1085 °C.

## Explaining the four properties

### Melting and boiling points

High when strong bonds throughout a **giant** structure must be overcome (ionic, giant molecular, metallic). Low for **simple molecular** substances because only intermolecular forces are overcome. Within simple molecular substances, the strength order of intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding > pd-pd > id-id, and more electrons → stronger id-id) explains differences, which is why C₆₀ sublimes at a higher temperature than I₂.

**Classic comparison: CO₂ and SiO₂.** Both have formula XO₂. CO₂ is simple molecular: separate O=C=O molecules held by weak id-id forces, so it is a gas at room temperature (it sublimes at −78 °C). SiO₂ is giant molecular: melting needs many Si–O covalent bonds to break, so it melts at about 1700 °C. The formula alone does not tell you the structure.

### Electrical conductivity

Conduction needs **mobile charged particles**.

| Structure | Solid | Liquid | Charge carriers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | no | yes (also in aqueous solution) | ions, once free to move |
| Simple molecular | no | no | none |
| Diamond, SiO₂ | no | — | none (all electrons in localised bonds) |
| Graphite | yes, along the layers | — | delocalised electrons within each layer |
| Giant metallic | yes | yes | delocalised electrons |

Graphite conducts well **along** its layers but poorly **between** them, because the delocalised electrons move within a layer.

### Solubility

Solubility depends on whether the attractions that form between solute and solvent can compensate for breaking the attractions in the solute and in the solvent.

- **Ionic compounds** often dissolve in **water**. Water is polar: the δ− oxygen atoms are attracted to cations and the δ+ hydrogen atoms to anions. These ion–water attractions help to separate the ions from the lattice. (Not every ionic compound dissolves; MgO is only very slightly soluble.) Ionic compounds are generally insoluble in non-polar solvents such as hexane, which cannot attract ions.
- **Simple molecular, non-polar** substances, such as iodine and C₆₀, dissolve better in **non-polar solvents** (iodine dissolves in hexane; C₆₀ dissolves in some organic solvents) than in water. Water molecules hydrogen bond strongly to each other and would have to give up those attractions.
- **Simple molecular substances that can hydrogen bond with water**, such as ethanol, dissolve in water.
- **Giant molecular** substances (diamond, graphite, SiO₂) are **insoluble** in all common solvents: no solvent attraction can compensate for breaking covalent bonds.
- **Metals** are insoluble in water and organic solvents (some react with water, which is a chemical change, not dissolving).

## Deducing structure from data

### Worked example 1

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts as solid? | Conducts as liquid? | Soluble in water? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 1610 | no | no | no |
| Q | −95 | no | no | no; soluble in hexane |
| R | 770 | no | yes | yes |
| S | 660 | yes | yes | no |

*(Invented data for practice.)*

- **P**: very high melting point, no conduction in either state, insoluble → **giant molecular** (like SiO₂).
- **Q**: low melting point, non-conductor, dissolves in a non-polar solvent → **simple molecular**, non-polar.
- **R**: high melting point, conducts only when liquid, soluble in water → **giant ionic**.
- **S**: high melting point, conducts in both states → **giant metallic**.

### Worked example 2: an annotated explanation

**Question.** Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. [3]

- In diamond each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds, using all four outer electrons. *(one mark)*
- In graphite each carbon forms three bonds, so one outer electron per atom is delocalised over the layer. *(one mark)*
- These delocalised electrons can move and carry charge; diamond has no mobile electrons. *(one mark)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying simple molecular substances melt by "breaking covalent bonds".
- Calling graphite "simple molecular" because it has weak forces between layers; it is giant molecular, and melting breaks covalent bonds within layers.
- Saying ionic solids conduct because they contain ions; the ions must be **free to move**.
- Treating C₆₀ as giant molecular. It is simple molecular: separate C₆₀ molecules.
- Answering solubility questions with "like dissolves like" and nothing else. Name the attractions involved.
- Deducing structure from melting point alone; use **all** the data given.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a lattice: name the particles, how each is bonded or arranged (with the numbers 4, 3, 6 where relevant), and the force between particles.
- **Explain** a property: structure → what must be overcome → how strong → energy needed (or mobile charge carriers for conductivity).
- **Deduce**: work through each property in turn and eliminate structures, as in worked example 1.
