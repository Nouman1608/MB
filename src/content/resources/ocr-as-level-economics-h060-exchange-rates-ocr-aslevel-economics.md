This guide covers **Exchange rates** in The global context, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The specification asks you to explain and calculate exchange rates; to explain, with the aid of a diagram, the determination of exchange rates in fixed and floating exchange rate systems; and to evaluate the causes and consequences of exchange rate changes and the advantages and disadvantages of different exchange rate systems. All rates and figures below are invented, including those for real currencies.

## 1. What an exchange rate is

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency expressed in terms of another. If £1 = $1.30, the pound's price is 1.30 dollars, and equally the dollar's price is 1 ÷ 1.30 = £0.769.

A rise in one currency is a fall in the other. The vocabulary depends on the system:

- In a **floating** system a currency **appreciates** (rises in value) or **depreciates** (falls).
- In a **fixed** system the authorities **revalue** (raise the fixed rate) or **devalue** (lower it).

A depreciation of the pound means each pound buys less foreign currency, not that UK prices have fallen.

## 2. Calculating with exchange rates

Three calculations come up again and again.

**Converting a price.** Foreign-currency price = home price × exchange rate (foreign currency per unit of home currency). Home price = foreign price ÷ exchange rate.

**Percentage change in a rate.** (New rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100, always for the currency the question names.

**Worked example 1.** The pound falls from £1 = $1.30 to £1 = $1.17.

| | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value of £1 in dollars | 1.30 | 1.17 | −10.0% |
| Value of $1 in pounds | 0.769 | 0.855 | +11.1% |
| UK export priced at £40, price in the US | $52.00 | $46.80 | −10.0% |
| US import priced at $26, price in the UK | £20.00 | £22.22 | +11.1% |

The pound has depreciated by 10% against the dollar, but the dollar has appreciated by 11.1% against the pound, because each percentage is measured from a different base. A UK exporter that keeps its pound price unchanged becomes 10% cheaper to American buyers. A UK firm buying American inputs pays 11.1% more in pounds.

The exporter can pass the lower price on to sell more, or keep its dollar price at $52 and earn 52 ÷ 1.17 = £44.44 per sale instead of £40. Its choice affects how far trade volumes change.

## 3. Determination in a floating system

In a floating system the exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of the currency on the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the central bank.

The diagram has the **price of the pound in dollars** on the vertical axis and the **quantity of pounds traded** per period on the horizontal axis. Demand for pounds slopes downwards (a cheaper pound makes UK goods and assets cheaper to foreigners); supply slopes upwards.

| Demand for pounds comes from | Supply of pounds comes from |
|---|---|
| Foreign buyers of UK exports of goods and services | UK buyers of imports |
| Foreign tourists visiting the UK | UK tourists travelling abroad |
| Foreign firms investing in UK factories and offices | UK firms investing abroad |
| Foreign savers buying UK shares, bonds and bank deposits | UK savers buying foreign assets |
| Speculators expecting the pound to rise | Speculators expecting the pound to fall |

**Worked example 2.** Invented daily schedules for the pound (quantities in £ million):

| Price of £1 ($) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 1.10 | 980 | 820 |
| 1.20 | 940 | 860 |
| 1.30 | 900 | 900 |
| 1.40 | 860 | 940 |
| 1.50 | 820 | 980 |

The equilibrium rate is **$1.30**, with £900 million traded. Now a rise in UK interest rates attracts foreign savings and demand rises by £80 million at every rate. At $1.30 there is now excess demand of 80. The new demand schedule (980 at $1.30, 940 at $1.40) meets supply at **$1.40**, with £940 million traded. The pound has appreciated by 0.10 ÷ 1.30 × 100 = **7.7%**.

On the diagram, shift D to the right and mark the new, higher rate and quantity. A rise in supply of pounds, for example from more UK imports, shifts S to the right and lowers the rate.

## 4. Determination in a fixed system

In a fixed (pegged) system the government or central bank sets the exchange rate against another currency, or a basket of currencies, and acts to hold it there. Demand and supply still operate; the authorities offset any gap between them at the fixed rate.

**Tools to hold the peg:**

- **Buying or selling currency.** If there is excess supply of the home currency at the peg, the central bank buys it using its **foreign currency reserves**. If there is excess demand, it sells home currency and adds to its reserves.
- **Changing interest rates.** A higher interest rate attracts financial inflows, raising demand for the currency; a lower rate does the reverse.
- **Controls** limiting currency moved abroad.

**Worked example 3.** Suppose the pound in worked example 2 were pegged at $1.30 and demand fell by £80 million a day, to 820 at $1.30. Supply at $1.30 is 900, so excess supply is **£80 million a day**. To stop the pound falling, the central bank buys £80 million a day, paying 80 × 1.30 = **$104 million** a day from its reserves. With reserves of $5.2 billion, it could keep this up for 5,200 ÷ 104 = **50 days**. If the pressure lasts longer, it must raise interest rates or accept a devaluation.

On the diagram, the peg is a horizontal line; after demand shifts left, the horizontal gap between demand and supply at the peg is what the central bank must buy.

## 5. Evaluating the causes of exchange rate changes

| Cause | Direction of effect on the currency |
|---|---|
| Interest rates rise relative to other countries | Appreciation, as savers move money in |
| Inflation higher than in other countries | Depreciation over time, as exports lose competitiveness |
| Stronger demand for the country's exports | Appreciation |
| More inward direct investment | Appreciation |
| Speculators expect a fall, or political uncertainty | Depreciation, as money moves out |

**Evaluation points.**

- **Time scale.** Short-term movements are driven mostly by financial flows and expectations, which dwarf trade flows; long-run trends reflect relative inflation and competitiveness.
- **Relative and expected.** A UK rate rise causes little appreciation if other central banks match it, or if markets already expected it.

## 6. Evaluating the consequences of exchange rate changes

Take a depreciation; an appreciation broadly reverses each effect.

- **Current account.** Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. The current account improves only if trade volumes respond enough: with price elastic demand for exports and imports it improves; with price inelastic demand, at least in the short run, it may worsen because each import costs more.
- **Inflation.** Imported consumer goods cost more, and so do imported raw materials and components, raising firms' costs.
- **Growth and employment.** Higher net exports add to aggregate demand, raising output and jobs if there is spare capacity.
- **Firms differ.** Exporters gain; firms using imported inputs, and consumers of imports, lose.

**Worked example 4.** A UK manufacturer's imported components make up 40% of its costs. After the 10% fall in the pound in worked example 1, components cost 11.1% more in pounds. Its total costs rise by about 0.40 × 11.1 = **4.4%**, if no other cost changes. If its products are exported, the 10% fall in its dollar price outweighs this, but a firm selling only in the UK is simply worse off.

**Judging the overall effect** depends on the size and expected duration of the change, how price elastic trade is, how open the economy is, how much spare capacity exists (a depreciation near full employment adds mainly to inflation), and why the currency moved. A fall caused by a loss of confidence is a symptom, not a cure.

## 7. Advantages and disadvantages of fixed and floating systems

| | Floating | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Adjusts automatically to trade imbalances; interest rates can be set for domestic goals such as inflation; no need for large reserves | Certainty for exporters, importers and investors; discipline on inflation, since an uncompetitive economy cannot rely on depreciation |
| Disadvantages | Volatility makes planning and pricing harder; a sharp fall can import inflation; speculation can push the rate far from what trade alone would justify | Needs reserves; interest rates must defend the peg rather than steer the domestic economy; a peg set at the wrong level harms competitiveness; may face speculative attack |

A judgement should name the type of economy: a small, open economy trading mainly with one partner may value a peg's certainty; a large economy facing different shocks from its partners may value control of its own interest rates.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a percentage change for the wrong currency.
- Writing "the pound devalued" for a floating currency, or leaving the currency off the axis labels.
- Assuming a depreciation always improves the current account, ignoring elasticity.
- Forgetting that a fixed rate needs reserves or interest rate changes to defend it.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the rate as foreign currency per unit of home currency, convert, then check the direction.
- **Explain with a diagram:** name the curve that shifts, why, and the new rate and quantity.
- **Evaluate:** use at least two "it depends" factors from section 6 and reach a verdict tied to the economy in the data.
