Section 1.2 of the OCR A Level Biology A (H420) specification covers the practical skills that are assessed through the Practical Endorsement. Statement 1.2.1 lists the skills you develop through practical work: independent thinking, using scientific methods and practices, research and referencing, and using instruments and equipment. The Practical Endorsement is teacher-assessed and is reported separately from the A Level grade, but the same skills are tested indirectly in the written papers, so the work you do at the bench also earns marks in the examination room.

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.2.1(a) to (j) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the [development of practical skills study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-development-of-practical-skills/). Use the [practical skills revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-practical-skills-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [practical skills practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-practical-skills-practice/) to test yourself.

## How the Practical Endorsement works

- You carry out a **minimum of 12 practical activities**, drawn from the twelve Practical Activity Groups (PAG1 to PAG12). Together they must allow you to acquire all the skills in 1.2.1 and all the apparatus and techniques in 1.2.2.
- Your teacher assesses you against the **Common Practical Assessment Criteria (CPAC)**. To receive a Pass you must consistently and routinely meet the criteria in each competency before the end of the course.
- You may work in groups, but the evidence from each learner must show that **you** independently meet the criteria.
- You keep a record of your practical work, such as a log book or portfolio.
- The result is reported separately from the A Level grade, alongside it on the certificate.

## The five CPAC competencies

| Competency | What it asks of you |
|---|---|
| (1) Follows written procedures | Correctly follows instructions to carry out experimental techniques or procedures |
| (2) Applies investigative approaches and methods when using instruments and equipment | Uses instruments, apparatus and materials (including ICT) correctly with minimal prompting; works methodically and makes adjustments; identifies and controls significant quantitative variables; selects equipment and measurement strategies that give suitably accurate results |
| (3) Safely uses a range of practical equipment and materials | Identifies hazards and assesses risks; makes safety adjustments; uses appropriate safety equipment and approaches |
| (4) Makes and records observations | Makes accurate observations; obtains accurate, precise and sufficient data; records them methodically with units and conventions |
| (5) Researches, references and reports | Uses appropriate software and tools to process data, carry out research and report findings; cites sources of information |

## Independent thinking (a)

Independent thinking means that you decide how to approach a problem rather than simply following a list of steps. In practice you might:

- work out which variable to change and which to hold constant,
- choose the range and intervals of a series of measurements,
- decide how many repeats are needed,
- notice that a result is odd and decide what to do about it.

These are the same skills as in 1.1.1 Planning, used with real apparatus. The practical record is a good place to show your decisions: write down why you chose a particular range or why you added a repeat.

## Following instructions and keeping records (c to e)

**Follow the method.** Read the whole procedure before you start. Record any change you make and give the reason.

**Make a contemporaneous record.** That means writing down measurements and observations at the time, not afterwards from memory. A good record includes:

- the date and title of the practical,
- the aim or the question being tested,
- the apparatus and materials,
- the method (or a reference to the instructions, with changes noted),
- raw data in a table with headings and units,
- observations, in words,
- calculations and graphs,
- a conclusion,
- an evaluation with improvements, and
- references to any sources used.

**Worked example 1.** During a PAG4 practical on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction, you note: "Catalase from liver (3.0 g), 5 cm³ of 3 % hydrogen peroxide at 25 °C. Oxygen collected in a gas syringe: 6.5 cm³ in 60 s. At 20 s, a bubble got stuck in the delivery tube; I tapped the tube, and the volume jumped by 1 cm³." This record shows the conditions, the data, the observation and the problem. It lets a reader decide whether the 20 s reading is trustworthy.

## Safe working (b)

You must identify hazards, assess the risks and apply controls. A short risk assessment has three parts: the **hazard** (what could cause harm), the **risk** (how likely and how serious the harm is) and the **control** (what you do to reduce it).

| Hazard | Risk | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Scalpel | Cut to the hand | Cut onto a tile, away from the body; carry blades with the cover on |
| Hot water bath | Scald | Carry only small volumes; keep the bath away from the edge of the bench |
| 3 % hydrogen peroxide | Irritation to eyes and skin | Wear eye protection; rinse splashes with water |
| Bacterial cultures | Infection | Use aseptic technique; disinfect surfaces; wash hands; do not open sealed plates |
| Organisms (plants and animals) | Harm to the organism or to you | Handle gently; return organisms to their habitat; follow ethical guidelines |

## Processing, presenting and reporting data (f and g)

Use ICT where it helps: a spreadsheet, a data logger, graph software or modelling software.

**Worked example 2.** Repeats of 8.0, 8.5 and 9.0 cm³ of oxygen in 2 minutes are in cells A2 to C2 of a spreadsheet. The mean is calculated with `=AVERAGE(A2:C2)`, giving 8.5 in cell D2. The rate is `=D2/2`, giving 4.25 cm³ min⁻¹. If you copy the formula down a column it gives the same calculation for each row, which reduces slips. Always check the first result by hand.

Tables and graphs should follow the rules in 1.1.2 and 1.1.3: headings with units, an even scale, labelled axes and a line of best fit where appropriate.

## Research and referencing (h and i)

Use a range of sources, such as textbooks, journals, reputable websites and databases. Compare more than one source and judge which is reliable: who wrote it, when, whether it gives evidence, and whether it is a primary or secondary source.

**Citing sources** gives credit to authors and lets others find the source. The Practical Skills Handbook provides guidance on appropriate methods for citing information. A typical entry gives the author, year, title and where it was published. A short in-text reference, such as (Author, year), links the statement in your report to the list at the end.

**Worked example 3.** An invented example of a reference list entry: *Rahman, S. (2024) Enzymes in the classroom. Imaginary Biology Press, London.* In your text: "Catalase works fastest near body temperature (Rahman, 2024)."

## How the skills appear in written papers

The written papers include practical-context questions. They can ask you to describe how to use apparatus, to explain why a step is included, to plan an investigation, to process data, to say how a hazard should be handled, and to evaluate a method. Practical work done well makes these answers easier to write, because you remember what goes wrong and how it was put right.

## Exam technique

- **Use the language of practical work**: independent variable, control, repeat, anomaly, hazard, risk, resolution.
- **Link a technique to its purpose**: "serial dilution produces a range of concentrations from a stock solution".
- **Give specific safety control measures**, not "be careful".
- **Quote the unit and the resolution** of an instrument when you describe a measurement.

## Common mistakes

- Writing up a practical from memory instead of recording it as it happens.
- Giving a hazard without a risk or a control.
- Using group data as if they were your own evidence.
- Copying text from a website without citing it.
- Omitting units from a table of results.
- Skipping the evaluation of a practical.
