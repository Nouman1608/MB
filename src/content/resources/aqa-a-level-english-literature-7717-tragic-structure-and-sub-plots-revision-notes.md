**These notes condense the structural aspects of Option 1A:** "the structural pattern of the text as
it moves through complication to catastrophe, from order to disorder, through climax to resolution"
and "the use of plots and sub-plots". For full explanation and a model paragraph, use the
[tragic structure study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7717-tragic-structure-and-sub-plots/);
for the option as a whole, see the
[Aspects of Tragedy revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-1a-aspects-of-tragedy-7717-revision-notes/).

## The four structural pairs

- **Complication to catastrophe**: find the first complication and trace how it grows.
- **Order to disorder**: define the opening order before describing its collapse.
- **Climax to resolution**: locate the point of no return; ask what the ending settles.
- **Prosperity and happiness to the tragic end**: measure the height of the fall.

Treat each pair as a claim to test. Some texts begin in disorder; some resolutions resolve little.

## Structural shapes by text

| Text | Shape | Point to make |
|---|---|---|
| *Othello* | Rise to a central hinge | Act 3 Scene 3 turns a secure husband into a revenger |
| *King Lear* | Double plot converging | Lear and Gloucester plots mirror, then meet through Edmund |
| *Richard II* | See-saw | Richard falls as Bolingbroke rises; the two-buckets image |
| *Death of a Salesman* | Present interwoven with memory | The Boston discovery withheld until late in Act 2 |
| *Tess of the D'Urbervilles* | Seven named Phases | "Fulfilment" as an ironic title for the final phase |
| *The Great Gatsby* | Retrospective, fragmented past | The truth about James Gatz released after the dream is shown |
| "The Monk's Tale" | Episodic series of falls | Repetition makes fortune the subject |
| "La Belle Dame Sans Merci" | Circular | The ending returns to the opening and traps the knight |
| "The Eve of St. Agnes" | Escape, then deaths | The final stanza undercuts the lovers' happy flight |
| "The Convergence of the Twain" | Two movements meeting | Form enacts the collision it describes |
| "'Out, out…'" | Sudden fall, abrupt ending | The survivors turning back to their affairs |

## What a sub-plot does

1. **Mirror**: repeats the main pattern so the theme becomes general (Gloucester and his sons).
2. **Intensify**: makes a metaphor literal (Lear's blindness of judgement, Gloucester's blinding).
3. **Contrast**: shows another way of living (Charley and Bernard against Willy and Biff).
4. **Connect**: a character links the plots causally (Edmund).

When you write about a sub-plot, always say what it does to the main plot.

## Ending checklist

- Who speaks last, and with what authority?
- Is public order restored while private loss remains?
- Does the ending return to an opening image?
- Is the catastrophe shown on stage, reported, or withheld?
- Does any consolation survive, and for whom?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Peripeteia | Reversal of fortune, often at the turning point |
| Anagnorisis | Recognition of the truth, usually late |
| Catastrophe | The final disaster, typically the death of the hero |
| Complication | The event that begins the movement towards disaster |
| Sub-plot | A secondary line of action running beside the main plot |
| Double plot | Two plots of similar weight that mirror each other |
| Foil | A character whose contrast highlights the hero's qualities |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two scenes or ideas side by side for effect |
| Retrospective narration | A story told after the events by a narrator who knows the outcome |
| Circular structure | An ending that returns to the opening |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows what a character does not |
| Denouement | The unravelling and settling after the climax |

## Linking structure to the other aspects

- **Fate**: a structure that foreshadows the end makes it feel inevitable.
- **Villain**: the villain often drives the complication (Iago, Edmund).
- **The world**: disorder spreading from one family to a state shows the hero's effect on others.
- **Humour and happiness**: placing a moment of joy just before the catastrophe heightens the fall.
- **Audience**: pacing controls when pity and fear are felt.

## Exam traps

- Retelling events in order and calling it structure.
- Forcing a five-stage diagram onto a text that does not fit it.
- Writing about the sub-plot as if it were a separate story.
- Ignoring the ending, which is often the strongest evidence for a judgement.
- In Section A, failing to say where the passage sits in the play's structure.
- Claiming order is "restored" without saying what kind of order, and for whom.

## Self-test

1. Quote the four structural pairs AQA lists in the tragedy aspect.
2. What is peripeteia?
3. Which scene acts as the hinge of *Othello*?
4. Give two ways the Gloucester plot mirrors the Lear plot.
5. Which character links the two plots of *King Lear*?
6. What image does Richard use for the crown passing to Bolingbroke?
7. How many Phases does Hardy divide *Tess* into, and what is the last called?
8. Why does the withheld Boston scene matter to the structure of *Death of a Salesman*?
9. What structural effect does Nick's retrospective narration have in *The Great Gatsby*?
10. How does the final stanza of "The Eve of St. Agnes" complicate the lovers' escape?
11. Name the four jobs a sub-plot can do.
12. Which assessment objective covers structure, and what does the specification say about it?

## Answers

1. Complication to catastrophe; order to disorder; climax to resolution; the prosperity and happiness
   of the hero to the tragic end.
2. A reversal of fortune.
3. Act 3 Scene 3, the temptation scene.
4. Both fathers misjudge their children; each believes the deceitful child or children and banishes
   the loyal one; Lear's metaphorical blindness becomes Gloucester's physical blinding.
5. Edmund.
6. A well with two buckets, one rising as the other falls.
7. Seven; "Fulfilment".
8. It explains Biff's change towards Willy only near the catastrophe, so the audience understands the
   source of the conflict late, as Willy is losing control.
9. The reader knows a story is ending in loss, and Gatsby's past is released in fragments, which
   enacts the gap between his invented self and his origins.
10. It turns from the escape to the deaths of Angela and the Beadsman, so death enters the ending
    even though the lovers survive.
11. Mirror, intensify, contrast and connect.
12. AO2; it has "particular focus on the structures of texts as a form of shaping".
