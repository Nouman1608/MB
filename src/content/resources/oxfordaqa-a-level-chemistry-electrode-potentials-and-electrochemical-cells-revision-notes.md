## 3.1.9.1 Electrode potentials and cells (International A-level only)

### Key terms

- **Half-cell**: an electrode and its solution, such as Sn in Sn²⁺(aq), or Pt in a solution of V²⁺ and V³⁺.
- **Salt bridge**: filter paper soaked in an unreactive salt solution (such as KNO₃). Ions move through it; electrons do not.
- **Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)**: the reference half-cell, defined as 0.00 V.
- **Standard electrode potential, E⦵**: the EMF of a half-cell measured against the SHE at 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
- **Electrochemical series**: half-equations listed in order of E⦵.
- **EMF**: the potential difference of a cell when no current flows.

### The SHE at a glance

| Part | Detail |
|---|---|
| Electrode | Platinum (inert; conducts electrons to the H₂/H⁺ equilibrium) |
| Gas | H₂ at 100 kPa |
| Solution | [H⁺] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³ |
| Temperature | 298 K |
| Half-equation | 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) |
| In a cell diagram | Pt(s) \| H₂(g) \| H⁺(aq) |

### Conventions

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| IUPAC half-equation | Written as a reduction, electrons on the left, ⇌ |
| `\|` | Phase boundary |
| `\|\|` | Salt bridge |
| Left-hand electrode | Negative; oxidation happens here |
| Right-hand electrode | Positive; reduction happens here |
| Two aqueous species | Comma between them, Pt(s) on the outside |
| EMF | E⦵(right) − E⦵(left) = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative), always positive |

### Method in steps: build a cell

1. Look up both E⦵ values.
2. More negative half-cell → negative electrode → left.
3. Left side reads as an oxidation, electrode outermost: `Cd(s) | Cd²⁺(aq)`.
4. Right side reads as a reduction, electrode outermost: `Au³⁺(aq) | Au(s)`.
5. EMF = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left).
6. Overall equation: reverse the left half-equation, scale both to the same number of electrons, add, cancel. Never scale E⦵.

### Method in steps: is it feasible?

1. Find the half-equation with the oxidising agent on the **left**.
2. Find the half-equation with the reducing agent on the **right**.
3. Feasible if E⦵(oxidising agent's half-equation) − E⦵(reducing agent's half-equation) is positive.
4. The more positive half-equation goes forwards; the other goes backwards.

### Method in steps: unknown E⦵ from a measured EMF

1. Note which electrode is negative.
2. Write EMF = E(positive) − E(negative) and substitute the known value.
3. Solve for the unknown. Against the SHE, the reading is E⦵ with the sign of the unknown electrode.

### Effect of conditions (no Nernst equation)

| Change | Equilibrium shift | Electrode potential |
|---|---|---|
| More oxidised form (left-hand species) | Right | More positive |
| Less oxidised form | Left | More negative |
| More H⁺ where H⁺ is on the left | Right | More positive |
| Temperature or gas pressure changed | Shifts | Changes, so not standard |

Then ask: is this the positive or the negative electrode? A more positive positive electrode, or a more negative negative electrode, increases the EMF.

### Data used on these notes

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Al | −1.66 |
| Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Cr | −0.74 |
| Cd²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd | −0.40 |
| V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺ | −0.26 |
| Sn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn | −0.13 |
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ | 0.00 |
| VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O | +1.00 |
| Au³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Au | +1.52 |
| S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2SO₄²⁻ | +2.01 |

Always use the values given in the question.

### What E⦵ can and cannot tell you

| Can tell you | Cannot tell you |
|---|---|
| Feasibility under standard conditions | Rate of reaction |
| Relative strength of oxidising and reducing agents | Outcome far from standard conditions |
| EMF of a standard cell | Whether the activation energy is too high |

### Required practical 6: measure the EMF of a cell

- Emery paper on metal electrodes removes oxide.
- 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions for a standard value; Pt where both species are ions.
- Salt bridge ions must not react with or precipitate ions in either half-cell.
- High-resistance voltmeter: almost no current, so the reading is the EMF.
- Record which electrode is negative.
- Errors to evaluate: oxide, concentration, temperature, dry salt bridge.

## 3.1.9.2 Commercial applications (International A-level only)

### Three types of cell

| Type | Key idea |
|---|---|
| Non-rechargeable (irreversible) | Reactions not reversible in practice; discarded when used up |
| Rechargeable | External supply reverses the electrode reactions |
| Fuel cell | Reactants supplied continuously; no electrical recharging |

### Method in steps: commercial cell from given data

1. Positive electrode: the more positive half-equation, forwards (reduction).
2. Negative electrode: the less positive half-equation, reversed (oxidation).
3. Balance electrons and add for the overall discharge reaction.
4. EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative).
5. Rechargeable cell on charge: both electrode reactions reversed.

### Lithium cell (as given in the specification)

```
Positive electrode:  Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode:  Li → Li⁺ + e⁻
```

Lithium is oxidised (0 → +1); cobalt is reduced (+4 → +3). Li⁺ ions cross the electrolyte; electrons go round the external circuit. Charging reverses both reactions.

### Alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

```
Negative electrode:  H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻
Positive electrode:  O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
Overall:             2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

No net use of OH⁻. Hydrogen goes to the negative electrode, oxygen to the positive.

### How a current is generated

Oxidation at the negative electrode releases electrons → electrons flow through the external circuit → reduction at the positive electrode takes them up → ions move inside the cell to balance charge.

### Benefits and risks

| Cell | Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel cell | Only water at the point of use | Hydrogen production needs energy, often from fossil fuels; hydrogen is flammable and hard to store |
| Rechargeable | Less waste than throwaway cells | Lithium is reactive; damaged cells can catch fire |
| Any cell | Portable electrical energy | Toxic metal compounds if dumped; needs recycling |

## Must-know distinctions

| This | Not this |
|---|---|
| Negative electrode = oxidation | Negative electrode = reduction |
| Scale the half-equation | Scale the E⦵ |
| Sign of E⦵ from polarity | Sign from the size of the reading |
| Feasible | Fast |
| Rechargeable: external supply reverses reactions | Fuel cell: reactants replaced by continuous supply |

## Quick self-test

Use the data table above.

1. State the three standard conditions for E⦵.
2. Write the IUPAC half-equation for a chromium/chromium(III) electrode.
3. Calculate the EMF of a cadmium–gold cell.
4. Write the conventional representation and EMF of a chromium–tin cell.
5. Will S₂O₈²⁻ oxidise VO²⁺ to VO₂⁺? Give the EMF.
6. Will cadmium metal reduce V³⁺ to V²⁺? Give the equation.
7. Half-cell X is the positive electrode when connected to a standard cadmium half-cell. The EMF is 0.74 V. Find E⦵ for X.
8. In the cadmium–gold cell, [Cd²⁺] is increased. Predict the effect on the EMF.
9. In the lithium cell, which species is reduced during discharge?
10. Calculate the EMF of an aluminium–tin cell and write the overall equation.
11. In the alkaline fuel cell, which gas is supplied to the negative electrode?
12. Why is a non-rechargeable cell thrown away when it stops working?

### Answers

1. 298 K; 100 kPa; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
2. Cr³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ ⇌ Cr(s)
3. 1.52 − (−0.40) = **+1.92 V**
4. Cr(s) | Cr³⁺(aq) || Sn²⁺(aq) | Sn(s); EMF = −0.13 − (−0.74) = **+0.61 V**
5. Yes: 2.01 − 1.00 = **+1.01 V**. S₂O₈²⁻ + 2VO²⁺ + 2H₂O → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2VO₂⁺ + 4H⁺
6. Yes: −0.26 − (−0.40) = **+0.14 V**. Cd + 2V³⁺ → Cd²⁺ + 2V²⁺
7. E(X) = −0.40 + 0.74 = **+0.34 V**
8. Cd²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd shifts right, so the cadmium (negative) electrode becomes less negative and the EMF **decreases** below 1.92 V.
9. CoO₂ (cobalt +4 → +3).
10. −0.13 − (−1.66) = **+1.53 V**; 2Al + 3Sn²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Sn
11. Hydrogen.
12. Its reactions cannot be reversed in practice, so once a reactant is used up the cell cannot be restored.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing an electrode half-equation as an oxidation instead of an IUPAC reduction.
- Doubling or tripling an E⦵ value when the half-equation is scaled.
- Calculating left − right, getting a negative EMF, then dropping the minus sign.
- Leaving out Pt(s) where both species are ions, or H⁺(aq) where it appears in the half-equation.
- Quoting standard conditions as "room temperature" or "1 atm" instead of 298 K and 100 kPa.
- Taking the sign of an unknown E⦵ from the voltmeter instead of from which electrode is negative.
- Explaining a concentration change without saying which electrode it affects.
- Treating feasibility as a statement about rate.
- Saying a fuel cell is recharged, or that electrons cross the salt bridge.
- Writing acid-electrolyte half-equations for the alkaline fuel cell.
