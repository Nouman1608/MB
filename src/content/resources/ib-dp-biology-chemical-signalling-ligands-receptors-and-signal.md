## Ligands and receptors (C2.1.1)

A signalling chemical is called a **ligand**. A **receptor** is a protein with a **binding site** whose shape and charge distribution fit one ligand, or a small family of similar ligands. Binding is reversible and uses weak bonds. It changes the shape of the receptor, and that change is the start of the response.

Specificity explains why a hormone in the blood affects some cells and not others. Every cell is exposed, but only **target cells** carry the matching receptor. Cells with different receptors respond to the same ligand differently, because the pathway behind the receptor differs.

## Quorum sensing (C2.1.2)

Bacteria signal to one another by releasing small ligands called **autoinducers**. Each cell releases a little. At low population density the ligand diffuses away and its concentration stays low. As the population grows, the concentration rises. When it passes a threshold, enough ligand binds to receptors in the cells to switch on particular genes in the whole population at about the same time. This is **quorum sensing**: the population detects its own density.

*Vibrio fischeri* is a marine bacterium. In open seawater it is sparse and makes no light. In the light organ of a host such as a squid it reaches a very high density, and the genes for **bioluminescence** switch on. Light from one cell would be pointless, so a density-triggered response saves energy.

**Worked example.** A culture of *V. fischeri* was sampled at five densities (data supplied).

| Density (million cells per cm³) | 0.01 | 0.1 | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light output (arbitrary units) | 0.02 | 0.2 | 80 | 12 000 | 250 000 |

Light per million cells is 2 at 0.01 and 0.1, then 8 at 10, 120 at 100 and 250 at 1000. Density rose 100 000-fold from 0.01 to 1000 but light rose 12 500 000-fold. Light per cell rose 125-fold from 0.1 to 1000, so each cell is producing more light at high density. That fits an autoinducer that accumulates and switches on bioluminescence genes above a threshold, between 0.1 and 10 million cells per cm³ here.

## Categories of signalling chemical (C2.1.3 to C2.1.5)

| Category | Made by | Travels | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hormone | Endocrine gland cells | In the blood, to distant targets | Slow, often long-lasting |
| Neurotransmitter | Neurons | Across a synaptic gap, about 20 nm | Fast, brief, very local |
| Cytokine | Cells such as immune cells | Mostly short distances between nearby cells | Regulate immune and inflammatory responses |
| Calcium ions | Released from stores or let in through channels | Within a cell | Trigger responses such as exocytosis and muscle contraction |

Hormones can act far from their source because the blood system distributes them. They are diluted, so they work at low concentration and are removed by metabolism or excretion. A neurotransmitter is released into a gap so narrow that diffusion is quick and the concentration at the receptors is high. It is then removed, for example by enzyme breakdown or reuptake, so the signal stops within milliseconds.

**Chemical diversity.** Hormones include **amines** (epinephrine), **proteins** (insulin) and **steroids** (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone). Neurotransmitters include **amino acids** (glutamate), **peptides**, **amines** (dopamine) and the gas nitric oxide. The official wording gives this gas as nitrous oxide, but the neurotransmitter gas is nitric oxide, NO.

Reasons for such variety include: different molecules suit different jobs, so lipid-soluble steroids pass into cells and are carried bound to proteins, while water-soluble proteins and amines dissolve freely in blood; a large number of distinct shapes lets many ligands each have a unique receptor, which reduces cross-talk; and different molecular sizes and stabilities give different lifetimes.

## Where receptors sit (C2.1.6)

| Feature | Transmembrane receptor | Intracellular receptor |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Spans the plasma membrane | In cytoplasm or nucleus |
| Ligand | Hydrophilic, or too large to enter | Hydrophobic, passes through the membrane |
| Amino acids | Hydrophobic in the stretch in the bilayer; hydrophilic in the parts in water outside and inside | Mostly hydrophilic outside, because the protein is dissolved in the cytoplasm or nucleoplasm |
| Examples | Acetylcholine receptor, insulin receptor | Oestradiol and progesterone receptors |

The hydrophobic amino acids of the membrane-spanning part interact with the fatty acid tails of the phospholipids and hold the receptor in the bilayer.

## Signal transduction (C2.1.7)

**Signal transduction** is the sequence of responses inside a cell that follows ligand binding. The ligand does not need to enter the cell. Instead the receptor changes shape, and this activates the next component, often an enzyme. Many steps allow amplification, because one active enzyme makes many product molecules (for example, one active adenylyl cyclase makes many cAMP), and they give several points of control. A pathway also needs an off switch, otherwise the cell would respond for ever.

## The acetylcholine receptor (C2.1.8)

The acetylcholine receptor is a transmembrane protein that is also an **ion channel**. When acetylcholine binds, the channel opens and positively charged ions, mainly sodium ions, diffuse into the cell down their gradient. The inside of the membrane becomes less negative, a **depolarisation**. If this reaches a threshold, other changes follow, such as opening of voltage-gated channels and an action potential.

**Worked example.** A neuron has a resting potential of −70 mV. After acetylcholine arrives it is −52 mV. The threshold is −50 mV (data supplied).

- Change: −52 − (−70) = +18 mV, a depolarisation.
- The potential is 2 mV short of threshold, so no action potential is triggered.
- More receptors opening, from more acetylcholine, would bring the potential to −50 mV or above and trigger one.

## G protein-coupled receptors and epinephrine (C2.1.9 and C2.1.10)

Humans have a large number of **G protein-coupled receptors**. Each is a single protein crossing the membrane several times. On the inner face a **G protein** is attached. The sequence is:

1. The ligand binds on the outside and the receptor changes shape.
2. The G protein exchanges GDP for GTP and becomes active.
3. The active G protein activates an enzyme in the membrane.
4. The enzyme makes a **second messenger**, which spreads through the cytoplasm.
5. GTP is hydrolysed, the G protein switches off, and the second messenger is broken down.

**Epinephrine** (also called adrenaline) is a hormone from the adrenal gland. Both names come from words for "at/above the kidney". In a liver cell, epinephrine binds its receptor, the G protein activates **adenylyl cyclase**, which converts ATP to **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**. cAMP activates protein kinase A, starting a cascade that activates enzymes breaking glycogen down, so glucose is released into the blood. An enzyme, phosphodiesterase, breaks cAMP down and ends the signal.

**Worked example.** Liver cells were exposed to epinephrine at 0 s and it was washed away at 60 s. cAMP (arbitrary units) was 4 at 0 s, 60 at 15 s, 88 at 60 s, 31 at 120 s and 6 at 300 s (data supplied).

- At 60 s cAMP is 88/4 = 22 times the starting level.
- Between 60 s and 120 s it fell by 57 units, which is 64.8% (57/88 × 100).
- It falls once epinephrine is removed because the receptor stops activating G proteins and phosphodiesterase keeps destroying cAMP.

## Tyrosine kinase receptors and insulin (C2.1.11)

Insulin is a protein hormone that cannot cross the membrane. It binds to its receptor in the plasma membrane, which has **tyrosine kinase** activity. A kinase transfers a phosphate group from ATP to a protein. Binding causes **phosphorylation** of tyrosine inside the cell, which starts a sequence of reactions. It ends with **vesicles** carrying glucose transporters moving to the plasma membrane and fusing with it. More transporters means faster uptake of glucose by facilitated diffusion.

**Worked example.** Glucose uptake by muscle cells (µmol per g per hour, data supplied): no insulin 2.4; insulin 9.6; insulin plus a tyrosine kinase inhibitor 3.0.

- Insulin raises uptake by 9.6 − 2.4 = 7.2, a 300% increase (fourfold).
- The inhibitor removes 9.6 − 3.0 = 6.6 of that rise, which is 6.6/7.2 × 100 = 91.7%.
- This supports the idea that tyrosine kinase activity is needed for the insulin effect.

## Intracellular receptors (C2.1.12 and C2.1.13)

Steroid hormones are lipid-soluble and pass through the plasma membrane. Inside, the ligand binds to its receptor. Once activated, the receptor attaches to particular **DNA sequences** and promotes **transcription** of chosen genes. New mRNA and then new protein appear, so the response is slower than a membrane pathway but longer lasting.

- **Oestradiol** acts on the hypothalamic cells that release gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), changing GnRH secretion. In the usual account, modest oestradiol levels reduce GnRH release (negative feedback), while the high, sustained levels late in the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle increase it (positive feedback).
- **Progesterone** acts on cells of the endometrium, supporting the lining so that it is ready for an embryo to implant.

## Feedback (C2.1.14)

**Negative feedback** reverses a change, which stabilises a variable. Example: a high blood glucose concentration stimulates insulin release, glucose uptake lowers glucose, and insulin release falls. **Positive feedback** amplifies a change. Example: during labour, stretching of the cervix triggers oxytocin release, oxytocin strengthens contractions, and stronger contractions cause more stretching.

## Common errors

- Writing that the ligand "enters" a transmembrane receptor. It binds outside and the receptor changes shape.
- Saying a hormone only reaches its target. It reaches all cells; only target cells respond.
- Calling cAMP the first messenger. The hormone is the first messenger and cAMP the second.
- Mixing up the effect of GTP: the G protein is active with GTP bound.
- Describing insulin as entering cells.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a ligand?**
A ligand is a signalling chemical that binds reversibly to a specific binding site on a receptor protein, changing the receptor's shape and starting the response.

**How does insulin increase glucose uptake?**
Insulin binds a tyrosine kinase receptor in the plasma membrane, causing phosphorylation of tyrosine inside the cell. A sequence of reactions brings vesicles carrying glucose transporters to fuse with the membrane, so glucose enters faster by facilitated diffusion.

**Why are steroid hormone responses slower than membrane receptor responses?**
A steroid must cross the membrane, bind its intracellular receptor, and the receptor must bind DNA sequences to promote transcription before new mRNA and protein appear, so the response is slower but longer lasting.

**Why does a signalling pathway need an off switch?**
Without an off switch the cell would keep responding; for example, phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP and the G protein switches off when GTP is hydrolysed, ending the epinephrine signal.
