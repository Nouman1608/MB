These questions cover section 3.4.4, Circuits, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), shared International AS and A-level content. Learn the content first with the [circuits study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-circuits/) and the [circuits revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-circuits-revision-notes/). Supplies and cells have negligible internal resistance and meters are ideal unless stated otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the principles of conservation of charge and conservation of energy as they apply to dc circuits. **[2]**

**2.** Resistors of 4.0 Ω, 6.0 Ω and 12 Ω are connected in parallel. Calculate their combined resistance. **[2]**

**3.** A 470 Ω resistor carries a current of 20 mA.

**(a)** Calculate the power transferred in the resistor. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the pd across it. **[1]**

**4.** An electric shower is rated 9.5 kW at 230 V.

**(a)** Calculate the current it draws. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy it transfers in 8.0 minutes. **[1]**

**5.** Three identical 1.5 V cells are connected in parallel. This combination is connected in series with a fourth 1.5 V cell, all facing the same way.

**(a)** State the total emf. **[1]**

**(b)** Give one advantage of connecting identical cells in parallel. **[1]**

**6.** A current of 2.5 A enters a junction. Two wires carry currents of 0.90 A and 0.60 A away from it. Calculate the current in the fourth wire, and state its direction. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** An 18 V supply is connected to a 5.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel section. One branch of the parallel section is a 20 Ω resistor; the other is a 2.0 Ω resistor in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the total resistance of the circuit. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the supply current. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the current in the 20 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the power transferred in the 3.0 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**8.** A 12 V supply is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor in series with resistor X (8.0 Ω). A second 8.0 Ω resistor, Y, can be connected in parallel with X by closing a switch.

**(a)** With the switch open, calculate the current and the pd across X. **[2]**

**(b)** With the switch closed, calculate the supply current and the pd across X. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the pd across X falls when the switch is closed. **[2]**

**9.** Lamp A is rated 12 V, 36 W and lamp B is rated 12 V, 6.0 W. Assume each lamp's resistance is constant. The lamps are connected in series to a 12 V supply.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of each lamp. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the current and the power in each lamp. **[3]**

**(c)** State which lamp is brighter and explain why. **[1]**

**10.** A kettle is rated 2.2 kW at 230 V.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the resistance of its element. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the energy transferred in 3.0 minutes. **[1]**

**(d)** The supply pd falls to 220 V. Calculate the new power, assuming the resistance does not change. **[2]**

**11.**

**(a)** Show that n identical resistors, each of resistance R, connected in parallel have a combined resistance R/n. **[2]**

**(b)** A student has only 100 Ω resistors. Explain how three of them can be connected to give 150 Ω. **[2]**

**12.** Four identical cells, each of emf 1.5 V, are arranged as two parallel pairs. The two pairs are connected in series and drive a current through a 6.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** State the emf of the arrangement. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the current in the resistor and the current supplied by each cell. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why cells with different emfs should not be connected in parallel. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Charge: the total current into a junction equals the total current out [1]; energy: round any closed loop, the sum of the emfs equals the sum of the pds [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Name where each law is applied: junctions for charge, loops for energy.

**2.** 1/R = 1/4.0 + 1/6.0 + 1/12 = 0.50 Ω⁻¹ [1]; R = 2.0 Ω [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* 0.50 is 1/R, not R; take the reciprocal.

**3. (a)** P = I²R = 0.020² × 470 = 0.19 W [1]

**(b)** V = IR = 0.020 × 470 = 9.4 V [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* 20 mA is 0.020 A; squaring 20 gives a power 10⁶ times too large.

**4. (a)** I = P/V = 9500 / 230 = 41 A [1]

**(b)** E = Pt = 9500 × 480 = 4.6 × 10⁶ J [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* E = Pt is E = IVt with P = IV already combined.

**5. (a)** 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0 V [1]

**(b)** Any one: each cell supplies only a third of the current, so the cells last longer; a larger current can be drawn with less strain on each cell [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The parallel group behaves like one 1.5 V cell, so it adds only 1.5 V.

**6.** Current out in the known wires = 1.50 A, so the fourth wire carries 2.5 − 1.50 = 1.0 A [1], away from the junction [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* State the direction: conservation of charge decides it.

**7. (a)** Lower branch = 5.0 Ω; parallel section = (20 × 5.0) / 25 = 4.0 Ω [1]; total = 5.0 + 4.0 = 9.0 Ω [1]

**(b)** I = 18 / 9.0 = 2.0 A [1]

**(c)** pd across parallel section = 18 − 2.0 × 5.0 = 8.0 V [1]; current = 8.0 / 20 = 0.40 A [1]

**(d)** Current in lower branch = 2.0 − 0.40 = 1.6 A [1]; P = I²R = 1.6² × 3.0 = 7.7 W [1] **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the branch current, not the supply current, in I²R for a component inside a parallel section.

**8. (a)** I = 12 / (4.0 + 8.0) = 1.0 A [1]; pd across X = 1.0 × 8.0 = 8.0 V [1]

**(b)** Parallel X and Y = 4.0 Ω, total 8.0 Ω, so I = 1.5 A [1]; pd across X = 12 − 1.5 × 4.0 = 6.0 V [1]

**(c)** Closing the switch reduces the total resistance, so the supply current rises [1]; the pd across the 4.0 Ω resistor rises (4.0 V to 6.0 V), leaving less of the 12 V across X [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Follow the chain: resistance, current, then pds; an explanation that starts with the pd across X is circular.

**9. (a)** R = V²/P: A = 144 / 36 = 4.0 Ω [1]; B = 144 / 6.0 = 24 Ω [1]

**(b)** I = 12 / 28 = 0.43 A [1]; P_A = 0.429² × 4.0 = 0.73 W [1]; P_B = 0.429² × 24 = 4.4 W [1]

**(c)** Lamp B, because with the same current in series the larger resistance has the larger power, I²R [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The rating gives the power only at the rated pd; in series, neither lamp has 12 V across it.

**10. (a)** I = 2200 / 230 = 9.6 A [1]

**(b)** R = 230 / 9.57 = 24 Ω [1]

**(c)** E = Pt = 2200 × 180 = 4.0 × 10⁵ J [1]

**(d)** P = V²/R = 220² / 24.0 [1] = 2.0 × 10³ W [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* A 4% drop in pd gives about an 8% drop in power, because P depends on V².

**11. (a)** 1/R_T = 1/R + 1/R + … (n terms) = n/R [1]; so R_T = R/n [1]

**(b)** Two in parallel give 50 Ω [1]; put this pair in series with the third resistor: 50 + 100 = 150 Ω [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* A diagram or a clear description of which resistors are in parallel is needed for the second mark.

**12. (a)** Each parallel pair has emf 1.5 V; two pairs in series give 3.0 V [1]

**(b)** I = 3.0 / 6.0 = 0.50 A [1]; each pair carries 0.50 A, shared equally by its two cells: 0.25 A each [1]

**(c)** Current would circulate from the higher-emf cell through the lower-emf one, wasting energy and discharging the cells even with no external load [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Parallel cells add capacity, not emf; series pairs add emf.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not taking the reciprocal in parallel calculations.
- Using the supply current or supply pd for one part of a network.
- Treating a power rating as fixed whatever the circuit.
- Adding emfs of cells in parallel.
- Forgetting to convert mA or minutes.
- Explaining changes in a circuit without the chain of reasoning.
