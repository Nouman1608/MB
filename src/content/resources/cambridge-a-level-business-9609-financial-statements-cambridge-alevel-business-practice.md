All businesses are invented. Figures are in $000 unless stated.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *statement of financial position*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** items that link the statement of profit or loss to the statement of financial position. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why an increase in dividends does not change a company's profit for the year. **[2]**

**4.** A printer bought a press for $64,000. Its residual value is $4,000 and its useful life is 5 years. Using the straight-line method, calculate:

**(a)** the annual depreciation charge **[1]**

**(b)** the net book value after 3 years. **[2]**

**5.** A clothing shop holds 200 coats that cost $55 each. They can be sold for $60 each, but each needs $12 of alterations first. Calculate the value of the coats in the accounts and the effect on gross profit. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** difficulty of valuing inventory for a mobile phone retailer. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Garston Tiles plc has revenue of 3,200, cost of sales of 2,000 and expenses of 800. Tax is 20% of profit from operations. Dividends are 150.

**(a)** Calculate gross profit, profit from operations, taxation, profit for the year and retained earnings. **[4]**

**(b)** The main supplier raises prices so that cost of sales rises by 10%. Revenue and expenses are unchanged. Calculate the new profit for the year and retained earnings. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **one** impact of this change on Garston's shareholders. **[2]**

**8.** Pellow Foods has: non-current assets 1,800; inventory 300; trade receivables 250; cash 150; current liabilities 400; non-current liabilities 600; issued shares 900; reserves 600.

**(a)** Calculate net current assets and net assets. **[2]**

**(b)** State the **two** effects on the statement of financial position of each transaction: (i) a van bought for 90 in cash; (ii) new shares issued for 200 cash; (iii) inventory of 50 bought on credit. **[6]**

**(c)** Calculate net assets after all three transactions. **[2]**

**9.** Ashwell Hotels plc had reserves of 2,400 at the start of the year. Profit for the year was 700 and dividends were 280.

**(a)** Calculate closing reserves. **[2]**

**(b)** Ashwell is profitable but has a cash shortage. Explain **two** possible reasons. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate how far the financial statements of a company give a reliable picture of its performance. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A financial statement showing a business's assets, liabilities and equity [1] at one particular date [1].

**2.** Any two: retained earnings, which are added to reserves [1]; closing inventory, which is a current asset and part of cost of sales [1]; depreciation; tax owed; trade receivables and payables.

**3.** Dividends are paid out of profit for the year after tax [1], so they appear below profit for the year and reduce retained earnings, not profit [1].

**4. (a)** (64,000 − 4,000) ÷ 5 = **$12,000** a year [1].

**(b)** Accumulated depreciation = 3 × 12,000 = 36,000 [1]; NBV = 64,000 − 36,000 = **$28,000** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** NRV = 60 − 12 = $48, below cost of $55, so value at NRV [1]: 200 × 48 = **$9,600** [1]. Write-down = 11,000 − 9,600 = $1,400, which increases cost of sales and reduces gross profit by **$1,400** [1].

**6.** Phones become out of date quickly when new models launch [1], so the selling price of older stock may fall below cost, and judging NRV requires estimates of future prices [1].

**7. (a)** Gross profit = 3,200 − 2,000 = **1,200**; profit from operations = 1,200 − 800 = **400** [1] [1]. Tax = 20% × 400 = **80**; profit for the year = **320** [1]. Retained earnings = 320 − 150 = **170** [1].

**(b)** Cost of sales = 2,000 × 1.1 = 2,200; gross profit = 1,000 [1]. Profit from operations = 1,000 − 800 = 200 [1]. Tax = 40; profit for the year = **160** [1]. Retained earnings = 160 − 150 = **10** [1].

**(c)** Profit for the year has halved, so the company may struggle to keep paying dividends of 150 [1]; if dividends are cut or the share price falls, shareholders' returns fall [1]. **[10]**

**8. (a)** Current assets = 300 + 250 + 150 = 700; net current assets = 700 − 400 = **300** [1]. Net assets = 1,800 + 300 − 600 = **1,500** [1].

**(b)** (i) Non-current assets +90 [1]; cash −90 [1]. (ii) Cash +200 [1]; issued shares +200 [1]. (iii) Inventory +50 [1]; current liabilities +50 [1].

**(c)** Non-current assets 1,890; current assets 350 + 250 + 260 = 860; current liabilities 450; net current assets 410 [1]. Net assets = 1,890 + 410 − 600 = **1,700**, which equals equity 1,100 + 600 [1]. **[10]**

**9. (a)** Retained earnings = 700 − 280 = 420 [1]. Closing reserves = 2,400 + 420 = **2,820** [1].

**(b)** Reason 1: Ashwell may have spent cash on non-current assets, such as refurbishing rooms [1]; this reduces cash immediately, but only depreciation is charged against profit each year [1]. Reason 2: corporate customers may pay on credit [1], so revenue counts in profit before the cash arrives, raising trade receivables instead of cash [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The statements are reliable in some respects: they follow accounting rules and are usually audited [1], and they show revenue, costs and profit over a full year, allowing comparison with previous years [1]. However, they depend on estimates. Inventory valued at NRV requires judgement about future selling prices [1], and depreciation depends on estimated useful life and residual value [1]; a longer assumed life raises reported profit [1]. They are historic, so they may not reflect current conditions [1]. They omit non-financial factors such as staff skills, brand reputation and customer loyalty [1]. Profit does not show cash flow, so a profitable business may face liquidity problems [1]. Judgement: the statements give a useful but incomplete picture [1]; their reliability depends on the reasonableness of the estimates and on being read alongside non-financial information and cash-flow data [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Recalculating only the changed line and not the lines below it.
- Treating dividends as an expense before profit for the year.
- Giving one effect of a transaction instead of two.
- Valuing stock at NRV when NRV is higher than cost.
- Saying depreciation is cash set aside.
