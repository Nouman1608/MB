## 4.1.1.1 Atoms, elements and compounds

All substances are made of atoms. An **atom** is the smallest part of an element that can exist. Each element
has a **chemical symbol**: O is an atom of oxygen, Na is an atom of sodium. There are about 100 elements, and they
are shown in the periodic table. You are given a periodic table in the exam, but must know the names and symbols of the
first 20 elements, Groups 1 and 7, and other elements in the specification.

A **compound** contains two or more elements **chemically combined in fixed proportions**. Compounds are formed
from elements by chemical reactions, and they can only be separated back into elements by chemical reactions.
A reaction always forms a new substance and often has a detectable energy change.

### Naming compounds from formulae

- Metal first, non-metal second.
- A non-metal on its own changes its ending to **-ide**: chloride, oxide, sulfide.
- Common groups keep their own names: CO₃ is carbonate, SO₄ is sulfate, NO₃ is nitrate, OH is hydroxide.

### Worked example 1: name and count

Name Na₂CO₃ and Mg(NO₃)₂, and count the atoms in one formula unit of each.

```
Na2CO3:     sodium carbonate
            Na = 2, C = 1, O = 3          total = 6 atoms

Mg(NO3)2:   magnesium nitrate
            the bracket is doubled:
            Mg = 1, N = 1 x 2 = 2, O = 3 x 2 = 6
                                          total = 9 atoms
```

A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside it.

### Writing and balancing equations

1. Write the word equation.
2. Replace each name with its correct formula. Never change a formula to make it balance.
3. Balance by putting numbers **in front of** formulae until each element has the same number of atoms on both sides.

### Worked example 2: balancing

Aluminium burns in oxygen to form aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃.

```
word:        aluminium + oxygen -> aluminium oxide
unbalanced:  Al + O2 -> Al2O3
balance O:   O2 has 2, Al2O3 has 3; lowest common multiple 6
             Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3
balance Al:  right side now has 4 Al
             4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3
check:       Al 4 = 4,  O 6 = 6
```

### Ionic and half equations (Higher tier only)

An **ionic equation** shows only the particles that change. Ions that are the same on both sides are spectator
ions; leave them out. A **half equation** shows one species gaining or losing electrons, written e⁻. Both kinds must
balance in atoms **and** in charge.

### Worked example 3 (Higher tier only)

(a) Hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide solution. Write the ionic equation.

```
H+ Cl-  +  Na+ OH-  ->  Na+ Cl-  +  H2O
Na+ and Cl- are unchanged, so remove them:
H+ + OH- -> H2O        charge: (+1) + (-1) = 0 on each side
```

(b) Potassium reacts with chlorine to form potassium chloride. Write the two half equations.

```
K -> K+ + e-           (each potassium atom loses one electron)
Cl2 + 2e- -> 2Cl-      (each chlorine molecule gains two electrons)
```

Lost electrons go on the right; gained electrons go on the left. Topic 4.4 develops this; see the
[chemical changes study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-changes/).

## 4.1.1.2 Mixtures

A **mixture** consists of two or more elements or compounds **not chemically combined**. Each substance keeps its
own chemical properties. Mixtures are separated by **physical processes**. These involve no chemical reaction and
make no new substances.

| Process | What it separates | Property used |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration | Insoluble solid from a liquid | The solid cannot pass through the filter paper |
| Crystallisation | Dissolved solid from its solution | The solvent evaporates; the solute does not |
| Simple distillation | Solvent from a solution | The solvent boils at a much lower temperature than the solute |
| Fractional distillation | Liquids that mix, with different boiling points | Each liquid condenses at its own boiling point up the column |
| Chromatography | Dissolved substances, such as dyes | Different attraction to the paper and solubility in the solvent |

**Crystallisation.** Heat to evaporate some solvent until the solution is saturated, leave it to cool so crystals
form, then filter and dry them. **Simple distillation.** The solvent boils, condenses in a condenser and is
collected; the solute stays in the flask.

### Worked example 4: suggesting a technique

A sample of muddy sea water contains sand, dissolved salt and water. Suggest how to obtain pure water.

1. **Filter** first to remove the sand. Sand is insoluble, so it stays on the filter paper.
2. **Distil** the filtrate (simple distillation). Water boils off and is condensed; salt stays in the flask.

Name the technique **and** the property that makes it work. Chromatography returns in topic 4.8 (required
practical 6); see the
[chemical analysis study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-analysis/).

## 4.1.1.3 The development of the model of the atom

New experimental evidence may lead to a scientific model being changed or replaced.

1. **Before the electron was discovered**, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
2. **The discovery of the electron** led to the **plum pudding model**: a ball of positive charge with negative
   electrons embedded in it.
3. **The alpha particle scattering experiment** led to the conclusion that the mass of an atom is concentrated at
   the centre (the **nucleus**) and that the nucleus is charged. This **nuclear model** replaced the plum pudding model.
4. **Niels Bohr** adapted the nuclear model: electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances. His theoretical
   calculations agreed with experimental observations.
5. **Later experiments** showed that the positive charge of a nucleus could be subdivided into a whole number of
   smaller particles, each with the same positive charge. These were named **protons**.
6. **James Chadwick's** experimental work gave evidence for **neutrons** in the nucleus, about 20 years after the
   nucleus became an accepted idea.

Details of Bohr's and Chadwick's experimental work are not required.

### Plum pudding versus nuclear model

| Plum pudding model | Nuclear model |
|---|---|
| Positive charge spread through the whole atom | Positive charge concentrated in a tiny nucleus |
| Mass spread through the whole atom | Almost all the mass in the nucleus |
| Electrons embedded in the positive ball | Electrons outside the nucleus |
| No empty space | Mostly empty space |

### Worked example 5: why the model changed

Explain why the results of the scattering experiment led to the plum pudding model being replaced.

- In the plum pudding model, charge and mass are spread thinly, so nothing could strongly deflect an alpha particle.
- Some were deflected through large angles. That needs concentrated positive charge and mass: a nucleus.
- The old model could not explain this, so it was replaced.

## 4.1.1.4 Relative electrical charges of subatomic particles

| Particle | Relative charge |
|---|---|
| Proton | +1 |
| Neutron | 0 |
| Electron | -1 |

An atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons, so it has **no overall charge**. The number of protons is the
**atomic number**: the same for every atom of one element, different for different elements.

## 4.1.1.5 Size and mass of atoms

- Atomic radius: about **0.1 nm = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** (1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m).
- Nuclear radius: less than **1/10 000** of the atom's radius, about **1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m**.
- Almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus.

| Particle | Relative mass |
|---|---|
| Proton | 1 |
| Neutron | 1 |
| Electron | Very small |

The **mass number** is the number of protons plus neutrons. **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with
different numbers of neutrons. An atom is written with the mass number top left and the atomic number bottom
left, for example ²³₁₁Na.

```
protons   = atomic number
neutrons  = mass number - atomic number
electrons = protons - charge on the ion   (atoms: charge 0)
```

### Worked example 6: protons, neutrons and electrons

| Particle | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ³¹₁₅P | 15 | 31 - 15 = 16 | 15 |
| ⁷⁹₃₅Br⁻ | 35 | 79 - 35 = 44 | 35 + 1 = 36 |
| ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺ | 26 | 56 - 26 = 30 | 26 - 3 = 23 |

A negative ion has gained electrons; a positive ion has lost them. The nucleus never changes.

### Worked example 7: size and scale

(a) A line of atoms, each 0.2 nm across (twice the radius), is 1 mm long. How many atoms are in the line?

```
diameter = 2 x 1 x 10^-10 m = 2 x 10^-10 m
1 mm     = 1 x 10^-3 m
number   = (1 x 10^-3) / (2 x 10^-10) = 5 x 10^6 atoms
```

(b) A model atom is built with a radius of 10 m. How big should its nucleus be?

```
nuclear radius = 10 m / 10 000 = 0.001 m = 1 mm
```

## 4.1.1.6 Relative atomic mass

The **relative atomic mass** (Ar) of an element is an average that takes account of the abundance of its isotopes.

```
Ar = (mass 1 x % 1 + mass 2 x % 2 + ...) / 100
```

### Worked example 8

A sample of copper is 69% copper-63 and 31% copper-65. Calculate the relative atomic mass to 1 decimal place.

```
Ar = (63 x 69 + 65 x 31) / 100
   = (4347 + 2015) / 100
   = 6362 / 100
   = 63.62  ->  63.6
```

Check: the answer is nearer 63, the more abundant isotope.

## 4.1.1.7 Electronic structure

Electrons occupy the **lowest available energy levels** (innermost available shells). For the first 20 elements,
the first shell holds 2, the second 8, the third 8, and the fourth takes the rest. You can answer in terms of
shells or energy levels.

### Worked example 9

Give the electronic structure of silicon (atomic number 14) and potassium (19), and describe the diagram for silicon.

```
silicon:    14 electrons -> 2, 8, 4
potassium:  19 electrons -> 2, 8, 8, 1
```

Silicon's diagram: a nucleus with three circles round it, carrying 2, 8 and 4 crosses from the inside out.

## Common errors

- Changing a small number inside a formula to balance an equation. Only add numbers in front.
- Counting atoms in Mg(NO₃)₂ as 1 + 1 + 3: the 2 multiplies the whole bracket.
- Half equations that balance atoms but not charge.
- Saying the scattering experiment showed electrons orbit in shells. That was Bohr's later change.
- Adding electrons for a positive ion. A 3+ ion has three **fewer** electrons than protons.
- Forgetting to divide by 100 in a relative atomic mass calculation.
- Putting 8 electrons in the first shell, or 3 in the first shell of lithium (2,1 is correct).
