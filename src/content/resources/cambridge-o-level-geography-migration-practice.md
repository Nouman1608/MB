## Section A

**1.** State the difference between a refugee and an internally displaced person. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each movement as internal or international **and** as voluntary or forced.

| Movement | Internal or international? | Voluntary or forced? |
|---|---|---|
| (i) A graduate moves from a village to the capital city for an office job | ? | ? |
| (ii) A family crosses a border to escape fighting | ? | ? |
| (iii) A retired couple move from a city to a coastal village | ? | ? |

**[3]**

**3.** Define remittances. **[1]**

**4.** A country of 6 million people recorded 42,000 immigrants and 60,000 emigrants in one year.

**(a)** Calculate net migration. **[1]**
**(b)** Express net migration as a rate per 1,000 people. Show your working. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** intervening obstacles that can prevent people from migrating. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows the age and sex of 200 migrants who left a rural district in one year.

| Age group | Males | Females |
|---|---|---|
| 0–14 | 6 | 6 |
| 15–29 | 78 | 34 |
| 30–44 | 38 | 18 |
| 45 and over | 12 | 8 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the migrants who were males aged 15–29. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the age and sex characteristics of the migrants. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **three** effects of this migration on the rural district. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why people migrate from rural areas to cities in low-income countries. Refer to
both push and pull factors. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** advantages and **two** disadvantages of international migration for the
receiving country. **[6]**

**9.** "Emigration does more harm than good to the source country." To what extent do you agree?
**[5]**

**10.** For a named example of international migration, describe the causes of the migration and
explain its impacts on the source area **and** the receiving area. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** A refugee has been forced to leave their home and has **crossed an international border**
[1]. An internally displaced person has been forced from home but **remains within their own
country** [1].

**2.** (i) Internal, voluntary [1]. (ii) International, forced [1]. (iii) Internal, voluntary
(counter-urbanisation) [1].

**3.** Money sent home by migrants to their family or community in the source area [1].

**4. (a)** 42,000 − 60,000 = **−18,000** [1].
**(b)** −18,000 ÷ 6,000,000 × 1,000 [1] = **−3 per 1,000** [1].

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: cost of travel; distance; immigration laws or visa requirements;
language barriers; lack of information about the destination; family responsibilities.

**6. (a)** 78 ÷ 200 × 100 = **39%** [1].
**(b)** Up to 3 marks: most migrants are **young adults**, with 15–29 the largest group (112 of 200,
56%) [1]; **more males than females** (134 males, 66 females, about two to one) [1]; very few children
or people aged 45 and over (32 in total) [1]. Credit use of figures.
**(c)** Any three, 1 mark each: a **shortage of farm labour**, so output may fall or women and older
people take on more work; a **gender imbalance** with more women than men in the 15–44 age groups; a
**fall in the birth rate** because people of childbearing age have left; an **ageing population**;
**remittances** sent home raise household incomes; less pressure on land and local jobs; reduced
demand for services such as schools, which may close.

**7.** Up to 6 marks; at least one push and one pull factor needed for full marks; 1 mark for each
factor and 1 for its development.
Push: **few jobs and low incomes** in farming [1], because farms are small or mechanisation reduces
the need for labour [1]; **poor services** [1], such as few secondary schools, clinics or electricity
[1]; **natural hazards** such as drought or flooding [1] that destroy crops and incomes [1].
Pull: **more jobs and higher wages** in factories, construction and services [1], so migrants can
send remittances home [1]; **better schools and hospitals** [1]; **perceived better quality of
life** [1], often based on reports from relatives already in the city [1].

**8.** Two advantages and two disadvantages, 1 mark each plus 1 mark for each developed point
(maximum 6):
Advantages: fills **labour shortages** [1], in sectors such as health care, construction and farming
[1]; migrants of working age **pay taxes** [1], helping to support an ageing population [1]; brings
**cultural diversity** and new skills [1].
Disadvantages: **pressure on housing, schools and health services** [1], especially in areas where
many migrants settle [1]; possible **social tension or discrimination** [1]; some **competition for
low-paid jobs** [1].

**9.** Mark in levels.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** simple points on one side only.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** developed points on both harm and benefit.
- **Level 3 (5):** balanced discussion with a supported judgement.

Indicative content. Harm: brain drain of doctors, teachers and engineers; loss of young workers;
ageing and gender imbalance; separated families. Good: remittances support household spending,
education and housing; less unemployment and pressure on land; returnees bring savings and skills.
A strong judgement depends on the type of migrant and the scale of remittances, for example
temporary labour migration with large remittances may benefit the source country more than the
permanent loss of highly skilled professionals.

**10.** Mark in levels.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** general causes and impacts with no named place, or one side only.
- **Level 2 (4–5):** named example with some specific causes and impacts on both areas.
- **Level 3 (6–7):** detailed, specific answer covering causes and impacts on both source and
  receiving areas.

Indicative content (Syria to Turkey and Europe, from 2011): civil war, bombing of cities, collapse
of services and persecution forced people out; Turkey received the largest number because it is a
neighbour, with others in Lebanon and Jordan and a smaller share moving on to Germany and other
European countries, especially in 2015. Source: loss of working-age people and skilled workers,
which makes reconstruction harder; families separated. Receiving: pressure on housing, schools and
health care in border areas and camps; humanitarian costs; over time some refugees fill labour
shortages and start businesses. Equivalent credit for another named migration such as Pakistan to
the Gulf states.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a table without quoting any figures.
- Answering question 10 for one country only.
- Writing "refugees migrate for jobs".
