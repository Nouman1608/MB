This guide covers subtopic **9.3 Alloys and their properties** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). [Alloys and Extraction of Metals](/resources/alloys-and-extraction-of-metals/) introduces the two named alloys and the structural explanation; this guide develops the explanation into a full-mark answer, explains why an alloy is a mixture rather than a compound, and works through the structure-diagram skill that outcome 4 tests.

## What the syllabus asks

1. Describe an alloy as a **mixture of a metal with other elements**, including **brass** (copper and zinc) and **stainless steel** (iron and other elements such as chromium, nickel and carbon).
2. Explain in terms of structure how alloys can be **harder and stronger** than the pure metals, because the **different sized atoms or ions** in alloys mean the **layers can no longer slide** over each other.
3. Describe the uses of alloys in terms of their physical properties, including **stainless steel in cutlery** because of its **hardness and resistance to rusting**.
4. **Identify representations of alloys** from diagrams of structure.

## What an alloy is

An alloy is a **mixture**, not a compound. The added elements are spread through the main metal's lattice but are not chemically combined with it in fixed proportions. That has three consequences you can use in answers:

- An alloy has **no fixed chemical formula**; the proportions can be varied to change its properties. Brass with more zinc has different properties from brass with less.
- The main metal keeps its **metallic** character: alloys still conduct electricity and heat and are still metals in their bonding.
- The added element can be a metal (zinc in brass, chromium and nickel in stainless steel) or a **non-metal** (carbon in steel). The definition says "other elements", not "other metals".

| Alloy | Main metal | Other elements | Property gained |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brass | Copper | Zinc | Harder and stronger than copper |
| Stainless steel | Iron | Chromium, nickel, carbon | Hard and resistant to rusting |

## Why alloys are harder and stronger

### The pure metal

In a pure metal, every atom (strictly, every positive ion in the lattice) is the **same size**. The ions are packed in regular rows and layers. When a force is applied, whole layers slide over one another easily. This is what makes pure metals malleable and ductile, but it also makes many of them relatively soft. Pure copper and pure iron bend or dent quite easily.

### The alloy

In an alloy, some of the positions are taken by atoms of a **different size**. These atoms distort the regular layers. A larger atom bulges a layer out; a smaller atom leaves it pinched. The layers are no longer flat, so they **can no longer slide over each other** easily. More force is needed to change the shape of the metal, so the alloy is **harder and stronger**.

### Worked example: a full-mark explanation

*Explain, in terms of structure, why brass is harder than pure copper. [3]*

Model answer, annotated:

- "In pure copper, all the atoms are the same size and are arranged in regular layers, which can slide over each other easily." *[Mark 1: describes the pure metal.]*
- "In brass, zinc atoms have a different size from copper atoms, which disrupts the regular layers." *[Mark 2: different sized atoms; "different size" is the key phrase.]*
- "So the layers can no longer slide over each other easily, and more force is needed to change the shape." *[Mark 3: the consequence for the layers.]*

Two errors cost marks in this answer more than any others: writing that zinc atoms are "bigger" (the syllabus wording is **different sized**; in steel, carbon atoms are much smaller than iron atoms, and the effect is the same), and writing that the layers "cannot move at all" (they can move; it is just much harder).

## Uses of alloys

The required example is **stainless steel for cutlery**, because of two properties together:

- **Hardness**: knives keep their edge and forks do not bend in use.
- **Resistance to rusting**: cutlery is washed and wetted every day, and pure iron or ordinary steel would rust. The chromium and nickel in stainless steel give it this resistance.

Brass is harder than copper and resists corrosion, and its uses (such as fittings, decorative items and musical instruments) follow the same property-then-use reasoning. Only the stainless steel example is named in the syllabus, but the method for any alloy use is the same as in [Uses of metals](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-uses-of-metals/): name the property and say why it suits the use.

## Identifying alloys from diagrams

Outcome 4 is tested with particle diagrams. The text sketches below use **O** for a large atom and **o** for a smaller one.

```
Diagram P              Diagram Q              Diagram R
O O O O O O            O O O O O O            + - + - + -
O O O O O O            O O o O O O            - + - + - +
O O O O O O            O O O O O o            + - + - + -
O O O O O O            o O O O O O            - + - + - +
```

- **P: pure metal.** All particles the same size, in regular layers.
- **Q: alloy.** Mostly one kind of atom, with some atoms of a **different size** spread through the lattice, disrupting the layers.
- **R: not an alloy.** Alternating positive and negative ions are an **ionic compound**, such as sodium chloride.

Other diagrams that are **not** alloys: separate small groups of atoms with spaces between them (a simple molecular substance), and two kinds of atoms bonded in a fixed repeating ratio (a compound). The checklist for an alloy diagram:

1. Particles closely packed, as in any solid metal.
2. Most particles the same; a smaller number of a **different size**.
3. The different particles **scattered within** the main lattice, not in a separate block.

### Worked example

*A diagram shows a regular lattice of large circles with a few small circles fitted into some positions between them. State whether this represents an alloy, and explain your answer.*

Yes, it is an alloy (it resembles steel, where small carbon atoms sit among the much larger iron atoms). The main metal's atoms are arranged in a lattice, and atoms of a different size are mixed among them, disrupting the layers.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling an alloy a compound.** It is a mixture.
- **"Steel is an element."** It is an alloy of iron with other elements.
- **"The added atoms are bigger."** Say **different sized**.
- **"The layers cannot move."** They cannot **slide easily**.
- **Giving only one property for stainless steel cutlery.** The syllabus pairs hardness with resistance to rusting.
- **Listing brass as copper and tin.** Brass is copper and **zinc**.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an alloy: "a mixture of a metal with other elements", then name the elements.
- **Explain** hardness: pure metal layers, different sized atoms, layers cannot slide.
- **Describe** a use: alloy, use, property (or properties).
- **Identify** a diagram: name it and point to the feature (different sized particles in one lattice).
