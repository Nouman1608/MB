This guide covers **section 7.3 Efficiency and market failure** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus lists six statements: "definitions of productive efficiency and allocative efficiency", the "conditions for productive efficiency and allocative efficiency", "Pareto optimality", the "definition of dynamic efficiency", the "definition of market failure" and the "reasons for market failure".

The broader [efficiency, market failure and externalities study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-efficiency-market-failure-and-externalities/) uses a cost equation for a monopoly. This page goes further: it reads both efficiency conditions from a cost table, shows how the two conditions can separate in the short run and meet in the long run, connects efficiency to the production possibility curve, and links each reason for market failure to the condition it breaks. All firms and figures are invented.

## 7.3.1 and 7.3.2 Two kinds of static efficiency

**Productive efficiency** means producing output at the lowest possible average cost. For a firm, the condition is output at the minimum point of the average cost (AC) curve, which is where marginal cost (MC) equals AC. For the whole economy, it means producing on the production possibility curve (PPC), with no resources wasted.

**Allocative efficiency** means resources are allocated so that the goods and services produced are the ones consumers value most, in the right quantities. The condition is **price = marginal cost**. Price measures the value consumers place on the last unit, and marginal cost measures the value of the resources used to make it. If P > MC, consumers value one more unit above its cost, so output is too low; if P < MC, the last unit costs more than it is worth, so output is too high.

**Why MC = AC at the minimum of AC.** When the cost of the next unit is below the average, it pulls the average down; when it is above, it pulls the average up. So AC stops falling and starts rising exactly where MC crosses it.

## Worked example 1: reading both conditions from a table

A firm in a perfectly competitive market has these costs (fixed cost 40):

| Output | Total cost | MC | AC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 | 20 | 60.00 |
| 2 | 76 | 16 | 38.00 |
| 3 | 90 | 14 | 30.00 |
| 4 | 108 | 18 | 27.00 |
| 5 | 130 | 22 | 26.00 |
| 6 | 155 | 25 | 25.83 |
| 7 | 189 | 34 | 27.00 |
| 8 | 228 | 39 | 28.50 |

**Productive efficiency.** AC is lowest at 6 units (25.83). The MC of the sixth unit (25) is below the AC of 5 units, so AC falls; the MC of the seventh unit (34) is above the AC of 6 units, so AC rises. Six units is the productively efficient output.

**Allocative efficiency at a market price of 34.** The firm produces every unit whose MC is no greater than the price. Units 1 to 7 have MC at or below 34, so it produces **7 units**, where P = MC. Output is allocatively efficient.

**But not productively efficient.** At 7 units, AC = 27, above the minimum of 25.83. The firm earns supernormal profit of (34 − 27) × 7 = 49.

**In the long run,** that profit attracts new firms. Market supply rises and the price falls towards the minimum AC. At a price of about 26, the firm produces 6 units, where P = MC and AC is at its minimum. Both conditions now hold together, which is why long-run perfect competition is the benchmark for static efficiency.

## Efficiency on the production possibility curve

- Every point **on** the PPC is productively efficient: the economy cannot make more of one good without making less of another.
- Points **inside** the curve are productively inefficient: resources are unemployed or wasted.
- Only **one** point on the curve is allocatively efficient for a given set of consumer preferences: the combination society values most.

So productive efficiency is necessary for allocative efficiency in the economy as a whole, but it is not enough.

## 7.3.3 Pareto optimality

An allocation of resources is **Pareto optimal** when it is impossible to make one person better off without making at least one other person worse off. A change that makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off is a **Pareto improvement**. If such a change exists, the starting allocation was not Pareto optimal.

**Worked example 2: a trade.** Amira has 6 oranges and no mangoes; Ben has 6 mangoes and no oranges. Each would prefer a mix of fruit. If they swap 3 for 3, both are better off and nobody is worse off. That is a Pareto improvement, so the starting point was not Pareto optimal.

Two points to make in evaluation:

- When every market is both productively and allocatively efficient, the economy is Pareto optimal.
- Pareto optimality says nothing about fairness. If Amira had all 12 pieces of fruit and Ben had none, giving any to Ben would make Amira worse off, so that allocation is also Pareto optimal. Many Pareto optimal allocations exist, and they can be very unequal.

## 7.3.4 Dynamic efficiency

**Dynamic efficiency** is efficiency over time: improvements in production methods (process innovation) and in products (product innovation) that lower costs or raise quality in future. It depends on investment in research, development and new capital.

There can be a trade-off with static efficiency. A firm with market power may produce where P > MC today, but use its profit to fund research that cuts costs for years to come. A perfectly competitive firm earns only normal profit in the long run, so it may lack the funds to innovate. Judge any market on both dimensions.

## 7.3.5 and 7.3.6 Market failure and its reasons

**Market failure** occurs when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so that welfare could be increased by a different allocation. It is **complete** when the market fails to provide a good at all, and **partial** when it provides the wrong quantity.

| Reason | Which condition fails | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Negative externalities | Market output too high: the last unit's social cost exceeds its value | Pollution from a cement works |
| Positive externalities | Market output too low | Vaccination |
| Public goods | No provision at all: the free-rider problem prevents charging | Street lighting |
| Merit and demerit goods (information failure) | Consumption too low or too high because consumers misjudge private benefits or costs | Under-use of preventive health checks |
| Asymmetric information | Mutually beneficial trades do not happen, or the wrong ones do | Buyers unable to judge used-car quality |
| Market power | P > MC: output restricted below the allocatively efficient level | A monopoly supplier |
| Factor immobility | Resources stay in declining uses, so the economy operates inside its PPC | Workers whose skills do not match new jobs |

Linking each reason to the condition it breaks turns a list into analysis.

**Annotated model answer.** *Question:* Explain why a monopoly may be allocatively inefficient but dynamically efficient.

> A profit-maximising monopoly restricts output to raise price, so price is above marginal cost. **[Condition broken.]** Consumers value extra units above the cost of producing them, but those units are not produced, so resources are under-allocated to the good. **[Meaning of the inefficiency.]** However, barriers to entry let the monopoly keep supernormal profit in the long run, which it can invest in research and new methods. **[Source of funds.]** If this lowers costs or improves products over time, the monopoly is dynamically efficient, though only if it faces some pressure, such as the threat of new technology, to innovate. **[Condition for the claim.]**

## Common mistakes

- Giving MC = AC as the condition for allocative efficiency. That is productive efficiency.
- Defining allocative efficiency as "no waste". That describes productive efficiency.
- Claiming that Pareto optimality means a fair outcome.
- Treating dynamic efficiency as a point on a cost curve; it is about change over time.
- Listing reasons for market failure without saying what goes wrong with resource allocation.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Define:** precise meaning plus the condition.
- **Explain:** connect the condition to what it means for resources.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** weigh static against dynamic efficiency, and efficiency against equity; Paper 4 essays are unstructured, so plan these comparisons before writing.
