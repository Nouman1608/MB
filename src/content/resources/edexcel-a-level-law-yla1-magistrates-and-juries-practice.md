These questions cover two bullets of content area 1.2.22, "Lay people in the law": magistrates, including the Justices of the Peace Act 1979, and jurors, including the Juries Act 1974.

---

## Section A

**1.** State **two** of the six key qualities required of a magistrate. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the role of the legal adviser in a magistrates' court. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** requirements for a person to be eligible for jury service under the Juries Act 1974. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a majority verdict and when one may be accepted. **[3]**

**5.** Describe the stages by which a jury is selected for a trial in the Crown Court. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain **three** functions of magistrates in criminal cases. **[6]**

**7.** Ellie has been summoned for jury service. She is 22, has lived in the UK all her life, and two years ago received a community order for shoplifting.

Advise Ellie on whether she is eligible to serve. **[3]**

**8.** Darren is a serving police officer. He is selected for a jury in a trial where the main issue is whether the evidence of two police officers from his own station should be believed.

Explain whether the conviction could be challenged on appeal. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the courts have dealt with jurors who research their case on the internet, using **two** decided cases. **[5]**

**10.** Explain **two** disadvantages of using magistrates, with an example or case for each. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why the secrecy of the jury room can make it difficult to correct a miscarriage of justice, using **two** decided cases. **[5]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that juries should continue to decide serious criminal cases. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two [1] each: good character; understanding and communication; social awareness; maturity and sound temperament; sound judgement; commitment and reliability.

**2.** The legal adviser is a qualified lawyer who **advises the magistrates on law, practice and procedure** [1], but must **not take part in deciding** guilt or sentence [1].

**3.** Any three [1] each: registered on the **electoral register**; aged **18 to 75**; **ordinarily resident** in the UK for at least **five years since the age of 13**; not a mentally disordered person; **not disqualified**.

**4.** A majority verdict is a verdict on which the jury is **not unanimous** [1]. Under s 17 of the Juries Act 1974 it can be **11–1 or 10–2** [1], and may be accepted only after the jury has deliberated for **at least two hours** and the judge has directed that a majority is acceptable [1].

**5.** One mark each: names are chosen **at random from the electoral register** by the Jury Central Summoning Bureau [1]; those summoned attend court and form a **panel** [1]; **twelve are chosen by ballot** in the courtroom [1]; jurors may be **challenged for cause** or stood by by the prosecution before being sworn [1]. (Credit routine police vetting for disqualification as an alternative point.)

**6.** Any three, two marks each (function identified [1], explained [1]):

- **Trying summary offences** [1]: all minor offences, such as most driving offences, are tried and sentenced by magistrates [1].
- **Trying either-way offences** [1] where the defendant agrees and the magistrates accept jurisdiction, such as theft [1].
- **First hearings for indictable offences** [1]: they deal with preliminary matters and send the case to the Crown Court [1].
- **Bail** [1]: deciding whether the defendant is released or remanded in custody before trial [1].
- **Warrants** [1]: issuing search and arrest warrants to the police [1].
- **Youth court** [1]: specially trained magistrates hear cases involving defendants aged 10 to 17 [1].

**7.** Ellie meets the **age, residence and registration** requirements [1]. However, a person who has had a **community order in the last ten years** is **disqualified** under the Juries Act 1974 (as amended) [1]. She is therefore **not eligible** to serve and should tell the court [1].

**8.** Since the **Criminal Justice Act 2003** police officers are no longer ineligible [1]. But in *R v Abdroikov* [2007] the House of Lords held that a conviction should be quashed where a **fair-minded observer would see a real possibility of bias** [1]. Where the case turns on a conflict between the defendant's account and the evidence of officers, and the juror shares a **workplace** with them, that risk is clear [1], so the defendant could appeal with a good prospect of the conviction being **quashed** [1].

**9.** Jurors must decide only on the evidence given in court [1]. In *R v Fraill* [2011] a juror contacted a defendant on **Facebook** and was jailed for **contempt of court** [1]. In *Attorney General v Dallas* [2012] a juror **researched the defendant online** and told other jurors; she was also **jailed** [1]. Such conduct can make a trial unfair and lead to a retrial [1]. The **Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015** made researching a case an offence [1].

**10.** For each of two disadvantages: disadvantage identified [1], explained with an example or case [1].

- **Prosecution bias** [1]: in *ex p Jowitt* (1974) a chairman said he always believed police evidence, and the conviction was quashed [1].
- **Inconsistent sentencing** [1]: different benches may give different sentences for similar offences, despite sentencing guidelines [1].
- **Unrepresentative** [1]: magistrates tend to be older and from professional backgrounds, as the time commitment makes it hard for young workers to serve [1].
- **Reliance on the legal adviser** [1]: lay magistrates may be unduly influenced on law and procedure [1].

**11.** Deliberations are secret, and disclosure is an offence [1], so evidence of what happened in the jury room usually cannot be used on appeal [1]. In *R v Mirza* [2004] the House of Lords refused to investigate allegations of bias in the jury room [1], so a possible miscarriage could not be examined. By contrast, in *R v Young (Stephen)* [1995] the conviction was quashed because jurors used a ouija board in their **hotel**, which was **outside** the jury room [1], showing that the line between what can and cannot be examined depends on where the misconduct happened [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: qualifications and selection under the Juries Act 1974; the role in the Crown Court; majority verdicts; secrecy; trial without a jury under the CJA 2003, s 44.
- **For the view**: democratic participation and public confidence; independence from the state (*Bushell's Case*); jury equity (*Ponting*, *Kronlid*); twelve people reduce individual bias; secrecy protects jurors from pressure.
- **Against the view**: no reasons for verdicts; possible bias (*Sander v UK*, *Abdroikov*); misconduct and internet research (*Young*, *Fraill*, *Dallas*); difficulty in long or complex fraud trials; cost and time; jury tampering (*Twomey*).
- **Evaluation**: weigh democratic legitimacy against reliability and reach a conclusion, for example that juries should remain for serious cases, with safeguards such as the 2015 offence and judge-alone trials in exceptional cases.

Top-level answers use cases accurately, consider both sides and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying magistrates are paid or legally qualified.
- Saying the jury decides the sentence.
- Using old jury age limits or forgetting disqualification for community orders.
- Discussing *Abdroikov* without applying the "fair-minded observer" test.
- In the evaluation, giving one side only or no conclusion.
