Condensed for the final weeks. For the full explanation and worked examples, use the [Turning effects of forces study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-turning-effects-of-forces/); for exam-style questions, the [Turning effects of forces practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-turning-effects-of-forces-practice/). These notes cover subtopic **4.1 Turning effects of forces** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series.

## The four learning outcomes

1. Weight may be taken as acting at a single point: the **centre of gravity**.
2. Define and apply the **moment of a force**.
3. A **couple** is a pair of forces that produces rotation only.
4. Define and apply the **torque of a couple**.

## Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Centre of gravity | The point at which the whole weight of an object may be taken to act |
| Moment of a force | Force × perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force |
| Couple | A pair of equal, opposite, parallel forces whose lines of action do not coincide |
| Torque of a couple | One of the forces × the perpendicular distance between their lines of action |

Unit for both moment and torque: **N m**.

## Moments of forces at an angle

```
moment = F d sin(theta)
theta = angle between the force and the line joining the point to where the force acts
d sin(theta) = perpendicular distance to the line of action
F sin(theta) = component of F perpendicular to the lever
```

- θ = 90°: maximum moment, F × d.
- θ = 0° (force along the lever, through the pivot): zero moment.

**Recall example:** 60 N on a spanner 0.25 m long, at 70° to the spanner: moment = 60 × 0.25 × sin 70° = 14 N m (compared with 15 N m at 90°).

## Couple versus single force

| | Single force off-centre | Couple |
|---|---|---|
| Resultant force | Non-zero | Zero |
| Effect | Turning and change in linear motion (unless another force balances it) | Turning only |
| Turning effect | Depends on the point chosen | Same about every point |

**Recall example:** a tap key with two 15 N forces whose lines of action are 0.12 m apart has torque 15 × 0.12 = 1.8 N m. Not 2 × 15 × 0.12.

## Centre of gravity: what to know

- Uniform, symmetrical object: at the geometric centre.
- Non-uniform object: nearer the heavier part; find it from a balance (moments) or by experiment.
- May lie outside the material (ring, L-shape).
- In any moments calculation, include the object's own weight, acting at its centre of gravity.
- For an object made of parts (a rod carrying a load), treat each part's weight as acting at that part's own centre of gravity.

**Locating it by calculation:** a non-uniform 2.4 m plank of weight 120 N rests on supports at its ends. The support at end A pushes up with 80 N, so the other support pushes with 40 N. Moments about A: 40 × 2.4 = 120 × x, so x = 0.80 m from A.

## Toppling and stability

- Stable while the vertical through the centre of gravity falls inside the base.
- Topples when that vertical passes outside the base edge.
- Tipping angle for a symmetrical object: tan θ = (half the base width) ÷ (height of centre of gravity).
- More stable with a **lower** centre of gravity and a **wider** base.

**Recall example:** a vehicle with wheels 2.0 m apart and its centre of gravity 1.6 m high topples when tilted beyond tan⁻¹(1.0/1.6) = 32°.

## Exam traps

- Leaving "perpendicular" out of a definition.
- Measuring distance to where the force is applied instead of to its line of action.
- Torque of a couple calculated as 2 × F × d.
- A couple described without "parallel" or without "lines of action do not coincide".
- Ignoring the weight of a beam or lever.
- Assuming the centre of gravity of a non-uniform object is at its middle.
- Mixing up clockwise and anticlockwise when combining moments.

## Self-test

1. Define the centre of gravity.
2. Define the moment of a force.
3. Give the unit of moment.
4. A 40 N force acts at the end of a lever 0.50 m long, perpendicular to it. Find its moment about the pivot.
5. The same force acts at 30° to the lever. Find its moment.
6. Define a couple.
7. Why does a couple produce no linear acceleration?
8. Two forces of 25 N form a couple; their lines of action are 0.20 m apart. Find the torque.
9. Why is the torque of a couple the same about any point?
10. A uniform rod 1.6 m long and of weight 24 N is hinged at one end and held horizontal by a vertical string at the other end. Find the tension.
11. Where is the centre of gravity of a uniform metre rule?
12. Give two ways to make a lamp less likely to topple.

### Answers

1. The point at which the whole weight of an object may be taken to act.
2. The product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
3. N m.
4. 40 × 0.50 = 20 N m.
5. 40 × 0.50 × sin 30° = 10 N m.
6. A pair of equal, opposite, parallel forces whose lines of action do not coincide.
7. The two forces are equal and opposite, so the resultant force is zero.
8. 25 × 0.20 = 5.0 N m.
9. Moving the point increases the moment of one force by exactly as much as it decreases the moment of the other, so the total stays F × d.
10. Moments about the hinge: T × 1.6 = 24 × 0.80, so T = 12 N.
11. At the 50 cm mark (its midpoint).
12. Lower its centre of gravity (a heavy base) and widen its base.
