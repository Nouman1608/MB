These questions cover section 3.1.1 Economic foundations (economic activity, the factors of production and making choices) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) specification, for exams from May/June 2025 onwards. All people, places and figures are invented, and money is in unnamed currency units. Revise first with the [economic foundations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-economic-foundations-oxfordaqa-igcse-economics/) and the [economic foundations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-economic-foundations-oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: multiple choice and short answers

**1.** The central purpose of economic activity is: **[1]**
A. to make a profit for firms  B. to produce goods and services to satisfy needs and wants  C. to collect taxes for government  D. to use up natural resources

**2.** Which of these is an example of capital as a factor of production? **[1]**
A. a bank loan  B. a forest  C. a delivery van  D. a shop manager

**3.** The reward to enterprise is: **[1]**
A. rent  B. wages  C. interest  D. profit

**4.** A firm decides whether to use robots or hire more workers on its production line. Which key economic decision is this mainly about? **[1]**
A. what to produce  B. how to produce  C. who is to benefit  D. where to sell

**5.** Opportunity cost is best defined as: **[1]**
A. the money paid for a product  B. the total of all alternatives given up  C. the benefit of the next best alternative given up  D. the cost of producing one more unit

**6.** Explain, using an example, how a want can become a need over time. **[3]**

**7.** Lina is a self-employed taxi driver. She owns her car, drives it herself and decides which hours to work and which areas to serve.

**(a)** Identify two factors of production that Lina supplies herself. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why Lina's income is a mix of more than one factor reward. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why using an economic resource always involves an opportunity cost. **[3]**

**9.** A town council has 2 million to spend. It has three options.

| Option | Cost (million) | Estimated benefits (million) |
|---|---|---|
| Library refurbishment | 2.0 | 2.9 |
| Sports centre | 2.0 | 3.3 |
| Road repairs | 2.0 | 2.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the net benefit of each option. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the opportunity cost of choosing the option with the highest net benefit. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the council might not choose the option with the highest net benefit. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in the wages that producers pay their workers might affect consumers and government. **[4]**

### Section B: extended answers

**11.** Using an example, analyse how scarcity forces a government to make choices. **[6]**

**12.** The government of Country P must choose how to produce more electricity over the next 20 years.

| | Option A: coal power station | Option B: solar farm |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | 400 million | 520 million |
| Value of electricity produced | 900 million | 950 million |
| Estimated cost of harm to health from air pollution | 200 million | Very small |
| Jobs during construction | 1,200 | 800 |

Evaluate which option the government should choose. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]
*Examiner insight:* Profit (A) may be a firm's objective, but the purpose of economic activity is production to meet needs and wants.

**2.** C [1]
*Examiner insight:* A bank loan is finance used to buy capital, not capital itself; a forest is land and a manager is labour.

**3.** D [1]
*Examiner insight:* Rent, wages and interest reward land, labour and capital.

**4.** B [1]
*Examiner insight:* Choosing between methods or resources is always the "how" decision.

**5.** C [1]
*Examiner insight:* Option B, the total of all alternatives, is the most common wrong choice.

**6.** A want is something desired but not essential, while a need is required to live at a basic level [1]. For example, a mobile phone was a want for most people when it was new [1], but as banking, school work and government services moved online, many people now need one to take part in everyday life [1].
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the reason for the change, not just the claim that it changed.

**7. (a)** Labour, because she does the driving [1]; enterprise, because she organises the business and takes the risk [1]. (Capital, the car, is also accepted as one of the two, since she has supplied it from her own funds.)
**(b)** Part of her income is a reward for her work, like a wage [1], and whatever is left after paying her costs is profit, the reward for taking the risk of running the business [1].
*Examiner insight:* Saying that one person can supply two factors is good understanding, not a contradiction.

**8.** Economic resources are limited in supply (scarce) [1]. Each resource has alternative uses, so using it for one purpose means it cannot be used for another [1]. The benefit of the best of those other uses is lost, and that is the opportunity cost [1].
*Examiner insight:* The answer needs both scarcity and alternative uses to reach the idea of a cost.

**9. (a)** Library: 2.9 − 2.0 = **0.9 million** [1]; sports centre: 3.3 − 2.0 = **1.3 million** [1]; road repairs: 2.6 − 2.0 = **0.6 million** [1].
**(b)** The library refurbishment, worth a net benefit of 0.9 million [1].
**(c)** The benefits are only estimates, so the sports centre's 3.3 million may not be reached [1]; the council may prefer the library because its benefits go to groups in greater need, such as students without books at home, which the figures do not show [1]. Other valid reasons: road repairs may be needed for safety; the sports centre's running costs may be high.
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the opportunity cost is the next best option only; adding the roads as well loses the mark.

**10.** Higher wages mean workers, who are also consumers, have more income [1], so they can buy more goods and services, raising their living standards [1]. However, producers' costs rise, so they may raise prices, which reduces what consumers can buy with the extra income [1]. Government may collect more income tax from higher wages and more sales tax from higher spending [1].
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links at least two groups, showing an interaction rather than one effect.

**11.** A government has a limited budget because tax revenue is limited, while the demands on it, such as health, education and defence, are unlimited [1]. So it must choose which services to provide and how much of each [1]. For example, if it spends 300 million on a new motorway, that money cannot be spent on new schools [1]. The opportunity cost is the benefit those schools would have brought, such as better-educated workers in future [1]. The government must weigh the benefits of each option against its costs, for example faster journeys for firms against better education [1]. Whatever it chooses, some groups gain and others lose, so the choice also involves judgements about fairness [1].
*Examiner insight:* The chain is scarcity, then choice, then something given up, then the consequence. The example must carry the analysis, not sit at the end.

**12.** Net benefit of A before health effects: 900 − 400 = 500 million [1]. Including health harm: 500 − 200 = 300 million [1]. Net benefit of B: 950 − 520 = 430 million [1]. So on the full costs and benefits, B is higher by 130 million [1]. A creates 400 more construction jobs, which matters if unemployment is high in Country P [1]. B costs 120 million more at the start, which has an opportunity cost in other government spending if the budget is tight [1]. The health figure is only an estimate, but the coal plant also uses a non-renewable resource and adds to pollution for future generations, so sustainability favours B [1]. Judgement: the government should choose the solar farm, because once the cost of harm to health is counted it gives the higher net benefit; the decision would change only if the health estimate is far too high or the extra 120 million cannot be found [1].
*Examiner insight:* The top marks go to answers that include the external health cost before comparing, then weigh the job and budget arguments, then reach a clear judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a bank loan or money as an example of capital.
- Giving all alternatives as the opportunity cost instead of the next best one.
- Comparing benefits without subtracting costs.
- Writing about one economic group when the question asks about interactions.
- Ending an evaluation with "it depends" and no decision.
