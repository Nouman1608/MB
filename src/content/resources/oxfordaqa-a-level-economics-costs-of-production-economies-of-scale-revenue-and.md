## 3.1.3.3 Costs of production

**Short run and long run.** These are not fixed lengths of time. In the short run, one or more inputs are locked in, such as the size of the factory or the number of ovens. In the long run every input can be varied, so the firm can change its scale.

**Fixed and variable costs.** Fixed costs stay the same whatever the output while the short run lasts (rent, a lease on equipment, insurance). They are still paid at zero output. Variable costs rise as output rises (ingredients, packaging, hourly wages for extra shifts). A cost is only "fixed" for a stated period: in the long run every cost is variable.

**Total and average costs.**

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost (TC) | fixed cost (FC) + variable cost (VC) |
| Fixed cost per unit, AFC | FC ÷ output |
| Variable cost per unit, AVC | VC ÷ output |
| Average cost (AC), also called average total cost | TC ÷ output, which equals AFC + AVC |

### Worked example 1: a small bakery

The bakery's output is measured in batches of 20 loaves a day. Fixed costs are 90 a day (currency units, invented). Total costs are given; you complete the rest.

| Batches | TC | VC (TC − 90) | AFC | AVC | AC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 150 | 60 | 90.0 | 60.0 | 150.0 |
| 2 | 190 | 100 | 45.0 | 50.0 | 95.0 |
| 3 | 240 | 150 | 30.0 | 50.0 | 80.0 |
| 4 | 300 | 210 | 22.5 | 52.5 | 75.0 |
| 5 | 390 | 300 | 18.0 | 60.0 | 78.0 |
| 6 | 540 | 450 | 15.0 | 75.0 | 90.0 |

Working for 5 batches: VC = 390 − 90 = 300; AFC = 90 ÷ 5 = 18; AVC = 300 ÷ 5 = 60; AC = 390 ÷ 5 = 78, and AFC + AVC = 18 + 60 = 78, which checks the result.

**Reading the table.** AFC falls at every step because the 90 is shared among more batches. AVC first falls, then rises. AC falls until 4 batches (75), then rises, so the short-run average cost curve is U-shaped. The specification asks you to appreciate this shape; it does not expect a formal link to diminishing returns in this unit, which is left to Unit 3. A safe explanation: at low output the fixed cost is spread thinly, and at high output the firm's fixed plant becomes crowded, so extra output costs more.

```

 |  \            /

```

### Worked example 2: a rise in fixed cost

Rent rises so that fixed costs go from 90 to 150. At 5 batches, VC is still 300. New TC = 150 + 300 = 450. New AC = 450 ÷ 5 = 90, up from 78 (+15.4%). AVC is still 300 ÷ 5 = 60. A change in fixed cost moves AFC and AC but leaves AVC unchanged.

**A2 note (QS6).** For A2 assessments you also need marginal values: marginal cost is the change in total cost from one extra unit. From the bakery table, going from 3 to 4 batches raises TC from 240 to 300, so the marginal cost of the fourth batch is 60. Marginal values are not part of the section 3.1.3.3 wording itself.

## 3.1.3.4 Economies and diseconomies of scale

Economies of scale are falls in long-run average cost as the scale of production increases. Diseconomies of scale are rises in long-run average cost as scale increases further. Both belong to the long run, because only then can a firm change all its inputs.

**Internal economies** arise from the growth of the firm itself:

- **Technical**: larger machines or production lines that cost less per unit; equipment that only makes sense above a certain output.
- **Purchasing (bulk-buying)**: suppliers give lower prices per unit for large orders.
- **Managerial**: a large firm can employ specialist managers, such as a full-time accountant, instead of one owner doing everything.
- **Financial**: larger firms are often seen as less risky and can borrow at lower interest rates.
- **Marketing**: an advertising campaign costs about the same whatever the output, so the cost per unit sold falls.

**External economies** arise from the growth of the industry or its location, whatever the size of any single firm. Examples: a cluster of firms in one region attracts specialist suppliers and repairers; a local college trains the skills the industry needs; better roads or ports built because the industry is large. Every firm in the area benefits.

| Feature | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| Source | growth of the individual firm | growth of the industry or area |
| Who gains | that firm | all firms in the industry or area |
| Effect on LRAC | movement down along the curve | the whole curve shifts down |

**Diseconomies of scale** have causes you can state and apply:

- **Co-ordination and communication**: more layers of management slow decisions and distort information.
- **Loss of control**: owners and managers cannot monitor everything, so waste and error rise.
- **Worker motivation**: employees in a very large organisation can feel remote from its aims, so effort and productivity can fall.
- **Duplication**: separate departments or sites repeat tasks.

**The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve.** It is the lowest average cost at which each output can be made when all inputs can change. Falling LRAC means economies of scale; flat LRAC means neither; rising LRAC means diseconomies. The smallest output that reaches that lowest LRAC is the minimum efficient scale (MES).

### Worked example 3: a juice producer

Output is in thousands of bottles a week; LRAC is the average cost per bottle (invented figures).

| Output | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRAC | 12.0 | 9.6 | 8.0 | 7.2 | 7.2 | 7.2 | 7.8 | 9.0 |
| Total cost | 120 | 192 | 240 | 288 | 360 | 432 | 546 | 720 |
| Index (10 = 100) | 100.0 | 80.0 | 66.7 | 60.0 | 60.0 | 60.0 | 65.0 | 75.0 |

Total cost is LRAC × output (for example 9.6 × 20 = 192). The index is each LRAC ÷ 12.0 × 100 (for example 7.2 ÷ 12.0 × 100 = 60.0).

- Economies of scale between 10 and 40: doubling output from 10 to 20 cuts LRAC by (9.6 − 12.0) ÷ 12.0 = −20.0%.
- Constant costs between 40 and 60; MES is 40.
- Diseconomies above 60: from 60 to 80 output rises 33.3% (20 ÷ 60) and LRAC rises (9.0 − 7.2) ÷ 7.2 = 25.0%.
- A new entrant at output 20 would have costs (9.6 − 7.2) ÷ 7.2 = 33.3% above a firm at MES, which is a barrier to entry for small firms.

### Worked example 4: how bulk-buying reaches average cost

A bakery pays 1.20 a kilo for flour. A supplier offers 1.05 a kilo on orders of at least 1,000 kg. The saving on 1,000 kg is 1,200 − 1,050 = 150, a fall of 12.5% in the flour price. If flour is 0.80 of an average cost of 2.00 a loaf, flour cost per loaf falls to 0.80 × 0.875 = 0.70, so AC becomes 2.00 − 0.80 + 0.70 = 1.90, a fall of 5.0%. A 12.5% saving on one input is a smaller saving on total AC.

### Evaluating economies of scale

Weigh the benefit against limits:

1. Do the economies exist at this scale? The firm may already be at MES.
2. Is demand large enough?
3. Time: economies are long-run, but the costs of expansion (borrowing, building) come first.
4. Diseconomies: past some scale, managing the larger firm can cost more than the saving.
5. Do consumers benefit? Only if lower costs are passed on in lower prices.

## 3.1.3.5 Average revenue, total revenue and profit

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total revenue (TR) | selling price × units sold |
| Average revenue (AR) | TR ÷ quantity |
| Profit | TR − TC |
| Profit per unit | AR − AC |

If the firm sells every unit at the same price, AR equals price. Buyers decide how many units to take at each price, and that is what the firm sells. Plotting price (AR) against quantity therefore gives the demand curve, so **the average revenue curve is the firm's demand curve**. A change in demand shifts the AR curve; a change in price moves the firm along it. (If a firm charges different prices to different buyers, AR is the average of those prices.)

### Worked example 5: a tailoring workshop

Demand follows price = 48 − 2 × quantity (shirts a day). Total costs are given.

| Shirts | AR (price) | TR | TC | AC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 44 | 88 | 100 | 50.00 | −12 |
| 4 | 40 | 160 | 140 | 35.00 | 20 |
| 6 | 36 | 216 | 190 | 31.67 | 26 |
| 8 | 32 | 256 | 250 | 31.25 | 6 |
| 10 | 28 | 280 | 330 | 33.00 | −50 |
| 12 | 24 | 288 | 430 | 35.83 | −142 |

Working at 4 shirts: TR = 40 × 4 = 160; profit = 160 − 140 = 20. Cross-check with profit per unit: AR − AC = 40 − 35 = 5, and 5 × 4 = 20. Profit is highest, at 26, at 6 shirts. The workshop makes a loss at 2 shirts and again from 10 shirts.

### Worked example 6: more revenue is not more profit

Cutting the price from 36 to 32 raises sales from 6 to 8 shirts. TR rises from 216 to 256, an increase of 18.5% (40 ÷ 216). Profit falls from 26 to 6, because TC rises from 190 to 250, by 60, more than the 40 gained in revenue. Now suppose demand rises so that the price at every quantity is 4 higher (the AR curve shifts up). At 6 shirts the price is 40, TR is 240, and profit is 240 − 190 = 50.

**A2 note.** Marginal revenue, the change in TR from one extra unit, is a marginal value. Between 4 and 6 shirts, TR rises by 56 over 2 shirts, or 28 each, while marginal cost is (190 − 140) ÷ 2 = 25 each.

## Common errors

- Dividing only FC by output to get AC, or forgetting that AC = AFC + AVC.
- Saying fixed costs are "zero" at zero output; they are not.
- Drawing the short-run AC curve as a straight line, or using it to show economies of scale. Short-run shape and long-run shape have different explanations.
- Mixing up internal and external economies: ask whether it is the firm or the industry that has grown.
- Saying a firm "makes a profit" because revenue is high. Profit needs costs compared.
