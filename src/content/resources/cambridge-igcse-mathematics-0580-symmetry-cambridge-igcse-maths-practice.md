These questions cover subtopic 4.5, Symmetry, in the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. Questions labelled **(Extended)** use statement E4.5.2 (planes and axes of symmetry of solids), which is examined only on Papers 2 and 4; every other question suits both tiers. None of the questions needs a calculator.

For explanations, read the [symmetry study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-0580-symmetry/); for a one-page summary, use the [symmetry revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-symmetry-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** For each shape, write down the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry.

**(a)** A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus **[1]**

**(b)** A rhombus that is not a square **[1]**

**(c)** A regular hexagon **[1]**

**2.** A shape fits exactly onto itself when it is turned through 40° about its centre, and through no smaller angle. Write down its order of rotational symmetry. **[1]**

**3.** Name the quadrilateral that has exactly one line of symmetry, where that line is one of its diagonals. **[1]**

**4.** A regular polygon has rotational symmetry of order 10.

**(a)** Write down the number of lines of symmetry. **[1]**

**(b)** Work out the size of one exterior angle. **[1]**

**5.** A triangle has rotational symmetry of order 3. Name the type of triangle and write down the size of each of its angles. **[2]**

**6.** (Extended) Write down the number of planes of symmetry of

**(a)** a cuboid measuring 2 cm by 3 cm by 5 cm, **[1]**

**(b)** a right pyramid with a square base. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A quadrilateral has vertices P(1, 2), Q(4, 5), R(7, 2) and S(4, −1).

**(a)** Show that PQRS is a square. **[2]**

**(b)** Write down the equations of all the lines of symmetry of PQRS. **[2]**

**(c)** Write down the order of rotational symmetry and the coordinates of the centre of rotation. **[2]**

**8.** The line x = 3 is a line of symmetry of a hexagon. Four of its vertices are (3, 0), (1, 1), (1, 3) and (3, 5).

**(a)** Write down the coordinates of the other two vertices. **[2]**

**(b)** Does the hexagon have rotational symmetry of order 2 about the point (3, 2.5)? Give a reason. **[2]**

**9.** Each interior angle of a regular polygon is 140°.

**(a)** Work out the number of sides. **[2]**

**(b)** Write down the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why none of its lines of symmetry passes through two vertices. **[1]**

**10.** Triangle ABC is isosceles with AB = AC = 13 cm and BC = 10 cm. Its line of symmetry meets BC at M.

**(a)** Write down the length of BM and the size of angle AMB, using the symmetry of the triangle. **[2]**

**(b)** Work out the area of triangle ABC. **[2]**

**11.** Rhombus PQRS has diagonals PR = 16 cm and QS = 12 cm.

**(a)** Use the symmetry of a rhombus to explain why the diagonals cross at right angles. **[1]**

**(b)** Work out the length of one side of the rhombus. **[2]**

**(c)** Kim says, "A rhombus has four lines of symmetry because it has four equal sides." Explain why Kim is wrong. **[1]**

**12.** (Extended) A right prism is 20 cm long. Its cross-section is an equilateral triangle.

**(a)** Work out the number of planes of symmetry of the prism. **[2]**

**(b)** Write down the order of rotational symmetry of the prism about the axis that joins the centres of its two triangular faces. **[1]**

**13.** (Extended)

**(a)** Write down the number of planes of symmetry of a right circular cone. **[1]**

**(b)** A solid cylinder is cut in half along a plane that contains its axis. Work out the number of planes of symmetry of one of the two half-cylinders. **[2]**

**(c)** A cuboid has a square base of side 4 cm and a height of 9 cm. Write down the number of planes of symmetry. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 0 lines of symmetry, order 2 [1]

**(b)** 2 lines of symmetry (the diagonals), order 2 [1]

**(c)** 6 lines of symmetry, order 6 [1]

*Examiner insight:* a general parallelogram has rotational symmetry but no lines of symmetry; folding along a diagonal does not match the halves.

**2.** 360 ÷ 40 = **9** [1]

**3.** **Kite** [1]

*Examiner insight:* a rhombus also has diagonals as lines of symmetry, but it has two of them, not exactly one.

**4. (a)** **10** [1]

**(b)** 360° ÷ 10 = **36°** [1]

**5.** **Equilateral** triangle [1]; each angle is **60°** [1]

**6. (a)** **3** [1]

**(b)** **4** [1]

*Examiner insight:* for the square-based pyramid, the four planes all pass through the apex: two through opposite edges of the base and two through midpoints of opposite base edges.

**7. (a)** Each side is √(3² + 3²) = √18, so all four sides are equal [1]; the diagonals PR and QS are both 6 units long (or PQ has gradient 1 and QR has gradient −1, so angle PQR = 90°), so PQRS is a square [1]

**(b)** The diagonals: **y = 2** and **x = 4**; the lines through midpoints of opposite sides: **y = x − 2** and **y = −x + 6**. All four correct [2] (one mark for two or three correct)

**(c)** Order **4** [1]; centre **(4, 2)** [1]

*Examiner insight:* the centre of rotation is where the diagonals cross; the midpoint of PR, ((1 + 7)/2, (2 + 2)/2), gives it directly.

**8. (a)** Reflecting in x = 3 sends (x, y) to (6 − x, y): (1, 1) → **(5, 1)** [1]; (1, 3) → **(5, 3)** [1]

**(b)** **No** [1]. A half turn about (3, 2.5) sends (1, 1) to (5, 4), which is not a vertex of the hexagon [1]

*Examiner insight:* a half turn about (a, b) sends (x, y) to (2a − x, 2b − y). Testing one vertex that fails is enough to show the symmetry is not there.

**9. (a)** Exterior angle = 180° − 140° = 40° [1]; number of sides = 360 ÷ 40 = **9** [1]

**(b)** **9** lines of symmetry and order **9** [1]

**(c)** The polygon has an odd number of sides, so opposite each vertex there is a side, not a vertex; every line of symmetry joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side [1]

**10. (a)** BM = **5 cm** and angle AMB = **90°**, because the line of symmetry cuts the base in half at right angles [2]

**(b)** AM = √(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12 cm [1]; area = ½ × 10 × 12 = **60 cm²** [1]

**11. (a)** Each diagonal is a line of symmetry, and a line of symmetry meets the line joining a point to its mirror image at 90°; reflecting in PR swaps Q and S, so QS is perpendicular to PR [1]

**(b)** The diagonals bisect each other, so the half-diagonals are 8 cm and 6 cm, meeting at 90° [1]; side = √(8² + 6²) = **10 cm** [1]

**(c)** A rhombus has only **2** lines of symmetry, its diagonals; the lines joining midpoints of opposite sides are not lines of symmetry unless the angles are all 90° (a square) [1]

**12. (a)** The equilateral cross-section has 3 lines of symmetry, giving 3 planes along the prism [1]; plus 1 plane across the middle of the prism, so **4** [1]

**(b)** **3** [1]

*Examiner insight:* the plane across the middle is the one most often missed, because it does not show up when you look at the cross-section.

**13. (a)** **Infinitely many** (every plane containing the axis) [1]

**(b)** The cross-section is a semicircle, which has 1 line of symmetry, giving 1 plane along the solid [1]; plus 1 plane across the middle, so **2** [1]

**(c)** **5** [1]

*Examiner insight:* in (c) the square ends give four planes along the height, and the plane halfway up gives the fifth.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing order 0 instead of order 1 for a shape with no rotational symmetry.
- Treating the diagonals of a rectangle or parallelogram as lines of symmetry.
- Confusing the interior angle of a regular polygon with the angle of turn.
- On a grid, reflecting a point in x = a by moving it the wrong distance: the image of (x, y) is (2a − x, y).
- (Extended) Missing the plane across the middle of a prism, or adding one to a pyramid or cone.
- (Extended) Giving a cuboid with a square end only 3 planes, as if all its edges were different.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many planes of symmetry does a prism have?**
For a right prism, count the lines of symmetry of its cross-section and add one for the plane across the middle. A prism with a regular n-sided cross-section has n + 1 planes, unless it is a cube, which has 9.

**How do you find a missing vertex using a line of symmetry?**
Measure how far the known vertex is from the mirror line and go the same distance on the other side, at right angles to the line. For the line x = a, the image of (x, y) is (2a − x, y).
