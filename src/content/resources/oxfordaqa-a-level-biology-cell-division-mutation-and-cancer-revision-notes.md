## 3.2.10.1 Cell cycle at a glance

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| G1 | Cell grows; protein content rises; more organelles (mitochondria, ribosomes) |
| S | DNA replicated |
| G2 | Rapid growth and protein synthesis |
| Mitosis | Nucleus divides; two daughter cells with identical DNA |

G1, S and G2 together make up interphase, the period between mitotic divisions.

**Worked reminder: time in each phase.** 360 cells are classified from a culture with an 18 hour cycle: 162 in G1, 99 in S, 63 in G2, 36 in mitosis. A phase's share of the cells matches its share of the cycle time.

- S: 99 / 360 = 27.5%, so 0.275 × 18 = 4.95 hours (about 5.0 hours)
- Mitosis: 36 / 360 = 10.0%, so 1.8 hours
- G1 is 8.1 hours and G2 is 3.15 hours; all four sum to 18.0 hours

## 3.2.10.2 Mitosis: one-line summaries

| Stage | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| Prophase | Chromosomes condense and become visible; each is two chromatids joined at a centromere |
| Metaphase | Chromosomes line up at the equator, centromeres attached to spindle fibres |
| Anaphase | Centromeres divide; fibres shorten and pull chromatids to opposite poles |
| Telophase | Chromosomes at poles; nuclear envelopes reform; cell divides |

**Method: recognise a stage from a photograph.**

1. Are chromosomes visible as separate structures? If not, it is interphase.
2. If visible but scattered, it is prophase.
3. If in a single line across the middle, it is metaphase.
4. If in two groups moving apart, it is anaphase.
5. If in two groups at the ends with nuclei forming, it is telophase.

**Counting reminder.** A cell with 2n = 14: 14 chromosomes in G1; 14 chromosomes (28 chromatids) after S phase; 28 chromosomes moving in anaphase; 14 in each daughter. If a G1 nucleus has 6.0 pg DNA, it has 12.0 pg after S phase and 6.0 pg in each daughter cell.

## 3.2.10.3 Binary fission and conjugation

| Feature | Binary fission | Mitosis |
|---|---|---|
| Cell type | Prokaryote | Eukaryote |
| DNA copied | Circular DNA and plasmids | Linear chromosomes |
| Spindle | None | Yes |
| Daughter cells | Two; one copy of circular DNA each, plasmid number may vary | Two with identical DNA |

**Conjugation:** DNA (a plasmid copy) passes from one bacterium to another, which may be a different species. This is **horizontal gene transmission**. Vertical transmission is from parent to daughter cell.

**Worked reminder.** 15 cells divide every 40 minutes for 4 hours. Divisions = 240 / 40 = 6. Cells = 15 × 2⁶ = 15 × 64 = 960.

## 3.2.11.1 Gene mutations

- Gene mutation: any alteration to the order of bases in DNA.
- Mutations arise spontaneously during DNA replication.
- Mutagens are physical or chemical agents that increase the frequency of mutations.
- Examples of mutation: base substitution and base deletion.
- Degenerate code: more than one triplet can code for the same amino acid, so some substitutions do not change the amino acid sequence.

**Method: predict the effect on the amino acid sequence.**

1. Write the original mRNA codons (if given DNA, build the complementary mRNA).
2. Apply the change to the correct base.
3. Regroup in threes from the start codon.
4. Read each codon from the code table supplied.
5. Compare with the original sequence and state what has changed.

**Worked reminder.** Original mRNA: AUG UCC AAG GGU CAU UGA, giving Met - Ser - Lys - Gly - His - stop.

| Change | New codons | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution, UCC to UCA | AUG UCA AAG GGU CAU UGA | Ser still; no change to sequence |
| Substitution, AAG to AAU | AUG UCC AAU GGU CAU UGA | Lys replaced by Asn; one change |
| Deletion of the fourth base (U) | AUG CCA AGG GUC AUU GA | Met - Pro - Arg - Val - Ile; everything after Met altered |

A deletion changes the reading frame, so it generally has a larger effect than a substitution. Substitutions can have a large effect too, for example when they create a stop codon.

## 3.2.11.2 Cancers

| | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Slower | Faster |
| Edges | Often encapsulated | Invasive, irregular |
| Spread | Stays in place | Spreads, forming secondary tumours |
| Cells | Similar to normal | Often abnormal in shape and nucleus |

**Genes controlling division**

| Gene type | Normal role | After mutation |
|---|---|---|
| Proto-oncogene | Stimulates cell division | Becomes an oncogene: division stimulated too much |
| Tumour suppressor gene | Slows cell division | Inactivated: slowing lost, rate of division increases |

**Method: evaluate risk-factor data.**

1. Quote figures from the table, including units (cases per 100 000 per year).
2. Compare as a ratio or difference between groups.
3. Describe the trend (rises with exposure, or no pattern).
4. Say whether it shows correlation only, and what else could explain it.
5. Comment on sample size, matching of groups and any known mechanism.

**Worked reminder.** Illustrative figures for oral cancer, cases per 100 000 per year, by daily alcohol intake: none 3.1; 1 to 2 units 4.0; 3 to 4 units 7.9; 5 or more units 16.5. Ratio for 5 or more units against none: 16.5 / 3.1 = 5.3, so over five times. The increase is steady, which suggests a link. It does not prove cause, because heavy drinkers may differ in other ways, such as smoking.

**Drugs and the cell cycle.**

| Drug action | Cell cycle link |
|---|---|
| Blocks DNA replication | Acts in S phase; cell cannot complete the cycle |
| Prevents spindle formation | Acts in mitosis; chromatids cannot be separated |

Side effects arise because healthy cells that divide often, such as hair follicle and gut lining cells, are also affected.

## Required practical 4: root tip squash

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Cut the tip and warm it in hydrochloric acid | Softens tissue so cells separate |
| Add stain (such as acetic orcein) | Makes chromosomes visible |
| Squash under a coverslip | One thin layer of cells |
| Count cells in each stage | Gives data for the mitotic index |

Mitotic index: cells in mitosis divided by all cells counted (× 100 for a percentage).

**Worked reminder.** Untreated tip: 48 cells in mitosis out of 380. Index = 48 / 380 = 12.6%. Treated tip: 19 out of 360 = 5.3%. The treated tip has a lower index, so fewer cells were dividing. A reliable comparison needs several fields of view for each tip, with the same total counted.

## Model answer shapes

**Explain how spindle fibres separate chromatids.** Spindle fibres are attached to the centromeres. At anaphase the centromeres divide and the fibres shorten. This pulls one chromatid of each pair to each pole, so each daughter nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome.

**Explain how a mutation in a tumour suppressor gene can lead to a tumour.** The mutation inactivates the gene, so it no longer slows cell division. The rate of division increases and cells divide in an uncontrolled way, forming a mass of cells.

**Explain why a mutagen increases cancer risk.** A mutagen raises the frequency of mutation. A mutation in a proto-oncogene or a tumour suppressor gene is then more likely, and that can cause the rate of division to rise.

**Suggest why a root tip, not a mature part of the root, is used.** The cells just behind the tip are dividing, so more cells are in mitosis and a count is possible.

## Must-know distinctions

- Chromatid or chromosome: after S phase the chromosome has two chromatids; after anaphase each chromatid is a chromosome.
- Proto-oncogene or oncogene: the first is the normal gene, the second the mutated form.
- Deletion or substitution: deletion shifts the frame; substitution changes one codon.
- Horizontal or vertical: horizontal moves genes between cells; vertical passes them to daughters.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the phase in which DNA is replicated.
2. State what increases in number in G1 besides protein.
3. A cell has 2n = 10. How many chromatids are present at metaphase?
4. Which structure do spindle fibres attach to?
5. In which stage do chromatids separate?
6. State what happens to plasmid number in binary fission.
7. Name the process that passes a plasmid between bacteria.
8. A substitution changes CUU to CUC (both Leu). Give the effect on the protein.
9. Give the mitotic index as a percentage: 24 cells in mitosis out of 200.
10. Name the mutated form of a proto-oncogene.

### Answers

1. S phase.
2. Organelles, such as mitochondria and ribosomes.
3. 20 chromatids (10 chromosomes, each with two).
4. The centromere.
5. Anaphase.
6. It may vary between the two daughter cells.
7. Conjugation.
8. No change, because the code is degenerate.
9. 24 / 200 × 100 = 12.0%.
10. An oncogene.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that DNA replicates during prophase instead of S phase.
- Using "chromosome" and "chromatid" as if they were the same thing.
- Describing anaphase without mentioning the shortening spindle fibres and the centromere.
- Giving only "the code is degenerate" without linking it to the same amino acid being coded.
- Not stating that a deletion alters the reading frame of all later codons.
- Calling every tumour cancerous, instead of separating benign from malignant.
- Making a claim of cause from risk-factor data that show correlation only.
- Dividing by the dividing cells only when calculating the mitotic index.
