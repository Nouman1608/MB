## Section A

**1.** Which of these legitimate aims appears in Article 8.2 but not in Article 10.2? A: national security. B: economic well-being of the country. C: prevention of disorder or crime. D: protection of health or morals. **[1]**

**2.** Which case held that "necessary in a democratic society" implies a pressing social need? A: Handyside v United Kingdom. B: Malone v United Kingdom. C: Norwood v United Kingdom. D: Hatton v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**3.** State the three requirements for a lawful restriction under Article 10.2. **[3]**

**4.** State the meaning of "prescribed by law" given in Sunday Times v United Kingdom (1979). **[2]**

**5.** Identify what the final sentence of Article 11.2 allows. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why the stop and search power considered in Gillan and Quinton v United Kingdom (2010) breached Article 8. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the four questions of proportionality set out in Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2) (2013). **[4]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by the margin of appreciation, and when it is likely to be wide or narrow. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how a court should approach a case in which Article 8 and Article 10 conflict, referring to Re S (A Child) (2004). **[4]**

**10.** Explain the role of Article 17 of the Convention, referring to Norwood v United Kingdom (2004). **[3]**

**11.** *A police force uses a facial recognition camera van at a football match. There is no statute or published code governing its use; officers decide on the day where to park it and which images to keep.* Suggest why the use of the van may breach Article 8. **[4]**

**12.** *The government introduces a regulation, made under an Act of Parliament, banning all public assemblies of more than 20 people in a city for one month after a series of violent clashes between rival groups. A peaceful charity fun-run of 300 people is cancelled.* Advise the charity whether the restriction is likely to be justified under Article 11.2. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** B: economic well-being of the country. **[1]**

**2.** A: Handyside v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**3.** The restriction must be **prescribed by law** [1], pursue a **legitimate aim** listed in Article 10.2 [1], and be **necessary in a democratic society** for that aim [1].

**4.** The law must be **adequately accessible** [1] and formulated with **sufficient precision** to enable the citizen to regulate their conduct, if need be with appropriate advice [1].

**5.** It allows **lawful restrictions** on the exercise of the Article 11 rights [1] by members of the **armed forces, the police or the administration of the state** [1].

**6.** Sections 44 to 47 of the Terrorism Act 2000 allowed stop and search **without reasonable suspicion** in authorised areas [1]. The Court held the power was **not in accordance with the law** [1] because it was **insufficiently circumscribed**, with authorisations and their renewal subject to weak checks [1] and **inadequate legal safeguards against abuse** by individual officers [1].

**7.** (1) Is the **objective sufficiently important** to justify limiting a fundamental right [1]? (2) Is the measure **rationally connected** to the objective [1]? (3) Could a **less intrusive measure** have been used without unacceptably compromising the objective [1]? (4) Does the measure strike a **fair balance** between the rights of the individual and the interests of the community [1]?

**8.** The margin of appreciation is the **latitude the European Court allows national authorities** in deciding whether a restriction is necessary [1], because they are often better placed to judge local conditions [1]. It is **wide** where there is no European consensus or the issue is moral, social or economic, such as Handyside (morals) or A, B and C v Ireland (abortion) [1]. It is **narrow** where an intimate aspect of private life or political speech is at stake, such as Dudgeon v UK [1]. It is an **international doctrine**; English courts instead give weight to Parliament's judgment on policy [1].

**9.** **Neither Article has precedence** over the other [1]. Where their values conflict there must be an **intense focus on the comparative importance of the specific rights** claimed in the individual case [1]. The **justifications for interfering with or restricting each right** must be taken into account [1], and the **proportionality test must be applied to each** [1].

**10.** Article 17 prevents anyone using the Convention to **destroy or excessively limit the rights of others** [1]. In **Norwood v UK** a man convicted for displaying a poster attacking Muslims [1] could not rely on Article 10, because the expression was aimed against the Convention's values; the application was **inadmissible** [1].

**11.** Capturing and storing facial images engages **private life** under Article 8.1 [1]. An interference must be **in accordance with the law** [1]. With **no statute or published code**, the use is neither accessible nor foreseeable (Sunday Times; Malone) [1], and leaving the decisions to officers on the day gives **no safeguards against arbitrary use**, as in Gillan and Quinton [1].

**12.** The ban interferes with **peaceful assembly** under Article 11.1; the fun-run is plainly peaceful [1]. **Prescribed by law**: a regulation made under an Act is likely to have a sufficient legal basis if published and clear [1]. **Legitimate aim**: prevention of disorder and public safety after the clashes [1]. **Pressing social need**: violent clashes may justify some measures [1]. **Rational connection**: limiting gatherings may reduce clashes, but a charity run has no link with the rival groups [1]. **Less intrusive means**: targeted conditions on the rival groups' events, or exemptions for clearly peaceful events, would achieve the aim (Bank Mellat question 3) [1]. **Fair balance**: a blanket city-wide ban on all assemblies over 20 for a month is severe [1]. Conclusion: the restriction is likely to be **disproportionate** and a breach of Article 11 [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing the three requirements but not applying each to the facts.
- Naming "public interest" instead of a specific legitimate aim from the Article.
- Missing less restrictive alternatives, which usually decide proportionality.
- Treating the margin of appreciation as a doctrine applied by English courts.
- Using this test for Article 5, which is a limited right.
