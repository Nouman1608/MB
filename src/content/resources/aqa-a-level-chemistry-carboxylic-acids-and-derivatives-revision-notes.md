## 3.3.9.1 Carboxylic acids and esters

### Key definitions

- **Carboxylic acid**: contains –COOH. Suffix -oic acid. The –COOH carbon is carbon 1.
- **Ester**: contains –COO– between two carbon chains. Named alkyl (from the alcohol) + -oate (from the acid).
- **Weak acid**: only partially dissociates in water. RCOOH ⇌ RCOO⁻ + H⁺.
- **Glycerol**: propane-1,2,3-triol, HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH.
- **Fats and oils**: esters of glycerol with three long-chain carboxylic acids.
- **Soap**: salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.
- **Biodiesel**: a mixture of methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids.

### Ester names at a glance

| Formula | Name | Made from |
|---|---|---|
| HCOOCH₂CH₃ | ethyl methanoate | methanoic acid + ethanol |
| CH₃COOCH₃ | methyl ethanoate | ethanoic acid + methanol |
| CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ | propyl propanoate | propanoic acid + propan-1-ol |
| CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₃ | methyl butanoate | butanoic acid + methanol |

**Method: naming an ester**

1. Find the C=O carbon.
2. The chain containing it is the acid part: count its carbons, add -oate.
3. The group on the other oxygen is the alcohol part: methyl, ethyl, propyl...
4. Write the alcohol part first, as a separate word.

### Reactions table

| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Equation (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Acid + carbonate | Na₂CO₃ or NaHCO₃ solution | 2HCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2HCOONa + CO₂ + H₂O |
| Esterification | Alcohol, acid catalyst (conc. H₂SO₄), warm | CH₃COOH + CH₃OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O |
| Acid hydrolysis of ester | Dilute acid, heat under reflux | CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + CH₃OH |
| Alkaline hydrolysis of ester | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | CH₃COOCH₃ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + CH₃OH |
| Soap making | Fat or oil + NaOH(aq), heat | Fat + 3NaOH → 3 soap + glycerol |
| Biodiesel | Vegetable oil + methanol, catalyst | Oil + 3CH₃OH → 3 methyl esters + glycerol |

Observation with a carbonate: **effervescence**; the gas (CO₂) turns limewater cloudy.

### Must-know distinctions

| | Acid hydrolysis | Alkaline hydrolysis |
|---|---|---|
| Extent | Reversible, incomplete | Goes to completion |
| Acid product | Carboxylic acid | Carboxylate salt |
| Why | Equilibrium | Carboxylate ion does not react with the alcohol |

| | Soap | Biodiesel |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent with the fat or oil | NaOH (alkali) | Methanol + catalyst |
| Main product | Sodium salts of long-chain acids | Methyl esters of long-chain acids |
| By-product | Glycerol | Glycerol |
| Ratio | 1 fat → 3 salt | 1 oil → 3 methyl ester |

### Uses of esters

Solvents, plasticisers, perfumes and food flavourings (the four the specification lists).

## 3.3.9.2 Acylation

### Structures

- **Acyl chloride**: R–COCl. Ethanoyl chloride, CH₃COCl.
- **Acid anhydride**: R–CO–O–CO–R. Ethanoic anhydride, (CH₃CO)₂O.
- **Amide**: R–CONH₂ (ethanamide, CH₃CONH₂) or R–CONHR′ after a primary amine
  (N-ethylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃).

### Product finder

| Nucleophile | Product | By-product with acyl chloride | By-product with anhydride |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂O | Carboxylic acid | HCl | Carboxylic acid |
| ROH | Ester | HCl | Carboxylic acid |
| NH₃ (2 mol) | Amide | NH₄Cl | Ammonium carboxylate |
| RNH₂ (2 mol) | N-substituted amide | RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ | Alkylammonium carboxylate |

Worked reminder: CH₃CH₂COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂NH₃Cl.
Product: N-ethylpropanamide.

### Method: nucleophilic addition–elimination (acyl chloride)

1. Lone pair on the nucleophile (O of water or alcohol; N of ammonia or amine) attacks the δ+ carbonyl C.
2. Curly arrow from the C=O bond to O. Intermediate: O⁻ on the old carbonyl O, + on the attacking O or N.
3. Curly arrow from O⁻ back to re-form C=O.
4. Curly arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl; Cl⁻ leaves.
5. H⁺ is lost from the attacking atom (to Cl⁻, or to a second NH₃ or amine molecule).

Name it in full: **nucleophilic addition–elimination**.

Worked reminder with ammonia (ethanoyl chloride):

```
NH3 lone pair → C(δ+);  C=O → O
Intermediate: CH3–C(O⁻)(Cl)–N⁺H3
O⁻ → C=O re-forms;  C–Cl → Cl⁻ leaves
second NH3 removes H⁺ from N⁺H3 → NH4⁺
Overall: CH3COCl + 2NH3 → CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl
```

Why the carbonyl carbon is attacked: O and Cl are both more electronegative than C, so the
carbonyl carbon carries a δ+ charge and attracts the lone pair of a nucleophile.

### Observations to recall

| Mixture | What you see |
|---|---|
| Ethanoyl chloride + water | Vigorous reaction; misty fumes of hydrogen chloride |
| Carboxylic acid + NaHCO₃(aq) | Effervescence; gas turns limewater cloudy |
| Ester formed in a test tube | Sweet, fruity smell |

### Aspirin: why ethanoic anhydride

- By-product is ethanoic acid, not corrosive, toxic hydrogen chloride gas.
- Reacts less vigorously, so it is easier to control.
- Hydrolysed less readily by water, so it is easier to store and handle.

Equation: HOC₆H₄COOH + (CH₃CO)₂O → CH₃COOC₆H₄COOH + CH₃COOH.

## Required practical 10 in steps

**Pure organic solid (for example aspirin)**

1. Filter the crude solid under reduced pressure.
2. Dissolve in the minimum volume of hot solvent.
3. Hot-filter if insoluble impurities are present.
4. Cool to crystallise; soluble impurities stay in solution.
5. Filter under reduced pressure, wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
6. Melting point: pure gives a sharp value matching the data value; impure melts lower over a range.

**Pure organic liquid (for example an ester)**

1. Reflux reactants with the catalyst; distil off the crude product.
2. Shake with NaHCO₃(aq) in a separating funnel to remove acid; release CO₂ pressure.
3. Run off and discard the aqueous layer.
4. Dry with an anhydrous salt; decant or filter.
5. Redistil and collect the fraction at the product's boiling point.

## Quick self-test

Use Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5.

1. Name CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₃.
2. Give the formula of the ester made from methanoic acid and propan-1-ol.
3. How many moles of CO₂ form when 0.10 mol of ethanoic acid reacts with excess sodium carbonate?
4. Why is a carboxylic acid described as weak?
5. State the products of heating methyl ethanoate under reflux with sodium hydroxide solution.
6. How many moles of NaOH are needed to hydrolyse 0.0200 mol of a triglyceride completely?
7. Give the reagent used with a vegetable oil to make biodiesel.
8. Write an equation for ethanoic anhydride with ethanol.
9. Name the product of ethanoyl chloride with methylamine.
10. What is the maximum mass of ethanamide from 0.050 mol of ethanoyl chloride with excess ammonia?
11. Calculate the percentage atom economy for CH₃COCl + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + HCl.
12. Why do you use the minimum volume of hot solvent in recrystallisation?

### Answers

1. Ethyl butanoate.
2. HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃ (propyl methanoate).
3. 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O, so **0.050 mol**.
4. It only partially dissociates in water.
5. Sodium ethanoate, CH₃COONa, and methanol.
6. **0.0600 mol** (three ester groups per triglyceride).
7. Methanol (with a catalyst).
8. (CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + CH₃COOH.
9. N-methylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₃.
10. 0.050 × 59.0 = **2.95 g**.
11. 88.0 ÷ (78.5 + 46.0) × 100 = **70.7%**.
12. So the solution is saturated when cool and as much product as possible crystallises out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the ester name in the wrong order (acid part first).
- Saying alkaline hydrolysis gives a carboxylic acid; it gives the carboxylate salt.
- Leaving out "acid catalyst" or "reversible" when describing esterification.
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio between a fat and its soap or methyl ester instead of 1 : 3.
- Forgetting the second NH₃ or amine molecule, so the equation gives free HCl.
- Arrows that start at an atom instead of a lone pair or bond in the mechanism.
- Missing the + and – charges on the tetrahedral intermediate.
- Saying ethanoic anhydride is preferred because of higher atom economy. For aspirin, the
  ethanoyl chloride route has the higher atom economy; the anhydride wins on safety and control.
- Describing melting point as "pure if it melts" rather than sharp and matching the data value.
- Not releasing pressure from the separating funnel when NaHCO₃ produces CO₂.
