Section **3.1.2.3 Social identities and social change** in OxfordAQA International AS/A level
Sociology (9690) is examined in Unit 2 Section A. The specification lists "Individual and
collective identities"; "The social construction of identities through socialisation"; identities
based on "age and generation/cohort; sex and gender; race and ethnicity; social class; religion;
ability/disability; nationality"; and "Social change and identities: gender identities including
masculinity and femininity; hybrid ethnic identities and diasporas; online identities (in social
media and virtual communities); greater consumption and globalisation as influences on
identities". Fanon, Goffman, Mead and Zuboff, all named for 3.1.2, are especially useful. The unit
overview is in the [Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

## What identity means

**Identity** is a person's sense of who they are and how others see them. An **individual
identity** is what makes a person distinctive (personality, biography, appearance); a **collective
identity** is a sense of belonging to a group (a nation, faith, class or generation). Identities
have three features that answers should use:

- **Socially constructed**: produced through socialisation and interaction rather than given by
  biology (3.1.2.2). Mead's account of the self developing through taking the role of others is the
  classic interactionist explanation.
- **Relational**: identities form in contrast with others; "us" requires a "them", which links to
  othering in 3.1.2.4.
- **Multiple and changing**: people hold several identities at once, and their importance shifts
  with context and over time.

## The seven bases of identity

| Basis | Key idea | Useful sociology |
|---|---|---|
| **Age and generation/cohort** | Age stages (childhood, youth, old age) are defined differently across societies; a cohort is people born in the same period who share formative events | Karl Mannheim's essay *The Problem of Generations* (1928): shared historical experiences in youth create generational consciousness |
| **Sex and gender** | Sex refers to biological differences; gender to socially constructed masculinity and femininity | Oakley (1972) drew the sex/gender distinction; Judith Butler (*Gender Trouble*, 1990) argued gender is **performative**, created through repeated acts |
| **Race and ethnicity** | "Race" is a socially constructed category often based on appearance; ethnicity refers to shared culture, history and sometimes language or religion | Frantz Fanon (*Black Skin, White Masks*, 1952) on colonial racism and the internalisation of the coloniser's gaze |
| **Social class** | Position in economic structure, shaping lifestyle, taste and outlook | Marx's distinction between a class "in itself" and "for itself" (with class consciousness); Mike Savage and colleagues (2013) argued class now combines economic, cultural and social capital |
| **Religion** | Beliefs, practices and community membership | Religion can be a primary identity, especially for minorities and diasporas |
| **Ability/disability** | Impairment is physical; disability may be created by social barriers | Mike Oliver's **social model of disability**; Goffman's *Stigma* (1963) on managing "spoiled" identities |
| **Nationality** | Belonging to a nation-state, often with shared symbols and stories | Benedict Anderson's **imagined communities** (1983); Michael Billig's **banal nationalism** (1995): flags, weather maps and sports coverage remind people daily of the nation |

A good exam habit is to say how each identity is **constructed** (through which agencies) and how it
can **change**.

## Social change and identities

### Gender identities: masculinity and femininity

Changes in work, education and law have widened the range of acceptable gender identities. Raewyn
Connell's concept of **hegemonic masculinity** describes the dominant, culturally honoured form of
masculinity (strength, heterosexuality, emotional control), alongside subordinate masculinities.
Máirtín Mac an Ghaill described a **crisis of masculinity** among young working-class men facing the
loss of manual jobs that once underpinned male breadwinner identities. Femininities have also
diversified as women's education and employment have expanded, although expectations about
appearance and care persist, and social media can intensify pressure over bodies.

### Hybrid ethnic identities and diasporas

A **hybrid identity** combines elements of more than one culture. A **diaspora** is a population
dispersed from a homeland that keeps a shared identity and ties to it. Stuart Hall argued that
cultural identity in diasporas is a matter of "becoming" as well as "being": identities are
continually reworked rather than fixed by origin. Second- and third-generation migrants may switch
between languages and norms at home, school and online, and create new cultural forms in music,
fashion and food. Robin Cohen's *Global Diasporas* (1997) classified diasporas, for example as
victim, labour, trade or imperial diasporas. (Robin Cohen is a different sociologist from Stanley
Cohen, who is named in the specification for his work on moral panics and denial.)

### Online identities

Social media and virtual communities allow people to create and manage identities. Goffman's
dramaturgical idea of **impression management** in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*
(1959) is often applied to profiles and posts: users perform a "front stage" self. Online
communities can give marginalised or isolated people collective identity and support. However,
**Shoshana Zuboff** (*The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019) argues platforms harvest behavioural
data to predict and shape users' behaviour for profit, so online identities are formed inside
systems designed by corporations. Algorithms that reward certain content can also narrow the
identities people present.

### Consumption and globalisation

Rising consumption allows people to express identity through brands, music, fashion and leisure.
Zygmunt Bauman argued that in consumer societies people increasingly define themselves through
what they buy, while Anthony Giddens described the **reflexive project of the self**: in late
modernity individuals continually construct and revise their self-identity. Globalisation spreads
images and products across borders, producing global youth cultures, but also **glocal** blends and
defensive reassertions of local, national or religious identities.

**Evaluation.** Postmodernists see identity as increasingly a matter of choice; structural
sociologists reply that class, gender, ethnicity and disability still constrain which identities
are available and how they are judged. Consumption-based identity depends on money, so poorer groups
have fewer choices.

## Worked example: annotated 20-mark plan

*Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that social class is no longer an
important source of identity.*

- **For:** decline of manual industry and trade unions weakens traditional working-class identity;
  consumption and lifestyle (Bauman) replace class; online and global identities cut across class;
  Giddens' reflexive self suggests choice.
- **Against:** Savage and colleagues show class reworked through economic, cultural and social
  capital; class still shapes education, health and life chances; consumption itself is
  class-based; class identity may be weaker even where class inequality is strong.
- **Application:** named society, for example growth of a new middle class in a rapidly
  industrialising economy.
- **Conclusion:** class may be less consciously claimed but remains powerful in shaping identities.

## Common mistakes

- Treating sex and gender as the same thing.
- Confusing **race** (a social category based on perceived physical difference) with **ethnicity**
  (shared culture).
- Mixing up Stanley Cohen and Robin Cohen.
- Describing online identity only positively, without Zuboff's critique.
- Writing that identities are "chosen" without discussing constraints.

## Exam technique

When explaining a basis of identity, cover **definition**, **agency of construction** and
**change**. In evaluation questions on change, set postmodern ideas of choice against structural
constraints and apply to a named society.
