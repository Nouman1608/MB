Section 2.4 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028), headed "Ion and ionic bonds", has five outcomes. You must describe the formation of positive ions (**cations**) and negative ions (**anions**); describe the giant lattice of an ionic compound "as a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions"; state that an ionic bond is "a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions"; describe ionic bond formation between metals and non-metals, "including the use of dot-and-cross diagrams"; and describe and explain two properties: high melting and boiling points, and electrical conductivity when molten or aqueous but not when solid.

The [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding guide](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) introduces ionic bonding with sodium chloride and magnesium oxide. This guide uses other compounds, including ones where the ions are not in a 1 : 1 ratio, which is where most dot-and-cross marks are lost.

## Forming ions

An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons, so it carries a charge.

- **Metal atoms lose** their outer-shell electrons, forming **positive ions (cations)**. The charge equals the number of electrons lost.
- **Non-metal atoms gain** electrons to fill their outer shell, forming **negative ions (anions)**. The charge equals the number of electrons gained.

In both cases the ion usually ends up with the electronic configuration of a noble gas.

| Group | Outer electrons | Change | Ion | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 1 | lose 1 | 1+ | Li → Li⁺ + e⁻ |
| II | 2 | lose 2 | 2+ | Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ |
| III | 3 | lose 3 | 3+ | Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ |
| V | 5 | gain 3 | 3− | N + 3e⁻ → N³⁻ |
| VI | 6 | gain 2 | 2− | S + 2e⁻ → S²⁻ |
| VII | 7 | gain 1 | 1− | F + e⁻ → F⁻ |

Non-metal anions are named with **-ide**: nitride, oxide, sulfide, fluoride, chloride, bromide. The metal keeps its name: calcium ion, aluminium ion.

**Worked example 1.** Describe the formation of a calcium ion from a calcium atom.

*Answer.* A calcium atom (2,8,8,2) loses its two outer-shell electrons. It forms a calcium ion, Ca²⁺, with configuration 2,8,8: a full outer shell, like argon. The ion has 20 protons but only 18 electrons, so its charge is 2+.

## The ionic bond and the giant lattice

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are **transferred** from metal atoms to non-metal atoms. The oppositely charged ions that form attract each other strongly.

**An ionic bond is a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.**

The ions do not pair off. They pack together in a **giant lattice**: a regular, three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions that extends in every direction. Each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge, and is attracted to all of them. In sodium chloride, for example, each Na⁺ ion has six Cl⁻ ions as its nearest neighbours, and each Cl⁻ ion has six Na⁺ ions.

Because the attractions act in all directions, there is no such thing as a "molecule" of an ionic compound. The formula, such as CaF₂, gives only the **ratio** of ions in the lattice.

## Dot-and-cross diagrams

A dot-and-cross diagram shows the outer-shell electrons of each atom, with one atom's electrons as dots and the other's as crosses. For ionic compounds you usually draw the **ions formed**, each in square brackets with its charge written outside the top right.

### Getting the ratio right

The total positive charge must balance the total negative charge, so:

1. Work out each ion's charge from the group number.
2. Find how many of each ion are needed for the charges to cancel.
3. Draw that number of each ion.

**Worked example 2.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for lithium oxide.

Lithium (2,1) loses one electron to form Li⁺. Oxygen (2,6) needs two electrons to form O²⁻. Two lithium atoms are needed for each oxygen atom: Li₂O.

The diagram shows three ions, each in its own square brackets:

| Ion drawn | Number drawn | Outer shell shown | Charge written |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Li]⁺ | 2 | empty: its one outer electron (a dot) has been transferred; the full first shell of 2 is not usually drawn | ⁺ |
| [O]²⁻ | 1 | 8 electrons: 6 crosses (oxygen's own) and 2 dots (one from each lithium) | ²⁻ |

In a written answer, say in words what the diagram shows: each lithium atom transfers **one** electron to the oxygen atom; the oxide ion has eight outer electrons, six of its own and two from lithium.

**Worked example 3.** Describe, as you would label a diagram, the bonding in calcium fluoride.

Calcium (2,8,8,2) loses two electrons to form Ca²⁺. Each fluorine (2,7) gains one electron to form F⁻. So **one** calcium atom gives **one** electron to **each of two** fluorine atoms: CaF₂. The diagram shows [Ca]²⁺ and two separate [F]⁻ ions. Each fluoride ion has seven crosses and one dot (8 electrons); the calcium ion is shown with no outer electrons, or with its full shell of 8 crosses if the next shell in is drawn.

### Diagram checklist

- Square brackets around each ion, with the charge outside.
- Only outer shells (unless the question asks for all shells).
- Correct number of each ion for the formula.
- Electrons transferred shown as the other symbol in the anion's shell.
- Total transferred electrons = total charge on the anions.

## Properties of ionic compounds

The syllabus asks you to **describe and explain** two properties.

### (a) High melting points and boiling points

**Describe:** ionic compounds are solids at room temperature with high melting points and boiling points.

**Explain:** melting requires the ions to be freed from their fixed positions in the lattice. There are **many strong electrostatic attractions** between oppositely charged ions throughout the **giant lattice**, so a **large amount of energy** is needed to overcome them.

Compounds whose ions have larger charges usually have higher melting points, because the attractions are stronger. Calcium oxide (Ca²⁺ and O²⁻) melts at a far higher temperature than potassium fluoride (K⁺ and F⁻).

### (b) Electrical conductivity

**Describe:** ionic compounds conduct electricity when **molten** or in **aqueous solution**, but not when **solid**.

**Explain:** to conduct, a substance needs **charged particles that are free to move**. In an ionic compound these are the **ions**.

- Solid: the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move; no conduction.
- Molten or dissolved: the lattice has broken down and the ions are free to move and carry charge; conduction.

This is why molten or aqueous ionic compounds can be electrolysed (Topic 4).

## Common mistakes

- Writing that ionic compounds conduct because "electrons move". In ionic compounds it is the **ions** that move.
- Calling the ionic bond a "shared pair" or a "transfer". The transfer forms the ions; the bond is the **attraction** between them.
- Drawing one F⁻ for CaF₂, or one Li⁺ for Li₂O.
- Forgetting the square brackets or the charges.
- Saying "ionic bonds are hard to break" without mentioning the giant lattice and the many attractions.
- Describing a lattice of "molecules" of sodium chloride.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** formation: name the atom, say how many electrons are lost or gained, give the ion's symbol and configuration.
- **State** the definition: learn "strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions".
- **Explain** properties: use the four words **giant, lattice, strong, many** for melting points, and **ions, free to move** for conductivity.
- **Draw**: plan the ratio first, then draw; a correct ratio is often a separate mark.
