The course is untiered. The section is short, but almost every later calculation depends on it: converting between mass and moles, and between moles and numbers of particles. ## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.6.3 a | The relative formula mass of a substance, in grams, is one mole of that substance; use Mᵣ to calculate moles from mass and mass from moles |
| 3.6.3 b | One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms or molecules: Avogadro's constant |

## What a mole is

A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, but enormous: **6.02 × 10²³** particles. Chemists use it because atoms are far too small to count, yet reactions happen atom by atom.

The useful link is with mass. One mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its Mᵣ (or Aᵣ for an element made of single atoms):

| Substance | Aᵣ or Mᵣ | Mass of one mole |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon, C | 12 | 12 g |
| Oxygen gas, O₂ | 32 | 32 g |
| Water, H₂O | 18 | 18 g |
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | 58.5 | 58.5 g |

Each of these masses contains the same number of formula units: 6.02 × 10²³.

## The two key equations

**moles = mass (g) ÷ Mᵣ**

**number of particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³**

Rearranged: mass = moles × Mᵣ, and moles = number of particles ÷ 6.02 × 10²³.

A route map helps:

mass (g) ⇄ (÷ or × Mᵣ) ⇄ **moles** ⇄ (× or ÷ 6.02 × 10²³) ⇄ number of particles

Always pass through moles. There is no direct step from mass to particles.

## Worked example 1: mass to moles to particles

*How many water molecules are in 9.0 g of water? How many atoms is that?* (Aᵣ: H = 1, O = 16)

1. Mᵣ H₂O = 2 + 16 = 18.
2. Moles = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.50 mol.
3. Molecules = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **3.01 × 10²³**.
4. Each molecule contains 3 atoms, so atoms = 3 × 3.01 × 10²³ = **9.03 × 10²³**.

Step 4 is where marks are lost: the question asked about atoms, and Avogadro's constant counted molecules.

## Worked example 2: moles to mass

*Calculate the mass of 0.150 mol of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃.* (Aᵣ: C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23)

- Mᵣ = 2 × 23 + 12 + 3 × 16 = 106.
- Mass = 0.150 × 106 = **15.9 g**.

## Atoms or molecules?

The specification says one mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ "atoms or molecules", so you must read which particle a question means.

| Sample | Moles of molecules | Moles of atoms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mol of He | 1 (helium exists as single atoms) | 1 |
| 1 mol of O₂ | 1 | 2 |
| 1 mol of CO₂ | 1 | 3 |
| 1 mol of CH₄ | 1 | 5 |

### Worked example 3: which sample has more atoms?

*Compare 4.0 g of helium with 16 g of oxygen gas.* (Aᵣ: He = 4, O = 16)

- Helium: 4.0 ÷ 4 = 1.0 mol of atoms.
- Oxygen: 16 ÷ 32 = 0.50 mol of O₂ molecules, which is 0.50 × 2 = 1.0 mol of atoms.

Both contain **the same number of atoms**, 6.02 × 10²³, even though the oxygen has four times the mass. The oxygen sample has fewer **molecules**.

## Worked example 4: the mass of one atom

*Calculate the mass of one carbon atom.* (Aᵣ: C = 12)

One mole, 12 g, contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms, so one atom has mass 12 ÷ (6.02 × 10²³) = **1.99 × 10⁻²³ g**.

This shows why counting individual atoms is impossible and moles are needed.

## Using moles to find the equation

If you know the masses that react, converting them to moles gives the ratio of coefficients. This combines 3.6.3 with 3.6.1.

### Worked example 5: iron and chlorine

5.6 g of iron reacts exactly with 10.65 g of chlorine, Cl₂, to form 16.25 g of iron(III) chloride, FeCl₃. Deduce the balanced equation. (Aᵣ: Fe = 56, Cl = 35.5)

| | Fe | Cl₂ | FeCl₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass / g | 5.6 | 10.65 | 16.25 |
| Mᵣ | 56 | 71 | 162.5 |
| Moles | 0.10 | 0.15 | 0.10 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 1.5 | 1 |
| × 2 | 2 | 3 | 2 |

**2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃**

Check with conservation of mass: 5.6 + 10.65 = 16.25 g.

## Worked example 6: particles to mass

*A sample of ammonia, NH₃, contains 9.03 × 10²² molecules. Calculate its mass.* (Aᵣ: N = 14, H = 1)

1. Moles = 9.03 × 10²² ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 0.150 mol.
2. Mᵣ NH₃ = 14 + 3 = 17.
3. Mass = 0.150 × 17 = **2.55 g**.

If the question had given the number of **atoms** instead, you would first divide by 4 (one N and three H in each molecule) to get the number of molecules.

## Why the mole matters in later sections

- **Reacting masses (3.6.1):** a balanced equation gives a ratio of moles, not of grams, so masses must be converted to moles first.
- **Concentrations (3.6.4):** concentration in mol/dm³ counts moles of solute in each cubic decimetre of solution.
- **Gas volumes (3.6.4 d):** one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure, because equal moles contain equal numbers of molecules.

## Standard form and significant figures

- Avogadro's constant is given to 3 significant figures, so give particle numbers to 3 significant figures.
- Write large numbers in standard form: 3.01 × 10²³, not 301000000000000000000000.
- On a calculator, enter 6.02 × 10²³ with the exponent key, not by typing "× 10 ^ 23" without brackets, which can give wrong answers in a longer calculation.
- Keep unrounded values in the calculator between steps; round only the final answer.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing Mᵣ by mass instead of mass by Mᵣ. Sense check: a small mass should give a small number of moles.
- Using Aᵣ of oxygen (16) for oxygen gas, which needs Mᵣ = 32.
- Forgetting to multiply by the atoms per molecule when atoms are asked for.
- Writing moles with the unit "g" or "g/mol".
- Thinking equal masses contain equal numbers of particles. Equal **moles** contain equal numbers of particles.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate the number of moles:** give the unit, mol, and show mass ÷ Mᵣ.
- **Calculate the number of atoms/molecules:** show the multiplication by 6.02 × 10²³ and give the answer in standard form.
- **Show that:** write each step fully, ending with the given value.
- **Deduce the equation:** set out a mass, Mᵣ, moles, ratio table as in worked example 5.
