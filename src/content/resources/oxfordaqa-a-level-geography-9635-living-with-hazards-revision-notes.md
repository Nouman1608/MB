## Hazard, risk and perception

A **hazard** is a natural event with the potential to cause damage; a **disaster** is when it
overwhelms a community's ability to cope.

```
Risk = hazard x vulnerability / capacity to cope
```

- **Vulnerability:** poverty, building quality, population density, age structure, warning systems.
- **Capacity to cope:** governance, wealth, infrastructure, preparedness.
- **Perception:** people accept risk fatalistically, adapt to it, or seek to dominate it through
  engineering.
- **Park model:** quality of life after a disaster through relief, rehabilitation and
  reconstruction; compare curves for different countries.
- **Hazard management cycle:** preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation.

**Forms of hazard** named in the specification: **geophysical** (earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunami),
**atmospheric** (tropical storms, and the weather conditions behind wildfires) and **hydrological**
(floods, storm surges).

### What shapes perception

Perception has **economic** and **cultural** determinants. Wealthier people can insure, move or
rebuild, so they may perceive a hazard as manageable; poorer people may have no alternative
location and accept the risk. Religious or cultural beliefs can lead to fatalism; past experience of
a hazard can raise awareness or, after a long quiet period, encourage complacency.

### Characteristic human responses

| Response | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Fatalism** | Accepting the hazard as uncontrollable or as fate; little action taken |
| **Prediction** | Forecasting when and where an event will occur, to give warnings |
| **Adjustment / adaptation** | Changing behaviour or buildings to live with the hazard |
| **Mitigation** | Reducing the severity of impacts, for example by building codes or defences |
| **Management** | Coordinated planning by governments and agencies across the whole cycle |
| **Risk-sharing** | Spreading the cost, for example through insurance or community funds |

The choice of response depends on the hazard's **incidence, intensity, magnitude, distribution** and
the community's **level of development**.

### Reading the Park model

The curve shows quality of life falling sharply at the event, then recovering through **relief**
(hours to days: search and rescue, medical care, shelter), **rehabilitation** (weeks to months:
temporary housing, restoring services) and **reconstruction** (months to years). A steep, deep fall
and slow recovery indicate high vulnerability; a quick return, possibly to a higher level than before
because of improved building, indicates strong capacity to cope.

## Tectonic hazards

| Margin | Process | Hazards |
|---|---|---|
| Constructive | Plates diverge, magma rises | Shield volcanoes, shallow earthquakes |
| Destructive | Oceanic plate subducts | Composite volcanoes, strong earthquakes, tsunami |
| Collision | Continental meets continental | Fold mountains, major earthquakes, no volcanoes |
| Conservative | Plates slide past | Major earthquakes, no volcanoes |

Plate movement is driven by mantle convection, ridge push and slab pull. Collision and conservative
margins make no magma, so have no volcanoes.

- **Ridge push (gravitational sliding):** new crust at an ocean ridge is hot and elevated, so plates
  slide away from it under gravity.
- **Slab pull:** the cold, dense edge of an oceanic plate sinks into the mantle and drags the rest of
  the plate behind it; it is now thought to be a major driving force.
- **Seafloor spreading** at ridges is shown by symmetrical bands of magnetic polarity either side of
  the ridge.
- **Magma plumes (hot spots):** columns of hot mantle rise beneath a plate; as the plate moves over a
  stationary plume, a chain of volcanoes forms, older with distance -- the **Hawaiian islands** are
  the classic example.
- **Landforms:** young fold mountains, rift valleys, ocean ridges, deep sea trenches and island arcs.

- **Magnitude** (moment magnitude scale) is a property of the event; **intensity** (Mercalli scale)
  is the effect at a place. One earthquake, one magnitude, many intensities.
- **Earthquake hazards:** ground shaking, liquefaction, landslides, tsunami.
- **Volcanic hazards:** lava flows, pyroclastic flows, lahars, ash falls, gases. Eruptions can often
  be predicted from precursors (seismicity, ground deformation, gas); earthquakes cannot be reliably
  predicted.

**Specification terms to use:** **nuées ardentes** (glowing clouds of hot gas and ash, a type of
pyroclastic flow), **tephra** (all fragments ejected), **mudflows (lahars)** formed when ash mixes
with rain, snowmelt or lake water -- the 1985 lahar from Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia buried the town
of Armero -- and gases that cause **acid rain**. For earthquakes, know **shockwaves**, **liquefaction**
(saturated sediment loses strength and behaves like a liquid) and **tsunamis** generated when the
seabed is suddenly displaced.

Describe distributions using **spatial distribution, randomness, magnitude, frequency, regularity
and predictability** -- the exact terms the specification uses.

## Storm hazards

Tropical storms need sea temperatures above about 27 degrees Celsius to a depth of 60 m, latitudes
5 to 30 degrees (Coriolis deflection), low wind shear and convergence in the lower atmosphere.
Forms of hazard: high winds, **storm surges** (usually the greatest killer), coastal flooding, river
flooding, landslides.

**How a storm develops:** warm, moist air rises, condensing and releasing **latent heat**, which
powers further uplift; air rushes in at the surface to replace it and is set spinning by the
Coriolis effect; a calm, cloud-free **eye** of sinking air forms at the centre, ringed by the
**eyewall** of the strongest winds. Storms weaken over land or cooler water because their supply of
heat and moisture is cut off. Intensity is commonly described on the **Saffir-Simpson scale**
(categories 1 to 5) by wind speed.

River flooding responses: **hard** (dams, levees, channelisation: effective but expensive, can shift
the problem downstream) and **soft** (floodplain zoning, afforestation, wetland restoration: cheaper,
more sustainable, slower), plus forecasting and warnings. Risk management is by preparedness,
mitigation, prevention and adaptation.

## Fires in nature

Intense wildfires depend on vegetation type, fuel characteristics, climate and recent weather, and
fire behaviour. Causes are natural (lightning) or human (accidental or deliberate). Impacts are
primary and secondary, and environmental, social, economic and political.

- **Fuel characteristics:** dry, fine fuels such as grass and leaf litter ignite easily; oily
  vegetation such as eucalyptus burns intensely; continuous fuel lets fire spread.
- **Climate and recent weather:** a long dry season or drought, high temperatures, low humidity and
  strong winds increase risk.
- **Fire behaviour:** ground fires smoulder in peat and roots; surface fires burn undergrowth; crown
  fires leap through tree tops and are hardest to control. Fires spread faster uphill and can throw
  embers ahead of the front.
- **Responses:** controlled (prescribed) burning to reduce fuel, firebreaks, building regulations,
  public warnings and evacuation plans, aerial fire-fighting.

## Required case studies

A recent volcanic event and a recent seismic event; two recent tropical storms in contrasting areas;
a recent wildfire event; a multi-hazardous environment; and a local-scale case study of a place in a
hazardous setting. Know impacts and human responses, with dates and figures you are sure of.

**Well-documented options to build from:**

- **Seismic -- Nepal (Gorkha), 25 April 2015:** magnitude 7.8 on the collision margin between the
  Indian and Eurasian plates; nearly 9 000 deaths; landslides and an avalanche at Everest base camp;
  historic buildings in Kathmandu destroyed; mountain villages cut off, so relief relied on
  helicopters; poor rural housing raised vulnerability.
- **Volcanic -- Eyjafjallajökull, Iceland, 2010:** an eruption beneath an ice cap on the constructive
  Mid-Atlantic margin; meltwater floods and a fine ash plume that closed much of European airspace
  for several days, disrupting travel and trade far beyond Iceland -- a strong example of
  political and economic impacts at a distance.
- **Contrasting tropical storms -- Hurricane Katrina (USA, 2005) and Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines,
  2013):** Katrina's storm surge broke the levees of New Orleans, flooding most of the city; Haiyan's
  storm surge devastated Tacloban. Compare vulnerability, warnings and recovery, not just wind speed.
- **Wildfire -- Australia's 2019-20 "Black Summer":** fires burned across south-eastern Australia
  after record heat and drought, destroying thousands of homes and killing very large numbers of
  animals; it led to a national Royal Commission on disaster arrangements.
- **Multi-hazardous environment:** for example the Philippines (typhoons, earthquakes, volcanoes
  such as Pinatubo, landslides) or Japan.

## Exam traps

- Using hazard and disaster as if they meant the same.
- Giving primary effects when secondary are asked for.
- Placing volcanoes at collision or conservative margins.
- Saying earthquakes can be predicted.
- Explaining a difference in impact by magnitude alone, not vulnerability and capacity to cope.
- Calling a hot-spot volcano a plate-margin volcano.
- Listing responses without judging how well they worked, for whom and over what timescale.

## Self-test

1. Write the risk equation.
2. Why do collision margins have no volcanoes?
3. Distinguish magnitude from intensity.
4. State two conditions needed for a tropical storm.
5. Why do two earthquakes of equal magnitude cause different impacts?
6. Distinguish ridge push from slab pull.
7. How does a hot spot create a chain of volcanoes?
8. What is a lahar?
9. Name the three stages of recovery shown on the Park model.
10. Why do tropical storms weaken over land?
11. Give two conditions that favour an intense wildfire.
12. What is risk-sharing? Give an example.

**Answers:** 1. Risk = hazard x vulnerability / capacity to cope. 2. No magma is generated there.
3. Magnitude is the size of the event; intensity is its effect at a specific place. 4. For example
sea temperature above about 27 degrees Celsius and low wind shear. 5. Vulnerability and capacity to
cope differ: building quality, warnings, emergency response and recovery funding. 6. Ridge push is
gravitational sliding away from an elevated ridge; slab pull is the sinking dense edge of an oceanic
plate dragging the plate. 7. A plate moves over a stationary plume, so new volcanoes form above it
while older ones are carried away. 8. A volcanic mudflow of ash mixed with water. 9. Relief,
rehabilitation and reconstruction. 10. Their supply of heat and moisture from warm sea is cut off,
and friction increases. 11. For example dry fuel after drought and strong winds. 12. Spreading the
cost of losses, for example through insurance.
