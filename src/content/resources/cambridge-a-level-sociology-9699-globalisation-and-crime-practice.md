This set covers section 8.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: reasons
for the emergence of global crimes, who benefits from them, and policing and prosecution. Section A
tests concepts and short explanations; Section B asks for developed explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *global crime*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between human trafficking and people smuggling. **[3]**

**3.** Identify the three elements of trafficking set out in the UN Trafficking Protocol (2000).
**[3]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *time–space compression* and how it relates to global crime. **[3]**

**5.** Give **one** example of a primary green crime and **one** example of a secondary green crime.
**[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** way in which deregulated global finance creates opportunities for crime.
**[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** feminist argument about who benefits from global crime. **[3]**

**8.** Identify **two** ways in which new technology helps police global crime. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** reasons for the emergence of corporate crime on a global scale. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** challenges in prosecuting crimes against the environment. **[8]**

**11.** "Global crime mainly benefits the ruling class." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Globalisation and new technology have made global crime easier to police." Evaluate this
view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** Crime whose organisation, victims or harm cross national borders [1], for example human
trafficking, corporate crime or pollution that affects several countries [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Smuggling is helping someone cross a border illegally, usually for a fee [1], and the
relationship normally ends on arrival [1]; trafficking involves moving or holding a person for
exploitation, which may continue after arrival and need not involve crossing a border [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The act (recruitment, transport, transfer, harbouring or receipt of people) [1]; the means
(force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or vulnerability) [1]; the purpose of
exploitation, such as forced labour or sexual exploitation [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Time–space compression is the shrinking of effective distance through faster transport and
communication (Harvey, 1989) [1]. It allows criminal networks to move goods, people and money
across borders quickly [1], for example coordinating drug shipments or fraud from another
continent in real time [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Primary, any one: air or water pollution; illegal logging or deforestation; killing of
protected species [1]. Secondary, any one: illegal dumping of hazardous waste in breach of
regulations; state violence against environmental campaigners, such as the sinking of the
*Rainbow Warrior* (1985) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Identify [1], explain [1], example [1]: offshore financial centres and shell companies allow
ownership to be hidden [1], so criminal profits can be laundered or tax evaded without detection
[1]; the Panama Papers leak (2016) showed how offshore companies were used to conceal wealth [1].
**[3]**

**7.** Identify [1], explain [1], develop [1]: women and girls are disproportionately the victims of
trafficking for sexual exploitation and domestic servitude [1], so global crime benefits men who
buy sexual services and the networks that profit from them [1]; Sassen's feminisation of survival
explains why poverty pushes women into risky migration where this exploitation occurs [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Any two: digital forensics [1]; international databases and data sharing [1]; tracking of
financial transactions [1]; surveillance of online communication [1]; rapid circulation of
Interpol notices [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a concept or study [1],
example [1].
- **Regulatory arbitrage** [1]: TNCs can locate production where regulation is weakest [1], so
  unsafe practices that would be illegal at home are cheaper abroad [1]; Pearce (1976) argued such
  crimes of the powerful are rarely treated as "real crime" [1].
- **Global markets and competition** [1]: pressure to cut costs and beat competitors worldwide [1]
  encourages firms to cheat regulations [1]; the Volkswagen emissions scandal (2015) used software
  to pass tests in many countries [1].
- Also creditable: offshore finance enabling tax evasion; weak or corrupt states; complex supply
  chains that hide responsibility. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per challenge: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example [1].
- **Jurisdiction** [1]: the harm may occur in one country while the decision was made in another
  [1], so it is unclear which courts can act and extradition may be refused [1]; the *Probo Koala*
  waste was generated through international trade but dumped in Abidjan (2006) [1].
- **Definitions and law** [1]: some environmental harms are legal or lightly regulated in certain
  states [1]; green criminologists therefore study harm rather than only illegal acts [1], which
  shows how law reflects the interests of those who make it [1].
- Also creditable: power and resources of corporations; lack of enforcement capacity; difficulty
  proving cause and effect for pollution. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level uses Marxist and feminist perspectives explicitly to
answer "who benefits", applies named examples, and reaches a supported judgement. Indicative
content:
- **For the view (Marxist):** corporate and financial crime brings profit to capitalist
  enterprises and elites; Pearce (1976) on crimes of the powerful; offshore finance and the Panama
  Papers; corporate harm is often punished by fines; laws reflect ruling-class interests; Taylor
  (1997) links neo-liberal globalisation to crime among elites.
- **Against the view:** many global crimes are organised by networks outside any ruling class;
  poor people also gain small incomes as couriers or smugglers, though at great risk; some victims
  are corporations and wealthy individuals (cyber fraud).
- **Feminist challenge:** the main beneficiaries of trafficking may be men who buy sexual services
  and criminal networks; victims are disproportionately women and girls; power is gendered as well
  as class-based. Agustín (2007) adds that women's agency should not be ignored.
- **Evaluation:** depends on crime type; ruling-class benefit is clearest for corporate and
  environmental crime; trafficking needs a gendered analysis.
- **Judgement:** for example, the powerful gain most and are policed least, but "ruling class" is
  too narrow a description of who profits from all global crime. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs benefits against challenges, uses specific bodies
and agreements, and judges by crime type. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** Interpol notices and Europol coordination; the International Criminal Court
  (working from 2002); the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001); the Financial Action Task
  Force's anti-money laundering standards; digital forensics and financial tracking; leaks such
  as the Panama Papers exposing hidden wealth.
- **Against the view:** encryption, anonymising networks and cryptocurrencies help offenders;
  jurisdiction and sovereignty limit cooperation; extradition can be slow or refused; corruption
  and lack of resources in some states; powerful corporations relocate, lobby and settle.
- **Theory:** Marxists argue enforcement targets the powerless; feminists argue trafficked women
  are often deported rather than protected; links to 7.3 on the limits of the nation state.
- **Evaluation:** capacity to police has grown, but so has the scale and complexity of crime;
  success is greater for some crimes (cross-border fraud with clear victims) than others
  (corporate environmental harm).
- **Judgement:** for example, globalisation has made policing *possible* across borders but not
  necessarily *easier*, because the same technologies serve offenders. **[35]**
