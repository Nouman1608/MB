## Section A

**1.** Define the term lag time. **[1]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by residual risk behind a flood defence. **[2]**

**3.** A river has 59 years of annual maximum flow records. Calculate the recurrence interval of the second-largest flood. **[2]**

**4.** State the annual probability of a flood with a recurrence interval of 25 years, as a percentage. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one reason why the lead time of a flood warning is often short in a small urban catchment. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows two storms on the same basin. Each delivered 40 mm of rain with peak rainfall at 14:00.

| | Storm A | Storm B |
|---|---|---|
| Baseflow (cumecs) | 3 | 3 |
| Peak discharge (cumecs) | 11 | 27 |
| Time of peak discharge | 21:00 | 17:00 |

**(a)** Calculate the lag time for each storm. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the rise from baseflow to peak for each storm. **[1]**

**(c)** Storm B occurred after a week of heavy rain. Explain how this accounts for the differences shown. **[3]**

**7.** A town's new houses lie inside the 50-year flood outline.

**(a)** Calculate the probability that at least one flood of this size occurs during a 25-year period. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons why the true probability may be higher than your answer. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a storage reservoir upstream of a city can reduce flood risk, and one way in which it can fail to do so. **[4]**

## Section C

**9.** Examine the view that building levees increases flood risk in the long term. **[10]**

**10.** With reference to examples, evaluate the effectiveness of relocating communities away from floodplains as a flood mitigation strategy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The risk of flooding that remains after a defence is built [1], because a flood larger than the design standard can overtop it, or the defence can breach or fail through poor maintenance [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Recurrence interval = (n + 1) / m = (59 + 1) / 2 [1] = **30 years** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 1 / 25 = 0.04, so **4 per cent** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Impermeable surfaces and drains send rain to the channel within minutes to an hour or two [1], so there is little time between the rain falling and the river peaking, and no long upstream reach where a flood wave can be detected in advance [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Storm A: 21:00 - 14:00 = **7 hours** [1]. Storm B: 17:00 - 14:00 = **3 hours** [1].

**(b)** Storm A: 11 - 3 = 8 cumecs; Storm B: 27 - 3 = 24 cumecs [1].

**(c)** After a week of heavy rain the soil was saturated [1], so little of Storm B's rain could infiltrate and it moved as saturation-excess overland flow, which is fast [1]. More water therefore reached the channel sooner, giving a shorter lag time (3 hours rather than 7) and a rise three times larger (24 cumecs rather than 8) [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Annual probability p = 1 / 50 = 0.02 [1]. Probability of no flood in 25 years = 0.98 to the power 25 = 0.60 [1]. Probability of at least one flood = 1 - 0.60 = **0.40, about 40 per cent** [1].

**(b)** Any two [1] each: the record may be too short to include the largest floods; urban growth or deforestation upstream may have made the basin flashier since the record was made; climate change may be increasing heavy rainfall; the calculation assumes each year is independent and conditions are unchanged. **[5]**

**8.** The reservoir stores storm runoff and releases it slowly [1], lowering and delaying the flood peak downstream at the city [1]. It can fail if prolonged rain fills its storage before the peak arrives [1], so operators must release large volumes to protect the dam while the city is already flooding (as examined after the 2011 Brisbane floods) [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content:

- **For the view:** levees confine water to the channel, so floodplain storage is lost and water levels downstream rise [1]; faster flow passes the flood peak to unprotected places [1]; the levee effect encourages building behind defences, increasing exposure [1]; when a flood exceeds the design standard, overtopping or breaching causes deep, sudden flooding that can be worse than undefended flooding [1]; defences need maintenance, and ageing levees become more likely to fail [1].
- **Against the view:** levees give reliable protection against frequent floods up to their design standard, protecting existing towns where relocation is impossible [1]; risk can be managed if levees are combined with zoning, warnings and spillways that release water onto chosen land [1]; set-back levees, as in the Dutch Room for the River programme, give space to the river while still protecting towns [1].
- **Examination:** the outcome depends on whether planning restricts building behind the levee and whether the whole basin is managed, not on the levee alone [1].
- **Conclusion:** a supported judgement, for example that levees used alone tend to raise long-term risk by increasing exposure and residual risk, but used within integrated catchment management they can reduce it [1]. **[10]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Definition and how it works:** relocation removes people and property from the hazard zone, reducing exposure rather than changing the flood [1].
- **Example:** after the 1993 upper Mississippi flood, Valmeyer, Illinois, was rebuilt on higher ground, and federal buy-outs removed many floodplain properties [1].
- **Strengths:** permanent reduction of damage for those moved [1]; returns floodplain to natural storage, benefiting places downstream [1]; avoids the recurring costs of maintaining and raising defences [1].
- **Limitations:** high upfront cost of buying homes and building new infrastructure [1]; loss of community ties, heritage and livelihoods tied to the river, so residents may resist [1]; impractical for large cities or places where high ground is scarce [1].
- **Equity:** voluntary schemes may leave poorer residents behind, or move them to less convenient sites [1].
- **Conclusion:** highly effective for small, repeatedly flooded settlements with nearby safe land; limited for dense cities, where it works only alongside defences and zoning [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* In Section C, marks reward evaluation that depends on place and scale. A judgement such as "effective for small towns, not for megacities" is stronger than "it is good and bad".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring lag time from the start of rain instead of peak rainfall.
- Treating a 50-year flood as one that happens once in 50 years.
- Forgetting to subtract baseflow when asked for the rise in discharge.
- Evaluating structures only, with no mention of planning, warnings or relocation.
