These notes cover section 10.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: how
groups are represented, the media and gender socialisation, moral panics, and the media and
popular culture. Full explanations are in the
[media representations study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-media-representations/);
exam-style questions are in the
[media representations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-media-representations-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

Media representations are selective constructions. Groups with less power — the working class,
women, ethnic minorities, the young and the old — are more often under-represented, stereotyped or
othered, while dominant groups are normalised. Representations contribute to gender socialisation
by providing scripts for masculinity and femininity, and moral panics turn groups into folk devils.
Critics stress active audiences, diversifying images and the power of folk devils to answer back.
Popular culture is both a product of the culture industry and a resource audiences use creatively.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Representation** | Media construction of images of groups, places and events |
| **Stereotype** | Simplified, generalised image applied to a whole group |
| **Under-representation** | Appearing less often than a group's share of the population |
| **Misrepresentation** | Appearing in distorted or narrow roles |
| **Othering** | Presenting a group as fundamentally different, often as a problem |
| **Hegemonic masculinity** | Dominant, idealised form of masculinity (Connell, 1987) |
| **Inferential racism** | Racist assumptions built into apparently neutral reporting (Hall, 1981) |
| **Social abjection** | Making groups objects of public disgust (Tyler, 2013) |
| **Performativity** | Gender as something repeatedly done (Butler, 1990) |
| **Folk devil** | Group blamed in a moral panic |
| **Culture industry** | Standardised production of entertainment (Adorno and Horkheimer, 1944) |
| **Cultural capital** | Tastes and knowledge that signal class status (Bourdieu, 1979) |

## Representations by group

| Group | Pattern | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Class** | Middle class normalised; working class comic, criminal or dependent | *Benefits Street* (2014) criticised as "poverty porn"; Tyler (2013) |
| **Gender** | Hegemonic masculinity; women sexualised, judged on appearance; older women invisible | Connell (1987); Bechdel test (1985) |
| **Ethnicity** | Overt and inferential racism; minorities linked to conflict and threat | Hall (1981); Poole, *Reporting Islam* (2002) |
| **Age** | Youth as risk; older people frail or absent; children as innocent | News coverage of youth crime; advertising's "active pensioner" exception |

## Media and gender socialisation

- Role models and repeated images teach gender norms.
- Advertising: Kilbourne, *Killing Us Softly* (from 1979).
- Butler (1990): media supply scripts for performing gender.
- Active audiences negotiate and resist; online media spread alternative images.
- Evaluation: choice exists, but within a narrow range of profitable images.

## Moral panics: Goode and Ben-Yehuda (1994)

1. **Concern** — heightened worry.
2. **Hostility** — group seen as enemy.
3. **Consensus** — wide agreement the threat is real.
4. **Disproportionality** — reaction exceeds harm.
5. **Volatility** — sudden rise and fall.

**Examples by group**
- Class: "welfare scroungers", "chavs".
- Gender: lone mothers; "ladettes".
- Ethnicity: "mugging" (Hall et al., 1978); asylum seekers.
- Age: "video nasties" and the Video Recordings Act 1984; online gaming and social media.

**Critique:** McRobbie and Thornton (1995) — folk devils can answer back in a multi-mediated world;
some subcultures welcome hostile coverage.

## Media and popular culture

- **Mass culture:** Adorno and Horkheimer — culture industry keeps audiences passive.
- **Class and taste:** Bourdieu — high/popular distinction is a form of power.
- **Active use:** Fiske (1989) — audiences make resistant meanings.
- **Global flows:** South Korean pop music shows non-Western popular culture spreading globally.

## Essay plan in five steps

*"Moral panics are an effective way of controlling deviant groups." Evaluate.*

1. Define moral panic and folk devil; apply the five criteria to one case.
2. For: Cohen's amplification of control; Hall et al. (1978) — panic justified tougher policing
   during a crisis of hegemony; Video Recordings Act 1984 as a legal outcome.
3. Against: panics can amplify deviance instead of reducing it.
4. Today: McRobbie and Thornton (1995) — folk devils answer back; fragmented audiences weaken
   consensus.
5. Judge: panics can justify control, but their effect is less predictable in multi-mediated
   societies.

## Exam traps

- Use analytical terms (under-representation, othering, normalisation), not just lists of
  stereotypes.
- Gender includes masculinity.
- Apply the five criteria before calling something a moral panic.
- Representations change; evaluate how far.
- The popular culture bullet asks about the *relationship* with the media, not just a definition.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish under-representation from misrepresentation.
2. What is othering?
3. What did Connell mean by hegemonic masculinity?
4. What does the Bechdel test measure?
5. Distinguish overt from inferential racism (Hall, 1981).
6. What did Tyler (2013) mean by social abjection?
7. What did Butler argue about gender?
8. List Goode and Ben-Yehuda's five criteria of a moral panic.
9. Give one example of a moral panic about age.
10. What did McRobbie and Thornton (1995) argue?
11. What is the culture industry?
12. How does Bourdieu link popular culture to class?

### Answers

1. Under-representation: a group appears less often than its population share. Misrepresentation:
   it appears, but in distorted or narrow roles.
2. Presenting a group as fundamentally different from "us", often as a problem or threat.
3. The dominant, culturally idealised form of masculinity, associated with strength, control and
   heterosexuality.
4. Whether a film has two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man.
5. Overt racism openly expresses racist views; inferential racism builds racist assumptions into
   apparently neutral reporting.
6. Making groups such as the poor or migrants into objects of public disgust, which justifies
   harsher treatment.
7. That gender is performed through repeated acts rather than being a fixed identity.
8. Concern, hostility, consensus, disproportionality, volatility.
9. Any one: "video nasties" in the 1980s UK; online gaming; social media and children; youth
   subcultures.
10. That in a multi-mediated world folk devils can answer back, so the classic model is too simple.
11. Adorno and Horkheimer's term for the standardised production of entertainment that keeps
    audiences passive.
12. Tastes signal class status; preferring "high" culture is a form of cultural capital, so the
    high/popular distinction expresses class power.
