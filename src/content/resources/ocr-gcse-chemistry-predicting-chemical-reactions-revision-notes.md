## The one idea behind C4.1

Same group → same number of outer electrons → similar reactions. Down a group → one extra shell each time → outer electrons sit further out, behind more inner shells.

- **Losing** electrons (metals) gets **easier** down a group → reactivity rises.
- **Gaining** electrons (non-metals) gets **harder** down a group → reactivity falls.
- Full outer shell (Group 0) → no tendency to react.

## Group 1 summary (C4.1a, C4.1b)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Outer electrons | 1; forms a 1+ ion |
| Physical | soft, low density, low melting point; melting point **falls** down the group |
| Storage | under oil (reacts with air and water vapour) |
| + water | metal hydroxide + hydrogen; alkaline solution |
| + oxygen | metal oxide, e.g. 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O |
| + chlorine | white metal chloride, e.g. 2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl |
| Reactivity | **increases** down the group |

Water observations: lithium fizzes steadily; sodium melts into a ball and whizzes around; potassium gives a lilac flame.

**Why reactivity rises:** outer electron further from the nucleus + more shielding → weaker attraction → electron lost more easily.

## Group 7 summary (C4.1a, C4.1b)

| Halogen | Room temperature | In water |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | pale green gas | very pale green |
| Bromine, Br₂ | red-brown liquid | orange |
| Iodine, I₂ | grey-black solid | brown |

- Diatomic molecules. Melting and boiling points **rise** down the group (bigger molecules, stronger forces between them).
- React with metals to give halide salts; with hydrogen to give hydrogen halides (acidic in water).
- **Reactivity decreases** down the group: an extra electron is attracted less strongly by a nucleus that is further away and more shielded.

**Displacement (PAG C1):** a halogen pushes a lower halogen out of its halide salt solution.

Example: Cl₂ + 2NaBr → 2NaCl + Br₂ (colourless solution turns orange).

| Added → to solution ↓ | chlorine | bromine | iodine |
|---|---|---|---|
| chloride | – | no change | no change |
| bromide | orange | – | no change |
| iodide | brown | brown | – |

## Group 0 summary (C4.1a, C4.1b)

- Colourless, monatomic gases; very unreactive.
- Full outer shell: 2 electrons (helium) or 8 (the rest).
- Boiling point and density **increase** down the group.

## Transition metals (C4.1c)

Typical examples: iron, copper, nickel, manganese, chromium.

| Property | Example |
|---|---|
| High melting point | iron 1538 °C |
| High density | copper 8.96 g/cm³ |
| Low reactivity compared with Group 1 | copper does not react with water or dilute acid |
| Ions with different charges | Fe²⁺ / Fe³⁺; Cu⁺ / Cu²⁺ |
| Coloured compounds | Cu²⁺ blue; Fe²⁺ pale green; Fe³⁺ orange-brown |
| Catalysts | iron (Haber process); nickel (adding hydrogen to alkenes); manganese(IV) oxide (decomposing hydrogen peroxide) |

## Predicting from position (C4.1d)

| Position | Likely behaviour |
|---|---|
| Group 1 or 2, left side | metal; forms positive ions; basic oxide; may react with water |
| Central block | transition metal; coloured compounds; variable charge |
| Groups 6 and 7, right side | non-metal; forms negative ions with metals; acidic oxide (Group 6) |
| Group 0 | no reaction expected |

A reaction is likely when a metal meets a non-metal, or when a more reactive element meets a compound of a less reactive one in the same group or series.

## Reactivity of metals (C4.1e, C4.1f)

A useful order, most reactive first: potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver.

- metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
- metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen, e.g. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
- The more easily the metal forms its positive ion (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻), the faster the reaction.

**Method in steps: deducing an order from results**

1. For a displacement grid, count the reactions for each metal. Most reactions = most reactive.
2. For temperature data, subtract the start temperature from each final value. Biggest rise = most reactive (same mass of metal, same volume and concentration of solution).
3. For gas collection, divide volume by time to get a mean rate in cm³/s. Fastest = most reactive.
4. A metal that does not react with a salt solution is less reactive than the metal in the salt.
5. Check that your order is consistent with every result, not just one.

Worked reminder: sodium in water gives 45 cm³ of hydrogen in 15 s. Mean rate = 45 ÷ 15 = **3.0 cm³/s**.

## Practical links

| PAG | C4.1 activity | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | halogen displacement; metals with water and acid; metal displacement | small amounts, careful mixing, record colour changes |
| C5 | investigating transition metal compounds | identify coloured ions; compare with Group 1 compounds |
| C7 | metal and salt reactions | the displacement product is a new salt solution plus a metal |
| C8 | gas volume or temperature change | same mass and surface area of metal; same volume and concentration of solution; repeat and average |

Safety: Group 1 metals are handled with tongs, in small pieces, behind a safety screen. Halogens are toxic, so use dilute solutions in a well-ventilated room.

## Empirical formulae and balancing (CM4.1i)

**Method in steps**

1. Mass of the added element = product mass − metal mass.
2. Moles of each element = mass ÷ Ar.
3. Divide both by the smaller number to get the whole-number ratio.

Worked reminder: 1.10 g of manganese gives 1.74 g of an oxide. Oxygen = 0.64 g. Mn: 1.10 ÷ 55 = 0.020 mol; O: 0.64 ÷ 16 = 0.040 mol. Ratio 1 : 2, so **MnO₂** (manganese(IV) oxide).

Balancing with ratio: 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O. 0.35 g of lithium is 0.050 mol, which makes 0.025 mol of Li₂O (Mr 30), a mass of **0.75 g**. The oxide is heavier than the lithium because oxygen has been added.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Group 1 vs Group 7 trend:** reactivity rises down Group 1 but falls down Group 7.
- **Chlorine vs chloride:** chlorine is the element (Cl₂); chloride is the ion (Cl⁻) in a salt.
- **Group 1 vs transition metals:** low vs high density and melting point; one ion vs ions with different charges; white vs coloured compounds.
- **Displacement vs no reaction:** colour change or temperature rise means a reaction; no change means the added element is less reactive.

## Quick self-test

1. State the number of outer electrons in a Group 7 atom.
2. Name the gas made when lithium reacts with water.
3. Write a balanced equation for sodium reacting with chlorine.
4. Explain why argon is unreactive.
5. Predict the state of iodine at room temperature and give its colour.
6. Will iodine solution react with potassium bromide solution? Explain.
7. Give two properties that make iron different from potassium.
8. Name the transition metal catalyst used in the Haber process.
9. 1.17 g of potassium reacts with bromine to give 3.57 g of product. Find the empirical formula. (Ar: K = 39, Br = 80)
10. Metal Q displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate but not iron from iron(II) sulfate. Place Q in the series iron, copper.
11. Why does potassium react faster with water than lithium?
12. Copper(II) chloride solution is blue. What property of transition metals does this show?

### Answers

1. Seven.
2. Hydrogen.
3. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
4. Its atoms have a full outer shell (stable), so they do not lose, gain or share electrons.
5. Solid; grey-black.
6. No reaction: bromine is the more reactive halogen, so iodine cannot take its place.
7. Any two: higher melting point; higher density; much less reactive; forms ions with different charges (Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺); coloured compounds.
8. Iron.
9. Br = 3.57 − 1.17 = 2.40 g. K: 1.17 ÷ 39 = 0.030 mol; Br: 2.40 ÷ 80 = 0.030 mol. Ratio 1 : 1, so **KBr**.
10. Between them: iron > Q > copper.
11. Potassium has two more occupied shells than lithium, so its outer electron sits further out, is shielded more and is lost more readily.
12. Transition metal compounds are often coloured.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying halogens get more reactive down the group because they "have more shells".
- Explaining reactivity with "wants a full shell" instead of attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron.
- Writing Na₂ or Cl in equations; sodium is Na, chlorine is Cl₂.
- Describing potassium's flame as "orange" or "yellow"; it is lilac.
- Calling bromine a gas or a solid at room temperature.
- Forgetting that the added metal must be more reactive for displacement to occur.
- Using a final temperature instead of the temperature change to rank metals.
- Dividing masses rather than moles when finding an empirical formula.
- Saying a metal "loses mass" when it forms an oxide or chloride.
