Subtopic 2.5 Zonation, succession and change in ecosystems closes Topic 2 Ecology in IB Diploma
Programme Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The IB's public subject
brief names Topic 2 but not its subtopics, so this title follows the course's provisional checklist;
confirm it in your school's copy of the ESS guide.

The [climate, biomes and succession study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-climate-biomes-succession/)
distinguishes zonation from succession, compares primary and secondary succession, tabulates what
changes between pioneer and climax stages, works a P:R ratio example and explains plagioclimax. This
guide goes further: the main kinds of zonation and how to sample them, how to read a kite diagram,
the stages of two named seres, the mechanisms that drive succession, and how disturbance and human
activity redirect change.

## What 2.5 asks you to do

- Explain zonation along environmental gradients and how to measure it.
- Describe primary and secondary succession, with named examples of seres.
- Explain the changes in soil, community, productivity and diversity during succession.
- Explain how disturbance, human activity and climate change alter ecosystems over time.

## Kinds of zonation

| Gradient | Main abiotic changes | Example of zones |
|---|---|---|
| Altitude on a mountain | Temperature falls, wind and exposure rise, growing season shortens | Forest → shrubs → alpine meadow → bare rock and snow |
| Rocky shore | Time exposed to air rises up the shore: drying, temperature swings, wave action | Lower-shore kelps → mid-shore wracks → upper-shore lichens |
| Distance from the sea on dunes | Salt spray and wind fall, soil develops inland | Embryo dunes → yellow dunes → grey dunes → heath or woodland |
| Lake edge | Water depth and waterlogging fall towards the shore | Open water plants → reeds → wet woodland |

Zones form because each species' range of tolerance covers only part of the gradient, and
competition then decides which species dominates where conditions overlap. On rocky shores, the
upper limit of a species is usually set by physical stress (drying), and the lower limit often by
competition or predation.

## Measuring zonation

- **Line transect:** a tape laid along the gradient; record species touching it at regular
  intervals.
- **Belt transect:** quadrats placed continuously along the tape; slower but more complete.
- **Interrupted belt transect:** quadrats at regular intervals; a compromise for long gradients.
- Measure abiotic factors at each sampling point (soil pH, moisture, organic matter, wind speed,
  height above low tide) so that changes in the community can be linked to them.

### Kite diagrams

A kite diagram shows the abundance of each species along the transect as a band whose width is
proportional to abundance, drawn symmetrically about a central line. Read it by asking where each
band starts, peaks and ends, and which abiotic change coincides.

### Worked example 1: dune transect (fictional data)

| Distance inland (m) | 0 | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 | 250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marram grass cover (%) | 10 | 60 | 45 | 15 | 5 | 0 |
| Heather cover (%) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 10 | 40 | 70 |
| Soil pH | 8.6 | 8.2 | 7.4 | 6.1 | 5.0 | 4.4 |
| Soil organic matter (%) | 0.3 | 0.8 | 2.0 | 4.5 | 7.5 | 11.0 |

```
Change in pH, 0 m → 250 m = 4.4 − 8.6 = −4.2 units
Organic matter at 250 m vs 0 m = 11.0 / 0.3 ≈ 37 times higher
Marram peaks at 50 m; heather increases steadily from 150 m inland
```

Interpretation: shell fragments make young dune sand alkaline; inland, older soils lose carbonate by
leaching and gain organic acids from decaying litter, so pH falls while organic matter rises. Marram
thrives where sand is still being deposited; heather tolerates acidic soils and dominates where they
have developed. Because inland dunes are older, the zonation in space records succession in time.

## Two seres in detail

### Psammosere (sand dune succession)

1. **Embryo dunes:** salt-tolerant pioneers such as sea couch grass trap blown sand.
2. **Yellow dunes:** marram grass dominates; it grows up through burial and binds the sand with long
   roots and rhizomes.
3. **Grey dunes:** decaying organic matter darkens the soil; mosses, lichens and many flowering plants
   join; marram declines as sand supply falls.
4. **Dune slacks:** wet hollows where the water table reaches the surface, with their own community.
5. **Dune heath or woodland:** acidic soils support heather, then shrubs and trees towards climax.

### Hydrosere (succession in fresh water)

1. **Open water** with submerged and floating plants.
2. Sediment and dead plant matter build up, making the water shallower.
3. **Reed swamp:** reeds and rushes colonise the margins and trap more sediment.
4. **Fen** or marsh: the surface is only seasonally flooded.
5. **Carr woodland:** water-tolerant trees such as alder and willow, moving towards drier woodland if
   the ground continues to build up.

## How succession works

- **Facilitation:** early species change conditions in ways that allow later species to establish
  (adding organic matter, shelter, nitrogen fixation, water retention).
- **Competitive replacement:** later, larger, longer-lived species out-compete the pioneers for
  light, water and nutrients.
- **Changes in strategy:** early communities favour r-strategists; later ones favour K-strategists.

**Climax community:** the relatively stable end point in balance with the climate and soil. A
**climatic climax** is set by climate. Where local soil or drainage prevents the climatic climax, the
result is sometimes called an **edaphic climax**, such as wet woodland on permanently waterlogged
ground.

## Disturbance and change

- **Natural disturbance:** fire, storms, floods, landslides, grazing by wild herbivores.
- **Human disturbance:** clearing, ploughing, grazing livestock, burning, trampling, pollution.
- **Intermediate disturbance hypothesis:** diversity is often highest where disturbance is
  moderate, because both pioneer and later-stage species can persist; very frequent disturbance
  keeps only tolerant pioneers, very rare disturbance lets a few dominant competitors exclude others.
- **Deflected succession:** human activity holds or diverts the community away from climax, producing
  a plagioclimax.
- **Abandoned farmland** shows secondary succession: annual weeds, then perennial grasses and herbs,
  then shrubs, then woodland.
- **Climate change** can change which climax is possible, or push a system past a tipping point into
  a different stable state; see the systems ideas in the
  [1.2 Systems study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-1-2/).

### Worked example 2: comparing species richness (fictional data)

Plots disturbed at different frequencies are surveyed in the same grassland.

| Disturbance (mowings per year) | 0 | 1 | 3 | 6 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant species per plot | 9 | 17 | 24 | 15 | 6 |

```
Peak richness = 24 species at 3 mowings per year
Increase from 0 to 3 mowings = (24 − 9) / 9 × 100 = 167 % (3 s.f.)
Fall from 3 to 12 mowings    = (24 − 6) / 24 × 100 = 75 %
```

The hump-shaped pattern fits the intermediate disturbance hypothesis. With no mowing, tall competitive
grasses exclude smaller species; with very frequent mowing, only a few tolerant species survive.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing zonation (space) with succession (time).
- Naming a sere's stages without explaining what each stage changes.
- Saying pioneers "make way" for later species without explaining facilitation or competition.
- Assuming disturbance always reduces diversity.
- Treating the climax as fixed whatever the climate does.

## Exam technique

"Describe" transect data: quote where each species starts, peaks and stops, and the matching
abiotic values. "Explain" zonation: link tolerance to the gradient and add competition. "Explain"
succession: give the change at each stage and how it allows the next.
