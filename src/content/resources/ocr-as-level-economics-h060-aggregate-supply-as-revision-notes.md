These notes condense **Aggregate supply (AS)**, topic 1.3 in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). For full explanations and worked examples use the [aggregate supply study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as/); for exam-style work use the [aggregate supply practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification wording | In one line |
|---|---|
| Explain aggregate supply | Total planned output at each price level |
| Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the relationship between aggregate supply and price level in the short run and long run | SRAS slopes up; LRAS is vertical (neo-classical) or flat, rising, then vertical (Keynesian) |
| Explain, with the aid of a diagram, shifts in the aggregate supply curve in the short run and long run | SRAS shifts with costs; LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of factors and technology |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregate supply | Total planned output of goods and services at each price level |
| Short run (macro) | Period in which money wages and some other input prices are fixed |
| Long run (macro) | Period in which all input prices can adjust |
| SRAS | Short-run aggregate supply; upward-sloping, drawn for given costs |
| LRAS | Long-run aggregate supply; shows productive capacity |
| Potential output | The level of output when all resources are fully employed |
| Spare capacity | Unused resources: unemployed labour and idle capital |
| Unit labour cost | Labour cost per unit of output: wage per hour / output per hour |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked |
| Bottleneck | A shortage in one industry or skill that raises costs as output expands |

## The curves at a glance

| Curve | Shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SRAS | Upward-sloping | Contracted costs are fixed, so a higher price level raises profit per unit; costs rise as firms push towards capacity |
| Neo-classical LRAS | Vertical at potential output | Wages and prices are fully flexible, so output depends only on capacity |
| Keynesian LRAS | Horizontal, then rising, then vertical | Spare capacity at low output; bottlenecks in the middle; full capacity at the end |

## What shifts each curve

| Shifts SRAS | Shifts LRAS |
|---|---|
| Money wages relative to productivity | Size of the labour force (migration, participation, retirement age) |
| Raw material and energy prices | Capital stock (net investment) |
| Import prices via the exchange rate | Education, training and health |
| Indirect taxes and producer subsidies | Technology and research and development |
| Regulation costs | Competition, property rights and institutions |

A change that lowers costs **and** raises capacity, such as a permanent productivity gain, shifts both curves right. A temporary cost shock shifts only SRAS.

### Worked reminder: unit labour costs

Wages up 4%, productivity up 4%: unit labour costs **unchanged** (1.04 / 1.04 = 1). Wages up 3%, productivity down 1%: unit labour costs up 1.03 / 0.99 - 1 = **4.0%**, so SRAS shifts left.

### Worked reminder: cost shares

Costs per unit: labour 60, materials 25, energy 15 (total 100). A 20% rise in material prices adds 5, so costs rise **5%**. A 10% fall in energy prices cuts 1.5. Net effect: costs up **3.5%**, so SRAS shifts left, by less than the materials shock alone.

### Worked reminder: potential output

Labour force +1.0%, output per worker +1.0%: potential output rises by about 1.01 x 1.01 - 1 = **2.01%**. The labour force alone falls by 0.5% while output per worker rises by 2.0%: potential output rises by about 0.995 x 1.02 - 1 = **1.49%**.

## Keynesian versus neo-classical: what to say

- Neo-classical: the economy tends to return to potential output; demand policy affects prices, not long-run output.
- Keynesian: the economy can stay below capacity for a long time because wages are sticky downwards; demand policy can raise output when there is spare capacity.
- Choose the Keynesian curve when a question mentions high unemployment or a recession; state which you are using.

## Exam traps

- A change in the price level moves along SRAS; it does not shift it.
- Higher wages shift SRAS left only if they outpace productivity.
- An exchange rate fall raises import costs: SRAS **left**, even though it also raises AD.
- A higher indirect tax shifts SRAS left; a producer subsidy shifts it right.
- LRAS shifts take time: education and investment pay off over years.
- On a vertical LRAS, more demand cannot raise long-run output.
- Axes: **price level** and **real output**.

## Self-test

1. Define aggregate supply.
2. Give two reasons why SRAS slopes upwards.
3. What is the shape of the neo-classical LRAS, and why?
4. Describe the three sections of the Keynesian LRAS.
5. Hourly wages rise by 5% and output per hour by 1%. Calculate the change in unit labour costs.
6. State two causes of a leftward shift of SRAS.
7. State two causes of a rightward shift of LRAS.
8. Why does a depreciation of the currency shift SRAS to the left?
9. Explain why a temporary rise in oil prices shifts SRAS but not LRAS.
10. Labour force +0.4%, output per worker +1.6%. Estimate the growth of potential output.
11. Which LRAS model would you use to analyse an economy with 10% unemployment, and why?
12. How is a rightward shift of LRAS linked to the production possibility curve?

### Answers

1. The total output firms plan to produce and sell at each price level, over a period.
2. Costs such as money wages are fixed in the short run, so a higher price level raises profit per unit; costs per unit rise as firms push output towards capacity.
3. Vertical at potential output, because flexible wages and prices remove any lasting link between the price level and output.
4. Horizontal (spare capacity), upward-sloping (bottlenecks) and vertical (full capacity).
5. 1.05 / 1.01 - 1 = **+3.96%**.
6. Any two: wages rising faster than productivity; higher raw material or energy prices; depreciation; higher indirect taxes; costly regulation.
7. Any two: a larger labour force; net investment; better education and training; new technology; stronger competition.
8. Imported inputs become dearer in domestic currency, raising firms' costs.
9. It raises costs at existing capacity, but the economy's resources and technology are unchanged.
10. 1.004 x 1.016 - 1 = about **2.0%**.
11. Keynesian, because high unemployment means spare capacity on the flat or rising section.
12. Both show a rise in productive capacity: the PPC shifts outwards as LRAS shifts right.
