## Section A — Communication

**1.** Outline the difference between Piaget's theory and the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis of the relationship between language and thought. **[4]**

**2.** Describe Von Frisch's bee study. **[4]**

**3.** Identify **two** functions of animal communication and **one** property of human communication that is not found in animal communication. **[3]**

**4.** Leila and her friend are talking in a café. Leila notices that her friend has crossed her arms and keeps looking away while Leila speaks. Using your knowledge of non-verbal communication, explain what this might show. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** piece of evidence that non-verbal behaviour is innate and **one** piece of evidence that it is learned. **[4]**

## Section B — Mental health

**6.** Explain **two** reasons why the recorded incidence of significant mental health problems may change over time. **[4]**

**7.** Outline **one** effect of a significant mental health problem on the individual and **one** effect on society. **[2]**

**8.** Distinguish between an obsession and a compulsion, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**9.** Describe and evaluate the biological explanation of obsessive-compulsive disorder. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how dual representation theory accounts for flashbacks in post-traumatic stress disorder. **[4]**

**11.** A psychologist is treating a patient with PTSD who avoids driving after a car crash. Explain how prolonged exposure therapy could be used to treat the patient. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **Piaget** argued that **language depends on thought** [1]: children develop concepts first, for example object permanence, and then acquire words for them [1]. The **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** argues the opposite — **thinking depends on language** [1], so the words a language provides shape what its speakers notice, think and remember [1].

**2.** Von Frisch observed **honeybees** returning to the hive after finding food [1]. A bee performed a **round dance** if the food was close and a **waggle dance** if it was further away [1]. The **angle** of the waggle dance showed the **direction** of the food relative to the sun [1], and the length of the waggling showed the **distance** [1]; other bees then flew to the food.

**3.** Any two functions: **survival**, **reproduction**, **territory**, **food** [1] [1]. One property: humans can **plan ahead and discuss future events** (or talk about the past, or about things that are not present) [1].

**4.** Crossed arms are a **closed posture** [1], which can signal defensiveness, disagreement or lack of interest [1]. Looking away reduces **eye contact**, which normally signals attention and regulates the flow of conversation [1], so the friend may be uncomfortable or not engaged with what Leila is saying [1].

**5.** **Innate:** newborn babies (**neonates**) show facial expressions before they could have learned them, **or** people born blind produce the same basic expressions as sighted people without having seen them [1] [1]. **Learned:** **Yuki's study of emoticons** found that Japanese participants relied more on the **eyes** and American participants on the **mouth** when interpreting emotion, showing culture shapes how expressions are read [1] [1].

**6.** Any two, 2 marks each: **increased recognition and less stigma** — more people understand mental health problems and are willing to seek help, so more cases are diagnosed [1] [1]; **challenges of modern living** such as **isolation** may increase the real number of people with problems [1] [1]; **cultural variations in beliefs** — whether behaviour is seen as a mental health problem varies between cultures and over time, which changes what is reported [1] [1].

**7.** Individual, any one: damage to **relationships**; difficulty **coping with day-to-day life**; negative impact on **physical wellbeing** [1]. Society, any one: need for more **social care**; **increased crime rates**; costs to the **economy**, such as lost working days [1].

**8.** An **obsession** is a persistent, unwanted, **intrusive thought, image or urge** that causes anxiety [1], for example a fear of being contaminated by germs [1]. A **compulsion** is a **repetitive behaviour or mental act** carried out to reduce that anxiety [1], for example repeatedly washing the hands [1].

**9.** *Description (up to 3):* OCD may be **genetic** — it runs in families and concordance is higher in identical than non-identical twins [1]; it is linked to low levels of the neurotransmitter **serotonin** [1]; and unusual activity in **frontal brain circuits** may make it hard to stop worrying thoughts [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* drugs that increase serotonin reduce symptoms for many patients, supporting the neurochemical explanation [1]; however, identical twin concordance is not 100%, so genes cannot be the whole explanation and environmental factors must play a part [1]; the explanation also ignores faulty thinking, which the cognitive explanation and the success of CBT suggest is important [1].

**10.** Dual representation theory says a trauma is stored in **two memory systems** [1]: a **verbal, contextual** memory that can be deliberately recalled and described, and a **sensory, situational** memory of the raw sights, sounds and feelings [1]. In PTSD the sensory memory is very strong but **poorly linked** to the contextual memory [1], so when a reminder triggers it, it is re-experienced automatically as a **flashback** that feels as if it is happening now [1].

**11.** The therapist first explains how **avoidance keeps PTSD going** [1]. In **imaginal exposure**, the patient repeatedly recounts the crash in detail in the session until the memory becomes less distressing [1]. In **in-vivo exposure**, the patient gradually approaches what they avoid — for example sitting in a parked car, then short drives as a passenger, then driving [1]. Because avoidance stops, the patient learns the situation is safe and the anxiety reduces [1].

## Frequently asked questions

**Which paper are communication and mental health on?**
Paper 2, Social context and behaviour: 2 hours, 100 marks, 50% of the International GCSE. Communication is Section A and mental health Section C, each worth 25 marks.

**Which studies do I need for mental health?**
Kearns's study of cognitive behaviour therapy for OCD, and a study of prolonged exposure therapy for PTSD; know the method, results and one strength and limitation of each.

**Do I need to learn the ICD criteria word for word?**
No, but you should describe the main characteristics and diagnostic criteria the specification names for OCD and PTSD.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing Von Frisch's dances without stating that they show direction and distance.
- Reversing Piaget and Sapir-Whorf.
- Describing the biological explanation of OCD without evaluating it when the question says "evaluate".
- Naming prolonged exposure therapy without applying it to the scenario given.
