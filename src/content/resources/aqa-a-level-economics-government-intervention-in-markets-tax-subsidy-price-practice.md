## Questions

**1.** Explain how a specific tax differs from an ad valorem tax. **[3]**

**2.** In Country B the market for notebooks has demand Qd = 200 − 5P and supply Qs = −40 + 15P.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** A specific tax of 4 cu per notebook is collected from sellers. Find the new price paid by buyers and the new quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the revenue raised, and how much of it falls on buyers and how much on sellers. **[3]**

**3.** For a good, price elasticity of demand is −0.4 and price elasticity of supply is 1.6. A tax of 10 cu per unit is imposed. Using the elasticity values, explain who bears more of the tax and estimate the share each bears. **[4]**

**4.** In Country B the market for evening language classes has demand Qd = 260 − 8P and supply Qs = −60 + 12P.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** A subsidy of 5 cu per class is paid to providers. Calculate the price learners pay and the new quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost to the government. **[1]**

**5.** Bus passes in Country B have demand Qd = 450 − 15P and supply Qs = −90 + 30P. The government sets a maximum price of 9 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortage that results at the maximum price. **[2]**
**(c)** Use the figures to explain why an illegal market might develop. **[2]**

**6.** A demerit good has demand Qd = 360 − 10P and supply Qs = −60 + 18P. The government sets a minimum price of 20 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the surplus at the minimum price. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate price elasticity of demand at the original equilibrium and compare consumer spending before and after the minimum price. **[2]**

**7.** Three factories in Country B each emit 70 tonnes a year. The government limits total emissions to 150 tonnes and gives each factory 50 permits. Cutting a tonne costs factory A 5 cu, factory B 12 cu and factory C 20 cu. A and B can each cut at most 40 tonnes and C at most 30.

**(a)** Find the total cost of the cut if each factory reduces emissions by the same amount and trading is not allowed. **[2]**
**(b)** The permits can now be traded. Find the cheapest total cost of meeting the limit, and state the permit price. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the saving from trading. **[1]**

**8.** A lake in Country B is open to any fishing boat. Total catch is T = 24n − n² tonnes with n boats, each boat costs 8 tonnes-worth of fish to run, and net gain is T − 8n.

**(a)** Calculate the number of boats and the net gain under open access (boats join until the average catch equals 8). **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of boats and net gain if one owner controls the lake and sets marginal catch equal to 8. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the owner uses fewer boats. **[1]**

**9.** A government pays for a vaccination programme for 6,000 people at a cost of 20 cu each. Each person gains a private benefit of 15 cu, and each vaccination gives other people an external benefit of 8 cu. Evaluate whether the programme is worth funding on these figures. **[4]**

**10.** A government wants to cut use of a demerit good and also to protect low-income households. Explain how these objectives could conflict if it uses an indirect tax, and how it might respond. **[6]**

**11.** A factory in Country B releases fumes that harm nearby residents. Evaluate a tax, tradable permits and regulation as ways to reduce the harm. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A specific tax charges the same amount on every unit sold [1], whereas an ad valorem tax charges a set percentage of the selling price [1]. So a specific tax shifts supply up in parallel, while an ad valorem tax pivots it, because the tax per unit rises as price rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A question on how two things differ is best answered by setting them side by side; defining each alone leaves the contrast unstated.

**2. (a)** 200 − 5P = −40 + 15P, so 240 = 20P [1]. **P = 12 and Q = 140** [1].
**(b)** Sellers need 4 more per unit, so Qs = −40 + 15(P − 4) = −100 + 15P [1]. Setting 200 − 5P = −100 + 15P gives **P = 15** [1] and **Q = 125** [1].
**(c)** Revenue = 4 × 125 = **500 cu** [1]. Buyers bear (15 − 12) × 125 = **375** [1]. Sellers keep 15 − 4 = 11, so they bear (12 − 11) × 125 = **125** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the adjusted supply equation. It is an easy step to get wrong, and writing it out lets a reader follow your later working.

**3.** Demand is inelastic (0.4) and supply is elastic (1.6) [1]. The less elastic side bears more of the tax, so buyers bear more [1]. The burden splits in the ratio PES : PED, which is 1.6 : 0.4 = 4 : 1 [1]. Buyers bear about 8 cu (80%) and sellers about 2 cu (20%) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that the split is an estimate; the ratio rule is exact only in simple cases.

**4. (a)** 260 − 8P = −60 + 12P, so 320 = 20P. **P = 16 and Q = 132** [1].
**(b)** Providers receive P + 5, so Qs = −60 + 12(P + 5) = 12P [1]. Setting 260 − 8P = 12P gives **P = 13** [1] and **Q = 156** [1].
**(c)** Cost = 5 × 156 = **780 cu** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Learners pay 13 but providers receive 18. State both prices so the gap equals the subsidy.

**5. (a)** 450 − 15P = −90 + 30P, so 540 = 45P. **P = 12 and Q = 270** [1].
**(b)** At 9: Qd = 450 − 135 = 315 [1] and Qs = −90 + 270 = 180 [1]. **Shortage = 135.**
**(c)** Buyers would pay up to (450 − 180) ÷ 15 = 18 cu for the last pass supplied [1], well above 9, so sellers and buyers gain from trading illegally at higher prices [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that the ceiling is below equilibrium before calculating a shortage; above it, nothing changes.

**6. (a)** 360 − 10P = −60 + 18P, so 420 = 28P. **P = 15 and Q = 210** [1].
**(b)** At 20: Qd = 160 [1] and Qs = 300. **Surplus = 140** [1].
**(c)** PED = −10 × 15 ÷ 210 = **−0.71** [1]. Spending rises from 15 × 210 = 3,150 to 20 × 160 = **3,200** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the sign and size of elasticity, and tie the spending result back to inelastic demand.

**7. (a)** Cap 150 means 60 tonnes must be cut, so each cuts 20 [1]. Cost = 20 × 5 + 20 × 12 + 20 × 20 = 100 + 240 + 400 = **740 cu** [1].
**(b)** The cheapest cuts happen: A cuts 40 (its limit) [1] and B cuts 20 [1]. Cost = 200 + 240 = **440 cu**. The permit price is **12 cu**, set by B as the marginal cutter [1].
**(c)** Saving = 740 − 440 = **300 cu** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain why the permit price equals 12 cu: it is the cost of the last tonne cut, and it is below the 20 cu that factory C would otherwise pay.

**8. (a)** 24 − n = 8, so **n = 16** [1]. T = 384 − 256 = 128 and net gain = 128 − 128 = **0** [1].
**(b)** 24 − 2n = 8, so **n = 8** [1]. T = 192 − 64 = 128 and net gain = 128 − 64 = **64** [1].
**(c)** The owner counts the cost that an extra boat imposes on the others, whereas open-access boats ignore it [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Note that the catch is identical in both cases; the gain comes from lower cost, which is the point of the comparison.

**9.** Cost = 6,000 × 20 = 120,000 cu [1]. Private benefit = 6,000 × 15 = 90,000, a net private loss of 30,000 [1]. Adding the external benefit of 6,000 × 8 = 48,000 gives total benefit of 138,000 [1]. Net social benefit = 138,000 − 120,000 = **18,000 cu**, so it is worth funding if the external benefit is reliable, but not otherwise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement is needed, not only the sum, and the judgement should rest on how certain the external benefit is.

**10.** A tax raises price and cuts demand [1]. If demand is inelastic, consumption falls little, so the first aim is only partly met [1]. Low-income households spend a larger share of income on the good, so the tax is regressive [1]. This conflicts with the aim of protecting them [1]. The government could use revenue for support or health services, or choose regulation instead [1]. The choice depends on which objective carries more weight [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the conflict and then make a decision on it; listing both aims without a resolution leaves the question half answered.

**11.** Tax: it raises costs, shifting supply up and cutting output [1]. Set at the external cost, it gives the social optimum [1]. But the external cost is hard to measure [1], and revenue can fund clean-up or support [1]. Permits: the cap fixes the total [1], and trading finds the cheapest cuts [1], but the cap, allocation and monitoring are hard, and a market needs many firms [1]. Regulation: a standard is simple and certain [1], but it ignores differences in cost between firms and needs enforcement [1]. Evaluation: with one factory, a permit market would be thin, so a tax or standard is better [1]. If the harm is severe or a safe limit is known, a standard gives certainty; if costs vary and the harm can be measured, a tax is cheaper [1]. Reasoned overall judgement that weighs these [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the three tools against each other in this setting rather than describing each in turn; a conclusion that uses the factory context is stronger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Moving the demand curve when a tax or subsidy changes costs.
- Not adjusting the supply equation correctly when a tax or subsidy is added.
- Treating the price paid and the price received as the same after a tax.
- Calculating a shortage or surplus without checking the control binds.
- Giving an elasticity without a sign, or without linking it to who bears a tax.
- Describing a policy but not the failure it addresses.
- Listing strengths and weaknesses with no judgement.
- Ignoring cost and what the government gives up.
