## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by emotional labour. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two forms of mass transport named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what Baudrillard meant by sign value. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between Fordism and post-Fordism. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two effects of mass car ownership on people's lives. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Corvenna.

| Share of employment (%) (fictional) | 1960 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Extraction (mining, quarrying, fishing, farming) | 30 | 8 |
| Manufacturing | 35 | 22 |
| Services | 35 | 70 |

**(a)** Calculate the change, in percentage points, in the share of employment in services and in extraction between 1960 and 2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data support Bell's idea of post-industrial society, and one limitation of using the data this way. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> At the fictional Brightline call centre, staff answer customer calls using scripts shown on screen. A computer system records the length of every call and flags any that last longer than four minutes. Supervisors listen to random calls and score staff on whether they "sound friendly and positive". Staff must smile while speaking, because managers say customers can "hear a smile". Those with low scores lose their monthly bonus.

**(a)** Identify two ways in which technology is used to control work at Brightline. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how technology has changed the nature of service work. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which railways changed social life. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three cases used to support social constructionist views of technology. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse the effects of air travel on people and on the environment. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that consumption of material goods is mainly about displaying status. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent have machines deskilled workers? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Managing one's own feelings and their display as part of a job [1], to produce the right emotion in customers, as Hochschild found among flight attendants [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: cars and roads [1]; railways [1]; air travel. **[2]**

**3.** The meaning a good carries about its owner [1], such as status, taste or identity [1], which Baudrillard argued matters more to consumers than the good's practical use (use value) [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Fordism produces standardised goods in very large runs for mass markets [1], while post-Fordism produces smaller runs of varied goods for niche markets [1]. Fordist workers repeat fixed, simple tasks on an assembly line [1], while post-Fordist production uses flexible, multi-skilled teams and computer-controlled machines [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Cars give personal freedom to travel and allowed suburbs to grow [1], so people can live far from where they work and commute daily [1]. Cities designed around roads disadvantage people without cars, such as the young, elderly and poor [1], and cars bring traffic deaths, injuries and air pollution [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Services rose by 35 percentage points (70 - 35) [1]; extraction fell by 22 percentage points (30 - 8) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Services doubled their share to 70% while extraction and manufacturing both fell [1], supporting Bell's argument that service and knowledge work come to dominate post-industrial societies [1]. Limitation: "services" includes very different jobs, from finance to cleaning [1], so the data cannot show whether work has become more skilled, as Bell claims, or remains routine, as Braverman would argue [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: scripts shown on screen [1]; recording and flagging the length of calls [1]; recorded calls scored by supervisors; bonuses linked to scores. **[2]**
**(b)** Scripts on screen remove workers' control over what they say [1], which fits Braverman's argument that technology separates planning from doing and deskills work [1]. Timing every call and flagging long ones applies Taylorist time discipline to service work [1], intensifying the pace of work [1]. Scoring staff on sounding friendly and requiring a smile shows emotional labour (Hochschild) [1], with technology now monitoring feelings as well as output, so control reaches into workers' emotions [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Railways cut journey times and required shared timetables [1], which pushed Britain towards a single standard time and changed how people experienced space and time (Schivelbusch) [1]. Cheap rail travel allowed working-class day trips to the seaside [1], and lines allowed suburbs to grow so people could live away from city centres [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Pinch and Bijker's study of the bicycle [1]: the safety bicycle won because of how social groups interpreted bicycles, not technical superiority [1]. Cowan's study of household technology [1]: washing machines did not cut women's housework, because standards and gender roles shaped their use [1]. Winner's Long Island bridges [1]: designs can build social choices about class and race into objects, though his evidence was later disputed [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Jet travel from the 1950s made long-distance journeys fast [1].
- Mass tourism became possible for many in richer countries [1].
- Air travel supports diaspora visits and migration [1].
- Benefits are unequal: a minority of people take most flights [1].
- Aviation emits greenhouse gases that affect everyone [1].
- Airports bring noise and take land, affecting nearby communities who may rarely fly [1].
- Responses such as "flight shame" show cultural change [1].
- Analysis: benefits concentrated, costs dispersed, a pattern of environmental inequality [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Veblen: conspicuous consumption displays wealth and status [1].
- Bourdieu: taste reflects class and cultural capital [1].
- Consumption reproduces class boundaries through "good taste" [1].
- Baudrillard: goods are bought for sign value [1].
- Application: own-society example of branded goods as status symbols [1].
- Planned obsolescence pushes people to buy new goods [1].
- Counter: much consumption is ordinary and necessary [1].
- Consumption can express identity and creativity, as in subcultures [1].
- Status display is not only about wealth; it may mark religion, gender or age [1].
- Postmodernists see consumption as free identity choice [1].
- Marxists see it as driven by capitalism's need to sell, not by consumers [1].
- Judgement: status display is important for some goods and groups, but not the main purpose of most consumption [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Braverman: machines and scientific management deskill workers [1].
- Taylorism separates planning from doing [1].
- Fordist assembly lines reduced jobs to repeated simple tasks [1].
- Own-society application: factory or garment work with one repeated task [1].
- Luddites resisted the use of machines to replace skilled workers [1].
- Service work is scripted and monitored, as in call centres [1].
- Counter: Blauner found automated process work less alienating [1].
- Bell: post-industrial society creates skilled knowledge work [1].
- Post-Fordism (Piore and Sabel): multi-skilled flexible teams [1].
- Own-society application: growth of technical or professional jobs [1].
- Deskilling and upskilling can happen at the same time for different workers [1].
- Gender: women have often been placed in deskilled roles (Wajcman) [1].
- Hochschild: new skills, such as emotional labour, are demanded but not recognised [1].
- Constructionist point: management choices, not machines alone, decide outcomes [1].
- Analysis: effects differ by sector, period and bargaining power [1].
- Judgement: machines have deskilled many routine jobs, but technology does not determine this alone [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish the machine from how management uses it; that distinction turns a descriptive answer into an evaluative one.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using Fordism to mean any factory.
- Quoting data without saying what it can and cannot show.
- Describing transport without its effects on people and environment.
- Treating Veblen and Baudrillard as saying the same thing.
- Giving determinist arguments with no constructionist counter.
