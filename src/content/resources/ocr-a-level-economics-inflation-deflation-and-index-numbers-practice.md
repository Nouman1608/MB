## Questions

**1.** Define hyperinflation. A good costs 40 z. Prices rise 60% a month for three months. Calculate its price at the end. **[3]**

**2.** Country Y's price index starts at 100. Inflation is 6.0% in year 1, 3.5% in year 2 and −0.5% in year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the index at the end of years 2 and 3. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the year of disinflation and the year of deflation. **[2]**

**3.** Country Y's price index is 138.2 in 2023 and 143.6 in 2024. Calculate the inflation rate to one decimal place. **[3]**

**4.** A worker earns 28,000 z when the index is 104.0 and 30,100 z four years later when it is 119.6.

**(a)** Calculate her later pay in the earlier year's prices. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in real pay and comment on the nominal rise. **[2]**

**5.** An index (2019 = 100) is 107.5 in 2021, 121.0 in 2023 and 127.8 in 2025.

**(a)** Calculate the inflation rate between 2023 and 2025 to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Rebase the index so that 2023 = 100 and give the values for 2021 and 2025. **[3]**

**6.** A basket has these categories. Treat each weight as the category's share of spending and its contribution to inflation as weight × price change.

| Category | Weight (%) | Price change (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 28 | 5.0 |
| Housing | 34 | 2.0 |
| Transport | 16 | 9.0 |
| Leisure | 12 | 3.0 |
| Other | 10 | −1.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the overall inflation rate. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain which category contributed most. **[2]**

**7.**

**(a)** Explain how the official UK measure of inflation is constructed. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why a pensioner's experience of inflation may differ from the headline figure. **[2]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that the Retail Prices Index is a less useful measure of inflation than the Consumer Prices Index. **[6]**

**9.** In an economy, wages rise 7% and output per worker rises 2.5%.

**(a)** By what percentage do unit labour costs increase? Give one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how this could cause cost-push inflation. **[3]**

**10.** In Country X, spending grows 6% a year while productive capacity grows 2% a year. The currency also falls 15% against trading partners, and imports are a large part of firms' inputs. Evaluate the main causes of inflation in Country X. **[9]**

**11.** A household has a fixed debt of 75,000 z and an income of 30,000 z. Prices and incomes then fall 3% a year for three years.

**(a)** Calculate the debt in the original prices and the debt-to-income ratio after three years. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether deflation is always harmful. **[6]**

**12.** Explain why governments aim for low and stable inflation rather than zero inflation. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Hyperinflation is extremely rapid, usually accelerating, inflation [1]. Price = 40 × 1.6³ [1] = **163.84 z** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compounding is the point. Adding 60% three times (to 112 z) is the common slip, so show the power.

**2. (a)** Year 2: 100 × 1.06 × 1.035 = **109.71** [1]. Year 3: 109.71 × 0.995 = **109.16** [1].
**(b)** Year 2 is disinflation, since inflation falls from 6.0% to 3.5% but prices still rise [1]. Year 3 is deflation, since the rate is negative and the index falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Back each label with the numbers, not just the word.

**3.** Change = 143.6 − 138.2 = 5.4 [1]. 5.4 ÷ 138.2 × 100 [1] = **3.9%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The old index is the denominator. Writing the formula before substituting shows your method.

**4. (a)** Real pay = 30,100 ÷ 119.6 × 104.0 [1] = **26,174 z** [1].
**(b)** Change = (26,174 − 28,000) ÷ 28,000 = **−6.5%** [1]. Nominal pay rose 7.5% but prices rose 15.0%, so she can buy less [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comment on the gap between nominal and real, not only the figure.

**5. (a)** (127.8 − 121.0) ÷ 121.0 × 100 [1] = **5.6%** [1].
**(b)** Divide by the 2023 value and multiply by 100 [1]. 2021: 107.5 ÷ 121.0 × 100 = **88.8** [1]. 2025: 127.8 ÷ 121.0 × 100 = **105.6** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A rebased value below 100 for an earlier year is expected, so a sense check helps.

**6. (a)** Contributions: food 1.40, housing 0.68, transport 1.44, leisure 0.36, other −0.10 [1]. Sum = 3.78 [1]. Inflation = **about 3.8%** [1].
**(b)** Transport contributed most (1.44 points), because its large 9.0% price rise outweighs its smaller weight of 16% [1], though food (1.40) is close because its weight is larger [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Weight and price change both matter. Name both factors to explain a contribution.

**7. (a)** Choose a basket of goods and services that households buy [1]. Collect prices regularly [1]. Weight categories by their share of household spending [1]. Set a base year equal to 100 and express later prices against it, updating the basket and weights over time [1].
**(b)** The weights reflect an average household [1]. A pensioner may spend a larger share on heating or food, so if those prices rise faster, personal inflation is higher than the headline [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers to (b) link a specific spending pattern to a specific price movement.

**8.** For the view: RPI uses an arithmetic average, which tends to give higher figures [1], so it may overstate the rise in the cost of living [1]. Against: RPI includes owner-occupier housing costs and council tax [1], which CPI mostly excludes, so it can reflect a homeowner's costs better [1]. Judgement: usefulness depends on purpose: CPI suits comparison with other countries and policy [1], while RPI may suit those concerned with housing costs. [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with a judgement that names who or what each index suits.

**9. (a)** Unit labour cost rise = 1.07 ÷ 1.025 [1] − 1 [1] = **4.4%** [1].
**(b)** Costs per unit of output rise 4.4% [1], reducing profit unless prices rise [1]. Firms pass costs on, shifting SRAS left and raising the price level while output falls [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare wages with productivity; wage growth alone does not show a rise in unit costs.

**10.** Demand-pull: spending growth of 6% exceeds capacity growth of 2% [1], so AD shifts right faster than supply and prices rise as spare capacity is used [1]. Cost-push: a 15% fall in the currency raises import prices [1], and imports are a large part of inputs, so unit costs rise and SRAS shifts left [1]. Interaction: stronger export demand from the weaker currency adds to AD [1]. Evidence: the gap between 6% and 2% suggests demand pressure is persistent [1], while the currency fall may be a one-off shift in the price level [1]. Judgement: demand-pull is probably the main cause if the gap persists [1], but missing information (spare capacity, wage data, how fast costs are passed on) limits the conclusion [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given and then say what further information would settle the judgement.

**11. (a)** Debt in original prices = 75,000 ÷ 0.97³ [1] = **82,176 z** [1]. Income = 30,000 × 0.97³ = 27,380 z [1]. Ratio = 75,000 ÷ 27,380 = **2.74**, up from 2.5 [1].
**(b)** Harmful: real debt rises, so spending on other goods falls [1]. Households postpone purchases expecting lower prices, cutting AD [1]. Falling revenue with fixed costs reduces profit and jobs [1]. Not always harmful: deflation from lower costs or higher productivity raises real incomes without lost output [1]. Exports become cheaper [1]. Judgement: the cause, size and persistence decide [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate deflation caused by weak demand from deflation caused by supply gains.

**12.** Low inflation protects purchasing power [1]. Stable inflation makes prices predictable, helping contracts and investment [1]. A small positive rate leaves room for real wages to adjust without money pay cuts [1], and reduces the risk of deflation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say why zero is not the target; describing low and stable inflation on its own leaves the question unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling slower inflation as deflation.
- Using the new index value as the denominator.
- Adding percentage changes instead of compounding them.
- Leaving real values unadjusted or forgetting to multiply by 100.
- Rebasing with the wrong year.
- Describing cost-push without a shift of SRAS or any link to unit costs.
- Listing effects of inflation without saying who gains and who loses.
- Concluding without a reasoned judgement.
- Giving advantages of CPI or RPI with no reason.
