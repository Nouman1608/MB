## Section A

**1.** Define *climate forcing* and give one natural example. **[2]**

**2.** Outline how a large volcanic eruption can cause temporary global cooling. **[2]**

**3.** Outline why orbital (Milankovitch) cycles cannot explain the warming of the last 150 years. **[2]**

**4.** State two ways in which the land and ocean carbon sinks may become less effective as the climate warms. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how dissolving carbon dioxide in seawater affects shell-building organisms. **[3]**

**6.** Define *tipping element* and name one example. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) Annual mean CO₂ concentrations at a fictional station:

| Year | 1965 | 1985 | 2005 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ / ppm | 320 | 346 | 380 | 425 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of increase for 1965–1985 and for 2005–2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the trend shown by the data. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in 2025 above a pre-industrial concentration of 280 ppm. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest why the rate of increase is lower than the rate of human emissions alone would cause. **[1]**

**8.** (Data response) Fictional emissions data for three countries:

| Country | Annual emissions / Mt CO₂ | Population / millions | Cumulative emissions since 1850 / Gt CO₂ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Darvo | 900 | 60 | 80 |
| Ember | 2400 | 300 | 30 |
| Fallon | 15 | 25 | 0.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the emissions per person for each country. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why Darvo and Ember might disagree about which of them should cut emissions faster. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why Fallon may suffer severe impacts despite its low emissions. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why the observed cooling of the stratosphere supports the conclusion that recent warming is caused by greenhouse gases. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why reducing methane emissions slows warming more quickly than reducing carbon dioxide emissions by the same CO₂-equivalent amount. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how a tipping element in one part of the Earth system could trigger changes in another. **[4]**

**12.** "The impacts of climate change will fall most heavily on those least responsible for causing it." Discuss. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A factor that changes the balance between energy entering and leaving the Earth system, causing warming or cooling [1]. Natural example, any one: changes in solar output; orbital cycles; volcanic aerosols [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The eruption injects sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it forms sulfate aerosols [1]; these reflect incoming sunlight back to space, reducing the energy reaching the surface until they settle out after a year or two [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Orbital cycles change over tens of thousands of years [1], whereas recent warming has happened over about 150 years and follows the rise in greenhouse gases [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: warmer water dissolves less CO₂ [1]; stronger ocean stratification reduces mixing of CO₂ into deep water [1]; drought and heat reduce plant growth; wildfires release stored carbon; thawing permafrost releases carbon. **[2]**

**5.** CO₂ forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and lowers pH [1]. Hydrogen ions combine with carbonate ions, reducing the carbonate available [1], so corals, molluscs and some plankton build shells and skeletons more slowly or have them weakened [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A large part of the Earth system that can cross a threshold and shift to a new state that is hard to reverse [1]; example, any one: Greenland ice sheet, West Antarctic ice sheet, Amazon rainforest, permafrost, Atlantic overturning circulation, coral reefs [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 1965–1985: 26 ÷ 20 = **1.3 ppm per year** [1]; 2005–2025: 45 ÷ 20 = **2.25 ppm per year** [1].
**(b)** CO₂ rose throughout, from 320 to 425 ppm [1], and the rate of increase accelerated, from 1.3 to 2.25 ppm per year, a rise of about 70% between the first and last periods [1].
**(c)** (425 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = **51.8%** [1].
**(d)** Oceans and land ecosystems absorb roughly half of human emissions each year [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Darvo: 900 ÷ 60 = **15 t per person** [1]; Ember: 2400 ÷ 300 = **8 t per person** [1]; Fallon: 15 ÷ 25 = **0.6 t per person** [1].
**(b)** Darvo might argue that Ember should cut faster because Ember's total annual emissions are more than two and a half times Darvo's [1]. Ember might argue that Darvo should cut faster because Darvo's emissions per person are nearly double Ember's [1] and its cumulative emissions since 1850 (80 Gt) are much larger, so it has contributed more to warming so far [1].
**(c)** Fallon may be highly exposed, for example low-lying or dependent on rain-fed farming [1], and with little wealth it has low adaptive capacity to build defences or recover from disasters [1]. **[8]**

**9.** A brighter Sun would warm the whole atmosphere, including the stratosphere [1]. Extra greenhouse gases absorb more infrared in the lower atmosphere, so less reaches the stratosphere, while greenhouse gases in the stratosphere emit more energy to space [1]. The observed warming troposphere and cooling stratosphere therefore fits the greenhouse explanation and rules out the Sun as the main cause [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Methane is broken down in the atmosphere mostly within about a decade [1], so when emissions fall its concentration and warming effect fall quickly [1]. Much CO₂ remains for centuries to thousands of years, so cutting it stops further warming but the warming already caused persists [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Example: Greenland ice-sheet melting [1] adds freshwater to the North Atlantic, reducing the density of surface water [1]. Less dense water sinks less, so the Atlantic overturning circulation may weaken [1]. This changes heat transport and rainfall patterns elsewhere, for example shifting tropical rain belts that affect forests such as the Amazon [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Any valid chain earns credit, for example permafrost thaw releasing methane and CO₂ that increase warming of ice sheets, provided each link is explained.

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- Wealthy, early-industrialising countries have the largest cumulative and per-person emissions [1].
- Many low-income countries emit little per person but are highly exposed: small islands, deltas, drylands [1].
- Low adaptive capacity (money, infrastructure, insurance) increases their losses [1].
- Within countries, poorer people, outdoor workers and the elderly suffer most from heat and floods [1].
- Future generations will bear impacts of past emissions, which is an intergenerational injustice [1].
- However, wealthy countries also face severe impacts, such as heatwaves, wildfires and coastal flooding [1].
- Some large emerging economies now have high total emissions as well as high vulnerability [1].
- Responses such as loss-and-damage funding and adaptation finance aim to address the imbalance [1].
- Judgement: largely true, because exposure and low adaptive capacity coincide with low responsibility, although no country escapes impacts [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving rates without units or not stating that the rise is accelerating.
- Dividing Mt by millions and giving the answer in Mt instead of tonnes per person.
- Mentioning only total emissions when discussing responsibility.
- Describing acidification as "the ocean getting warmer".
- Writing a tipping-point chain with no explained links.
