These questions practise quantitative skill QS9, interpreting, applying and analysing information in written, graphical and numerical forms, from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. The sources are original and every figure is invented. Revise first with the [QS9 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills/) and the [QS9 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs9-quantitative-skills-revision-notes/); the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) lists the topics these questions draw on.

**Source A (invented): tourism in Country Z**

| Year | Tourist arrivals (m) | Average hotel price per night | Hotel rooms available (thousand) | Consumer price index (2021 = 100) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 2.0 | 80 | 50 | 100 |
| 2022 | 2.6 | 86 | 51 | 104 |
| 2023 | 3.1 | 95 | 52 | 109 |
| 2024 | 3.4 | 104 | 56 | 113 |
| 2025 | 3.3 | 108 | 61 | 116 |

**Source B (original extract):** *Country Z's tourism board says that "rising hotel prices show the industry is booming, and the government should help investors build more hotels". A residents' group replies that "tourists are pushing up local rents and crowding the roads, and the benefits go mainly to hotel owners". New hotels take two to three years to plan and build.*

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Using Source A, calculate the percentage change in tourist arrivals between 2021 and 2025. **[1]**

**2.** Calculate the average hotel price in 2025 in 2021 prices, and the real percentage change in the hotel price between 2021 and 2025. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the percentage change in hotel rooms available between 2021 and 2025, and compare it with the change in arrivals. **[2]**

**4.** Using Source A, describe the trend in tourist arrivals between 2021 and 2025. **[3]**

**5.** A diagram shows a market in equilibrium at a price of 20 and a quantity of 500. After demand rises, the new equilibrium is at a price of 24 and a quantity of 560. Calculate the percentage changes in price and quantity, and estimate the price elasticity of supply along the supply curve. **[3]**

**6.** A country's unemployment rate falls from 8% to 6%. Which statement is correct? **[1]**

A: Unemployment fell by 2%.

B: The rate fell by 2 percentage points, which is a fall of 25%.

C: The rate fell by 25 percentage points.

D: The rate fell by 33%.

### Section B

**7.** Using Source A, explain how the data for 2021 to 2024 are consistent with an increase in demand for hotel rooms. **[4]**

**8.** Using Source A and Source B, analyse how far the supply of hotel rooms responded to the rise in hotel prices between 2021 and 2025. **[6]**

**9.** In 2025 arrivals fell while the hotel price rose. Using Source A, explain two possible reasons for this. **[4]**

**10.** Using both sources, assess the tourism board's claim that "rising hotel prices show the industry is booming". **[6]**

**11.** The residents' group publishes this data for six regions of Country Z (invented).

| Region | A | B | C | D | E | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tourists per resident | 0.5 | 1.2 | 2.0 | 2.4 | 3.1 | 4.0 |
| Rise in average rent (%) | 2.1 | 3.0 | 4.2 | 3.8 | 5.5 | 6.1 |

Explain whether the data show that tourism causes higher rents. **[4]**

**12.** Using both sources and your knowledge of economics, evaluate whether the government of Country Z should subsidise the building of new hotels. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** (3.3 - 2.0) / 2.0 x 100 = **+65%** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 108 / 116 x 100 = **93.10** in 2021 prices [1]. Real change = (93.10 - 80) / 80 = **+16.4%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The money price rose 35%, but most of that is general inflation; the real figure is the fair comparison.

**3.** Rooms: (61 - 50) / 50 = **+22%** [1]. Arrivals rose by 65%, three times as fast, so rooms per tourist fell: about 40 arrivals per room in 2021 against 54 in 2025 [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Arrivals rose overall from 2.0m to 3.3m (+65%) [1]. Growth slowed each year, from 30% in 2022 to 19% in 2023 and 10% in 2024 [1]. In 2025 arrivals fell slightly, by about 3%, the only fall in the period [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Direction, shape and exception, each with a figure, earns all three marks; "arrivals went up" earns one at most.

**5.** Price: 4 / 20 = **+20%** [1]. Quantity: 60 / 500 = **+12%** [1]. PES is about 12 / 20 = **0.6**, inelastic [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A rise in demand moves the market along the supply curve, so the two equilibria give a point estimate of PES, not PED.

**6.** **B** [1]. The rate fell by 2 percentage points; 2 / 8 = 25% of the starting rate. **[1]**

**7.** Arrivals rose by 70% between 2021 and 2024 (2.0m to 3.4m) [1]. At the same time the hotel price rose from 80 to 104, faster than general prices (index 113), so the real price rose [1]. Price and quantity rising together is what a rightward shift of demand along an upward-sloping supply curve produces [1]. Rooms available rose only 12%, so demand grew faster than capacity and pushed price up [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Rooms rose by 22% while the money price rose by 35% [1], giving a rough PES of 0.63; using the real price rise of 16.4%, the rough figure is about 1.3 [1]. Most of the increase in rooms came in 2024 and 2025 (52 to 61 thousand) [1], after prices had been rising for two years, which fits Source B's point that hotels take two to three years to build [1]. So supply was inelastic in the short run and became more responsive later [1]. The estimate is rough, because rooms also depend on factors other than price, such as finance and planning rules [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the real price changes the estimate a lot; saying which you use, and why, is analysis.

**9.** Reason 1: the price rise may have started to deter tourists, a movement along the demand curve [1], although the real price rose only about 1% in 2025 (3.8% money rise against 2.7% inflation), so this is unlikely to be the whole story [1]. Reason 2: something else reduced demand, such as weaker incomes in the countries tourists come from or crowding and congestion [1]; meanwhile prices kept rising because hotels' costs rose with general inflation [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Support: arrivals rose 65% and the real hotel price rose 16%, so demand was strong [1], and investment in rooms has accelerated [1]. Against: much of the money price rise is general inflation (index up 16%) [1]; arrivals fell in 2025, so growth may have peaked [1]; higher prices partly reflect a shortage of rooms, which means some demand was turned away rather than met [1]. Judgement: the data show a strong market up to 2024, but "booming" overstates 2025, and the board has an interest in the claim [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separating money from real price changes, and noting who made the claim, are the evaluation points examiners look for.

**11.** There is a positive correlation: regions with more tourists per resident generally had larger rent rises, from 2.1% in A to 6.1% in F [1]. Region D breaks the pattern, with more tourists than C but a smaller rent rise [1]. Correlation does not prove cause: regions with many tourists may also be large cities with strong job growth, which would raise rents anyway [1]. Six regions is a small sample, and the residents' group may have chosen the data to support its case [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For a subsidy: room supply has lagged behind arrivals (rooms +22% against arrivals +65%) [1], and the real price rise suggests a shortage [1]; extra rooms could lower prices, attract more visitors and create jobs and incomes [1]. Against: arrivals fell in 2025, so demand may be levelling off, and new rooms already planned (61 thousand in 2025) may be enough [1]; investors are already responding to high prices without public money, so a subsidy may pay for building that would happen anyway [1]. Source B points to external costs, congestion and higher rents for residents, which more tourism would add to [1], and the benefits may go mainly to hotel owners [1]. Judgement: a general subsidy is hard to justify on this data; if anything, policy should tackle the external costs, for example through a tourist tax, unless evidence shows a lasting shortage [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from Source A on both sides, bring in the external costs from Source B, and finish with a judgement that names the evidence it depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing each year in turn instead of the overall pattern.
- Using money prices when the real price makes the point.
- Mixing up percentage points and percentages.
- Treating the tourism board's or residents' claims as facts.
- Calling correlation proof of cause.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
