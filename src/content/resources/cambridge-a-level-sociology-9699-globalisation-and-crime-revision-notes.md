These notes cover section 8.3 *Globalisation and crime* of Cambridge International A Level
Sociology (9699) Paper 4: why global crimes have emerged, who benefits from them, and how they are
policed and prosecuted. Full explanations are in the
[globalisation and crime study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-crime/);
exam-style questions are in the
[globalisation and crime practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-crime-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

The forces that globalised trade, finance and communication also globalised crime. Offenders use
time–space compression, offshore finance, weak states and new technology to operate across
borders. Marxists argue the powerful gain most and are policed least; feminists show that the
victims of trafficking and exploitation are disproportionately women and girls. Policing has
become more international, but jurisdiction, sovereignty, corruption and encryption still favour
well-resourced offenders.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Global crime** | Crime whose organisation, victims or harm cross national borders |
| **Glocal organised crime** | Networks with global reach rooted in local contacts and markets (Hobbs and Dunnighan, 1998) |
| **Time–space compression** | Shrinking of effective distance by faster transport and communication (Harvey, 1989) |
| **Human trafficking** | Act + means + purpose of exploitation (UN Trafficking Protocol, 2000) |
| **People smuggling** | Helping someone cross a border illegally, usually for a fee |
| **Corporate crime** | Offences committed by or for a company to benefit it |
| **Regulatory arbitrage** | Locating activity where rules are weakest |
| **Money laundering** | Disguising the criminal origin of money |
| **Green criminology** | Study of harms to the environment, legal or illegal |
| **Primary / secondary green crime** | Direct environmental destruction / flouting rules meant to prevent it (Carrabine et al., 2004) |
| **Feminisation of survival** | Growing dependence of households and states on women's earnings abroad (Sassen, 2000) |
| **Extradition** | Legal transfer of a suspect from one state to another |

## Why global crime emerged

- Time–space compression: goods, people and money move faster.
- Deregulated finance: offshore centres, shell companies; Panama Papers (2016).
- Inequality and insecurity: Taylor (1997) — neo-liberal globalisation produces crime at both the
  bottom and the top.
- Demand in richer countries for drugs, cheap labour, sexual services and wildlife.
- Weak or corrupt states lower the risk for offenders.
- New technology: online fraud, data theft, encrypted communication.

## The three named crimes

**Human trafficking**
- Act: recruit, transport, transfer, harbour, receive.
- Means: force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or vulnerability.
- Purpose: sexual exploitation, forced labour, slavery, organ removal.
- Children: "means" not required.
- Not the same as smuggling, which ends on arrival; trafficking is ongoing exploitation.

**Corporate crime**
- Pearce, *Crimes of the Powerful* (1976): great harm, little punishment.
- TNCs relocate to weak regulation.
- Volkswagen emissions scandal (2015): test-cheating software, global effects.

**Crimes against the environment**
- Primary: pollution, deforestation, species decline.
- Secondary: breaking rules meant to protect the environment; state action against campaigners,
  such as the sinking of the *Rainbow Warrior* (1985).
- *Probo Koala* toxic waste dumped in Abidjan (2006): harm exported to a poorer country.

## Who benefits?

| Perspective | Argument | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Marxist** | Capitalism encourages crimes of the powerful; corporations fined rather than prosecuted; poor offenders take the risks and are caught | Less helpful for trafficking networks outside any ruling class, or crimes against corporations |
| **Feminist** | Victims of trafficking and exploitation are disproportionately women and girls; patriarchal demand sustains the trade; Sassen's feminisation of survival | Agustín (2007): "victim" framing can ignore migrant women's own choices |

## Policing and prosecution

**Benefits**
- Interpol notices, including Red Notices; Europol coordination.
- International Criminal Court (Rome Statute 1998; working from 2002).
- Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001).
- Financial Action Task Force (1989): anti-money laundering standards.
- Digital forensics, data sharing, financial tracking.

**Challenges**
- Jurisdiction and sovereignty; slow or refused extradition.
- Uneven resources; corruption.
- Encryption, anonymising networks, cryptocurrencies.
- Powerful offenders lobby, relocate and settle cases.
- A harm legal in one country may be criminal in another.

## Exam traps

- Trafficking ≠ smuggling: check which one an example actually shows.
- The syllabus says "such as": trafficking, corporate and environmental crime are examples, and
  cybercrime or drug trafficking can also be used.
- "Who benefits" is the focus of the theory bullet; make Marxist and feminist views answer it.
- The policing bullet has two sides: benefits **and** challenges of globalisation and technology.
- Name cases accurately; do not invent figures on the size of global crime.

## Self-test

1. What makes a crime global?
2. What did Hobbs and Dunnighan mean by "glocal" organised crime?
3. State the three elements of trafficking in the UN Trafficking Protocol.
4. Why is "means" not required when the victim is a child?
5. Give one difference between trafficking and smuggling.
6. What did Taylor (1997) argue about globalisation and crime?
7. Distinguish primary from secondary green crime, with an example of each.
8. Why does the *Probo Koala* case illustrate the global dimension of environmental crime?
9. Give one Marxist argument about who benefits from global crime.
10. What is the feminisation of survival?
11. Name two international bodies or agreements that help police global crime.
12. Give two challenges in prosecuting global crime.

### Answers

1. Its organisation, victims or harm cross national borders.
2. Networks that are global in reach but rooted in local places, contacts and markets.
3. An act (recruitment, transport, transfer, harbouring, receipt), a means (force, coercion,
   deception, abuse of vulnerability and so on) and a purpose of exploitation.
4. Children cannot consent to their own exploitation, so moving a child for exploitation is
   trafficking even without force or deception.
5. Smuggling is facilitated illegal border crossing that usually ends on arrival; trafficking is
   exploitation that may continue and need not cross a border.
6. Neo-liberal globalisation increased inequality and insecurity and promoted consumerism,
   encouraging crime among both the excluded poor and elites with new opportunities.
7. Primary: direct destruction such as pollution or deforestation. Secondary: breaking rules meant
   to prevent harm, or state violence against campaigners, such as the sinking of the *Rainbow
   Warrior*.
8. Waste produced through global trade was dumped in a poorer country, where local people bore the
   harm.
9. Corporations and elites profit from harmful crimes but are usually fined rather than
   imprisoned, while poor offenders take the risks.
10. Sassen's term for the growing dependence of households, communities and governments in poorer
    countries on women's earnings, often from risky migration and informal work.
11. Any two: Interpol, Europol, the International Criminal Court, the Budapest Convention, the
    Financial Action Task Force.
12. Any two: jurisdiction and extradition problems; corruption or lack of resources; encryption and
    cryptocurrencies; power of corporations; differences in national law.
