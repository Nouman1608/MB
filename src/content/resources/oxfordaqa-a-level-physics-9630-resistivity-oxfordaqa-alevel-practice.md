Learn the content first with the [resistivity study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-resistivity-oxfordaqa-alevel/) and the [resistivity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-resistivity-oxfordaqa-alevel-revision-notes/). Treat meters as ideal and supplies as having negligible internal resistance.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Write down the equation that defines resistivity, naming each symbol, and state the unit of resistivity. **[2]**

**2.** An alloy wire has resistivity 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m, diameter 0.60 mm and length 2.5 m. Calculate its resistance. **[2]**

**3.** A 0.80 m length of wire of diameter 0.36 mm has resistance 12 Ω. Calculate the resistivity of the material. **[2]**

**4.** A wire of resistance 5.0 Ω is stretched uniformly until its length is 1.2 times its original length. Its volume does not change. Calculate its new resistance. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the resistance of a metal wire increases when its temperature rises. **[2]**

**6.** State what is meant by the critical temperature of a superconductor, and describe the resistivity of the material above this temperature. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A student measures the resistance R of different lengths L of a wire of diameter 0.32 mm.

| L / m | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R / Ω | 1.15 | 2.10 | 3.05 | 4.00 | 4.95 |

**(a)** Calculate the gradient of a graph of R against L. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the gradient to calculate the resistivity of the wire. **[2]**

**(c)** The line does not pass through the origin. Suggest a cause, and explain why the gradient method still gives the correct resistivity. **[2]**

**8.** A copper power cable is 2.0 km long with a circular cross-section of diameter 10 mm. The resistivity of copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance of the cable. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the power transferred to heating in the cable when it carries a current of 80 A. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how replacing the cable with a superconducting one would affect this loss, and state one practical difficulty. **[2]**

**9.** An ntc thermistor has the following resistances.

| Temperature / °C | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R / kΩ | 28 | 18 | 12 | 8.0 | 5.5 | 3.9 |

**(a)** Describe and explain how the resistance changes with temperature. **[3]**

**(b)** The thermistor is connected alone to a 5.0 V supply and the current is 0.625 mA. Determine its temperature. **[2]**

**(c)** State, with a reason, whether the thermistor is a more sensitive sensor between 0 °C and 10 °C or between 40 °C and 50 °C. **[1]**

**10.** A metal tube of length 0.50 m has an outer radius of 4.0 mm and an inner radius of 3.0 mm. The resistivity of the metal is 2.8 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Calculate the resistance of the tube along its length. **[3]**

**11.** Wires P and Q are made of the same metal. Q is twice as long as P and has twice the diameter. They are connected in parallel across a supply.

**(a)** Calculate the ratio resistance of Q : resistance of P. **[2]**

**(b)** State the ratio current in Q : current in P. **[1]**

**12.** A metal wire and an ntc thermistor are each warmed from 20 °C to 60 °C.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of charge carriers and lattice vibrations, why their resistances change in opposite directions. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the thermistor could be used as part of a temperature sensor. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** ρ = RA/L, where R is resistance, A is cross-sectional area and L is length [1]; unit Ω m [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Ω m⁻¹ is a common wrong unit; work it out from Ω × m² / m.

**2.** A = π × (0.30 × 10⁻³)² = 2.83 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; R = ρL/A = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.5 / 2.83 × 10⁻⁷ = 9.7 Ω [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Halve the diameter before squaring.

**3.** A = π × (0.18 × 10⁻³)² = 1.02 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; ρ = RA/L = 12 × 1.02 × 10⁻⁷ / 0.80 = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Leaving the area in mm² gives an answer 10⁶ times too large.

**4.** Length × 1.2 and area ÷ 1.2, so R × 1.2² = 1.44 [1]; new R = 5.0 × 1.44 = 7.2 Ω [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* An answer of 6.0 Ω means the change in area was ignored.

**5.** As temperature rises, the positive ions in the lattice vibrate with greater amplitude [1]; free electrons collide with them more often, so the resistance increases [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The number of free electrons in a metal stays roughly the same; the extra collisions are the cause.

**6.** The temperature at and below which the material's resistivity is zero [1]; above it, the material has a normal, non-zero resistivity [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Very low resistance" is not accepted for a superconductor: it must be zero.

**7. (a)** Gradient = (4.95 − 1.15) / (1.00 − 0.20) [1] = 4.75 Ω m⁻¹ [1]

**(b)** A = π × (0.16 × 10⁻³)² = 8.04 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]; ρ = gradient × A = 4.75 × 8.04 × 10⁻⁸ = 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m [1]

**(c)** The intercept (0.20 Ω) is a constant extra resistance such as contact resistance at the clips or the leads [1]; it adds the same amount to every reading, so it shifts the line without changing its gradient [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Using one reading (such as 4.95 Ω at 1.00 m) would include the 0.20 Ω error; the gradient removes it.

**8. (a)** A = π × (5.0 × 10⁻³)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m² [1]; R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 2000 / 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.43 Ω [1]

**(b)** P = I²R = 80² × 0.433 [1] = 2.8 × 10³ W [1]

**(c)** Zero resistance means no energy is transferred to heating, so the loss becomes zero [1]; the cable must be kept at or below its critical temperature, which needs continuous cooling [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* 2.0 km must be 2000 m before it goes into the equation.

**9. (a)** The resistance decreases as the temperature rises [1], by smaller amounts at higher temperatures (a falling curve) [1]; heating releases more charge carriers in the semiconductor [1]

**(b)** R = V/I = 5.0 / 0.625 × 10⁻³ = 8.0 kΩ [1], so the temperature is 30 °C [1]

**(c)** Between 0 °C and 10 °C, because the resistance changes by 10 kΩ rather than 1.6 kΩ for the same temperature change [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Sensitivity means change in output per unit change in temperature; quote both changes.

**10.** A = π(4.0 × 10⁻³)² − π(3.0 × 10⁻³)² [1] = 2.20 × 10⁻⁵ m² [1]; R = 2.8 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.50 / 2.20 × 10⁻⁵ = 6.4 × 10⁻⁴ Ω [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Only the metal carries current, so subtract the area of the hole; subtracting the radii first and then squaring is wrong.

**11. (a)** R ∝ L/d², so R_Q / R_P = 2 / 2² [1] = 1 : 2 [1]

**(b)** Same pd, so current is inversely proportional to resistance: 2 : 1 [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* In parallel the pd is shared, so the lower-resistance wire takes the larger current.

**12. (a)** In both, lattice vibrations increase, which increases the rate of collisions [1]; in the metal the number of charge carriers stays roughly constant, so resistance rises [1]; in the thermistor many more charge carriers are released, which outweighs the extra collisions, so resistance falls [1]

**(b)** Place it in a potential divider with a fixed resistor so that the change in its resistance gives a change in output pd that indicates the temperature [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* An explanation of the thermistor that does not mention the number of charge carriers loses the key mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Diameter not halved, or area left in mm².
- Stretching questions that ignore the reduced area.
- Using a single reading instead of the gradient.
- Calling a superconductor's resistance "very small".
- Subtracting radii instead of areas for a tube.
- Thermistor explanations with no charge-carrier argument.
