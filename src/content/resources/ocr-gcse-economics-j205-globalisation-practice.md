## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a driving factor of globalisation?
A: Higher tariffs on imports.
B: Container shipping that lowers transport costs.
C: New quotas on imported goods.
D: Stricter controls on moving money between countries. **[1]**

**2.** Country G has GDP of $63 billion and a population of 21 million. What is its GDP per capita?
A: $300
B: $3,000
C: $30,000
D: $300,000 **[1]**

**3.** Define the term *multinational company*. **[2]**

**4.** Other than GDP per capita, state **two** measures of development named in the specification. **[2]**

**5.** In one year Country H's GDP grows by 6% and its population by 2%. Calculate the approximate percentage change in GDP per capita, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** way that information and communication technology has driven globalisation. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** The table shows data for two countries (illustrative).

| | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| GDP ($ billion) | 280 | 150 |
| Population (million) | 40 | 25 |
| Life expectancy (years) | 64 | 73 |
| Internet users per 100 people | 35 | 61 |
| Average years of schooling | 6.1 | 9.4 |

**(a)** Calculate GDP per capita for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the data, analyse which country is more developed. **[4]**

**8.** Merriton Apparel, a clothing firm in developed Country D, makes 500,000 garments a year at £9.00 each. It moves production to less developed Country L, where each garment costs £6.20 to make plus £0.80 to ship to Country D.

**(a)** Calculate the firm's annual cost saving. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **two** possible benefits of the move for workers in Country L. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain **one** possible cost of the move for workers in Country D. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how globalisation may affect environmental sustainability in a less developed country. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of globalisation for consumers in developed countries. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate whether globalisation benefits producers in less developed countries. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Lower transport costs make trade over long distances cheaper. A, C and D are barriers that slow globalisation. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. 63,000,000,000 ÷ 21,000,000 = $3,000. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Write both numbers out in full or in the same units (billions and millions). Most wrong answers come from mixing the two.

**3.** A firm that produces or operates in more than one country [1], usually with its headquarters in one country and factories, offices or outlets in others [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two, one mark each: life expectancy [1]; access to health care [1]; technology [1]; education [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 1.06 ÷ 1.02 = 1.0392 [1], so GDP per capita rises by about **3.9%** [1]. (Credit 4% from the approximation 6% − 2%, with working.) **[2]**

**6.** The internet lets firms communicate instantly with suppliers in other countries [1], so they can run global supply chains or offshore services such as call centres, increasing the links between economies [1]. (Credit selling online to customers abroad.) **[2]**

**7. (a)** Country X: 280,000,000,000 ÷ 40,000,000 = **$7,000** [1]. Country Y: 150,000,000,000 ÷ 25,000,000 = **$6,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)**
- Country X has the higher GDP per capita, $7,000 against $6,000, suggesting higher average income [1].
- But Country Y has higher life expectancy (73 against 64 years), suggesting better health care and diet [1].
- Country Y also leads on technology (61 against 35 internet users per 100) and education (9.4 against 6.1 years of schooling) [1].
- Conclusion: Country Y is more developed on most indicators; X's higher average income may be unequally shared or not spent on health and education [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from both columns. A conclusion based only on GDP per capita misses the point of having five indicators.

**8. (a)** New cost = 6.20 + 0.80 = £7.00 a garment [1]. Saving = (9.00 − 7.00) × 500,000 = **£1,000,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Benefit 1: new jobs are created [1], which may pay more than farming or informal work, raising household incomes [1]. Benefit 2: workers gain training and skills [1], which can raise their productivity and future earnings, or help them start their own firms [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** Workers in Country D's factory lose their jobs [1]; if their skills do not match other local jobs they may face long-term unemployment and lower income, or need to retrain [1]. **[2]**

**9.**
- Production often grows quickly in factories with weak environmental rules [1], so air and water pollution may rise, harming health and farming [1].
- Exporting goods long distances raises transport emissions [1].
- On the other hand, multinationals can bring cleaner technology and higher standards than local firms, which can improve sustainability [1]. **[4]**

**10.**
- Benefit: imports from lower-cost countries reduce prices [1], so consumers' real incomes rise and they can buy more [1].
- Benefit: wider choice of goods and services, including products from many countries and online services [1].
- Cost: some consumers are also workers in industries that move abroad, so they lose income [1].
- Cost: environmental effects of long supply chains, such as transport emissions, affect consumers as citizens, and local products may disappear [1].
- Cost: reliance on global supply chains means shortages or price rises if trade is disrupted [1].
- Evaluation: the gains are spread widely across almost all consumers, while the costs fall heavily on those whose jobs depend on declining industries [1].
- Judgement with a reason, for example: on balance consumers gain, but the net benefit is smaller for low-income households in areas of job loss unless retraining is available [1]. **[8]**

**11.**
- Benefit: access to large markets in developed countries, allowing more sales and economies of scale [1].
- Benefit: multinationals bring technology and management skills, which local suppliers can learn from [1].
- Benefit: foreign investment can fund roads, ports and power that local producers also use [1].
- Cost: local firms may be out-competed by multinationals with lower costs and stronger brands [1], so they lose market share or close [1].
- Cost: producers may come to depend on one export or one foreign buyer, making them vulnerable if world prices fall or the buyer leaves, which threatens economic sustainability [1].
- Evaluation: the effect depends on whether local firms become suppliers to multinationals or rivals to them, and on government policy such as infrastructure and support for small firms [1].
- A supported judgement, for example: producers linked into global supply chains benefit, while small producers competing directly with multinationals often lose [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Producers" in a less developed country include small farmers, local manufacturers and suppliers to multinationals. Distinguishing them is what turns description into evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing billions and millions in GDP per capita calculations.
- Ranking development on GDP per capita alone.
- Explaining a benefit with no consequence for the named group.
- Writing about the wrong type of country, or the wrong group.
- Leaving out environmental effects when sustainability is in the question.
- Giving "it depends" without naming the factor, or no judgement at all.
