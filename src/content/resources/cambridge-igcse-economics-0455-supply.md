## 2.4.1 Definition and diagram

**Supply** is the willingness and ability of producers to sell a product at a given price over a given period of time.

Willingness reflects whether selling at that price is worthwhile; ability reflects whether the firm has the resources to produce. A farm that would like to sell 100 tonnes of mangoes but whose trees produce only 60 can supply 60.

### From schedule to curve

A small brick works (invented) has this weekly supply schedule.

| Price per 1,000 bricks | Quantity supplied (thousand bricks per week) |
|---|---|
| 300 | 20 |
| 350 | 30 |
| 400 | 40 |
| 450 | 50 |
| 500 | 60 |

Drawing the curve:

- Price on the **vertical** axis; quantity supplied on the **horizontal** axis.
- Plot the pairs and join them. Label the line S.
- The curve slopes **upward** from left to right: a higher price is linked with a larger quantity supplied.

```
Price
500                *  S
450             *
400          *
350       *
300    *
       20  30  40  50  60   Quantity (thousand bricks per week)
```

### Why supply curves slope upward

- **Profit incentive.** A higher price, with costs unchanged, raises the profit on each unit, so producers want to sell more.
- **Covering rising costs.** Producing more often means paying overtime, running older machines or buying dearer materials. A higher price is needed to cover these extra costs.
- **New sellers.** At a higher price, firms with higher costs can now cover them and enter the market, and firms making other products may switch.

## 2.4.2 Movements along the supply curve

A change in the **price of the product itself** causes a movement along the existing supply curve.

- **Extension in supply:** a rise in price leads to a rise in quantity supplied (moving up the curve).
- **Contraction in supply:** a fall in price leads to a fall in quantity supplied (moving down the curve).

**Worked example.** The price of bricks rises from 350 to 450 per thousand.

1. Quantity supplied rises from 30 to 50 thousand a week, an extension of 20 thousand.
2. Percentage rise in quantity supplied = 20 ÷ 30 × 100 = **66.7%**.
3. Percentage rise in price = 100 ÷ 350 × 100 = **28.6%**.

The supply curve has not moved; the brick works has moved up along it.

## 2.4.3 Individual and market supply

**Individual supply** is one firm's supply. **Market supply** is the total supply of all firms in the market, found by **aggregation**: at each price, add the quantities supplied by every firm.

**Worked example.** Three bakeries supply bread rolls in a town (invented), per day.

| Price per roll | Bakery P | Bakery Q | Bakery R | Market supply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 100 | 50 | 0 | 150 |
| 0.30 | 160 | 90 | 40 | 290 |
| 0.40 | 220 | 130 | 90 | 440 |

Bakery R supplies nothing at 0.20 because that price does not cover its costs. As the price rises, R joins the market; this is the "new sellers" reason for the upward slope.

If Bakery Q closes, market supply at 0.30 falls from 290 to 200. A change in the **number of firms** shifts the market supply curve.

## 2.4.4 Conditions of supply: shifts

A change in anything **other than the product's own price** shifts the whole supply curve.

- **Increase in supply:** more is supplied at every price; the curve shifts right.
- **Decrease in supply:** less is supplied at every price; the curve shifts left.

| Condition | Change that increases supply (shift right) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Costs of production | Lower wages, raw material, energy or rent costs | Cheaper cement reduces the cost of each concrete block |
| Technology | Better methods or machinery raise output from the same inputs | Robotic milking raises milk output per farm |
| Indirect taxes | A cut or removal of a tax on the product | A tax on fuel is cut |
| Subsidies | A new or larger subsidy paid to producers | A subsidy on solar panels |
| Weather and natural events | Good harvests, no disease | A mild season raises the wheat crop |
| Number of firms | New firms enter the market | Three new coffee shops open |
| Prices of other products the firm could make | Another product becomes less profitable, so the firm switches to this one | Maize prices fall, so farmers grow more soya |

The opposite change decreases supply: higher costs, a new indirect tax, the removal of a subsidy, a drought, firms leaving, or another product becoming more profitable.

### Taxes and subsidies in numbers

An indirect tax is paid by the seller to the government on each unit sold, so it acts like an extra cost. A subsidy is a payment to the producer, so it acts like a cost reduction.

**Worked example: a tax of 50 per thousand bricks.** To be as willing as before to supply each quantity, the brick works now needs a price 50 higher.

| Quantity supplied | Price needed before the tax | Price needed after the tax |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 300 | 350 |
| 40 | 400 | 450 |
| 60 | 500 | 550 |

Read across at a price of 400: before the tax the firm supplied 40 thousand; after it supplies only 30 thousand (the quantity it used to supply at 350). The curve shifts **left** (or upward by 50): a **decrease in supply**.

**Worked example: a subsidy of 50 per thousand.** The price needed for each quantity falls by 50, so at 400 the firm now supplies 50 thousand. The curve shifts **right**: an **increase in supply**.

### Costs and technology

**Worked example: technology.** A new kiln lets the brick works produce 25% more at every price.

- At 400: 40 × 1.25 = **50** thousand.
- At 300: 20 × 1.25 = **25** thousand.

Supply has increased, so the curve shifts to the right.

### Movement or shift? A two-question test

1. Did the product's own price change? Then it is a movement: extension (price up) or contraction (price down).
2. Did a cost, tax, subsidy, technology, the weather or the number of firms change? Then it is a shift: increase (right) or decrease (left).

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a supply curve sloping downward.
- Saying a tax shifts supply right. A tax adds to costs, so it shifts supply left.
- Writing "supply increases" when the product's price rises. The correct term is "quantity supplied extends".
- Treating a change in demand as a cause of a supply shift. A rise in demand moves the market along the supply curve.
- Forgetting the time period in the definition.
- Mixing up a subsidy (paid to producers) with a tax (paid by producers).

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "the willingness and ability of producers to sell a product at a given price over a given period of time".
- **Draw:** label P and Q, S1 and S2, the arrow for the shift, and the quantities at the original price.
- **Calculate:** market supply (add across), percentage changes, and new quantities after a percentage shift.
- **Explain:** link the condition to cost or profit, then to the curve: "A rise in wages raises the cost of each unit, so producers are willing to sell less at every price, so supply decreases and the curve shifts left."
- **Identify:** sort a list of events into movements and shifts.
