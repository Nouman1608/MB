## Core recall

### Endocrine versus nervous

- Endocrine: hormones from ductless glands, carried in blood, slower, longer-lasting, widespread targets with receptors.
- Nervous: action potentials along neurones, very fast, short-lived, precise targets.
- Both: chemical messengers bind to specific receptors.
- Syllabus hormones: ADH (posterior pituitary → collecting ducts), glucagon (α cells → liver), insulin (β cells → muscle and liver).

### Neurones

| Neurone | Carries impulses | Cell body |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory | Receptor → CNS | Just outside the spinal cord, part-way along the fibre |
| Intermediate (relay) | Sensory → motor, within CNS | In CNS |
| Motor | CNS → effector | In CNS, with many dendrites and a long axon |

### Receptors

- Convert stimulus energy into a **receptor potential**.
- Threshold reached → action potentials in sensory neurone.
- Stronger stimulus → higher **frequency** of action potentials, not bigger ones.

### Taste bud (salt)

Na⁺ in through channels → depolarisation → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ in → neurotransmitter released → sensory neurone depolarised → action potential.

### Membrane potentials

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| Resting (about −70 mV) | Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in); membrane more permeable to K⁺; negative proteins inside |
| Depolarisation | Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ diffuses in; inside reaches about +30 mV |
| Repolarisation | Na⁺ channels close; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ diffuses out |
| Hyperpolarisation | K⁺ outflow overshoots below resting |
| Restoration | K⁺ channels close; pump restores ion distribution |

### Refractory period

- Na⁺ channels cannot reopen straight away.
- Keeps impulses separate, makes them one-way, sets the maximum frequency.

### Saltatory conduction

Myelin (Schwann cells) insulates → ion flow only at nodes of Ranvier → local circuits jump node to node → much faster conduction.

### Cholinergic synapse in six steps

1. Action potential arrives at presynaptic knob.
2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
3. Vesicles fuse with membrane; ACh released by exocytosis.
4. ACh diffuses across cleft; binds to receptors on postsynaptic membrane.
5. Na⁺ channels open; postsynaptic membrane depolarised.
6. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses ACh; choline recycled into the knob.

### Muscle contraction

- Neuromuscular junction → sarcolemma depolarised → T-tubules → sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺.
- Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → binding sites on actin exposed.
- Myosin heads bind (cross-bridges) → power stroke → actin pulled towards M line.
- ATP binds → head detaches → ATP hydrolysed → head re-cocked.
- Relaxation: Ca²⁺ actively pumped back into sarcoplasmic reticulum.

### Sarcomere bands

| Band | Contains | During contraction |
|---|---|---|
| A band | Full length of myosin (with overlapping actin) | Stays the same |
| I band | Actin only | Shortens |
| H zone | Myosin only | Shortens (may disappear) |
| Sarcomere (Z to Z) | Whole unit | Shortens |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Threshold | Potential that must be reached for an action potential to start |
| Voltage-gated channel | Channel protein that opens or closes when membrane potential changes |
| Node of Ranvier | Gap in the myelin sheath where depolarisation occurs |
| Acetylcholinesterase | Enzyme in the synaptic cleft that hydrolyses ACh |
| T-tubule | Infolding of the sarcolemma carrying depolarisation into the fibre |
| Sarcoplasmic reticulum | Membrane store of Ca²⁺ in a muscle fibre |
| Troponin / tropomyosin | Proteins on actin that control access to myosin-binding sites |
| Cross-bridge | Link formed when a myosin head binds to actin |

## Exam traps

- Depolarisation is Na⁺ **diffusing in**, not the pump.
- Myelin stops ion movement; it does not "conduct" the impulse.
- Ca²⁺ enters the **presynaptic** knob at a synapse.
- ATP is needed for **detachment** of myosin heads, not attachment.
- Filaments do not shorten; they slide.
- "Compare" needs at least one similarity.
- Convert ms to s and mm to m before calculating speed.
- Write potentials with signs: "from −70 mV to +30 mV".

## Self-test

1. Give two differences between nervous and endocrine coordination.
2. Where is the cell body of a motor neurone?
3. What is a receptor potential?
4. Which ions enter a taste bud chemoreceptor first when it detects salt?
5. Give two factors that maintain the resting potential.
6. Which channels open during repolarisation?
7. Give two consequences of the refractory period.
8. Why is conduction faster in myelinated neurones?
9. What triggers the fusion of vesicles with the presynaptic membrane?
10. Name the enzyme that removes ACh from the cleft.
11. What does Ca²⁺ bind to in a muscle fibre?
12. Which bands shorten when a sarcomere contracts?

### Answers

1. Any two: nervous is faster; nervous effects are shorter; nervous targets are precise, hormones reach all cells with receptors; nervous uses action potentials along neurones, endocrine uses hormones in the blood.
2. In the central nervous system (spinal cord or brain).
3. A change in membrane potential of a receptor cell caused by a stimulus.
4. Sodium ions.
5. Any two: sodium–potassium pumps; greater permeability to K⁺ than Na⁺; large negative ions inside.
6. Voltage-gated potassium ion channels.
7. Any two: separate impulses; one-way transmission; a maximum frequency of impulses.
8. Myelin insulates the axon, so depolarisation happens only at nodes of Ranvier and the impulse jumps between them.
9. Calcium ions entering the presynaptic knob.
10. Acetylcholinesterase.
11. Troponin.
12. The I band and the H zone (and the sarcomere as a whole).
