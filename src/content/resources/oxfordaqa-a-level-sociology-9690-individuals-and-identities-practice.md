## Questions

**1.** Define what is meant by a **subculture**. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the difference between an **individual** and a **collective** identity. **[2]**

**3.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which stereotypes may affect individuals.
**[4]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which nationality may form part of social
identity. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **two** ways in which C. Wright Mills's idea of the sociological imagination helps
sociologists understand society. **[8]**

**6.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that globalisation and consumption
are now the most important influences on social identity. **[20]**

### Extra practice

**7.** Explain **one** way in which ethnic subcultures are maintained in multicultural societies.
**[4]**

**8.** Explain how age and generation/cohort can shape identity. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which gender identities may be changing. **[8]**

## Mark schemes

**1. [2]** A group within a wider society whose members share norms, values or practices that
differ in some ways from the mainstream culture, for example an ethnic, religious or youth subculture
(2). Partial: "a smaller group in society" (1).

**2. [2]** An individual identity is a person's sense of their unique self — personality, choices,
biography (1); a collective identity is a sense of belonging to and identifying with a group such as
a nation, faith, ethnicity or class (1).

**3. [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: stereotypes can lead to prejudice and discrimination in jobs or
education; people may internalise stereotypes, affecting self-esteem; stereotypes can create a
self-fulfilling prophecy; they can lead to othering and stigmatised identities (link to Goffman's
*Stigma*, 1963).

**4. [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: national symbols, rituals and holidays create shared belonging;
language and education transmit national history and values; citizenship gives legal rights and
status; national identity may be strengthened by sport or conflict; diaspora members may keep a
national identity abroad.

**5. [8]** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify, explain, develop, example):
- **Linking personal troubles to public issues** — Mills (1959) argued that problems that feel
  private, such as one person's unemployment or divorce, become public issues when they affect many
  people and reflect social structures such as the economy or changing family norms.
- **Connecting biography and history** — the sociological imagination places individuals within
  historical change, showing how events such as migration or industrialisation shape life chances.
- **Challenging common sense** — it encourages questioning taken-for-granted explanations that blame
  individuals.

**6. [20]** Levels: 17–20 detailed, analytical, a range of evidence and examples from a named
society, sustained evaluation, justified conclusion; 13–16 good knowledge, some evaluation; 9–12
reasonable knowledge, limited evaluation; 5–8 basic; 1–4 fragments.

*For the view:*
- Global media, brands and platforms supply identities across borders; young people in many
  societies share global styles.
- Consumption: people express identity through what they buy and display; postmodernists argue
  identity is chosen.
- Online identities let people construct multiple selves; Zuboff (2019) shows platforms shaping
  behaviour through data.
- Hybrid ethnic identities and diasporas reflect global movement.

*Against the view:*
- Identities based on class, gender, ethnicity, religion, age and nationality remain powerful and are
  learned through socialisation from birth.
- Ability to consume depends on income, so class limits choice.
- Globalisation can strengthen national or religious identities in reaction (cultural defence).
- Fanon (1952) shows how race and power shape identity in ways individuals cannot simply choose.
- Ascribed identities such as age and sex are imposed by others' reactions.

*Application:* reward specific, accurate examples from a named society.

*Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement — for example that globalisation and consumption widen the
range of identities but operate within structural limits.

**7. [4]** For example: religious institutions, community organisations and family socialisation
pass on language, customs and values (2); festivals, media in community languages and transnational
links with the homeland maintain belonging, and experiences of discrimination can strengthen group
identity (2).

**8. [4]** Age categories such as childhood, youth and old age carry social expectations and status
(2); a cohort or generation shares formative experiences — for example growing up with the internet
— that shape tastes, values and identity (2).

**9. [8]** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify, explain, develop, example):
- **Changing femininities** — rising female participation in higher education and paid work in many
  societies has widened the range of acceptable female identities beyond the domestic role, though
  expectations about caring and appearance often remain.
- **Changing masculinities** — the decline of manual industry and growth of service work have
  weakened the identity of the male breadwinner; more men take an active role in childcare, while
  some respond by reasserting traditional masculinity.
- **Online and media influences** — social media and global media offer varied gender
  representations and communities, allowing people to explore gender identities, although
  stereotypes are also reinforced online.
- **Law and policy** — equality legislation and parental-leave policies in some societies change
  expectations of men's and women's roles.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Mills named in the specification?** Yes — "the sociological imagination (Mills)" is part of
3.1.2.1, and Mills is among the suggested sociologists for section 3.1.2.

**Which question needs examples from my own society?** Practise using them in the 20-mark question;
the specification requires them in selected extended responses.

**How do I evaluate in question 6?** Weigh global and consumer influences against structural bases
of identity, and reach a justified conclusion.
