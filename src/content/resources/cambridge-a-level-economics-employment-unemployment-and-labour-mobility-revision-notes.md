## Definitions to recall

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Full employment (9.3.1) | All those prepared and able to work at the prevailing wage can obtain a job; some frictional and structural unemployment remains |
| Equilibrium unemployment (9.3.2) | Unemployment that exists when the labour market clears at the equilibrium wage |
| Disequilibrium unemployment (9.3.2) | Labour supplied exceeds labour demanded at the going wage (real-wage or demand-deficient) |
| Hysteresis (9.3.2) | Unemployment that persists after its cause ends, because the equilibrium rate itself has risen |
| Voluntary unemployment (9.3.3) | Choosing not to accept work at the going wage |
| Involuntary unemployment (9.3.3) | Would accept work at the going wage and can do it, but no vacancy exists |
| Natural rate (9.3.4) | Unemployment remaining once the labour market has cleared: frictional, structural and voluntary |
| Geographical mobility (9.3.6) | Ability and willingness to move area for work |
| Occupational mobility (9.3.6) | Ability and willingness to change type of job |

## Formulas and quick calculations

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Unemployment rate (%) | Number unemployed divided by labour force, times 100 |
| Participation rate (%) | Labour force divided by working-age population, times 100 |
| Employment rate | Employed ÷ working-age population × 100 |
| Natural unemployment (number) | Natural rate × labour force |
| Cyclical unemployment | Actual unemployment − natural unemployment |
| Replacement rate | Out-of-work benefit ÷ net pay in work × 100 |
| Payback period (moving or retraining) | One-off cost ÷ net annual gain |
| Jobs from extra output | Rise in national income ÷ output per worker |
| Cost per additional job | Cost per participant ÷ share who gain a job they would not otherwise have had |

**Worked reminder.** Country C has a labour force of 40.0 million, an actual rate of 8.0% and a natural rate of 5.5%.

```
Actual unemployed  = 8.0% × 40.0m = 3.2 million
Natural            = 5.5% × 40.0m = 2.2 million
Cyclical           = 3.2 − 2.2    = 1.0 million (2.5 percentage points)
```

If supply-side reform cuts the natural rate to 4.5%, natural unemployment falls to 1.8 million.

## Method boxes

**Labour market diagram (disequilibrium).**
1. Label the vertical axis as the wage and the horizontal axis as labour quantity. Draw a downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve.
2. Mark the equilibrium wage and quantity.
3. Draw the wage floor above equilibrium. Read employment from the demand curve and the labour supplied from the supply curve.
4. The horizontal gap is the number unemployed. The part not willing to work at the lower equilibrium wage was never involuntarily unemployed.

*Example.* Demand L = 500 − 10w, supply L = 10w − 100, in thousands of workers. Equilibrium is w = 30, L = 200. At a floor of 36, demand is 140 and supply is 260, so 120 thousand are unemployed.

**Natural rate and AD (chain of reasoning).**
1. Government raises AD to push unemployment below the natural rate.
2. Output moves beyond full employment, an inflationary gap opens, and labour is scarce.
3. Firms raise wages and prices, so the gain in jobs is only temporary.
4. Lasting reduction needs a supply-side shift that lowers the natural rate.

**Hysteresis (chain).** Recession → long spells of joblessness → skills decay, contacts are lost, employers screen out long gaps → equilibrium rate rises → unemployment stays high even when demand recovers.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Key point |
|---|---|
| Full employment vs zero unemployment | Full employment still has natural unemployment |
| Equilibrium vs disequilibrium | Equilibrium is on the supply and demand crossing; disequilibrium is above it or from a demand fall |
| Voluntary vs involuntary | Willing to work at the going wage or not |
| Cyclical vs natural | Cyclical responds to AD; natural responds to supply-side factors |
| Geographical vs occupational | Moving place vs changing job type |
| Demand-side vs supply-side policy | AD for cyclical; skills, incentives and mobility for structural and frictional |

## Determinants of the natural rate

- **Raise it:** generous benefits, strong unions and minimum wages, skills mismatch, low mobility, poor information, hysteresis.
- **Lower it:** flexible labour markets, effective training, good job-matching, housing that allows moves.

## Mobility factors

- **Geographical:** housing costs and availability, moving costs, family and community ties, information about vacancies, transport, immigration rules and language.
- **Occupational:** training cost and time, transferable skills, licences and entry requirements, age and employer attitudes, knowledge of other careers.
- **Worked reminder.** A worker gains 3,600 cu a year in pay, pays 1,800 cu more in rent and faces a one-off cost of 7,200 cu. Net gain = 3,600 − 1,800 = 1,800 cu. Payback = 7,200 ÷ 1,800 = 4.0 years.

## Patterns and trends: what to look for

- The cycle: unemployment rises in recessions and falls in booms, often with a lag.
- Structural change: employment shifts between sectors, so some regions and age groups lose out.
- Group differences: youth, the low-skilled, long-term unemployed and particular regions.
- Participation: a falling rate may reflect workers leaving the labour force.

**Worked reminder.** A country has a working-age population of 30.0 million, 17.1 million employed and 0.9 million unemployed. Labour force = 18.0 million; unemployment rate = 0.9 ÷ 18.0 = 5.0%; participation = 18.0 ÷ 30.0 = 60.0%. If 0.3 million of the unemployed stop looking, unemployed = 0.6 million, labour force = 17.7 million and the rate = 0.6 ÷ 17.7 = 3.4%, yet nobody has found a job.

## Policy summary

| Policy | Best against | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal or monetary expansion | Cyclical | Inflation, deficit, lags |
| Training, education | Structural | Slow, costly |
| Lower or time-limited benefits | Voluntary | Poverty, weaker demand |
| Lower employer labour taxes, wage subsidies | Structural | Cost, deadweight |
| Job-matching, information | Frictional | Small scale |
| Relocation help, regional policy | Geographical | Cost, can drain the region |
| Labour market flexibility | Real-wage | Lower pay, insecurity |

**Worked reminder.** Government spending rises by 0.6 billion cu and the multiplier is 2.5, so national income rises by 0.6 × 2.5 = 1.5 billion cu. With output per worker of 30,000 cu, jobs created = 1.5bn ÷ 30,000 = 50,000. If cyclical unemployment is 120,000, 70,000 remain, and any structural unemployment is untouched.

**Evaluation checklist:** type of unemployment, size of the multiplier, time lags, cost, side effects (inflation, inequality, deficit), whether hysteresis has set in, and the short-run versus long-run view.

## Quick self-test

1. State the formula for the unemployment rate and calculate it for 0.8 million unemployed and 11.2 million employed.
2. A labour force of 25.0 million has a natural rate of 4.0% and an actual rate of 7.0%. How many are cyclically unemployed?
3. In the demand L = 500 − 10w and supply L = 10w − 100 market, find unemployment at a wage floor of 36.
4. Define hysteresis in one sentence.
5. Replacement rate: benefit 200 cu, net pay 10 cu × 35 hours at 10% tax. Calculate it.
6. Give two determinants that raise the natural rate.
7. Employment is 250 thousand in year 1 and 240 thousand in year 3. Calculate the year 3 index (year 1 = 100).
8. Why can an expansion of AD not permanently reduce unemployment below the natural rate?
9. Name two factors reducing occupational mobility.
10. A policy costs 3,000 cu per participant, and 50% gain jobs they would not otherwise have found. Find the cost per additional job.

### Answers

1. Unemployed ÷ (employed + unemployed) × 100 = 0.8 ÷ 12.0 × 100 = 6.7%.
2. (7.0% − 4.0%) × 25.0m = 0.75 million.
3. Demand = 500 − 360 = 140; supply = 360 − 100 = 260; unemployment = 120 thousand.
4. Unemployment that persists after the cause has ended, because the equilibrium rate has risen.
5. Net pay = 10 × 35 × 0.90 = 315 cu; 200 ÷ 315 × 100 = 63.5%.
6. Any two of: generous benefits, strong unions or minimum wages, skills mismatch, poor information, hysteresis.
7. 240 ÷ 250 × 100 = 96.0.
8. Output beyond potential creates an inflationary gap; wages and prices rise and the gain reverses. Only supply-side change lowers the natural rate.
9. Any two of: retraining cost, lack of transferable skills, licences and entry rules, age, employer prejudice.
10. 3,000 ÷ 0.50 = 6,000 cu.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining full employment as no unemployment.
- Drawing a wage floor below the equilibrium wage, which has no effect.
- Dividing the number out of work by the entire population instead of the labour force.
- Saying benefits cause unemployment without separating voluntary from involuntary.
- Applying demand-side policy to structural unemployment without comment.
- Listing policies with no evaluation of time lags, cost or side effects.
- Treating a fall in the rate as more jobs when people have left the labour force.
- Describing mobility factors without saying whether they affect geographical or occupational mobility.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can raising aggregate demand not permanently reduce unemployment below the natural rate?**
Pushing unemployment below the natural rate opens an inflationary gap and makes labour scarce, so firms raise wages and prices and the gain in jobs is only temporary. A lasting reduction needs a supply-side shift that lowers the natural rate.

**What raises the natural rate of unemployment?**
Generous benefits, strong unions and minimum wages, skills mismatch and low labour mobility can raise the natural rate. Flexible labour markets, effective training, good job-matching and housing that allows workers to move can lower it.

**What is the difference between geographical and occupational mobility of labour?**
Geographical mobility is affected by housing costs and availability, moving costs, family ties and information. Occupational mobility is affected by training cost and time, transferable skills, licences and entry requirements.

**Does a fall in the unemployment rate always mean more jobs?**
No. A falling rate may reflect people leaving the labour force, so check employment and the participation rate before concluding that more jobs have been created.
