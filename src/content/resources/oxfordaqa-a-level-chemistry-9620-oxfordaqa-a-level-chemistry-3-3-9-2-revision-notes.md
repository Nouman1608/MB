It is International A2 content, examined in Unit 4 and able to reappear in Unit 5, with Required practical 10 attached. For explanations and worked examples read the [acylation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-9-2/); then try the [acylation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-9-2-practice/).

## The subtopic in five lines

1. Acyl chlorides (–COCl), acid anhydrides (–CO–O–CO–) and amides (–CONH₂ or –CONHR) all contain the acyl group R–CO–.
2. Acyl chlorides and anhydrides react with water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines by nucleophilic addition–elimination.
3. Products: water gives a carboxylic acid, an alcohol an ester, ammonia a primary amide, a primary amine an N-substituted amide.
4. Acyl chlorides give HCl as the by-product; anhydrides give a carboxylic acid.
5. Industry makes aspirin with ethanoic anhydride rather than ethanoyl chloride.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Acyl group | R–CO–, the part transferred in acylation |
| Acylation | Replacing an H on O or N with an acyl group |
| Acyl chloride | R–COCl; name ends -oyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | (RCO)₂O; name ends -oic anhydride |
| Primary amide | R–CONH₂; name ends -amide |
| N-substituted amide | R–CONHR′; the N- prefix shows R′ is on nitrogen |
| Nucleophile | Electron pair donor that attacks a δ+ carbon |
| Addition–elimination | Nucleophile adds to C=O, then C=O re-forms and a small group (Cl⁻ or RCOO⁻) leaves |

## Products table

| Nucleophile | Product with RCOCl | By-product with RCOCl | By-product with (RCO)₂O |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂O | RCOOH | HCl | RCOOH (two RCOOH in total) |
| R′OH | RCOOR′ | HCl | RCOOH |
| NH₃ (two needed) | RCONH₂ | NH₄Cl | RCOO⁻NH₄⁺ |
| R′NH₂ (two needed) | RCONHR′ | R′NH₃⁺Cl⁻ | RCOO⁻ R′NH₃⁺ |

Worked equations to copy the pattern from:

- `CH₃CH₂COCl + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + HCl` (propyl propanoate)
- `(CH₃CH₂CO)₂O + CH₃OH → CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + CH₃CH₂COOH` (methyl propanoate)
- `CH₃CH₂CH₂COCl + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CONHCH₃ + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻` (N-methylbutanamide)

## Working backwards from a product

1. Find the bond that formed: C(=O)–O in an ester or acid, C(=O)–N in an amide.
2. The C=O side came from the acyl chloride or anhydride.
3. The O or N side came from the water, alcohol, ammonia or amine.
4. Example: CH₃CH₂COOCH(CH₃)₂ comes from propanoyl chloride (or propanoic anhydride) and propan-2-ol.
5. Example: C₆H₅CONHCH₂CH₃ comes from benzoyl chloride and two molecules of ethylamine.

## Mechanism checklist (acyl chlorides only)

1. Lone pair on O (water, alcohol) or N (ammonia, amine) → δ+ carbonyl carbon.
2. C=O π bond pair → O, giving O⁻.
3. Tetrahedral intermediate: O⁻ and O⁺ (or N⁺) both shown.
4. Lone pair on O⁻ → re-forms C=O; C–Cl bond pair → Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻.
5. H⁺ removed from O⁺ or N⁺ (by Cl⁻, solvent, or a second ammonia or amine molecule).

## Why acyl chlorides are so reactive

- Carbonyl carbon bonded to O and Cl, both electronegative, so it is strongly δ+.
- Cl⁻ is easily lost in the elimination step.
- Observation: steamy fumes (HCl) in moist air; violent reaction with water.
- Anhydrides react the same way but more slowly, and need warming.

## Making an ester: three routes

| Route | Speed | Extent | Catalyst needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid + alcohol | Slow | Equilibrium | Yes, conc. H₂SO₄ |
| Anhydride + alcohol | Moderate, warm | Complete | No |
| Acyl chloride + alcohol | Fast, room temperature | Complete | No |

## Aspirin

`HOC₆H₄COOH + (CH₃CO)₂O → CH₃COOC₆H₄COOH + CH₃COOH`

Advantages of ethanoic anhydride over ethanoyl chloride:

- cheaper
- less corrosive
- less vigorous, so easier to control
- less readily hydrolysed by water in the air
- by-product is ethanoic acid, not corrosive HCl gas

Atom economy: anhydride route 75.0%, acyl chloride route 83.1%. The anhydride is chosen despite the lower figure.

## Required practical 10 in brief

- Keep acylating agents away from water until the reaction is complete.
- Recrystallise: minimum hot solvent, filter hot, cool, filter under reduced pressure, wash with cold solvent, dry.
- Test purity: sharp melting point at the data-book value means pure; lower and broader means impure.

## Exam traps

- "Nucleophilic substitution": wrong name. Use nucleophilic addition–elimination.
- An arrow from H₂O or NH₃ "the molecule": start it at the lone pair.
- No charges on the intermediate, or no H⁺ lost at the end.
- HCl from an anhydride: wrong; the by-product is a carboxylic acid.
- One amine per acyl chloride: two are needed.
- Missing "N-" in N-substituted amide names.
- Claiming anhydrides have the better atom economy for aspirin.
- Giving a mechanism for an anhydride when the question asks for an acyl chloride, or vice versa.

## Self-test

Answer without looking, then check below.

1. Name (CH₃)₂CHCOCl.
2. Name CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃.
3. Give the structural formula of butanoic anhydride.
4. Which nucleophile turns an acyl chloride into a primary amide?
5. Write the equation for ethanoyl chloride with ethanol.
6. What is the by-product when an acid anhydride reacts with an alcohol?
7. Why are two molecules of ammonia needed per acyl chloride?
8. Name the mechanism by which acyl chlorides react with water.
9. Where does the first curly arrow start when an amine attacks an acyl chloride?
10. What is observed when ethanoyl chloride is exposed to moist air?
11. Give two reasons why ethanoic anhydride is used to make aspirin industrially.
12. How does melting point show that a recrystallised solid is pure?

### Answers

1. 2-Methylpropanoyl chloride.
2. N-Ethylpropanamide.
3. (CH₃CH₂CH₂CO)₂O, that is CH₃CH₂CH₂CO–O–COCH₂CH₂CH₃.
4. Ammonia.
5. CH₃COCl + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + HCl.
6. A carboxylic acid.
7. One acts as the nucleophile; the second reacts with the HCl formed (as a base), giving NH₄Cl.
8. Nucleophilic addition–elimination.
9. At the lone pair on the nitrogen atom of the amine.
10. Steamy (misty) fumes of hydrogen chloride.
11. Any two: cheaper; less corrosive; less vigorous; less readily hydrolysed; does not produce HCl gas.
12. It melts sharply (over a narrow range) at the data-book value.
