Section 3.1, *Perspectives on the role of the family*, opens Paper 2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It asks what the family does, for whom, and whether its role has changed. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Power, control and resistance* and *Socialisation, culture and identity*.

This guide adds the named theorists, studies and evaluation that the [Paper 2 family guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-the-family/) only outlines. Revise with the [3.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-role-of-family-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-role-of-family-practice/).

## What the syllabus lists

- Functionalist accounts of how the family benefits its members and society and how the functions of families have changed over time, including the "loss of functions" debate.
- Marxist accounts of how the family benefits capitalism, including ideological control, reproduction of labour and consumption.
- Feminist responses to functionalist and Marxist accounts of the role of the family.

Paper 2 includes a question asking you to evaluate a sociological theory by explaining its strengths and limitations, so learn each perspective together with its criticisms.

## Functionalist accounts

Functionalists see society as a system of connected institutions, each meeting needs that keep the whole stable. The family is judged by its **functions**.

### George Peter Murdock: four universal functions

Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949), a comparison of 250 societies, argued that the nuclear family exists in every society because it performs four functions:

| Function | For society | For members |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual | Stable relationships reduce conflict over sexual partners | A regular, approved partner |
| Reproductive | New members replace those who die | Children |
| Economic | Pooling of resources and division of labour | Food, shelter, financial support |
| Educational | Socialisation into shared norms and values | Learning the culture |

*Criticisms*: other institutions can perform these functions; some societies organise child-rearing through wider kin groups rather than nuclear families, so the nuclear family is not universal; Murdock presents a harmonious picture that ignores conflict.

### Talcott Parsons: two irreducible functions

Parsons argued that, in modern industrial societies, the family has become specialised in two "basic and irreducible" functions:

- **Primary socialisation** of children, so that society's values become part of their personality.
- **Stabilisation of adult personalities**, providing emotional security that relieves the stress of work (sometimes called the "warm bath" idea).

Parsons also described a division between the husband's **instrumental** role (breadwinner) and the wife's **expressive** role (care and emotional support), which he linked to biological differences.

**Functional fit.** Parsons argued that the **isolated nuclear family** fits industrial society, because a small family unit can move where work is (geographical mobility) and members' status is achieved at work, not ascribed by kin (social mobility). The extended family fitted pre-industrial, farming society.

### The loss of functions debate

| Position | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Loss of functions** (Parsons) | As society industrialised, specialist institutions took over functions: schools educate, the health service and welfare state care for the sick and poor, factories replaced the family as the unit of production. The family lost functions but became better at its remaining core functions. |
| **Functions still important** (Ronald Fletcher, *The Family and Marriage in Britain*, 1966) | The family has not lost functions. The state supports families rather than replacing them, and parents now take more responsibility for children's health, education and welfare than before. |

### Evaluating the functionalist account

- **Historical evidence**: Peter Laslett's research on English parish records found that households before industrialisation were mostly small and nuclear, challenging the idea that industrialisation created the nuclear family. Michael Anderson's study of Preston using the 1851 census (published 1971) found that extended households helped working-class families cope with early industrial life.
- **Persistence of kin networks**: Michael Young and Peter Willmott's *Family and Kinship in East London* (1957) found close extended family ties among working-class families in Bethnal Green.
- **Rosy picture**: functionalists ignore domestic violence, abuse and inequality.
- **Gender**: Parsons' instrumental and expressive roles describe one type of family and treat women's domestic role as natural.
- **Diversity**: functionalists focus on the nuclear family and neglect lone-parent, reconstituted and other forms.

## Marxist accounts

Marxists argue that the family benefits **capitalism** and the ruling class, not society as a whole.

**Inheritance of property.** Friedrich Engels' *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884) argued that the monogamous family developed with private property: men needed certainty about who their children were so that wealth passed to legitimate heirs. The family is therefore linked to class inequality from the start.

**Ideological control.** The family socialises children to accept hierarchy and authority, first from parents and later from employers, so they see inequality as normal. Family life also absorbs workers' frustrations, making revolution less likely.

**Reproduction of labour.** The family produces the next generation of workers and maintains today's workers (feeding, housing and caring for them) at no direct cost to employers.

**Consumption.** The family is a unit of consumption: it buys houses, cars, appliances and children's goods, creating profit. Advertisers target families, and children put pressure on parents to buy products. Eli Zaretsky (*Capitalism, the Family and Personal Life*, 1976) argued that the family appears as a private refuge from the alienation of work but is in reality shaped by capitalism and cannot meet all the emotional needs placed on it.

### Evaluating the Marxist account

- It ignores family diversity and assumes one family type serves capitalism.
- Functionalists reply that the family meets real needs of its members, not just of capitalism.
- Feminists argue that Marxists see class inequality but neglect gender inequality within the family.
- It can treat family members as passive, ignoring how people choose and shape their relationships.

## Feminist responses

Feminists agree with Marxists that the family is a site of inequality, but argue that the main beneficiaries are men, not only capitalism.

**Responses to functionalism.**
- Parsons' expressive role turns women's unpaid domestic work into something natural rather than socially constructed.
- Ann Oakley (*Housewife*, 1974) argued that the housewife role is a product of industrialisation and ideology, not biology.
- The "warm bath" stabilises men's personalities, often at the expense of women's own well-being.

**Responses to Marxism.**
- **Marxist feminists** extend the Marxist case. Margaret Benston (1969) argued that women's unpaid domestic labour reproduces labour power cheaply for capitalism. Fran Ansley (1972) argued that wives absorb the anger and frustration their husbands bring home from exploitative work, protecting capitalism from it.
- **Radical feminists** argue that the family is primarily patriarchal. Christine Delphy and Diana Leonard (*Familiar Exploitation*, 1992) saw the family as an economic system in which men benefit from women's unpaid work.
- **Liberal feminists** accept that the family has been unequal but argue that change through law, education and attitudes is reducing inequality; for example, equal pay legislation in many countries has strengthened women's position.

**Evaluating feminist responses.** Feminists show that the family's benefits are not shared equally, but critics argue they underestimate women's choice and agency and the extent of change. Feminists also differ: radical feminists see little progress, liberal feminists see a lot.

## Worked example: evaluating a theory

*Question (practice): Evaluate the functionalist theory of the family.*

> **Strength:** functionalism explains why some form of family is found in almost every society. Murdock's four functions show the family meeting needs, such as reproduction and socialisation, that every society has, and Parsons' idea of primary socialisation fits evidence that early family life shapes values.
>
> **Limitation:** functionalists present an idealised picture. Feminists such as Oakley argue that the expressive role naturalises women's unpaid labour, and the "dark side" of family life, including violence and abuse, shows the family can harm members rather than stabilise them.
>
> **Limitation:** historical evidence challenges functional fit. Laslett found nuclear households common before industrialisation, and Anderson found extended households useful during it.

Why it works: each point is identified, explained with named evidence and linked back to the theory's claims.

## Common mistakes

- Describing Murdock and Parsons without the loss of functions debate, which the syllabus names.
- Treating "Marxist" and "feminist" as the same; Marxist feminism is one strand among several.
- Using criticisms without saying which perspective makes them.
- Ignoring how functions have changed over time.
- Writing about "the family" as if only nuclear families exist.
