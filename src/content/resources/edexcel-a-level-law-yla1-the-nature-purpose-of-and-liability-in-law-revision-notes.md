## Rules and law (1.1.1 to 1.1.3)

A legal rule has **certainty** (a recognised source), **enforcement** (courts, police, prosecutors) and a **state sanction**. A school rule or rule of etiquette may have the first two in a weak form but never the third.

**Applying the test:** a football league's rule against foul play is written down and enforced by referees, but the sanction (a card, a ban) comes from the league, not the state. If the same tackle is so violent that it amounts to a criminal assault, the state's criminal law now applies, with state enforcement and sanction. The same act can therefore break a non-legal rule and a legal rule at once.

## Legal personality and liability (1.1.4 to 1.1.7)

- **Rights, duties, privileges, liabilities**: what the law allows, requires, protects and penalises.
- **Natural person** = a human being. **Legal person** = a company or other body with its own legal identity (*Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897]).

| | Civil | Criminal |
|---|---|---|
| Parties | Claimant v defendant | Prosecution (R) v defendant |
| Burden | Claimant | Prosecution (*Woolmington v DPP* [1935]) |
| Standard | Balance of probabilities | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| Result | Liable | Guilty |
| Outcome | Remedy: damages, injunction | Sanction: custody, community order, fine, discharge |

**Rights, duties, privileges and liabilities in one example:** a homeowner has a **right** to exclude others from their land; visitors are under a **duty** not to trespass; a police officer with a warrant has a **privilege** to enter despite the homeowner's wishes; a person who enters without permission incurs **liability** in the tort of trespass.

**Why legal personality matters:** because a company is a separate legal person (*Salomon*), it can own property, make contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and its shareholders' liability is usually limited to what they invested. Companies can also be criminally liable, for example under the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007.

**One event, two cases:** a driver who injures a cyclist can be prosecuted for a driving offence and sued in negligence. Different parties, courts and standards, so an acquittal does not block the civil claim.

**Links between the systems:** a criminal court can make a **compensation order** requiring the offender to pay the victim, so a criminal case can also give the victim a remedy. A victim can also bring a **private prosecution**, although these are rare.

## Theories of punishment (1.1.8)

Learn all **seven**, each with a sentence link:

1. **Revenge**: society hitting back; behind calls for long sentences.
2. **Retribution**: just deserts, proportionate; sentences scaled to seriousness.
3. **Denunciation**: public condemnation; exemplary sentences.
4. **Deterrence**: individual and general; custody, suspended sentences.
5. **Rehabilitation**: reform; community orders with treatment or supervision.
6. **Reparation**: amends; compensation orders, unpaid work.
7. **Restoration**: repair the offender–victim relationship; restorative justice.

**Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 142** purposes for adults: punishment, reduction of crime (including deterrence), reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, reparation.

**Evaluating the theories:**

| Theory | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Retribution | Proportionate: the punishment fits the crime, which feels fair | Looks only backwards; does nothing to prevent future offending |
| Deterrence | Aims to prevent crime, by the offender and by others | Assumes offenders weigh up consequences rationally; many offences are impulsive or committed under the influence |
| Rehabilitation | Tackles causes such as addiction, so may reduce reoffending | Can seem lenient to victims; depends on funding and the offender's willingness |
| Reparation and restoration | Involve the victim and repair harm | Only suitable where offender and victim are willing |
| Denunciation | Shows society's condemnation and reinforces shared values | Can lead to sentences heavier than the offence itself justifies |

## Legal theories (1.1.9 to 1.1.11)

| Theory | Core claim | Theorists |
|---|---|---|
| **Positivism** | Law is valid if made by the right authority and procedure, whatever its morality | Austin (command backed by sanction), Hart (primary and secondary rules, rule of recognition) |
| **Natural law** | A higher moral law; an unjust law is not truly law | Aquinas; Fuller (inner morality of law) |
| **Utilitarianism** | The right law gives the greatest happiness of the greatest number | Bentham, Mill (harm principle) |

**More detail to use in an answer:**

- **Austin**: law is a **command** of a sovereign, whom most people habitually obey, backed by a **sanction**. *Criticism* (Hart): this cannot tell law apart from a gunman's order, and does not explain rules that give powers, such as making a will.
- **Hart**: **primary rules** impose duties; **secondary rules** are the rules of **recognition** (how to identify valid law), **change** (how law is altered) and **adjudication** (how disputes are decided).
- **Fuller**: the "inner morality" of law has eight requirements: rules should be general, published, not retrospective, clear, not contradictory, possible to obey, reasonably constant over time, and applied by officials as they are written. A system that fails badly on these is not really law.
- **Bentham**: laws should be judged by their consequences, weighing pleasure against pain. **Mill**: the only reason to restrain someone against their will is to prevent harm to others. *Criticism*: the majority's happiness could be used to justify harming a minority.

## Law and morality (1.1.12 to 1.1.14)

- **Wolfenden Report (1957)**: private consensual homosexual acts between adults should not be criminal; private morality is "not the law's business".
- **Devlin**: shared morality holds society together, so law may enforce it.
- **Hart**: only harm to others justifies intervention (following Mill).
- **Public morality** = society's shared code. **Private morality** = individual conduct not harming others.

**Devlin's test**: the law may act where ordinary reasonable people would feel **intolerance, indignation and disgust** at the conduct. **Hart's reply**: disgust alone is not harm, and enforcing majority feeling risks oppressing minorities; morality also changes, as the Sexual Offences Act 1967 later followed Wolfenden's recommendation for England and Wales.

**More cases on the courts and morality:**

- *Knuller v DPP* [1973]: the House of Lords upheld a conviction for conspiracy to corrupt public morals over contact advertisements, following *Shaw*.
- *R v Gibson* [1990]: displaying earrings made from freeze-dried human foetuses was an outrage to public decency.
- *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* [2014]: the Supreme Court declined to declare the ban on assisted suicide incompatible with Article 8, treating the question as one mainly for Parliament.

**Using the cases:** sort them into those where the courts **enforced** a shared morality (*Shaw*, *Knuller*, *Brown*, *Gibson*), those where they **respected private choice or changing attitudes** (*Wilson*, *Gillick*, *R v R*), and those where they faced a **moral dilemma** they could not avoid (*Bland*, *Re A*, *Nicklinson*). A good answer then judges whether the courts or Parliament should take such decisions.

## Model evaluation paragraph

*Topic: should the law enforce morality?*

> Devlin argued that a society is held together by a shared morality, so the law may punish conduct that ordinary people find intolerable, and the House of Lords took a similar approach in *Shaw v DPP*, creating liability for conspiracy to corrupt public morals. However, Hart, following Mill, argued that only harm to others justifies criminal law, and that disgust is not harm. The Wolfenden Report took Hart's side, saying private morality is not the law's business. The courts have not been consistent: *Brown* enforced morality against consenting adults, while *Wilson* respected a private choice between spouses. Overall, the law does still enforce some morality, but changes such as *R v R* suggest it follows shifts in public morality rather than fixing it.

**Cases:** *Shaw v DPP* [1962] and *R v Brown* [1993] enforce morality; *R v Wilson* [1996] respects private choice; *R v R* [1991] and *Gillick* [1985] show law moving with attitudes; *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] and *Re A (Conjoined Twins)* [2000] show courts facing life-and-death moral questions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Liability | Legal responsibility for a wrong, leading to a remedy or sanction |
| Remedy | What a civil court orders to put right a wrong |
| Sanction | A penalty imposed by a criminal court |
| Legal personality | Capacity to hold rights and duties |
| General deterrence | Discouraging others by example |
| Rule of recognition | Hart's test for identifying valid law |
| Harm principle | Mill's idea that the state may only restrain conduct harming others |
| Primary rules | Hart's duty-imposing rules |
| Secondary rules | Hart's rules about rules: recognition, change and adjudication |
| Compensation order | A criminal court order for the offender to pay the victim |
| Public morality | The shared moral code of a society |
| Denunciation | Punishment expressing society's condemnation of the offence |

## Exam traps

- "Prosecutor" in a civil case, or "liable" in a criminal one.
- A theory of punishment with no sentence attached.
- Legal theories with no named theorist.
- Hart/Devlin retold without applying it to a case.
- Mixing up Hart's two debates: with Devlin about law and morality; with Fuller about whether an unjust law is still law.
- Writing that natural law theorists say every immoral law is ignored by the courts; their claim is about whether it deserves to be called law.
- Describing *Salomon* without saying what separate personality allows a company to do.
- Listing theories of punishment with no evaluation of whether they work.
