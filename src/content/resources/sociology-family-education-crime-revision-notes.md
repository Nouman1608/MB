## The Paper 2 rule

Candidates answer **two of the three** topics (Family, Education, Crime) — decide early which two you
will prepare in depth. The layout is fixed: Question 1 is always Family, Question 2 Education and
Question 3 Crime, each worth 40 marks with six parts (a)–(f). Cambridge expects two topics to be
taught; a working knowledge of a third is only a safety net.

## Family — one mechanism per perspective

| Perspective | Mechanism to name |
|---|---|
| Functionalism | "Warm bath" theory — family stabilises adult emotions |
| Marxism | Family as unit of consumption |
| Feminism | Joint vs segregated conjugal roles; domestic division of labour |

**New Right vs Postmodernist on family diversity** — a key contrast to learn:
- **New Right**: diversity = dysfunction; favours the traditional nuclear family.
- **Postmodernist**: diversity = positive, evidence of individual choice.

**Changing family life**: connect cause to effect directly — falling birth rates + rising life
expectancy → ageing population (4.3.1) → effects such as more generations alive at once (beanpole
families), the pivot or "sandwich generation", and changing roles of grandparents. "Boomerang children"
belong to changing roles of children (4.3.5), not to the effects of an ageing population.

Six named family structures: nuclear, extended, reconstituted, lone parent, empty nest, childless —
know how each varies by culture, class and ethnicity before moving to perspectives.

## Education — the hidden curriculum first

The **hidden curriculum** (norms, values, punctuality, conformity taught without being named as
lessons) is the single most-returned-to concept in this topic. Have an example ready beyond the
textbook's own (e.g. school uniform policy teaching conformity).

| Perspective | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Functionalism | Meritocracy and role allocation |
| Marxism | Reproduction of class inequality via cultural capital; the myth of meritocracy |
| Feminism | Patriarchal curriculum; gendered subject choice |

**Explanations of achievement differences** — sort by type, since questions often ask you to
categorise or compare:
- **Material**: e.g. access to digital technology.
- **Cultural**: e.g. cultural capital, linguistic factors.
- **In-school**: e.g. labelling, pro-/anti-school subcultures.

## Crime, deviance and social control

**Foundational distinction, always tested**: crime breaks the law; deviance breaks social norms. They
overlap but are not identical — some deviant behaviour is not criminal, and both are relative across
societies, cultures and time.

**Two types of control — learn as a paired list:**
- **Formal**: police, courts, armed forces, government, penal system.
- **Informal**: family, education, workplace, peer group, media, religion.

**Eleven named crime types** (ready vocabulary for classifying exam stimulus material): violent,
property, white-collar/corporate, expressive, instrumental, gang, green, organised/global, cyber,
hate, domestic.

## Exam traps

- Confusing the New Right and Postmodernist views on family diversity — one treats diversity as
  dysfunctional, the other as positive.
- Describing the hidden curriculum only in textbook words, without a concrete school example.
- Treating crime and deviance as synonyms instead of explaining overlap and relativity.
- Answering perspective questions generically ("Marxists think education benefits the rich") without
  naming the specific mechanism — cultural capital, the myth of meritocracy — the mark scheme wants.

## Build one table, reuse it everywhere

Construct a single three-column table — functionalist / Marxist / feminist — with one specific
mechanism per cell, per topic you choose to prepare. This table transfers directly into exam answers
across Family, Education, and (where perspectives are examined) Crime, rather than needing three
separate revision structures.

## Why Paper 1 knowledge is assumed throughout Paper 2

Every Paper 2 topic assumes candidates can already deploy the research methods and theoretical
perspectives introduced in Paper 1 without re-explaining them. A strong Family, Education or Crime
answer names functionalism, Marxism or feminism and applies its mechanism directly, rather than
re-describing what each perspective is from first principles — that groundwork belongs to Paper 1 and
re-stating it wastes time that should go toward application. Similarly, if a Paper 2 question draws on
research methods (for example, asking how a sociologist might investigate family diversity), the
strengths and limitations of methods like structured interviews or official statistics from Paper 1
are assumed knowledge, not something to re-derive.

## The internal pattern every Paper 2 topic follows

Family, Education and Crime are taught as three separate topics but are examined through the same
underlying pattern: factual content first (what different family types, school types or crime types
actually look like), then perspectives (how functionalists, Marxists and feminists interpret that
content), then change and explanation (why patterns are shifting, and what explains inequality within
them). Recognising this shared structure means that once you have mastered how to answer a
perspectives question in Family, the same approach — name the perspective, state its specific
mechanism, apply it to the scenario — transfers directly to Education and, where perspectives are
examined, to Crime as well.

## One more link worth making: education and crime

Some series draw a synoptic connection between Education and Crime — for example, asking whether
labelling in school (an in-school explanation from Topic 5) can contribute to later deviant behaviour
(Topic 6). Being ready to draw this kind of cross-topic link, rather than treating each of the three
Paper 2 topics as entirely sealed off from the others, is worth a small amount of extra preparation
time for candidates aiming at the top grades.

## Self-test

1. How many of Paper 2's three topics must a candidate answer?
2. What is functionalism's specific mechanism for the family's role?
3. Name the New Right's view on family diversity.
4. Give one material and one cultural explanation for achievement differences.
5. State the distinction between crime and deviance.

**Answers:** 1. Two. 2. "Warm bath" theory — stabilising adult emotions. 3. Diversity is a sign of
dysfunction; favours the traditional nuclear family. 4. Material: access to digital technology.
Cultural: cultural capital or linguistic factors. 5. Crime breaks the law; deviance breaks social
norms — they overlap but are not identical, and both are relative across time and place.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many questions do you answer on Cambridge IGCSE Sociology Paper 2?**
You answer two of the three Paper 2 questions, which cover Family, Education, and Crime, deviance and social control in Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495).

**What is the warm bath theory?**
The warm bath theory is the functionalist idea that the family stabilises adult emotions.

**How do you sort explanations of educational achievement?**
Group them into material explanations, such as access to digital technology, cultural explanations, such as cultural capital and linguistic factors, and in-school explanations, such as labelling and pupil subcultures.

**What crime types does the 0495 syllabus name?**
The syllabus names eleven types: violent, property, white-collar or corporate, expressive, instrumental, gang, green, organised or global, cyber, hate and domestic crime.
