Condensed recall for **8.3 Challenges and opportunities** (Unit 1: Changing population). For the
full explanation, read the
[Population Distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/).

## What to be ready to discuss

Population distribution and change create both **challenges** and **opportunities** for places. A
strong answer weighs them for a named place and for different groups of people.

| Population situation | Possible challenges | Possible opportunities |
|---|---|---|
| Densely populated area | Pressure on land, services and resources | Concentrated markets and labour, efficient service provision |
| Sparsely populated area | Costly services, limited markets | Space and lower pressure on resources |
| Young population (wide base) | Demand for schools and jobs | A growing workforce |
| Ageing population | Demand for health care and pensions, fewer workers | Experience, and potential for new services |
| Migration | Strain on services, integration | Skills, labour and remittances |

These are general patterns; check them against your own studied examples.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dependency ratio | Dependants (aged 0-14 and 65+) per 100 people of working age (15-64) |
| Demographic dividend | Economic boost possible when falling fertility leaves a larger share of working-age adults |
| Pro-natalist policy | Encourages people to have more children |
| Anti-natalist policy | Discourages births to slow population growth |
| Total fertility rate | Average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current rates |
| Replacement level | A total fertility rate of about 2.1, the level at which a population replaces itself without migration |

## Youthful and ageing populations

- **Youthful populations** (for example Niger, which has among the highest fertility rates in the
  world) need schools, maternal health care and, above all, jobs. If jobs are created, the large
  generation entering work can drive a **demographic dividend**; if not, youth unemployment and
  emigration pressure rise. The fast growth of several East Asian economies, such as South Korea,
  is often linked to a dividend combined with heavy investment in education.
- **Ageing populations** (for example Japan, where people aged 65 and over make up close to 30
  per cent of the population in the 2020s) face rising pension and health costs and a shrinking
  workforce. Responses include raising the retirement or pension age, encouraging older people and
  women into work, automation and robotics, and more openness to immigration. Japan introduced a
  new work visa for skilled foreign workers in 2019. Ageing also creates a "silver economy" of
  services and products for older people.

## Population policies

| Policy type | Example | Points to evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-natalist | China's one-child policy (introduced around 1980, replaced by a two-child policy in 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021) | Slowed growth, but contributed to a skewed sex ratio at birth, rapid ageing and the "4-2-1" family structure |
| Pro-natalist | France's long-standing family allowances, tax benefits and childcare support | France has had one of the higher fertility rates in Europe, but many factors affect fertility |
| Changing policy | Singapore urged smaller families in the 1970s, then from 1987 promoted "three or more if you can afford it" and later added baby bonuses | Shows policy reversing as the population structure changed; fertility has stayed low despite incentives |
| Ageing policy | Raising the state pension age in many countries, including the UK | Saves public money but is unpopular and hardest on manual workers |

**Gender equality** links to all of these: wider access to education and paid work for women is
consistently associated with lower fertility. The Indian state of Kerala is often cited as reaching
low fertility through high female literacy before it became wealthy.

Migration policy is part of this subtopic too. Points-based systems (used in Australia and Canada)
select migrants for skills, while international efforts against people trafficking try to protect
the most vulnerable migrants.

## Case-study bank to prepare

- A **densely populated** area and the factors behind it.
- A **sparsely populated** area and the factors behind it.
- An example of **significant population change** (migration or natural change) with named dates and
  figures.

If you cannot recall exact figures, write in general terms rather than inventing numbers.

## Links to other core themes

- **Climate:** dense coastal populations are vulnerable to sea-level rise.
- **Resources:** population size and distribution drive demand for food, water and energy.

## Data-response skill

Paper 2 uses unseen data, so move between **quantitative reading** (a figure or trend from a pyramid,
map or graph) and **qualitative explanation** using named factors.

**Worked example (invented figures):** a country has 30 million people aged 0-14, 60 million aged
15-64 and 10 million aged 65 and over.

- Dependency ratio = (30 + 10) / 60 x 100 = 66.7.
- Youth dependency = 30 / 60 x 100 = 50; old-age dependency = 10 / 60 x 100 = 16.7.
- Interpretation: about 67 dependants for every 100 working-age people, mostly children, so the
  country is youthful. If fertility falls, the youth share shrinks and a dividend becomes possible.

Remember the ratio assumes everyone aged 15-64 works and nobody over 65 does, which is not true
in practice.

## Model sentence for an evaluation

*"Japan's ageing population is a challenge for public finances, but it has also driven investment
in care robotics and services for older consumers, so whether it is mainly a problem depends on
how far the workforce can be supported by automation, later retirement and migration."*

## Exam traps

- Calling a high or low density "a problem" with no context.
- Offering a case study with no dates or figures.
- Treating challenges and opportunities as the same for every group in a place.
- Assuming a demographic dividend happens automatically once fertility falls.
- Judging a population policy only by its effect on numbers, ignoring human rights and social effects.

## Self-test

1. Give one challenge and one opportunity of an ageing population.
2. Why does population distribution matter for climate vulnerability?
3. What three case studies should you prepare?
4. What is the data-response skill for population questions?
5. Calculate the dependency ratio for 20 million aged 0-14, 50 million aged 15-64 and 15 million aged 65+.
6. What is a demographic dividend, and what does it depend on?
7. Distinguish pro-natalist from anti-natalist policy, with an example of each.
8. Give two consequences of China's one-child policy.
9. Name two responses to an ageing population.
10. Why does female education matter for population change?

**Answers:** 1. Challenge: health care and pension demand; opportunity: for example experience or new
services. 2. Dense coastal populations are exposed to sea-level rise. 3. A densely populated area, a
sparsely populated area and an example of significant population change. 4. Read the data
quantitatively, then explain it with named factors. 5. (20 + 15) / 50 x 100 = 70. 6. An economic boost
when a larger share of the population is of working age; it depends on jobs, education and
investment being available. 7. Pro-natalist encourages births (for example France); anti-natalist
discourages them (for example China's one-child policy). 8. For example a skewed sex ratio and rapid
ageing. 9. For example raising the pension age and encouraging immigration or automation. 10. It is
associated with later marriage, wider use of contraception and lower fertility.
