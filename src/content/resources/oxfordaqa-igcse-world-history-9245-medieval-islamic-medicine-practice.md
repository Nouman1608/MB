Content tested: "the 'Translation Movement'; the development of universities and centres of learning; Islamic physicians - ethics and knowledge, including Al-Razi and Ibn-Sina; hospitals - bimaristans; surgery, tools, techniques, including the work of Al-Zahrawi; developments in public health, hygiene, water supply, and sanitation; innovations in pharmacology, including the work of Al-Baytar, and Al-Kindi; contribution to medicine in Europe and Asia."

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three traditions or authors whose medical knowledge was translated into Arabic during the Translation Movement. **[3]**

**2.** Give three contributions of Al-Razi to medicine. **[3]**

**3.** Give three surgical techniques or tools described by Al-Zahrawi. **[3]**

**4.** Give three ways in which public health was supported in medieval Islamic cities. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which bimaristans improved care for the sick. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* influenced medicine in Europe. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which pharmacology advanced in the medieval Islamic world. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which Islamic physicians developed the medical knowledge they inherited from ancient Greece. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why medicine flourished in the medieval Islamic world. **[9]**

**10.** "Individuals were the most important factor in the development of medieval Islamic medicine." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "Preserving ancient knowledge was the most important contribution of medieval Islamic medicine." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: Greek medicine; Hippocrates; Galen; Dioscorides on medicinal plants; Persian medicine associated with Gundishapur; Indian (Ayurvedic) medical texts.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: he distinguished smallpox from measles; he stressed observation and recorded cases; he questioned Galen in *Doubts about Galen*; he directed hospitals in Rayy and Baghdad; his notes became *The Comprehensive Book*; he wrote a guide for people who could not reach a doctor.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: cauterisation to stop bleeding; tying blood vessels with ligatures; stitching, including with catgut; setting bones; removing bladder stones; dental procedures; instruments for childbirth; drawings of around two hundred instruments.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: ritual washing before prayer; public baths (*hammams*); aqueducts and underground channels (*qanats*); wells and public fountains; drains and waste removal; market inspectors (*muhtasib*) checking food and cleanliness.

**5.** Way identified [1]: patients received organised medical treatment, free of charge. Precise knowledge [2]: hospitals such as the Adudi in Baghdad (982) and the Mansuri in Cairo (1284) were funded by charitable endowments and had separate wards for different illnesses, pharmacies and trained physicians. Explanation [2]: because care was free and organised by illness, poor patients could be treated by qualified doctors rather than relying on family or folk remedies, and infectious patients could be separated. Further development [1]: hospitals also trained students and kept records, so they improved future care as well.

**6.** Way identified [1]: it became a standard textbook in European universities. Precise knowledge [2]: Ibn Sina completed the *Canon* around 1025, organising Greek and Islamic knowledge into five books; Gerard of Cremona translated it into Latin at Toledo in the twelfth century. Explanation [2]: because the *Canon* was clear and comprehensive, European medical schools such as Montpellier used it to teach physicians, so generations of doctors learned medicine through it. Further development [1]: it was still part of university teaching in the seventeenth century, showing how long its influence lasted.

**7.** Way identified [1]: drugs were measured and recorded more systematically. Precise knowledge [2]: Al-Kindi in ninth-century Baghdad used mathematics to calculate the strength of compound drugs; Ibn al-Baytar's *Compendium* described about 1,400 substances. Explanation [2]: this meant physicians could prescribe more consistent doses and choose from a much wider range of remedies, so treatment depended less on guesswork. Further development [1]: pharmacy became a separate profession, and pharmacists' shops were inspected.

**8.** Way one [4]: they questioned Greek authorities using observation [1]; Al-Razi wrote *Doubts about Galen* and kept careful case records [1]; so medicine began to rely on what physicians saw rather than only on ancient texts [2]. Way two [4]: they added new knowledge [1]; Al-Razi distinguished smallpox from measles, Al-Zahrawi described new instruments and techniques, and Ibn al-Baytar recorded many substances not known to the Greeks [1]; so the knowledge passed on to later centuries was larger and more practical than what they had inherited [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example Ibn Sina's rules for testing drugs in the *Canon*.

**9.** Reason one [4]: patronage by rulers and the wealthy [1]; caliphs such as al-Ma'mun supported the House of Wisdom, and charitable endowments funded hospitals [1]; so scholars and physicians were paid to translate, research and treat patients [2]. Reason two [4]: communication across a vast area [1]; trade routes linked the Islamic world with Byzantium, Persia, India and China, and paper made books cheaper [1]; so knowledge from many traditions could be gathered, copied and shared between cities such as Baghdad, Cairo and Córdoba [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example religious encouragement of charity and cleanliness.

**10.** Given factor [3]: Al-Razi's observations and distinction of smallpox and measles; Ibn Sina's *Canon*; Al-Zahrawi's surgery; Al-Kindi and Ibn al-Baytar in pharmacology; Hunayn ibn Ishaq's translations. Other factors [4]: government and patronage, from the House of Wisdom to the licensing of physicians in 931 and the market inspectors; religion, which encouraged charity, funding hospitals, and cleanliness; communication through translation, paper and trade routes; the inheritance of Greek, Persian and Indian knowledge. Comparison [3]: individuals produced the key works, but they depended on translated texts, paper and patrons; without the Translation Movement Al-Razi could not have questioned Galen. Judgement [2]: for example, individuals were the most visible factor, but communication and patronage made their work possible, so they were more fundamental.

**11.** Given factor [3]: the Translation Movement preserved the works of Hippocrates, Galen and Dioscorides, and Persian and Indian texts; many Greek works reached medieval Europe through Arabic translations. Other contributions [4]: new knowledge from Al-Razi, Ibn Sina and Al-Zahrawi; hospitals (bimaristans) with free care, wards and teaching; licensing and medical ethics; advances in pharmacology; public health measures; the transmission of all of this to Europe and to South Asia as Unani medicine. Comparison [3]: preservation made later progress possible, but the Islamic world's own advances, organised into works such as the *Canon*, were what Europe actually learned from for centuries. Judgement [2]: for example, the most important contribution was the combination of preservation and improvement, especially the *Canon*, rather than preservation alone.
