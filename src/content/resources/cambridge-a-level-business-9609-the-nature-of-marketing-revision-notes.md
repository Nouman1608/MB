Condensed notes for **3.1 The nature of marketing** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Worked examples and a mass-against-niche comparison are in the [Nature of marketing study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-the-nature-of-marketing/); exam-style questions are in the [Nature of marketing practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-the-nature-of-marketing-practice/).

## What 3.1 asks you to handle

3.1.1 marketing objectives and their link to corporate objectives · 3.1.2 demand and supply · 3.1.3 markets, orientation, share and growth · 3.1.4 product classification, B2C and B2B · 3.1.5 mass and niche · 3.1.6 segmentation · 3.1.7 CRM.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Marketing | Identifying customer needs and providing for them profitably |
| Marketing objective | A target for the marketing function that supports the corporate objective |
| Demand | Quantity customers are willing and able to buy at each price |
| Supply | Quantity producers are willing to offer at each price |
| Equilibrium price | Price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Product orientation | Starting from what the business can make |
| Market orientation | Starting from what customers want |
| Market share | Firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Market growth | Percentage change in total market sales |
| Mass market | Large market with broadly similar needs |
| Niche market | Small, specialised part of a larger market |
| Segmentation | Dividing a market into groups with shared characteristics |
| CRM | Building long-term relationships with customers using data on them |

## Marketing and other functions

New range → operations capacity, finance for development. Premium segment → higher quality standards. Sales growth → recruitment, more inventory. Price cuts → lower margins, pressure to cut unit costs.

## Demand and supply

- **Price change** → movement along the curve.
- **Other factor** → shift of the curve.
- Demand factors: income, substitutes, complements, tastes, advertising, population.
- Supply factors: costs, technology, number of producers, weather and other external events.

## Formulas

- Market share = firm's sales ÷ market sales × 100
- Market growth = (this year − last year) ÷ last year × 100
- Sales needed for a target share = target share × forecast market size

Change in share → **percentage points**. Change in a firm's sales → **per cent**.

## Implications of share and growth

- Sales up, share down → rivals growing faster; review the marketing mix.
- Share up in a shrinking market → relative strength but absolute decline.
- High share → economies of scale, bargaining power with retailers.

## Orientation

- Market orientation: lower risk, needs research spending.
- Product orientation: suits technically new products or engineering-led markets.
- Most firms blend both.

## Product classification

- Consumer: convenience (wide distribution), shopping (comparison, price), speciality (exclusive, brand).
- Industrial: raw materials, components, equipment, business services (specification, reliability, service).

## B2C against B2B

| | B2C | B2B |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers | Many individuals | Few organisations |
| Decision | Quick, often emotional | Slower, several people involved |
| Promotion | Mass media, branding | Sales teams, trade shows, specifications |
| Price | Fixed list prices | Often negotiated |
| Relationship | Shorter | Long-term contracts |

## Mass against niche

- Mass: volume, economies of scale; intense competition, low margins, high promotion costs.
- Niche: less competition, premium prices, loyalty; small sales, dependence on one group, large rivals may enter.
- Compare **total contribution**, not just margin per unit.

## Segmentation

- Geographic: climate, culture, regulation.
- Demographic: age, income, family size.
- Psychographic: values, lifestyle.
- Benefits: better targeting, less waste, finds gaps. Drawbacks: cost, shorter runs, hard to measure, small segments.

## CRM

- Methods: loyalty schemes, personalised messages, account managers, after-sales follow-up.
- Benefits: retention cheaper than acquisition; repeat sales; customer insight.
- Costs and risks: systems, training, rewards; poor data; weak loyalty; data protection duties.

## Exam traps

- Price change ≠ demand shift.
- Rising sales ≠ rising share.
- Percentage points ≠ per cent.
- Niche ≠ automatically more profitable.
- Choose a segmentation method that fits **this** product.

## Self-test

1. Give two factors other than price that shift demand.
2. What happens to equilibrium price if demand falls and supply rises?
3. A firm's sales are $6m in a market of $40m. Calculate its share.
4. The market grows from $40m to $46m. Calculate market growth.
5. If the firm's sales stay at $6m, by how many percentage points does its share fall?
6. Give one situation where product orientation might succeed.
7. Give two differences between B2B and B2C marketing.
8. Give one disadvantage of niche marketing.
9. Which segmentation method would suit an eco-friendly cleaning brand?
10. State one aim of CRM.
11. Give one risk of CRM.
12. Classify a family car: convenience, shopping or speciality product?

**Answers**

1. Any two: income, price of substitutes, price of complements, tastes, advertising, population.
2. It falls.
3. 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
4. (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
5. New share = 6 ÷ 46 × 100 = 13.0% (to 1 d.p.), a fall of about 2.0 percentage points.
6. For example, a technically new product that customers cannot yet imagine, or an engineering-led market where quality decides sales.
7. Any two: fewer, larger buyers; several people in the decision; negotiated prices; sales teams instead of mass media; long-term contracts.
8. For example, small sales volume, dependence on one customer group, or the risk that large rivals enter.
9. Psychographic, because buyers are driven by environmental values.
10. For example, keeping existing customers or raising repeat purchases.
11. For example, poor data leads to irrelevant messages, or misuse of personal data damages trust.
12. Usually a shopping product, because buyers compare models and prices before buying.
