Condensed notes on subtopic 6.1 Stress and strain (Cambridge 9702, AS Topic 6).
For full explanations and worked examples, use the
[stress and strain study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-stress-and-strain/);
for the whole topic, see the [Deformation of Solids revision notes](/resources/as-physics-deformation-of-solids-revision-notes/).

## What 6.1 asks you to do

- Understand that deformation is caused by tensile or compressive forces (one dimension only)
- Use the terms load, extension, compression and limit of proportionality
- Recall and use Hooke's law and k = F/x
- Define and use stress, strain and the Young modulus
- Describe an experiment to determine the Young modulus of a metal wire

## Equations

```
F = k x                  Hooke's law, up to the limit of proportionality
k = F / x                N m^-1   (gradient of linear F-x graph)
stress = F / A           Pa = N m^-2
strain = x / L           no unit  (L = ORIGINAL length)
E = stress / strain = F L / (A x)        Pa
x = F L / (A E)          extension for a given load
A = pi r^2 = pi d^2 / 4  r in metres
```

## Object or material?

| Quantity | Depends on | Changes if the wire is thicker? |
|---|---|---|
| Spring constant k | Object: material, length and area | Yes, k increases |
| Young modulus E | Material only | No |
| Stress for a given load | Area | Yes, stress decreases |
| Strain for a given load | Material, area and length | Yes, strain decreases |

## Proportional reasoning

For the **same material and the same load**: x ∝ L/A ∝ L/d².

- Double the length → double the extension.
- Double the diameter → area ×4 → extension ÷4.
- Double both → extension ÷2.

## Unit conversions that cost marks

- 1 mm = 10⁻³ m; 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²; 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m².
- 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa; 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa.
- Diameter of 0.50 mm → radius 0.25 × 10⁻³ m → A = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m².

## Young modulus experiment: the essentials

- **Long** wire: larger extension for a given strain, so smaller percentage uncertainty in x.
- **Thin** wire: smaller area, so larger stress and extension for manageable loads.
- **Micrometer** for the diameter, at several points and orientations, then a mean.
- **Metre rule or tape** for the original length, clamp to marker.
- **Vernier scale or marker** against a fixed scale for the extension; a **reference wire** of the same material cancels temperature changes and sagging of the support.
- Load in equal steps, then unload to check the wire returns to its original reading (elastic).
- Plot F against x: E = gradient × L/A. Or plot stress against strain: E = gradient.

**Uncertainty rule:** % uncertainty in A = 2 × % uncertainty in d, so the diameter usually dominates the uncertainty in E.

## Worked example

A wire of original length 1.80 m and diameter 0.46 mm extends by 2.4 mm under a load of 50 N.

```
A      = pi (0.23 x 10^-3)^2 = 1.66 x 10^-7 m^2
stress = 50 / 1.66 x 10^-7   = 3.01 x 10^8 Pa
strain = 2.4 x 10^-3 / 1.80  = 1.33 x 10^-3
E      = 3.01 x 10^8 / 1.33 x 10^-3 = 2.3 x 10^11 Pa
```

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to recall |
|---|---|
| Tensile force | A force that stretches an object |
| Compressive force | A force that squashes an object |
| Load | The force applied to deform the object |
| Extension | Increase in length from the original length |
| Compression | Decrease in length from the original length |
| Limit of proportionality | Point beyond which force is no longer proportional to extension |
| Hooke's law | Extension is proportional to force, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded |
| Spring constant | Force per unit extension |
| Stress | Force per unit cross-sectional area |
| Strain | Extension per unit original length |
| Young modulus | Stress ÷ strain, for a material obeying Hooke's law |

## Exam traps

- Diameter used in πr²: area four times too large, E four times too small.
- New length used in strain instead of original length.
- Young modulus given in N m⁻¹, or strain given a unit.
- Hooke's law stated without the limit of proportionality.
- "A thicker wire has a bigger Young modulus": it has a bigger spring constant.
- Extension read as the total length of the wire.
- Gradient of F–x quoted as E without multiplying by L/A.

## Self-test

1. State Hooke's law.
2. Define stress and strain.
3. A spring of spring constant 40 N m⁻¹ carries a load of 2.0 N. Find the extension.
4. A wire of original length 1.5 m extends by 0.75 mm. Find the strain.
5. A force of 200 N acts on a wire of cross-sectional area 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m². Find the stress.
6. Find the Young modulus when the stress is 5.0 × 10⁸ Pa and the strain is 2.5 × 10⁻³.
7. Convert 0.30 mm² into m².
8. Find the cross-sectional area of a wire of diameter 0.60 mm.
9. Two wires of the same metal and diameter carry the same load. One is half as long. Compare the extensions.
10. Why is a micrometer used for the diameter in the Young modulus experiment?
11. Give the units of the spring constant and of the Young modulus.
12. The diameter of a wire has a percentage uncertainty of 1.5%. What is the percentage uncertainty in its cross-sectional area?

**Answers:**
1. Extension is directly proportional to the applied force, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.
2. Stress: force per unit cross-sectional area. Strain: extension per unit original length.
3. x = F/k = 2.0 ÷ 40 = **0.050 m**.
4. 0.75 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.5 = **5.0 × 10⁻⁴**.
5. 200 ÷ 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ = **5.0 × 10⁸ Pa**.
6. 5.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻³ = **2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa**.
7. **3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²**.
8. π × (0.30 × 10⁻³)² = **2.8 × 10⁻⁷ m²**.
9. The shorter wire extends by **half** as much (x ∝ L).
10. The diameter is small, so a precise instrument is needed; its uncertainty is doubled in the area.
11. k: **N m⁻¹**. E: **Pa** (N m⁻²).
12. **3%**.
