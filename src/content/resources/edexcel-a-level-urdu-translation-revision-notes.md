## Translation principles

**Translate meaning, not words.** A word-for-word rendering usually produces something ungrammatical in the target language, and accuracy of *meaning* is what is assessed.

**The core structural difference:** Urdu is **Subject–Object–Verb**; English is **Subject–Verb–Object**. Urdu uses **postpositions** where English uses prepositions. So the sentence must be reassembled, not transposed.

**Method for Urdu → English:**

1. Read the whole passage first for sense.
2. Identify the **verb** — in Urdu it is at the end.
3. Identify subject and object, then rebuild in English order.
4. Check tense and register.
5. Reread the English alone — does it sound natural?

That last step catches most errors. If the English reads oddly, it is almost certainly too literal.

**Worked example.** An Urdu sentence whose literal English is *"On him anger came"*:

```
Literal   "On him anger came"     -- unnatural English
Too free  "He lost his temper"    -- idiomatic but loses register
Balanced  "He became angry"       -- accurate and natural
```

Check every version against three questions: is every element present, does it read as English a native speaker would write, and is the register (formal/informal) preserved from the source?

**Idiom must be rendered by sense, not word for word.** For example, آنکھوں کا تارا (literally "the star of the eyes") means "the apple of one's eye"; ہاتھ دھو بیٹھنا (literally "to sit having washed one's hands") means "to lose something for good", not "to wash one's hands of"; دال میں کچھ کالا ہونا (literally "there is something black in the lentils") means "there is something fishy about it". A literal rendering of any of these would be meaningless or misleading in English.

**Method for English → Urdu:** identify the verb first, move it to the end, convert prepositions to postpositions, then check **gender agreement** throughout.

## Grammar that decides marks

**Gender agreement** — every noun is masculine or feminine, and adjectives and verbs must agree. A gender error propagates through the sentence, so it costs more than a single mark.

**Tense range:**

| Tense | Marker |
|---|---|
| Present | *-ta/-ti/-te* + *hai/hain* |
| Past | *-a/-i/-e* + *tha/thi/the* |
| Future | *-ga/-gi/-ge* |
| Continuous | *rahaa/rahi/rahe* + auxiliary |
| Perfect | *chukaa/chuki/chuke* |

**Two further accuracy points in extended writing:** the ergative marker **نے** (*ne*) is required with transitive verbs in the perfective aspect (e.g. *us ne kitaab paṛhi*, "he/she read the book"), and **izafat** constructions (e.g. *jashn-e-azaadi*) belong to a more formal, Persianised register — using them correctly signals command of range, but forcing them where plain Urdu is expected reads as unnatural.

**Register** — Urdu distinguishes **آپ** (*aap*, formal), **تم** (*tum*, familiar) and **تو** (*tu*, intimate). Choosing the wrong one changes the tone of a whole passage. Formal written Urdu also draws more on Persian and Arabic vocabulary, informal on everyday usage — matching the register of the source is part of accurate translation.

## Reading comprehension

- Identify what each question actually asks: *what* questions want located detail, *why* questions want inference, and questions about the writer's attitude want evidence of tone — word choice, irony, emphasis.
- Read the questions **before** the passage, so you know what to look for.
- **Answer in the language requested** — losing marks for answering in the wrong language is avoidable and common.
- Use the **mark allocation** to gauge how many points are needed.
- For inference questions, quote or paraphrase the supporting evidence rather than asserting.
- Do not lift long stretches verbatim where the question asks for explanation in your own words.

## Summary tasks

Identify the main points and **exclude examples and repetition**. Write in continuous prose, in your own words, within the word limit. Content marks and language marks are separate, so a summary can contain every point and still lose the language marks by copying phrasing.

## Common errors

- Word-for-word translation that ignores SOV/SVO differences.
- Gender disagreement between noun, adjective and verb.
- Using only one tense.
- Mixing *aap* and *tum* within one piece.
- Answering in the wrong language.
- Copying from the passage where paraphrase is required.
- Adding explanatory material that is not present in the source text.
- Leaving no time at the end to check the accuracy of the Urdu script.
