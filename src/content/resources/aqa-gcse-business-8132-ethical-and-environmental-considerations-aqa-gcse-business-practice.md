These questions cover section 3.2.2 Ethical and environmental considerations of AQA GCSE Business (8132). Revise first with the [Ethical and environmental considerations study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-aqa-gcse-business/) and the [Ethical and environmental considerations revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *ethical behaviour*. **[1]**

**2.** Identify **two** environmental impacts of business activity named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** ways in which consumers can accept greater environmental responsibility. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit to a business of treating its suppliers ethically. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** drawback to a business of behaving ethically. **[3]**

**6.** A hotel spends £8,400 on a food-waste composting system. It saves £2,100 a year in waste collection charges.

**(a)** Calculate how many years it will take for the savings to cover the cost. **[1]**
**(b)** State **one** benefit of the system other than the cost saving. **[1]**

### Section B: longer and case-study questions

**7.** Fellgate Furniture makes tables from hardwood. It is considering switching to timber only from forests where new trees are planted to replace those cut down. This timber costs 15% more. Analyse whether Fellgate Furniture faces a trade-off between sustainability and profit. **[6]**

**8.** Larkhill Logistics runs a warehouse next to a housing estate. Lorries arrive and leave through the night.

**(a)** Identify **two** environmental impacts of Larkhill Logistics' activities on local residents. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** action the business could take to reduce one of these impacts and **one** cost of that action. **[4]**

**9.** Cotterill Chocolate sells 50,000 bars a year at £2.50. Each bar costs £1.10 to make. It plans to switch to certified fair trade cocoa, raising the cost of each bar by £0.20.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in yearly profit if sales and price stay the same. **[2]**
**(b)** Cotterill instead raises its price to £2.80 and sales fall to 46,000 bars. Calculate the new yearly profit on the bars, ignoring fixed costs, and compare it with the original. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how the behaviour of consumers can encourage businesses to act in a more environmentally responsible way. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse **one** way a business could reduce its contribution to global warming and the effect on its finances. **[4]**

**12.** Saltmarsh Cosmetics sells skincare products in plastic tubs. It is considering moving to refillable glass jars, which customers return to the shop. The change would raise packaging and handling costs by 20%. Its customers are mainly young adults, and a rival recently gained publicity for cutting plastic. Evaluate whether Saltmarsh Cosmetics should make the change. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Acting in ways that stakeholders consider to be both fair and honest [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two of: traffic congestion [1]; waste disposal or recycling [1]; noise pollution; air pollution. **[2]**

**3.** Any two of: choosing products with less packaging [1]; recycling at home [1]; buying second-hand or repairing goods; paying more for greener products; sharing car journeys. **[2]**

**4.** Paying suppliers a fair price, and on time, builds a good relationship [1]. Suppliers are then more likely to give the business priority when goods are in short supply, or to offer help with urgent orders [1], so the business can keep producing and avoid losing sales [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Ethical choices such as paying staff above the minimum required or buying fair trade inputs raise costs [1]. If the business does not raise prices, its profit margin falls [1]; if it does raise prices, customers who buy mainly on price may switch to cheaper rivals, reducing sales [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** £8,400 ÷ £2,100 = 4 years [1].
**(b)** For example: a better image with customers who care about the environment, or less waste sent to landfill [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Timber from replanted forests protects a scarce resource, so supplies remain available in future [1]. However, it costs 15% more, so Fellgate's cost per table rises [1] and, unless it raises prices, its profit per table falls [1]. If it raises prices, some customers may buy cheaper tables from rivals [1]. On the other hand, customers buying hardwood furniture may value sustainable sourcing and be willing to pay more, which could protect sales and allow a higher price [1]. So there is a short-term trade-off, which is smaller if Fellgate's customers care about sustainability and larger if they buy mainly on price [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Noise pollution at night [1]; traffic congestion or air pollution from lorries [1].
**(b)** Larkhill could restrict lorry movements to daytime hours [1], which would cut night-time noise for residents and improve relations with the community [1]. The cost is that the warehouse would handle fewer deliveries in a day [1], so it may need extra loading bays or staff to fit the same work into fewer hours, raising costs [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Extra cost = 50,000 × £0.20 = £10,000 [1], so yearly profit falls by £10,000 [1].
**(b)** Original profit on bars = 50,000 × (£2.50 − £1.10) = £70,000. New cost per bar = £1.30, so new profit = 46,000 × (£2.80 − £1.30) = 46,000 × £1.50 = £69,000 [1]. This is £1,000 less than the original [1], but £9,000 more than keeping the old price with fair trade cocoa, so the price rise limits most of the trade-off [1]. **[5]**

**10.** When consumers choose products with less packaging, or pay more for greener goods [1], businesses that make those changes gain sales and those that do not lose customers [1]. Consumers can also complain publicly, for example on social media, about pollution or waste [1], which harms a business's reputation and gives it a financial reason to change its decisions [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A business could install solar panels or switch to a renewable electricity supplier, reducing its use of fossil-fuel energy [1]. This has an upfront cost, or a higher price per unit of energy, which reduces profit in the short run [1]. Over time, panels can lower electricity bills [1], and the business can use the change in its marketing, which may attract customers and help profit in the long run [1]. **[4]**

**12.** **For the change:** young adults may value cutting plastic, so refillable jars could attract and keep these customers [1]. The rival's publicity shows the issue is noticed in this market [1], so if Saltmarsh does not act it may lose customers to the rival [1]. Returning jars to the shop brings customers back, creating more chances for repeat purchases [1]. **Against the change:** packaging and handling costs rise by 20% [1], which reduces profit per product unless prices rise [1], and some customers may find returning jars inconvenient and buy elsewhere [1]. **Judgement:** Saltmarsh should make the change, because its target market and the rival's success suggest the gain in reputation and loyalty is likely to outweigh the higher costs [1]. It could test the jars in one shop first, which would reduce the risk if customers do not return them [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest evaluations use the case's evidence, here the age of the customers and the rival's publicity, to decide which side is stronger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining ethics as "obeying the law".
- Naming environmental issues that the specification does not list, instead of its four impacts.
- Assuming every ethical or green choice cuts profit.
- Forgetting the consumer side of environmental responsibility.
- Calculating a cost change on one unit and forgetting to multiply by the quantity.
- Ending an evaluation without a decision.
