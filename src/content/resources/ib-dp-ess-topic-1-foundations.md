This guide covers **Topic 1 — Foundations**, for IB Diploma Programme
Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. Topic 1 is
deliberately the first thing every ESS student studies, and, as the
[full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-syllabus-guide/)
explains, its three concepts are explicitly "revisited throughout the
course" rather than assessed once and set aside.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Because the specification explicitly revisits perspectives, systems
and sustainability throughout every later topic, a student who has
genuinely internalised this vocabulary can apply it to unfamiliar
content on water, land, atmosphere or resources without having studied
that content in the same depth as a student who has not built this
foundation. Any Topic 4–8 question can, in principle, ask a student to
identify the environmental value system underlying a described policy
position, model a described environmental process as a system with
named inputs, outputs and feedback loops, or evaluate whether a
described resource-use pattern is within its natural income. Treating
Topic 1 as a short warm-up rather than a recurring toolkit is the
single most common reason for descriptive, rather than analytical, ESS
exam answers.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS AND SOCIETIES — TOPIC 1: FOUNDATIONS**

- **1.1 Perspectives** (3 hours) — differing individual and collective
worldviews on environmental issues, centred on the **environmental
value system (EVS)**: a particular world view or paradigm shaping how
an individual or group perceives and evaluates environmental issues,
influenced by cultural, religious, economic and socio-political
context. Three broad EVS categories recur across the course:
**ecocentrism** (nature-centred, valuing the environment for its own
sake), **anthropocentrism** (human-centred, managing the environment
for humanity's benefit), and **technocentrism** (belief that
technology and human ingenuity can solve environmental problems)
- **1.2 Systems** (5 hours) — systems theory as a tool for
understanding environmental structures and processes: a **system** is
an assemblage of interrelated parts interacting coherently; **inputs**
and **outputs** are energy or matter crossing the system boundary; a
**feedback loop** is a mechanism where a change in a system's output
affects its own input, distinguished into **negative feedback**
(stabilising, moving the system back toward equilibrium) and
**positive feedback** (amplifying, pushing the system further from its
original state); systems are further classified as **open** (exchange
both energy and matter), **closed** (exchange only energy), or
**isolated** (exchange neither)
- **1.3 Sustainability** (8 hours, the largest of Topic 1's three
sub-topics) — resource management and sustainability, framed as
meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future
generations to meet their own; **natural capital** (the stock of
natural resources providing goods and services of value) versus
**natural income** (the sustainable yield obtainable from that
capital); **renewable** versus **non-renewable** natural capital; the
**ecological footprint** as an environmental indicator measuring the
area of land and water required to sustainably provide consumed
resources and assimilate associated waste

## How to approach it

Always apply an EVS category to a specific described scenario or
stakeholder, rather than only defining the three terms in the
abstract — justify which specific words or actions in a case study
indicate ecocentrism, anthropocentrism or technocentrism. Keep
negative and positive feedback precisely distinct from a value
judgement: "negative" here means stabilising, not "bad," and
confusing the two is one of the most common ESS exam errors. Treat
"sustainable" as a threshold rather than a simple yes/no label — a
resource is being used sustainably only while consumption stays within
its natural income, and the same resource can move between sustainable
and unsustainable use over time as consumption changes.

## Worked example: applying Topic 1 concepts to a Topic 4 scenario

A case study describes a freshwater aquifer being extracted for
irrigation at a rate exceeding its natural recharge rate.

```
Systems framing:    the aquifer is a system with inputs (rainfall
                     recharge) and outputs (extraction for
                     irrigation); a positive feedback loop could
                     emerge if falling water tables push farmers to
                     drill deeper and extract even faster

Sustainability      natural capital = the water stored in the
framing:             aquifer; natural income = the recharge rate;
                     because extraction exceeds recharge, the
                     resource is being used UNSUSTAINABLY -- capital
                     is being depleted, not just income drawn down

EVS framing:         a policy prioritising irrigation output above
                     all else reflects an anthropocentric value
                     system; a policy capping extraction at the
                     recharge rate reflects a more ecocentric or
                     precautionary stance
```

Applying all three Topic 1 lenses to the same scenario, rather than
using only one, is what demonstrates the "revisited throughout the
course" application the specification expects.

## Common mistakes

Defining negative feedback as "bad" and positive feedback as "good" —
both terms describe the direction of the effect on the system
(stabilising versus amplifying), not a value judgement. Confusing
natural capital with natural income — capital is the stock, income is
the sustainable yield from that stock. Applying an EVS label without
evidence from the scenario. Treating sustainability as a fixed
property of a resource rather than a threshold that depends on the
current rate of consumption relative to natural income.

## Quick revision checklist

- Practise applying an EVS category to a specific scenario or
  stakeholder, with justification from the text.
- Keep negative and positive feedback distinct from good/bad value
  judgements.
- Distinguish natural capital (stock) from natural income (sustainable
  yield) precisely.
- Practise applying all three Topic 1 lenses (perspectives, systems,
  sustainability) to unfamiliar content from later topics.
- Treat Topic 1 as a recurring toolkit to apply throughout the course,
  not a topic revised once and set aside.
