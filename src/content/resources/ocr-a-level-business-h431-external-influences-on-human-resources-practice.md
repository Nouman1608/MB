## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain **one** way in which an ageing population could affect a business's human resource decisions. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a legal influence and an ethical influence on human resource management. **[3]**

**3.** A business hires 24 staff a year. When unemployment falls, the cost per hire rises from £700 to £1,500. Calculate the increase in annual recruitment cost. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a political influence on human resource management?

A: A fall in the exchange rate.

B: A new government subsidy for apprenticeships.

C: A shift towards remote working.

D: A rise in the cost of energy. **[1]**

**5.** Identify **two** ways in which technology has changed recruitment. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Hexley Finance employs 10 clerks on £21,000 each, with on-costs adding 20% to each salary. It plans to buy software costing £90,000, with running costs of £15,000 a year. After installation it will need 4 clerks and 1 analyst on £32,000, with the same on-costs.

**(a)** Calculate the current annual labour cost. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the new annual labour cost. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the payback period for the software, using the saving after running costs. **[3]**

**(d)** Evaluate the effect of the software on Hexley's workforce and other stakeholders. **[8]**

**7.** Saltwick Fisheries has 80 staff, of whom 25% will retire within five years. It costs £3,500 to recruit and train each replacement. The local labour market has low unemployment and few young applicants.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of replacing the retiring staff. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate how Saltwick could respond to this social and economic situation. **[8]**

**8.** Dorran Contact, a UK company, employs 25 staff in its call centre at £19,000 each. An overseas centre could do the same work for £8,000 per employee.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving in payroll cost. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate the international influences that Dorran should consider before moving the work. **[8]**

**9.** A new law raises the legal minimum wage by 6%. Evaluate the impact of this legal influence on a restaurant chain with many part-time staff and on its stakeholders. **[9]**

**10.** Wenham Textiles is a small manufacturer. Evaluate which **one** of the following will be the most important influence on its human resource management over the next five years: economic, technological or environmental. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An ageing population reduces the number of young people entering the labour market, so recruitment may be harder [1]. The business may respond by keeping older workers longer, offering phased retirement or planning knowledge transfer [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A legal influence is a rule set by law that the business must follow, for example minimum wage or equality law [1]. An ethical influence is what the business believes is right, beyond the law, such as paying a living wage above the legal minimum [1]. Breaking a legal rule brings penalties, while ethical failures risk reputation and motivation [1]. **[3]**

**3.** 24 × £700 = £16,800 and 24 × £1,500 = £36,000 [1]; increase = **£19,200** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any two: online job boards and social media advertising; online application and screening; video interviews; online testing; data analysis of applicants [1] [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 10 × £21,000 × 1.2 [1] = **£252,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Salaries: (4 × £21,000) + £32,000 = £116,000 [1]. With on-costs: £116,000 × 1.2 [1] = **£139,200** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Saving = £252,000 − £139,200 = £112,800; less running costs of £15,000 = £97,800 [1]. Payback = £90,000 ÷ £97,800 [1] = **0.92 years** (about 11 months) [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** The workforce falls from 10 posts to 5: six clerk posts go and one analyst post is created, so redundancy or redeployment is needed [1]. The remaining clerks may need training in the software [1]. Owners gain, as the saving of £97,800 a year repays the cost in under a year [1]. Employees who lose jobs lose income and may suffer stress [1]; remaining staff may fear further cuts, which lowers morale [1]. Customers may get faster service, though they may dislike less personal contact [1]. The legal duties of consultation and fair selection must be followed [1].
Judgement: the investment is strongly justified financially, but Hexley should retrain and redeploy staff where it can and explain the plan early, to keep morale and its reputation as an employer [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** 80 × 25% = 20 staff [1]; 20 × £3,500 = **£70,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Options: raise pay or benefits to attract the few applicants, which adds cost but may help [1]; train and promote existing staff, which retains knowledge but takes time [1]; offer phased retirement or part-time work so older staff stay longer and train successors [1]; use technology to reduce the number of staff needed [1]; recruit from outside the local area, with relocation help [1].
Costs and risks: pay rises persist, and apply to all staff [1]. Phased retirement relies on the older workers' willingness [1].
Judgement: a combination is best, with phased retirement first because it is cheapest and protects knowledge, backed by training for younger staff, since the problem is a long-term trend, not a short-term shortage [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** UK payroll = 25 × £19,000 = £475,000 [1]; overseas payroll = 25 × £8,000 = £200,000 [1]; saving = **£275,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Wage differences: the saving is large (58% of the UK payroll) [1]. Exchange rates: if the pound falls, the overseas wage bill in pounds rises [1]. Skills and language: customer satisfaction may fall if staff do not match UK expectations [1]. Time zones may reduce availability [1]. Trade and legal rules: contracts, data protection of UK customers and employment law abroad must be followed [1]. Reputation: UK staff redundancies and offshoring may attract criticism [1]. Costs left out: redundancy payments and set-up [1].
Judgement: the saving is large enough to justify a pilot, but the move should go ahead only if customer satisfaction can be protected [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Costs: wages are a large share of costs for a restaurant chain, so a 6% rise on minimum-wage staff increases the wage bill [1]. If the chain's wage bill for minimum-wage staff is high, the rise could reduce profit [1]. Responses: raise menu prices, cut hours, or improve productivity [1].
Benefits: staff earn more, which can raise motivation and cut turnover and recruitment costs [1]; all rivals face the same increase, so no one is undercut by lower pay [1].
Stakeholders: staff gain income, but if hours are cut some lose [1]; customers may pay more [1]; owners may earn less [1].
Judgement: the impact is significant because labour is a high share of costs, but it is manageable if prices can rise and productivity improves [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Economic: low unemployment makes recruitment harder and raises pay, while a recession could cut demand and force redundancies [1]; these effects are fast and large for a small firm [1].
Technological: automation could change the skills needed and reduce routine jobs [1], but a small firm may lack funds to adopt it [1].
Environmental: pressure to cut energy use or adopt green methods may need training and new skills [1], though the effect is usually slower [1].
Judgement: choose one, such as economic influences, as the most important because they affect pay, recruiting and job security directly and change quickly [1], while technology and environmental changes are slower and need capital the firm may lack [1]. State what would change the view, for example a competitor adopting automation [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing influences without linking them to a human resource decision.
- Giving no figure to show the size of the effect.
- Forgetting on-costs in labour cost calculations.
- Treating influences as threats only.
- Not choosing one influence as most important when asked.
