## Questions

**1.** Define primary production and state the units in which it is usually measured. **[2]**

**2.** A forest and a tundra site were compared. In the forest, plants fixed 2800 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ and released 1350 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ in their own respiration. In the tundra, plants fixed 350 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ and released 200 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the net primary production of each site. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the forest value is, and suggest one reason why the tundra value is low. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why secondary production in an ecosystem is lower than primary production. **[3]**

**4.** Describe how you would construct a diagram of the carbon cycle in an ecosystem, using photosynthesis, feeding and respiration. Refer to the direction of arrows. **[4]**

**5.** Two ecosystems were studied (all values g C m⁻² yr⁻¹).

| Ecosystem | Photosynthesis | Respiration by autotrophs | Respiration by heterotrophs and decomposers |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: managed woodland | 2000 | 900 | 1050 |
| B: drained wetland | 400 | 180 | 520 |

**(a)** Calculate the net carbon dioxide uptake of each ecosystem. **[3]**
**(b)** State which ecosystem is a carbon source and suggest why its respiration is so high relative to photosynthesis. **[2]**

**6.** Coal, peat and natural gas are all stores of carbon.

**(a)** Distinguish between peat and natural gas in terms of the organisms they came from and the time taken to form. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why combustion of fossil fuels increases atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration even though lightning fires also occur naturally. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows monthly carbon dioxide concentration (ppm) at a monitoring station in one year, and annual means in three years.

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ (ppm) | 415.5 | 416.5 | 417.0 | 418.5 | 419.5 | 418.5 | 416.5 | 414.5 | 413.5 | 413.8 | 415.0 | 416.2 |

| Year | 1980 | 2000 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual mean (ppm) | 338 | 369 | 414 |

**(a)** State the months with the highest and lowest concentration. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the range of the annual fluctuation. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the annual fluctuation. **[3]**
**(d)** Calculate the mean rate of increase in ppm per year for 1980 to 2000 and for 2000 to 2020. **[2]**
**(e)** Comment on what the two rates show about the long-term trend. **[1]**

**8.** Explain how aerobic respiration and photosynthesis depend on each other. **[3]**

**9.** Identical dried leaves were placed in litter bags and buried for 12 weeks. Coarse-mesh bags allowed small invertebrates, fungi and bacteria to enter. Fine-mesh bags excluded the invertebrates. The mean mass remaining was 38% of the starting mass in coarse-mesh bags and 71% in fine-mesh bags.

**(a)** Calculate the mean percentage mass lost in each type of bag. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why mass loss differed. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why decomposition matters for the recycling of elements such as phosphorus. **[2]**

**10.** The table gives net ecosystem carbon exchange for a temperate deciduous forest, where positive values mean net CO₂ uptake (g C m⁻² per month).

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net uptake | −20 | −22 | −15 | 10 | 65 | 110 | 100 | 75 | 30 | −25 | −30 | −25 |

**(a)** List the months in which the forest is a carbon source. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the net uptake for the whole year, and state whether the forest is a sink or a source over the year. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the forest is a source in winter. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why net uptake is greatest in June. **[2]**
**(e)** The forest is clear-felled and the wood is burned. Predict, with reasons, how the annual net uptake would change. **[2]**

**11.** A company proposes to plant a forest to offset carbon dioxide released by burning natural gas. Evaluate this proposal. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary production is the accumulation of carbon compounds in the biomass of autotrophs [1]. It is measured as carbon mass for each square metre in each year, usually g m⁻² yr⁻¹ [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that mentions only photosynthesis and not accumulation in biomass is incomplete, and units without "per area" or "per time" tend to lose the second point.

**2. (a)** Forest: 2800 − 1350 = 1450 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ [1]. Tundra: 350 − 200 = 150 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1450 ÷ 150 = 9.7 times [1]. Low temperature, short growing season or frozen soil limits photosynthesis (accept low light in winter or limited nutrients) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtracting the plants' own respiration is the step the data invite, so show it. Quote units on both answers.

**3.** Respiration converts carbon compounds to carbon dioxide and water [1], so carbon is lost from biomass at each stage of feeding [1]. Not all biomass is eaten, and some of what is eaten is egested rather than assimilated [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say only "energy is lost as heat" miss the carbon-based reasoning this question asks for. Refer to carbon compounds and respiration.

**4.** Draw boxes for CO₂ in air or water, producers, consumers, dead organic matter and decomposers. Draw an arrow from CO₂ to producers labelled photosynthesis [1]. Draw feeding arrows from the organism eaten to the feeder, for example producers to consumers [1]. Draw respiration arrows from producers, consumers and decomposers back to CO₂ [1]. Show death and egestion arrows from living organisms to dead organic matter, then to decomposers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Reversed feeding arrows and a missing decomposer box are two common ways a diagram loses credit. Label every arrow with its process.

**5. (a)** A: 2000 − 900 − 1050 = +50 [1]. B: 400 − 180 − 520 = −300 [1]. Units g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ given [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** B is a source because respiration exceeds photosynthesis [1]. Drainage lets oxygen in, so decomposers respire stored organic matter (peat) rapidly [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both types of respiration must be subtracted. Link the sign of the answer to "sink" or "source" explicitly.

**6. (a)** Peat forms from partly decayed plant material in waterlogged, acidic conditions over thousands of years [1]. Natural gas forms from buried remains of marine organisms over millions of years [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Human activities have greatly increased the rate of combustion compared with natural fires [1]. Fossil fuel carbon was removed from the atmosphere long ago, so burning returns it faster than photosynthesis can take it up [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for the idea of rate and for the age of the stored carbon, not for restating that "burning releases CO₂".

**7. (a)** Highest in May, lowest in September [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 419.5 − 413.5 = 6.0 ppm [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Most land and vegetation is in the northern hemisphere [1]. In spring and summer photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so CO₂ falls to a minimum by September [1]. In autumn and winter respiration (and decomposition) exceeds photosynthesis, so CO₂ rises to a peak by May [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** 1980 to 2000: (369 − 338) ÷ 20 = 1.55 ppm per year [1]. 2000 to 2020: (414 − 369) ÷ 20 = 2.25 ppm per year [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** The rate of increase has risen, so the long-term rise is accelerating, consistent with increasing combustion of fossil fuels [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the two features apart: the fluctuation is explained by photosynthesis and respiration, the long-term rise by combustion. Quote data when describing trends.

**8.** Aerobic respiration uses oxygen that was released by photosynthesis [1]. Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide released by respiration (and combustion) [1]. The yearly fluxes are enormous, so this is a major link between autotrophs and heterotrophs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both directions of dependence. A one-way answer usually earns only part of the credit.

**9. (a)** Coarse: 100 − 38 = 62% [1]. Fine: 100 − 71 = 29% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Invertebrates in coarse bags fragment the leaves, increasing surface area for fungi and bacteria [1]. Greater decomposer activity means faster digestion and respiration of the leaf carbon compounds [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Decomposers digest dead organic matter and release phosphate and other ions into the soil [1]. Autotrophs take these up again, so the element is recycled rather than locked in dead material [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each observation to a named process. Naming decomposers as bacteria and fungi, rather than "microbes", is more precise.

**10. (a)** January, February, March, October, November, December [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Sum = −20 − 22 − 15 + 10 + 65 + 110 + 100 + 75 + 30 − 25 − 30 − 25 = +253 g C m⁻² [1]. Positive, so a sink [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Leaves have fallen or light is low, so photosynthesis is low [1]. Respiration by trees, heterotrophs and decomposers continues and exceeds photosynthesis, releasing CO₂ [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Long days, high light and full leaf cover give a high rate of photosynthesis [1]. This greatly exceeds respiration, so net uptake peaks [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** Net uptake would fall, possibly becoming negative [1]. Photosynthesis would be removed or reduced, and burning and decomposition of residues release CO₂, so the site may become a source [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the sum of positive and negative values. Describe the forest in a given month as a source or sink using the net exchange, not the amount of photosynthesis alone.

**11.** Points that can be credited: growing trees take up CO₂ by photosynthesis, so a young forest is a sink [1]. Fossil fuel carbon was stored for millions of years, but the forest stores carbon only while biomass accumulates [1]. As the forest matures, respiration approaches photosynthesis and net uptake falls [1]. Fire, disease or felling can convert the forest into a source [1]. The rate of release from burning gas is rapid, and growth of trees is slow, so offsetting takes years and a large area [1]. A judgement is made, such as supporting the proposal only as a partial, temporary measure alongside reduced combustion [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation tends to gain credit when there are advantages, limitations and a final judgement. A list of facts with no conclusion is likely to fall short.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out units or using energy units for production.
- Subtracting only one form of respiration when the table has two.
- Confusing a sink with a large store of carbon, rather than a net uptake.
- Explaining the seasonal zigzag and the long-term rise with the same reasoning.
- Drawing feeding arrows backwards and omitting decomposers.
- Saying "respiration" without naming the products (CO₂ and water) when explaining lower secondary production.
- Not quoting data in graph descriptions.
- Writing that decomposers "create" nutrients. They release inorganic ions from dead organic matter.
