Revise with the [factors of production study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-factors-of-production/) and the [factors of production revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-factors-of-production-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Show your workings.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define enterprise as a factor of production. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each of these is land, labour, capital or enterprise.

**(a)** A bakery's delivery van. **[1]**
**(b)** The soil on a vegetable farm. **[1]**
**(c)** A chef working in a restaurant kitchen. **[1]**
**(d)** The owner who invests her savings and decides what the business will sell. **[1]**

**3.** Give two examples of labour-intensive activities. **[2]**

**4.** Brightwater Laundry has total yearly costs of 180,000. Wages are 54,000 and machinery costs are 81,000. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate wages as a percentage of total costs. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate machinery costs as a percentage of total costs. **[1]**
**(c)** State whether Brightwater Laundry is more capital-intensive or labour-intensive. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one reason why a rise in wages could lead a business to become more capital-intensive. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one disadvantage of capital-intensive production for a small business. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one way new technology can change the relationship between capital and labour in a bank. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Kinloch Car Wash employs 5 staff to wash cars by hand, each paid 21,000 a year. An automatic washing machine would cost 32,000 a year to run and would need 1 attendant paid 23,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly cost of each method. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the yearly wage per hand-wash worker at which the two methods would cost the same. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse one non-financial reason why Kinloch Car Wash might keep washing cars by hand. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse how a shortage of skilled bricklayers could change the mix of factors of production used by a building firm. **[4]**

**10.** Mirabel Pottery makes handmade mugs. It is considering a moulding machine.

- **Hand method:** 4 potters, each paid 24,000 a year, make 12,000 mugs a year. Handmade mugs sell for 18 each.
- **Machine method:** a machine costing 40,000 a year to run, plus 1 operator paid 26,000, makes 30,000 mugs a year. Machine-made mugs would sell for about 9 each.

**(a)** Calculate the labour and machine cost per mug for each method. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify which method Mirabel Pottery should use. **[6]**

**11.** Tamarind Supermarkets plc employs 400 checkout staff, each paid 20,000 a year. It plans to replace 60% of them with self-service checkouts, which would cost 1.5 million a year to lease and maintain.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly saving after the cost of the self-service checkouts. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Tamarind Supermarkets should make this change. **[9]**

**12.** **(a)** Give one example of land used by a fishing business. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why enterprise is still needed in a highly automated business. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The organising of the other factors of production and the making of decisions [1], by an owner or entrepreneur who takes the risk of the business failing [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Capital [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Land [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Labour [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Enterprise [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The van is capital because it is a man-made resource used to produce the service. Do not confuse capital with the money used to buy it.

**3.** Any two of: hairdressing, care work, tailoring, restaurant service, teaching, fruit picking by hand, cleaning [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 54,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **30%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 81,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **45%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** More capital-intensive [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Higher wages make each worker more expensive compared with machinery [1], so buying machines to do some tasks becomes the cheaper way to produce [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Machinery needs a large investment up front [1], which a small business may have to borrow, adding repayments [1], and the costs continue even if sales fall, so a quiet period could cause cash flow problems [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Online and mobile banking let customers carry out tasks themselves using the bank's software [1], so fewer staff are needed in branches to handle routine payments [1]. The bank's mix moves towards capital, while remaining staff focus on tasks such as advice that need people [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Hand wash: 5 × 21,000 = **105,000** [1]. Automatic: 32,000 + 23,000 = **55,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The two methods cost the same when 5 × wage = 55,000 [1], so wage = 55,000 ÷ 5 = **11,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Customers may believe hand washing is gentler and more thorough [1], so Kinloch can offer a quality service that rivals with machines do not [1]. This may let it charge a higher price or keep loyal customers [1], so revenue could fall if it switched, reducing the benefit of the 50,000 cost saving [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Another valid route is the effect on four staff who would lose their jobs and on the business's local reputation. Either way, link it to a business consequence.

**9.** If bricklayers are hard to find, the firm may have to pay higher wages to attract them [1], making labour more expensive relative to capital [1]. The firm may use more capital, such as prefabricated wall panels or machinery that reduces the bricklaying needed [1], so its mix moves towards capital, which may require new skills from the remaining workers and a larger investment from the owner [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Hand: 4 × 24,000 = 96,000 ÷ 12,000 = **8.00** per mug [1]. Machine: (40,000 + 26,000) = 66,000 ÷ 30,000 = **2.20** per mug [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For the machine: cost per mug falls from 8.00 to 2.20 [1], and output rises from 12,000 to 30,000, so Mirabel could supply larger customers [1]. Against the machine: machine-made mugs would sell for only 9, half the price of handmade ones, because the handmade quality is what customers pay for [1]. Mirabel would need to sell 30,000 mugs, which may be far more than its market will buy [1]. Three potters would lose their jobs, and the business loses its craft identity [1]. Judgement: for example, keep the hand method, because the profit per mug is 10 (18 − 8) against 6.80 (9 − 2.20), and it does not need to find buyers for 18,000 extra mugs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A lower cost per unit does not settle the question when the selling price changes as well. Compare profit per mug and the demand needed.

**11. (a)** 60% of 400 = 240 staff; 240 × 20,000 = 4,800,000 [1]; 4,800,000 − 1,500,000 = **3,300,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For the change: it saves 3.3 million a year [1], which could be used to lower prices and compete with rivals or to raise profit [1]. Self-service checkouts can reduce queues at busy times, because one staff member can supervise several machines [1]. Against: 240 jobs are lost, which may lead to bad publicity and lower morale among remaining staff [1]. Some customers, such as older shoppers, may prefer staffed checkouts, so they could switch to rivals [1]. Machines can break down, and losses from goods not scanned may rise without a cashier [1], which would reduce the saving [1]. Judgement: for example, go ahead, because the saving is very large for a large business, but keep enough staffed checkouts for customers who want them [1] and redeploy staff to roles such as online orders to reduce job losses [1]. **[9]**

**12. (a)** Any one of: the sea or rivers fished; the fish stock itself; the harbour site [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Machines do not decide what to produce or how much [1], so someone must still organise the factors, make decisions about investment and respond to changes in demand [1], and someone must take the risk that the large investment in machinery does not pay off [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing capital as money.
- Deciding capital- or labour-intensity from business size instead of cost shares.
- Comparing costs per unit but ignoring a change in selling price or demand.
- Treating job losses or quality as the only issue, or ignoring them altogether.
- Forgetting the role and risk of enterprise when the mix changes.
