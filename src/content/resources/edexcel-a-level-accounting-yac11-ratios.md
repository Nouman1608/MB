This guide covers outcomes **1.5.1 Ratios**, **1.5.2 The profitability, liquidity and use of asset ratios in appraising sole traders and partnership financial statements** and **1.5.3 Use of ratios to make future financial projections**. They make up Topic 1.5, Analysis of accounting statements, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

Use it with the [analysis of accounting statements revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-analysis-of-accounting-statements-revision-notes/) and the [ratios practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-ratios-practice/). The statements the ratios come from are taught in the [sole trader guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-financial-statements-of-sole-traders/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5.1 | Ten ratios | Calculate each ratio from financial statements |
| 1.5.2 | Profitability, liquidity and use of assets in appraising sole traders and partnerships | Interpret results, compare years, and make judgements |
| 1.5.3 | Use of ratios to make future financial projections | Use past ratios to forecast figures for a future year |

## The ten ratios

Show the formula, the substitution and the unit (percentage, times, ratio or days) in every answer.

| Group | Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Gross profit as a percentage of revenue (margin) | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| | Percentage mark-up | Gross profit ÷ cost of sales × 100 |
| | Profit for the year as a percentage of revenue | Profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100 |
| | Return on capital employed (ROCE) | Profit for the year ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| Use of assets | Non-current assets to revenue | Revenue ÷ non-current assets (times) |
| | Inventory turnover | Cost of sales ÷ average inventory (times); or average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 (days) |
| | Trade receivables collection period | Trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 (days) |
| | Trade payables payment period | Trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 (days) |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | Current assets : current liabilities |
| | Liquid (acid test) ratio | (Current assets − inventory) : current liabilities |

Capital employed for a sole trader or partnership is usually capital (including current accounts) plus non-current liabilities. It equals total assets less current liabilities. State the definition you use. Average inventory = (opening + closing) ÷ 2. If credit sales or credit purchases are not given, use revenue or cost of purchases and state that assumption.

## Worked example: Kiln & Co

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 200,000 | 240,000 |
| Cost of sales | 130,000 | 156,000 |
| Gross profit | 70,000 | 84,000 |
| Expenses | 48,000 | 54,000 |
| **Profit for the year** | **22,000** | **30,000** |
| Credit sales | 160,000 | 192,000 |
| Credit purchases | 125,000 | 150,000 |
| Average inventory | 18,000 | 22,000 |
| Non-current assets | 100,000 | 110,000 |
| Inventory (year end) | 20,000 | 24,000 |
| Trade receivables | 20,000 | 28,000 |
| Bank | 4,000 | 6,000 |
| Trade payables | 14,000 | 16,500 |

Current assets are 44,000 in 2025 (20,000 + 20,000 + 4,000) and 58,000 in 2026 (24,000 + 28,000 + 6,000). Capital employed = non-current assets + current assets − current liabilities, so 2025: 100,000 + 44,000 − 14,000 = 130,000 and 2026: 110,000 + 58,000 − 16,500 = 151,500.

| Ratio | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit % | 70,000 ÷ 200,000 = 35.0% | 84,000 ÷ 240,000 = 35.0% |
| Mark-up | 70,000 ÷ 130,000 = 53.8% | 84,000 ÷ 156,000 = 53.8% |
| Profit for the year % | 22,000 ÷ 200,000 = 11.0% | 30,000 ÷ 240,000 = 12.5% |
| ROCE | 22,000 ÷ 130,000 = 16.9% | 30,000 ÷ 151,500 = 19.8% |
| Non-current assets to revenue | 200,000 ÷ 100,000 = 2.00 times | 240,000 ÷ 110,000 = 2.18 times |
| Inventory turnover | 130,000 ÷ 18,000 = 7.2 times | 156,000 ÷ 22,000 = 7.1 times |
| Inventory days | 18,000 ÷ 130,000 × 365 = 50.5 days | 22,000 ÷ 156,000 × 365 = 51.5 days |
| Receivables collection | 20,000 ÷ 160,000 × 365 = 45.6 days | 28,000 ÷ 192,000 × 365 = 53.2 days |
| Payables payment | 14,000 ÷ 125,000 × 365 = 40.9 days | 16,500 ÷ 150,000 × 365 = 40.2 days |
| Current ratio | 44,000 : 14,000 = 3.14 : 1 | 58,000 : 16,500 = 3.52 : 1 |
| Liquid ratio | 24,000 : 14,000 = 1.71 : 1 | 34,000 : 16,500 = 2.06 : 1 |

## 1.5.2 Interpreting the results

Good interpretation compares year with year (or business with business), says what changed, gives **reasons**, and reaches a judgement.

**Profitability.** The gross profit percentage and mark-up are unchanged at 35.0% and 53.8%, so the business kept the same margin on each sale. The profit percentage rose from 11.0% to 12.5%. As gross margin is constant, the improvement came from expenses growing slower than revenue: expenses were 24.0% of revenue in 2025 (48,000 ÷ 200,000) and 22.5% in 2026 (54,000 ÷ 240,000). ROCE rose from 16.9% to 19.8%, so the owner earned more on the money invested. Revenue grew by 20% and profit by 36.4% (8,000 ÷ 22,000).

**Use of assets.** Non-current assets generated 2.18 of revenue for each 1 invested, up from 2.00, which suggests the assets were used more productively. Inventory turnover was almost unchanged (7.2 to 7.1 times), so stock is turning at the same pace. The receivables collection period lengthened from 45.6 to 53.2 days. This is a concern: customers are taking about a week longer to pay, which ties up cash and raises the risk of irrecoverable debts. The payables period shortened slightly, from 40.9 to 40.2 days. The business pays suppliers about 13 days before customers pay it (53.2 − 40.2), which puts pressure on cash.

**Liquidity.** The current ratio rose from 3.14 : 1 to 3.52 : 1 and the liquid ratio from 1.71 : 1 to 2.06 : 1, so the business could comfortably meet short-term debts. Very high ratios may indicate idle resources, however, particularly as part of the rise is slow-paying receivables, which are not cash.

**Judgement.** Overall performance improved: profit and ROCE are higher and liquidity is strong. The main weakness is the longer time customers take to pay. A recommendation might be to offer a small discount for early payment or to chase overdue accounts.

## Limits of ratio analysis

- Ratios use historical figures and do not predict the future.
- Year-end figures may not represent the year, for example if the business is seasonal.
- Different businesses use different accounting policies, so comparisons are unreliable without adjustment.
- Ratios ignore non-financial factors such as service quality, staff and the economic climate.
- Inflation affects comparisons over time.
- A ratio shows *what* changed but not *why*; further investigation is needed.

## 1.5.3 Using ratios to make future projections

If ratios are expected to stay similar, they can be used to estimate future statements.

**Example: Kiln & Co 2027.** Revenue is forecast to rise by 10% to 264,000, of which 80% is on credit. The gross profit percentage stays at 35%, the receivables period at 50 days and inventory turnover at 7.1.

- Gross profit = 264,000 × 35% = **92,400**; cost of sales = 264,000 − 92,400 = **171,600**.
- Credit sales = 264,000 × 80% = 211,200. Receivables = 211,200 × 50 ÷ 365 = **28,932** (rounded).
- Inventory = cost of sales ÷ turnover = 171,600 ÷ 7.1 = **24,169** (rounded).

A forecast like this is only as good as its assumptions. Test them: is the 10% growth realistic, can the business collect in 50 days when it now takes 53, and will costs change?

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong base: mark-up uses cost of sales, margin uses revenue.
- Using total sales for the receivables period when credit sales are given, or the reverse.
- Not stating the unit: days, times, percentage or ratio.
- Using closing inventory instead of average inventory, without saying so.
- Presenting a ratio without interpretation, such as "the current ratio is 3.52".
- Describing the change without a reason, a consequence, or a judgement.
- Treating a high current ratio as always good.
- Using 360 days when the question uses 365 (or the reverse).

## Exam technique

- Write the formula, the substitution and the answer, with units.
- For **comment**, state what the ratio shows and why that matters for the business.
- For **evaluate**, deal with profitability, liquidity and use of assets, give a strength and a weakness, and finish with a judgement or recommendation.
- When asked for **future projections**, state each assumption beside the figure.
- Keep to the data. Do not invent causes that the data cannot support; use words such as "may" or "this could be because".
