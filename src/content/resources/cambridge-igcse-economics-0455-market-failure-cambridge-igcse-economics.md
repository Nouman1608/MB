## 2.10.1 What market failure means

**Market failure** is a situation in which the price mechanism, left to itself, does not allocate resources in the best way for society as a whole. Too many resources go into some products and too few into others, or a product that people value is not supplied at all.

The cause is almost always the same: the people taking a decision look only at what affects them. Society, however, includes everyone else too.

### Three layers of costs and benefits

| Layer | Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Private | Paid by the person or firm taking the decision | Received by the person or firm taking the decision |
| External | Fall on third parties who are not part of the decision and are not paid compensation | Received by third parties who do not pay for them |
| Social | Private costs + external costs | Private benefits + external benefits |

A **third party** is anyone outside the transaction: neighbours, other road users, other firms, future generations.

### Classifying an unfamiliar case

**Worked example 1 (invented).** Amira drives 20 km to work each day instead of taking the bus.

| Item | Who bears or gains it | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel and parking, 6.00 a day | Amira | Private cost |
| Exhaust fumes and extra traffic delay for others, valued at 2.50 a day | Other residents and road users | External cost |
| Time saved and comfort, valued by Amira at 7.00 a day | Amira | Private benefit |

```
Social cost    = 6.00 + 2.50 = 8.50 a day
Social benefit = 7.00 + 0    = 7.00 a day
```

Amira drives because her private benefit (7.00) is greater than her private cost (6.00). For society the trip costs 8.50 and yields 7.00, a net loss of 1.50 a day. Multiply this by thousands of drivers and the market produces too much car use. This is the general logic of an external cost.

## 2.10.2 The causes

The syllabus names five areas: public goods; merit and demerit goods; external costs and external benefits; abuse of monopoly power; and factor immobility. Below, each of the paired areas is split so that every cause has its own heading.

### 1. Public goods

A **public good** has two features:

- **Non-excludable:** once it is provided, people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting.
- **Non-rival:** one person's use does not reduce the amount left for others.

A flood wall protecting a riverside town is an example. Every house behind it is protected whether or not its owner paid, and protecting one house does not reduce the protection of the next. Each household has an incentive to be a **free rider**: to wait for others to pay. A private firm cannot collect enough revenue, so the market supplies none, although the town values the wall highly.

Check both features before calling something a public good. A school place is excludable (a school can refuse a pupil who does not pay) and rival (a full class cannot take more), so education is a merit good, not a public good.

### 2. Merit goods

A **merit good** is one that society judges people should consume more of, because the benefit is greater than consumers recognise. Examples include education, health checks and vaccinations. Two reasons explain under-consumption:

- **Information failure:** consumers underestimate the private benefit, for example the long-term value of a dental check-up.
- **External benefits:** others gain too, so social benefit is above the private benefit the buyer weighs up.

Low income can make the problem worse, because people who would like the good cannot pay the market price.

### 3. Demerit goods

A **demerit good** is one that society judges people should consume less of, because the harm is greater than consumers recognise. Examples include cigarettes, alcohol and sugary drinks. Consumers may underestimate the damage to their own health, and use often harms others too, such as second-hand smoke.

### 4. External costs

Here the problem is in production or consumption that harms third parties. A cement works that releases dust onto nearby farms pays for its fuel and wages but not for the farmers' lost crops. Its price does not include the full social cost, so too much is produced and consumed.

### 5. External benefits

Here third parties gain without paying. A farmer who keeps bees for honey also pollinates a neighbour's orchard. The farmer decides how many hives to keep from the honey revenue alone, so the number of hives is lower than society would choose.

### 6. Abuse of monopoly power

A firm that is the only seller may restrict output and charge a high price, because buyers have nowhere else to go. Resources are under-allocated to the product, and consumers lose choice. If the monopolist also lacks pressure to cut costs, resources are wasted inside the firm.

### 7. Factor immobility

Resources may fail to move to where they are needed.

- **Occupational immobility:** workers lack the skills for available jobs, so a laid-off shipyard welder cannot easily become a nurse.
- **Geographical immobility:** workers cannot move to where jobs are, because of housing costs, family ties or the cost of moving.

Land and capital can also be immobile: a specialised machine may have no other use. The result is idle resources in one place alongside shortages in another.

## 2.10.3 Consequences: the direction of misallocation

The syllabus asks you to explain which way resources are misallocated.

| Cause | Market outcome | Compared with what is best for society |
|---|---|---|
| Demerit good | Over-consumption | Too many resources used |
| Good with external costs | Over-consumption and over-production | Too many resources used |
| Merit good | Under-consumption | Too few resources used |
| Good with external benefits | Under-consumption | Too few resources used |
| Public good | No provision | No resources used at all |
| Monopoly abuse | Output restricted, price high | Too few resources used |
| Factor immobility | Unemployed factors alongside shortages | Resources idle or in the wrong place |

**Worked example 2 (invented).** A town has 1,000 young people. A computing course costs 400. Each person's private benefit and the external benefit (to employers and taxpayers) are shown.

| Group | People | Private benefit each | External benefit each | Social benefit each |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 300 | 500 | 150 | 650 |
| B | 450 | 320 | 150 | 470 |
| C | 250 | 200 | 150 | 350 |

```
Market: only group A has private benefit above 400, so 300 enrol
Society: groups A and B have social benefit above 400, so 750 should enrol
Under-consumption = 750 - 300 = 450 places, or 60% of the best level
```

The 450 people in group B are the gap. They would pay up to 320, but the course costs 400. A subsidy of at least 80 per place would close that gap, although government intervention itself belongs to section 2.11.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Explain why a free market may lead to over-consumption of sugary drinks. [4]

> Sugary drinks are a demerit good [identifies the cause]. Consumers may underestimate the long-term harm to their health, such as tooth decay, so they buy more than they would with full information [information failure, developed]. Consumption also creates external costs, because treating diet-related illness uses health services paid for by taxpayers [external cost with a named third party]. As the price reflects only private costs, consumption is higher than the level best for society, so too many resources are used to produce the drinks [consequence stated as misallocation].

The final sentence turns the cause into the consequence the syllabus names.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any government-provided good a public good. Use the two tests, non-excludable and non-rival.
- Mixing up merit goods (consumers underestimate benefits) with goods that simply have high demand.
- Treating a firm's wages or raw materials as external costs. They are private costs because the firm pays them.
- Writing "the market fails" without saying which way: over-consumption or under-consumption.
- Drawing demand and supply diagrams for market failure when the question does not ask for one. The syllabus does not require them, and time spent drawing is lost from explanation.
- Giving examples of market failure from outside the syllabus list, such as income inequality. Keep to the areas named in 2.10.2.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** give a precise meaning; for a public good name both features.
- **Explain:** cause, then mechanism, then consequence, in linked sentences.
- **Analyse:** develop a longer chain, for example from external cost to price to output to misallocation.
- **Discuss:** give both sides, such as whether a good with some external benefits is really under-consumed if many people already buy it, and reach a judgement.
- **Calculate:** show social cost or benefit by adding private and external values, and state units.
