## The metal-aqua ions

When a salt of one of these metals dissolves in water, six water molecules act as ligands. Each forms a co-ordinate bond from a lone pair on its oxygen atom to the metal ion, giving an octahedral complex.

| Ion | Solution colour |
|---|---|
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | pale green |
| [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | blue |
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ | yellow-brown as usually seen |
| [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ | colourless |

Colour is a first clue only. Confirm the ion with a reagent.

## Acidity of metal-aqua ions

A metal-aqua ion can give a proton to a water molecule. It acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid:

```
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]⁺ + H₃O⁺
```

The H⁺ comes from one of the water ligands, so one H₂O ligand becomes an OH⁻ ligand and the charge on the complex falls by one.

### Why 3+ ions are more acidic than 2+ ions

Use charge/size ratio in your answer.

1. The M³⁺ ion has a higher charge than the M²⁺ ion and is smaller, so it has a **higher charge/size ratio** (charge density).
2. It is therefore **more polarising**: it attracts electron density from the oxygen atoms of the water ligands more strongly.
3. This **weakens the O–H bonds** in the co-ordinated water.
4. So H⁺ is released more easily, the equilibrium lies further to the right, and the solution has a **lower pH**.

### Worked example 1: comparing two iron solutions

Two solutions have the same concentration: iron(II) sulfate and iron(III) sulfate. Which has the lower pH, and why?

- Fe³⁺ has a 3+ charge and a smaller radius than Fe²⁺. Its charge/size ratio is greater.
- It polarises the co-ordinated water more, so the O–H bonds are weaker.
- [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ releases H⁺ more readily, so **iron(III) sulfate has the lower pH**.

## How bases make precipitates

All three bases in this section (OH⁻, NH₃ and CO₃²⁻) can remove H⁺ ions from the water ligands. Each H⁺ removed lowers the charge on the complex by one. When the complex reaches **zero charge** it is no longer attracted strongly to water molecules, so it comes out of solution as a **precipitate**.

- M²⁺ needs two H⁺ removed: [M(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]
- M³⁺ needs three H⁺ removed: [M(H₂O)₃(OH)₃]

Write these neutral hydroxides with square brackets and no charge. They can also be written M(OH)₂ and M(OH)₃.

## Reactions with hydroxide ions, OH⁻

Add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop, then in excess.

| Ion | Few drops of NaOH(aq) | Excess NaOH(aq) |
|---|---|---|
| Fe²⁺ | green precipitate, [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]; darkens to brown on standing in air | no further change |
| Cu²⁺ | pale blue precipitate, [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] | no further change |
| Fe³⁺ | brown precipitate, [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] | no further change |
| Al³⁺ | white precipitate, [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] | precipitate dissolves to give a colourless solution |

The iron(II) precipitate darkens because air oxidises iron(II) to iron(III) at the surface.

Equations:

```
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H₂O
[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H₂O
```

## Amphoteric aluminium hydroxide

An amphoteric substance reacts with both acids and bases. Aluminium hydroxide is the example the specification gives.

With excess OH⁻, a fourth H⁺ is removed, giving a soluble negative ion:

```
[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + OH⁻ → [Al(H₂O)₂(OH)₄]⁻ + H₂O
```

The product is also written [Al(OH)₄]⁻.

With acid, the hydroxide ligands gain H⁺ and the aqua ion re-forms:

```
[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺
```

### Worked example 2: hydroxide needed for aluminium

A solution contains 0.0150 mol of [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺. How many moles of OH⁻ are needed (a) to precipitate all of it and (b) to redissolve the precipitate completely?

```
(a) Al³⁺ : OH⁻ = 1 : 3     n(OH⁻) = 3 × 0.0150 = 0.0450 mol
(b) [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] : OH⁻ = 1 : 1   extra n(OH⁻) = 0.0150 mol
    total from the start = 0.0450 + 0.0150 = 0.0600 mol
```

## Reactions with ammonia, NH₃

Ammonia does two jobs here.

- **As a base**, in small amounts, it removes H⁺ from water ligands. You see the same hydroxide precipitates as with NaOH.
- **As a ligand**, in excess, it replaces water ligands on copper, so the copper precipitate dissolves.

| Ion | Few drops of NH₃(aq) | Excess NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|
| Fe²⁺ | green precipitate | no further change |
| Cu²⁺ | pale blue precipitate | precipitate dissolves to give a deep blue solution, [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ |
| Fe³⁺ | brown precipitate | no further change |
| Al³⁺ | white precipitate | no further change |

As a base, ammonia becomes the ammonium ion:

```
[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3NH₃ → [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3NH₄⁺
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2NH₄⁺
```

In excess, ammonia replaces the hydroxide and some water ligands on copper:

```
[Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2H₂O + 2OH⁻
```

The [transition metals study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/) shows the overall substitution from [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ and explains why the co-ordination number stays at 6.

Aluminium hydroxide does **not** dissolve in excess ammonia. Ammonia is a weak base, so it does not remove the fourth H⁺ the way excess OH⁻ does.

## Reactions with carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻

Add sodium carbonate solution. Here the 2+ and 3+ ions behave differently, and this is the part of the section you must explain, not just describe.

| Ion | Na₂CO₃(aq) | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Fe²⁺ | green precipitate | FeCO₃ |
| Cu²⁺ | blue-green precipitate | CuCO₃ |
| Fe³⁺ | brown precipitate and effervescence | [Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] and CO₂ |
| Al³⁺ | white precipitate and effervescence | [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] and CO₂ |

**2+ ions** are only weakly acidic. They are not acidic enough to protonate carbonate ions to carbon dioxide, so the metal carbonate precipitates:

```
[Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → FeCO₃ + 6H₂O
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CuCO₃ + 6H₂O
```

**3+ ions** are more acidic, because of their higher charge/size ratio. They give up H⁺ to the carbonate ions, which form carbon dioxide and water. The metal ion is left as the neutral hydroxide. No iron(III) or aluminium carbonate forms.

### Worked example 3: balancing the 3+ carbonate equation

Write the equation for [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ with carbonate ions.

```
Step 1: each Al³⁺ loses 3H⁺ to reach [Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃]
Step 2: each CO₃²⁻ takes 2H⁺ to give CO₂ + H₂O
Step 3: 2 Al³⁺ release 6H⁺, which 3 CO₃²⁻ take up
2[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3CO₃²⁻ → 2[Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃] + 3CO₂ + 3H₂O
```

Check charge: left 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0; right 0. The iron(III) equation has the same pattern.

### Worked example 4: mass of carbonate precipitate

25.0 cm³ of 0.160 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate is added to excess sodium carbonate solution. Calculate the mass of CuCO₃ formed. (Ar: Cu = 63.5, C = 12.0, O = 16.0)

```
n(Cu²⁺) = 0.160 × 25.0/1000 = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol
Cu²⁺ : CuCO₃ = 1 : 1
Mr(CuCO₃) = 63.5 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 123.5
mass = 4.00 × 10⁻³ × 123.5 = 0.494 g
```

## Required practical 11

The specification's wording is: carry out simple test-tube reactions to identify transition metal ions in aqueous solution.

A sound method:

1. Put about 1 cm³ of the unknown solution in a test tube. Note its colour.
2. Add sodium hydroxide solution **one drop at a time**, shaking after each drop. Record the colour of any precipitate.
3. Keep adding until the reagent is in **excess**, and record whether the precipitate dissolves.
4. Repeat steps 1 to 3 with fresh samples and aqueous ammonia, then sodium carbonate solution.
5. Leave any green iron precipitate to stand, and watch for darkening at the surface.

Safety: wear eye protection. Sodium hydroxide is corrosive, and ammonia solution is an irritant with a sharp smell, so use it in a well-ventilated room.

Fresh samples matter. If you add NH₃ to a tube that already holds NaOH, you cannot say which reagent caused what.

### Worked example 5: identifying an unknown

Solution P is colourless. It gives a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH(aq) that dissolves in excess. With Na₂CO₃(aq) it gives a white precipitate and bubbles.

- Colourless and a white precipitate: Al³⁺ (of the four ions in this section).
- Dissolves in excess NaOH: amphoteric hydroxide, so Al³⁺ again.
- Effervescence with carbonate: a 3+ ion.
- **P contains [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺.**

A white precipitate with NaOH can also come from Group 2 ions such as Mg²⁺, which you meet in [Group 2](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-group-2-the-alkaline-earth-metals/). Only the aluminium precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH.

### A classification key for the four aqua ions

Each step uses one feature only.

1. (a) The solution is colourless → Al³⁺. (b) The solution is coloured → go to 2.
2. (a) The solution is blue → Cu²⁺. (b) The solution is not blue → go to 3.
3. (a) NaOH(aq) gives a green precipitate → Fe²⁺. (b) NaOH(aq) gives a brown precipitate → Fe³⁺.

## Common errors

- Writing [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃ with no water. Either balance with 3H₂O and the full complex, or use the simple formulas consistently on both sides.
- Giving a charge to the neutral hydroxide, such as [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]²⁺.
- Saying iron(III) carbonate or aluminium carbonate precipitates. They do not form here.
- Forgetting the effervescence with 3+ ions and carbonate. "Bubbles" or "fizzing" is a separate observation.
- Saying the copper precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH. It dissolves in excess ammonia.
- Saying aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess ammonia. It dissolves only in excess NaOH (or in acid).
- Explaining acidity with "Fe³⁺ is bigger". It is smaller, with a greater charge.
- Describing a solution as "clear" when you mean "colourless". A blue solution is clear too.
