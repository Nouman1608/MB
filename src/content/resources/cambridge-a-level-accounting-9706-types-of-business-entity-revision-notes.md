These notes cover 1.1 Types of business entity in Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting 9706, syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028: AS Level content examined in Papers 1 and 2.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sole trader | Business owned by one person, who takes all profits and risks |
| Partnership | Business owned by two or more partners sharing profits, usually under a partnership agreement |
| Limited company | Business owned by shareholders, managed by directors, with its own legal identity |
| Public limited company (plc) | Limited company whose shares can be offered to the public |
| Separate legal entity | An organisation that can own assets, make contracts, sue and be sued in its own name |
| Unlimited liability | Owners' private assets can be taken to pay business debts |
| Joint liability | Each partner can be held responsible for the whole of the firm's debts |
| Limited liability | Shareholders can lose no more than the amount they agreed to pay for their shares |
| Stakeholder | Anyone with an interest in the business: owners, lenders, suppliers, employees, customers |
| Secured loan | Loan backed by a claim on a named asset |
| Unsecured loan | Loan with no claim on a specific asset |
| Bank overdraft | Short-term borrowing on a current account up to an agreed limit |
| Payment by instalments | Paying for an asset in regular amounts over time |
| Leasing/rental | Paying to use an asset without owning it |
| Trade credit | Buying goods now and paying the supplier later |
| Debenture | Long-term loan to a company, paying fixed interest |
| Rights issue | New shares offered to existing shareholders, usually at a discount |
| Bonus issue | Free shares created from reserves; raises no cash |
| Matching (of finance) | Funding short-term needs with short-term finance and long-term assets with long-term finance |

## The three entities in brief

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate in law | No | No | Yes |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited and joint | Limited |
| Control | Owner | Partners | Directors, accountable to shareholders |
| Continuity | Ends with owner | Changes with partners | Continues |
| Finance | Owner and borrowing | Partners and borrowing | Shares, debentures and borrowing |
| Main advantage | Full control and all the profit | More capital, skills and shared workload | Limited liability; easier to raise large sums |
| Main drawback | Unlimited liability; limited capital | Shared profit; disputes; liability for partners' decisions | Formation costs, regulation, loss of control for founders |

## Condensed recall

- The business entity concept separates business and owner in the accounts, but only a company is separate in law.
- Creditors of a failed partnership can pursue any partner for the whole shortfall.
- Lenders to small companies often ask for security or directors' personal guarantees, because shareholders' liability is limited.
- Shareholders in a plc can usually sell shares more easily than owners of other entities can sell their stake.
- Questions on Limited Liability Partnerships will not be set.

## Choosing finance

| Need | Suitable sources |
|---|---|
| Short-term, fluctuating (seasonal inventory, wages before customers pay) | Overdraft; trade credit |
| Medium-term asset (equipment, vehicle) | Payment by instalments; leasing; medium-term loan |
| Long-term asset (premises) | Secured loan; for a company, shares or debentures |
| Large expansion for a company | Share issue, rights issue or debentures |

Questions to ask in any finance decision: How long is the money needed? What does it cost? Is there an asset to offer as security? Who keeps ownership and control?

## Exam traps

- Calling a sole trader's business a separate legal entity.
- Giving partnerships limited liability.
- Treating a bonus issue as a source of finance.
- Using an overdraft for a long-term asset.
- Forgetting that trade credit can cost a lost cash discount.
- Listing advantages without the viewpoint (owner, lender, employee).
- Recommending without a balance of reasons.

## Self-test

1. Which type of business entity is a separate legal entity?
2. What does joint liability mean for a partner?
3. State one advantage of being a sole trader.
4. State one disadvantage of a partnership.
5. Why might a bank ask a company director for a personal guarantee?
6. Distinguish a secured loan from an unsecured loan.
7. Why is an overdraft unsuitable for buying premises?
8. Give one advantage of leasing a machine instead of buying it.
9. Does a bonus issue raise finance? Explain.
10. A supplier offers 3% discount for payment within 10 days on a $2 500 invoice. What does taking the full credit period cost?
11. Name the type of entity whose shares may be offered to the public.
12. State one reason a sole trader might convert the business into a limited company.

### Answers

1. A limited company.
2. The partner can be required to pay all of the firm's debts, not just a share, if the other partners cannot pay.
3. Any one: keeps all the profit; full control; quick decisions; simple and cheap to set up; privacy of accounts.
4. Any one: unlimited liability; profits are shared; disagreements; each partner is bound by the others' decisions.
5. Shareholders' liability is limited, so the bank cannot otherwise claim the owners' private assets if the company fails.
6. A secured loan gives the lender a claim on a named asset if repayments are missed; an unsecured loan does not, so it usually costs more.
7. It is short-term and can be withdrawn at short notice, while premises are a long-term asset; finance should match the life of the asset.
8. Any one: no large initial outlay; maintenance may be included; equipment can be upgraded when it becomes outdated.
9. No. It converts reserves into share capital; no cash comes into the company.
10. 3% × $2 500 = $75 of discount given up.
11. A public limited company (plc).
12. Any one: to gain limited liability; to raise more capital by issuing shares; to give the business continuity.
