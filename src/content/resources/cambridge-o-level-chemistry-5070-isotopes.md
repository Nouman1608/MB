Section 2.3 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has four outcomes. You must "define isotopes as different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons"; state that isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties "because they have the same number of electrons and therefore the same electronic configuration"; interpret and use symbols for atoms and ions, such as ¹²₆C and ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻; and calculate the relative atomic mass of an element from the masses and abundances of its isotopes. All four are required for every 5070 candidate.

The [Atomic Structure overview for O Level 5070](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-atomic-structure/) covers isotopes alongside the rest of the topic. This guide gives each outcome more space, with new worked examples, a reverse calculation and the wording that wins the "explain" marks.

## Defining isotopes

**Isotopes** are different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Every part of the definition matters:

- "**same element**" and "**same number of protons**" say the same thing: the proton number fixes the element.
- "**different numbers of neutrons**" is the difference, and it means the isotopes have different **mass numbers**.

Isotopes are named with the element and the mass number: carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 are three isotopes of carbon. All have 6 protons; they have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons respectively.

Hydrogen shows the idea most clearly. Its three isotopes have mass numbers 1, 2 and 3. Each has one proton and one electron; they have 0, 1 and 2 neutrons.

### Two things isotopes are not

- They are not different elements. Changing the number of neutrons never changes the element.
- They are not ions. Ions differ in their number of **electrons**; isotopes differ in their number of **neutrons**.

## Why isotopes have the same chemical properties

Chemical reactions involve **electrons**, particularly the outer-shell electrons. Isotopes of an element have:

1. the same number of protons, so
2. the same number of electrons in their neutral atoms, so
3. the same electronic configuration, including the same number of outer electrons.

So they react in exactly the same way. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both have the configuration 2,8,7, and both form Cl⁻ ions and the same compounds.

The extra neutrons change only the **mass** of the atom. That can make a small difference to physical properties that depend on mass, such as the density of the element, but not to chemical reactions.

A full-mark explanation states the chain: same protons → same number of electrons → same electronic configuration → same chemical properties. Writing "they are the same element" on its own is not enough.

## Interpreting nuclide symbols

A symbol such as ²⁷₁₃Al carries three pieces of information:

- the **top number** (left superscript) is the **mass number**, 27
- the **bottom number** (left subscript) is the **proton number**, 13
- the letters give the element, aluminium

For an **ion**, the charge is written top right: ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻ is a chloride ion with mass number 35.

**Worked example 1.** For each particle, give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons.

| Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¹³₆C | 6 | 13 − 6 = 7 | 6 |
| ³⁷₁₇Cl | 17 | 20 | 17 |
| ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺ | 26 | 30 | 26 − 3 = 23 |
| ¹⁸₈O²⁻ | 8 | 10 | 8 + 2 = 10 |

Notice that iron (proton number 26) is beyond the 1 to 20 range for configurations, but you can still count its particles from the symbol.

**Using symbols in the other direction.** You may be told the particles and asked to write the symbol. A particle with 12 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons has mass number 26, is magnesium (proton number 12), and has charge 12 − 10 = 2+: so ²⁶₁₂Mg²⁺.

### Identifying isotopes in a list

Two symbols show isotopes of the same element when the **bottom numbers are equal** and the **top numbers differ**. In the list ⁴⁰₁₈Ar, ⁴⁰₂₀Ca, ⁴²₂₀Ca, ³⁹₁₉K, the isotopes are ⁴⁰₂₀Ca and ⁴²₂₀Ca. Argon-40 and calcium-40 have the same mass number, but different proton numbers, so they are different elements.

## Calculating relative atomic mass from isotopes

The **relative atomic mass**, Ar, of an element is the average mass of its atoms, taking into account the abundance of each isotope, on a scale on which an atom of carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12. (Section 3.2 defines Ar formally.) Because it is an **average**, Ar is usually not a whole number.

Method:

1. Multiply each isotope's mass by its percentage abundance.
2. Add these products.
3. Divide by 100 (or by the total abundance, if the percentages do not add to 100).

**Worked example 2 (three isotopes).** Magnesium has three isotopes: magnesium-24 (78.99%), magnesium-25 (10.00%) and magnesium-26 (11.01%). Calculate the relative atomic mass of magnesium to one decimal place.

Ar = (24 × 78.99 + 25 × 10.00 + 26 × 11.01) ÷ 100
= (1895.76 + 250.00 + 286.26) ÷ 100
= 2432.02 ÷ 100
= 24.32, which is **24.3** to one decimal place.

**Sense check:** the answer must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses, and nearest the most abundant one. 24.3 is just above 24, because magnesium-24 is by far the most common isotope.

**Worked example 3 (two isotopes, small abundance).** Lithium is 7.6% lithium-6 and 92.4% lithium-7.

Ar = (6 × 7.6 + 7 × 92.4) ÷ 100 = (45.6 + 646.8) ÷ 100 = 6.924 = **6.9**.

### Working backwards

Some questions give the Ar and ask for the abundances. Let the percentage of one isotope be *x*; the other is (100 − *x*).

**Worked example 4.** An invented element R has two isotopes, R-50 and R-52, and a relative atomic mass of 50.5. Calculate the percentage of R-50.

50*x* + 52(100 − *x*) = 50.5 × 100
50*x* + 5200 − 52*x* = 5050
−2*x* = −150
*x* = **75%**, so R-52 is 25%.

Sense check: 50.5 is closer to 50 than to 52, so R-50 must be the more abundant isotope.

### Mass spectrum-style data

Data may be shown as a bar chart of relative abundance against mass. If the bars do not add up to 100, divide by their total instead. For example, bars of height 3 at mass 35 and 1 at mass 37 give Ar = (35 × 3 + 37 × 1) ÷ 4 = 142 ÷ 4 = 35.5.

## Common mistakes

- Defining isotopes as "atoms with the same proton number but different mass numbers" and then leaving out **of the same element**, or saying "different numbers of protons".
- Explaining identical chemistry with "same protons" alone, without electrons and electronic configuration.
- Mixing up top and bottom numbers in a symbol.
- Forgetting to divide by 100, giving an answer such as 2432.
- Giving a final Ar outside the range of the isotope masses: always sense check.
- Rounding too early. Keep full values until the last step.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: learn the syllabus wording exactly.
- **Explain** (same chemical properties): give the full chain to the electronic configuration.
- **Calculate**: show the expression with every multiplication; method marks are available even if the arithmetic slips. Give the answer to the precision asked for.
- **Deduce** from symbols: write protons, neutrons and electrons as separate numbers.
