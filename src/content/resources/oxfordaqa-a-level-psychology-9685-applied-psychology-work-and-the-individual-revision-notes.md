These notes condense the [work and the individual study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-applied-psychology-work-and-the-individual/), which has the full explanations and worked paragraphs.

They cover section 3.4.3 Applied psychology: work and the individual in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Psychology (9685) specification, Version 3.1 (International A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards). The specification places it in Section C of Unit 4: Approaches and application, a 1 hour 30 minute written exam worth 90 marks and 30% of the International A-level, with 30 marks for this section. Test yourself afterwards with the [work and the individual practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-applied-psychology-work-and-the-individual-practice/), and track progress on the [9685 checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/) and [9685 course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/psychology/).

## What the specification lists

The four bullets of 3.4.3 are your checklist:

1. **Group processes and individuals**: social facilitation, social loafing; group polarisation, risky shift, groupthink; deindividuation; social identity theory, in-group favouritism and out-group bias.
2. **Communication at work**: eye contact, facial expression, personal space; cultural universals in facial expression; cultural differences in personal space; e-mail: lack of non-verbal cues, egocentrism, feedback.
3. **Job motivation and satisfaction**: need theory, expectancy theory, goal-setting theory; job characteristics and well-being (Warr).
4. **Workplace stress**: effects of workload and control; the job demands-resources (J D-R) model.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social facilitation | Change in performance caused by the presence of others, as audience or co-actors |
| Dominant response | The best-learned response, made more likely by arousal (Zajonc) |
| Evaluation apprehension | Arousal from expecting to be judged by others (Cottrell) |
| Social loafing | Reduced individual effort when contributions are pooled |
| Collective effort model | People work hard in groups only if they expect their effort to matter for a valued outcome |
| Risky shift | Group decision riskier than the average of members' private decisions |
| Group polarisation | Discussion moves a group further in the direction it already leaned |
| Groupthink | Desire for agreement in a cohesive group overrides realistic appraisal |
| Mindguard | Member who shields the group from information that challenges its view |
| Deindividuation | Reduced self-awareness and accountability, often in crowds or under anonymity |
| Social identity | The part of self-concept that comes from group membership |
| In-group favouritism | Treating members of your own group better |
| Out-group bias | Negative judgement or treatment of members of other groups |
| Display rules | Learned cultural norms about who may show which emotion, to whom and when |
| Personal space | The area around a person that others should not enter uninvited |
| Egocentrism (e-mail) | Difficulty detaching from your own perspective when judging how a reader will interpret you |
| Expectancy / instrumentality / valence | Effort leads to performance / performance leads to outcome / value of outcome |
| Additional decrement (AD) | Warr: job feature that helps up to a point, then harms in excess |
| Constant effect (CE) | Warr: job feature that helps, with extra adding little but doing no harm |
| Decision latitude | Control over how and when work is done |
| Job demands | Aspects of work that need sustained physical or psychological effort |
| Job resources | Aspects of work that help achieve goals, reduce demands or support growth |

|---|---|---|---|
| Triplett (1898) | Cyclists; children winding string | Faster with competitors | Early study; showed the effect but not why |
| Zajonc (1965) | Drive theory | Arousal boosts dominant response | Explains both better and worse performance |
| Michaels et al. (1982) | Pool players watched | Skilled better, weak worse | Real setting, supports Zajonc |
| Ringelmann (1913); Ingham et al. (1974) | Rope pulling; pseudo-groups | Less effort in groups, even when others only pretend | Shows loss is motivational |
| Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979) | Shouting and clapping | Effort per person fell in groups | Lab tasks lack mundane realism |
| Karau and Williams (1993) | Meta-analysis | Collective effort model | Predicts practical remedies |
| Earley (1989) | US and China | Collectivists loafed less | Loafing not universal |
| Stoner (1961); Wallach, Kogan and Bem (1962) | Choice dilemmas | Groups chose riskier options | Later groups also shifted cautious |
| Moscovici and Zavalloni (1969) | Attitudes after discussion | Named group polarisation | Lab attitudes, low stakes |
| Burnstein and Vinokur (1977) | Polarisation mechanism | Persuasive arguments explain most of it | Social comparison is the competing account |
| Janis (1972, 1982) | Policy fiascos | Eight symptoms of groupthink | Retrospective cases, hindsight bias |
| Diener et al. (1976) | Halloween children | Anonymous groups took more | Natural setting, high ecological validity |
| Johnson and Downing (1979) | Robes vs nurse uniforms | Klan-style robes more shocks, nurse fewer | Supports SIDE: cues and norms matter |
| Tajfel (1970); Tajfel et al. (1971) | Minimal groups (Klee/Kandinsky) | In-group favouritism with trivial groups | Artificial task |
| Tajfel and Turner (1979) | Social identity theory | Categorise, identify, compare | Weak on when bias becomes hostility |
| Kendon (1967) | Gaze in conversation | Look away at start, back at end of turn | Signals turn-taking |
| Argyle and Dean (1965) | Eye contact and distance | Balance to keep intimacy comfortable | Explains compensation at close range |
| Ekman and Friesen (1971) | Fore of Papua New Guinea | Matched faces to emotion stories | Supports universals |

| Hall (1966) | Proxemics | Four zones of distance | Descriptive; distances are approximate |
| Sorokowska et al. (2017) | 42 countries | Preferred stranger distance varied widely | Self-report survey |
| Kruger et al. (2005) | Five e-mail experiments | People overestimate conveying tone | Controlled, causal; egocentrism |
| Maslow (1943) | Hierarchy of needs | Lower needs before higher | Wahba and Bridwell (1976): little support for ranking |
| McClelland (1961) | Achievement, affiliation, power | High nAch prefers moderate difficulty | Measured by TAT stories, open to interpretation |
| Vroom (1964) | Expectancy theory | MF = E x I x V | Assumes rational calculation |
| Locke (1968); Locke and Latham (1990) | Goal-setting | Specific, hard goals raise performance | Ordóñez et al. (2009): narrow goals can backfire |
| Warr (1987) | Vitamin model | AD and CE features | Partial support (Borkowska and Czerw, 2022) |
| Karasek (1979) | Demand-control model | High demand plus low control most stressful | Narrow set of predictors |

| Demerouti et al. (2001) | J D-R model | Health-impairment and motivational processes | Flexible but hard to falsify |
| Bakker, Demerouti and Euwema (2005) | 1,012 higher education staff | Resources buffered demands for exhaustion and cynicism | Not for professional efficacy |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Audience vs co-action.** Facilitation can come from watchers or from people doing the same task beside you.
- **Facilitation vs loafing.** Facilitation is about others being present while your own performance is visible. Loafing is about pooled output where yours is hidden.
- **Risky shift vs group polarisation.** Risky shift is a shift towards risk. Polarisation is a shift towards whichever pole the group already favoured.
- **Polarisation vs groupthink.** Polarisation is a shift in average position. Groupthink is a faulty decision process with symptoms, antecedents and remedies.
- **Classic deindividuation vs SIDE.** Classic view: anonymity removes restraint. SIDE: anonymity strengthens group identity, so people follow the group's norms.
- **Universal expressions vs display rules.** Expressions of basic emotions may be universal; when and how they are shown is cultural. This is a nature-nurture question, covered in the [issues and debates guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-issues-and-debates-in-psychology/).
- **Need theory vs process theories.** Need theory says *what* motivates. Expectancy and goal-setting explain *how* motivation turns into effort.
- **AD vs CE.** AD features (control, variety, demands) harm in excess. CE features (pay, security, equity) do not.
- **Demand-control vs J D-R.** Demand-control uses two variables. J D-R allows any demand and any resource, and adds a motivational process.

## Mini essay plan

**"Discuss the effects of group processes on decision-making at work." [16 marks]**

1. **Define** group polarisation, risky shift and groupthink in a sentence each (AO1).
2. **Polarisation:** Moscovici and Zavalloni; two explanations, social comparison and persuasive arguments; Burnstein and Vinokur favour arguments. Apply to a like-minded management team (AO2).
3. **Evaluate:** strong lab support, but low-stakes attitudes; real teams also face cost and accountability (AO3).
4. **Groupthink:** Janis, symptoms, antecedents (cohesion, directive leader, stress) and remedies (AO1).
5. **Evaluate:** retrospective case studies, hindsight bias, mixed empirical tests; but cheap, practical remedies such as a devil's advocate (AO3).
6. **Link:** social identity theory explains why cohesive groups resist outsiders' views (synoptic link to conformity from Unit 1).
7. **Judgement:** group processes can push decisions to extremes, but structure (impartial leader, outside input) reduces the risk.

## Quick self-test

1. What does Zajonc's drive theory predict for a hard task done in front of an audience?
2. Why did Ingham et al. use pseudo-groups?
3. State the collective effort model in one sentence.
4. How does group polarisation differ from risky shift?
5. Name three symptoms of groupthink.
6. What did Johnson and Downing (1979) show about deindividuation?
7. Name the three processes in social identity theory.
8. What do display rules explain?
9. Give two reasons e-mail is misread more often than speech.
10. In expectancy theory, why can a large bonus fail to motivate?
11. Give one AD and one CE feature from Warr's model.
12. What did Bosma et al. (1997) find about job demands?

### Answers

1. Worse performance, because arousal makes the dominant response likely and on a hard task it is often wrong.
2. To remove coordination loss, so any drop in effort had to be motivational.
3. People work hard in a group only if they expect their effort to matter for an outcome they value.
4. Risky shift is movement towards risk only; polarisation is movement in whatever direction the group already leaned.
5. Any three: illusion of invulnerability, belief in the group's morality, rationalising warnings, stereotyping opponents, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters, mindguards.
6. Cues matter: robes like the Ku Klux Klan's increased shocks, nurse uniforms reduced them.
7. Social categorisation, social identification, social comparison.
8. Why cultures differ in when and to whom emotions are shown, even if the expressions themselves are universal.
9. Lack of non-verbal cues; egocentrism of the writer; lack of instant feedback (any two).
10. If expectancy or instrumentality is near zero, the product is near zero however high the valence.
11. AD: personal control, skill use, variety, demands and goals, clarity, social contact. CE: pay, physical security, valued social position, supportive supervision, career outlook, equity.
12. Job demands were not related to coronary heart disease risk; low job control was.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining social facilitation as "working better with others" and missing the hard-task prediction.
- Describing Ringelmann without Ingham et al., so the motivational explanation is never shown.
- Using "risky shift" for a group that became more cautious.
- Listing groupthink symptoms with no application to the scenario's people and events.
- Treating deindividuation as always antisocial.
- Claiming all facial expressions are universal and ignoring Jack et al. (2012).
- Writing "e-mail lacks body language" without naming egocentrism or feedback, which the specification lists.
- Adding expectancy, instrumentality and valence instead of multiplying them.
- Saying Whitehall II shows high workload causes heart disease, when the finding was about control.
- Describing the J D-R model with only demands, leaving out resources and the motivational process.
