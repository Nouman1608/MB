These notes condense section 11.5, Alkenes, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). All six outcomes are required for every candidate. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [alkenes study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-alkenes/); then try the [alkenes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-alkenes-practice/).

## The six outcomes in one line each

1. Alkenes contain a C=C double covalent bond and are unsaturated hydrocarbons.
2. Cracking: larger alkanes → alkenes and hydrogen (or smaller alkanes), using high temperature and a catalyst.
3. Reasons for cracking.
4. Aqueous bromine test distinguishes saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons.
5. Addition reaction: only one product is formed.
6. Addition of bromine, hydrogen (nickel catalyst) and steam (acid catalyst); draw the products.

## Cracking

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Starting material | larger alkane molecules |
| Conditions | high temperature and a catalyst |
| Products | smaller alkane + alkene, or alkene + hydrogen |
| Example | C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ |
| Example giving hydrogen | C₂H₆ → C₂H₄ + H₂ |

**Completing equations**: subtract C and H atoms. C₁₆H₃₄ → C₁₀H₂₂ + ? gives C₆H₁₂ (an alkene).

**More than one alkene**: C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₂H₄.

**Model description (3 marks)**: "Large alkane molecules are heated to a high temperature and passed over a catalyst. The molecules break into smaller alkane molecules and alkene molecules. Some cracking reactions give an alkene and hydrogen instead."

## Reasons for cracking

- Long-chain fractions are produced in excess of demand.
- Short-chain fractions such as gasoline are in greater demand than supply.
- Cracking converts surplus long chains into useful shorter chains.
- It produces alkenes, needed for polymers and ethanol.
- It produces hydrogen, another useful product.

## Bromine test

| | Alkene | Alkane |
|---|---|---|
| Aqueous bromine before | orange | orange |
| After shaking | **colourless** | **orange** (no change) |
| Why | bromine adds across C=C | no C=C to react |

## Addition reactions

```
alkene + X–Y → one product, X and Y on adjacent carbons
```

| Reagent | Condition | Ethene gives | Propene gives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Br₂ / Br₂(aq) | room temperature | 1,2-dibromoethane CH₂BrCH₂Br | 1,2-dibromopropane CH₃CHBrCH₂Br |
| H₂ | nickel catalyst | ethane CH₃CH₃ | propane CH₃CH₂CH₃ |
| H₂O (steam) | acid catalyst | ethanol CH₃CH₂OH | propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol |

| Alkene | + Br₂ | + H₂ | + steam |
|---|---|---|---|
| But-1-ene | 1,2-dibromobutane | butane | butan-1-ol or butan-2-ol |
| But-2-ene | 2,3-dibromobutane | butane | butan-2-ol |

All addition products are saturated.

## How addition works

One of the two bonds in C=C breaks, leaving each of the two carbons with a spare bond. The two parts of the adding molecule (Br and Br, H and H, or H and OH) attach to these two carbons. Nothing is left over, which is why there is only one product. The rest of the chain is untouched, so the product keeps the same number of carbon atoms as the alkene. Use this to name products: same stem as the alkene, new ending for the new functional group (or -ane if hydrogen was added).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alkene | unsaturated hydrocarbon with a C=C bond; general formula CₙH₂ₙ |
| Unsaturated | one or more carbon–carbon bonds are not single |
| Cracking | breaking larger alkane molecules into smaller molecules using high temperature and a catalyst |
| Addition reaction | reaction in which only one product is formed |
| Catalyst | substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end |
| Hydrogenation | addition of hydrogen across C=C (nickel catalyst) |
| Hydration | addition of steam across C=C (acid catalyst) |

## Exam traps

- Say "**colourless**", not "clear", for decolourised bromine.
- "Makes smaller molecules" is a description of cracking, not a reason for it.
- Addition gives **one** product; no HBr or H₂O is formed alongside.
- Bromine atoms go on **adjacent** carbons, one each.
- Catalysts: **nickel** for hydrogen; **acid** for steam; cracking uses a catalyst too, with high temperature.
- Cracking equations must balance hydrogen as well as carbon.
- An alkane does not decolourise aqueous bromine: that is the whole point of the test.
- When asked for conditions, give both for cracking: high temperature **and** a catalyst.
- Propene and but-1-ene give two possible alcohols with steam; ethene and but-2-ene give one.

## Self-test

1. State why alkenes are described as unsaturated.
2. State the two conditions used in cracking.
3. Complete: C₈H₁₈ → C₆H₁₄ + ......
4. Give **two** reasons why cracking is carried out.
5. Describe the result of the bromine test with an alkene and with an alkane.
6. What is meant by an addition reaction?
7. Name the product of ethene with hydrogen and give the catalyst.
8. Write the equation for ethene reacting with steam.
9. Name the product of propene with bromine.
10. Name the alcohol formed when but-2-ene reacts with steam.
11. Write the structural formula of the product of but-1-ene with hydrogen.
12. Cracking ethane gives ethene and one other product. Name it.

## Answers

1. They contain a carbon–carbon double bond, so not all carbon–carbon bonds are single.
2. High temperature and a catalyst.
3. C₂H₄.
4. Any two: supply of long-chain fractions exceeds demand; demand for short-chain fractions such as gasoline exceeds supply; it produces alkenes needed for polymers and other chemicals; it produces hydrogen.
5. Alkene: orange to colourless. Alkane: stays orange.
6. A reaction in which only one product is formed.
7. Ethane; nickel.
8. C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH.
9. 1,2-dibromopropane.
10. Butan-2-ol.
11. CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃.
12. Hydrogen.
