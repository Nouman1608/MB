OCR A Level Biology A (H420) is linear and untiered, assessed by three examined components totalling
270 marks. Paper 1 (Biological processes) and Paper 2 (Biological diversity) are each 2 hours 15
minutes, 100 marks and 37%. Paper 3 (Unified Biology) is 1 hour 30 minutes, 70 marks and 26%, and
draws on **all modules synoptically**. Alongside these sits the **Practical Endorsement**, a non-exam
assessment requiring a minimum of twelve practical activities, internally assessed and reported
separately as Pass or Not Classified -- it contributes no marks to the 270-mark total that determines
the A*-E grade. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Foundations in Biology](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-foundations-in-biology/) and
[Development of Practical Skills](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-development-of-practical-skills/).

## Paper 3 is the whole course -- it cannot be revised as "whatever is left"

At 70 marks and 26%, Paper 3 is smaller than the other two but draws on every module and is explicitly
synoptic. **Exam-preparation priority**: reserve deliberate cross-module revision time close to the
exams rather than treating Paper 3 as a smaller version of Papers 1 and 2. The specific skill it
rewards is connecting material across modules -- linking biochemistry to physiology, or ecology to
genetics -- which is not built by revising one module at a time.

## The Practical Endorsement does not add marks -- and that changes nothing about revising practicals

Because the endorsement is reported separately as a Pass, it is tempting to treat practical work as
outside the graded qualification. That is a mistake: practical skills, techniques and the analysis of
experimental data are assessed *within the written papers*, which is where the 270 marks are.
**Exam-preparation priority**: revise the practical activities for what the written papers ask about
them -- method, variables, uncertainty, evaluation, data processing -- not for the endorsement.

## Papers 1 and 2 are 74% together, and they are the pacing challenge

Each gives 135 minutes for 100 marks, a fraction over 1.3 minutes per mark, across a wide range of
question types. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise complete papers to time. The pacing risk is
specific: extended-response and data-analysis questions carry substantial marks and appear throughout,
so a slow start cannot be recovered in a final sprint.

## Unfamiliar contexts are the norm, not the exception

A-level biology questions routinely present an organism, a study or a technique you have not studied,
and ask you to apply principles to it. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise on unfamiliar material
specifically. Revision that consists of re-reading known content builds recall but not the transfer
skill these papers test, and the two feel very different under exam conditions.

## Mathematical and statistical marks are guaranteed and often overlooked

Biology at this level requires calculation and statistical reasoning -- magnification, rates from
gradients, percentage change, standard deviation, and the interpretation of statistical tests.
**Exam-preparation priority**: drill these as a distinct topic. They are among the most reliably
scorable marks available and are lost far more often to arithmetic unfamiliarity than to biological
misunderstanding.

## Worked routine: applying principles to unfamiliar data

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - name the underlying biological principle first:
Before engaging with the specific organism or study, identify the
principle in play -- surface area and diffusion, water potential,
natural selection, feedback control.

Step 2 - read the axes, units and scale of any data:
A misread unit invalidates the whole answer. Check whether the
scale is linear or logarithmic.

Step 3 - describe what the data shows, with figures:
Direction, magnitude, and any point where the pattern breaks --
quoted with values and units.

Step 4 - explain using the principle from step 1:
Connect the principle to THIS organism or study, rather than
restating the principle in general terms.

Step 5 - state what the data does not establish:
Correlation without a controlled variable does not demonstrate
cause; saying so explicitly is regularly creditable.
```

Step 1 is what makes an unfamiliar context tractable: the organism may be new, but the principle
almost never is.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: reserve cross-module revision time for Paper 3 rather than revising module by
  module; revise practicals for what the written papers ask (method, variables, uncertainty,
  evaluation); practise on unfamiliar contexts, not only on known content; drill the mathematical and
  statistical skills as a distinct topic.
- **During Papers 1 and 2**: hold to about 1.3 minutes per mark from the start, since a slow opening
  cannot be recovered late.
- **During Paper 3**: expect questions that span modules and look for the connection rather than the
  single topic.
- **On any data question**: name the principle, read the axes, describe with figures, explain in
  context, and state the limits of what the data shows.

## Self-test

1. What are the three papers' weightings, and what is distinctive about Paper 3?
2. Does the Practical Endorsement contribute marks, and what follows for revision?
3. Why is re-reading known content insufficient preparation?
4. Which step makes an unfamiliar context tractable?

**Answers:** 1. Paper 1 and Paper 2 are 37% each (100 marks, 2h15); Paper 3 is 26% (70 marks, 1h30)
and is synoptic across all modules, so it requires deliberate cross-module revision. 2. No -- it is
reported separately as Pass or Not Classified and adds nothing to the 270-mark total; but practical
skills, techniques and data analysis are assessed within the written papers, so the practicals still
need revising for those. 3. Because the papers routinely present unfamiliar organisms, studies and
techniques and test the transfer of principles to them, which recall-based revision does not build.
4. Naming the underlying biological principle before engaging with the specific unfamiliar material.
