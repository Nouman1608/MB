> **Syllabus edition note.** This guide follows the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 2.3 follows soil erosion from cause to cure: why soil is lost, what that loss does to land,
rivers and people, and how farmers can hold soil in place. The
[Topic 2 Land guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-land/) lists the causes,
impacts and strategies. This page explains how wind and water actually move soil, traces each impact as a
chain, and shows how to match a strategy to a place.

## What the syllabus asks

- **2.3.1** Describe the causes of soil erosion, limited to: unsustainable agricultural practices;
  deforestation; farming on steep slopes; bare soil; wind and water erosion.
- **2.3.2** Describe the impacts of soil erosion, limited to: silting of rivers; desertification; mass
  movement (landslides, rockslides and mudslides); loss of habitats and biodiversity; reduction in crop
  yield; malnutrition and famine; displacement of people.
- **2.3.3** Describe and explain strategies to reduce soil erosion, limited to: terracing (reduces movement
  of soil and surface run-off); contour ploughing (reduces surface run-off); bunds (reduce surface run-off,
  wind speed and wind erosion); wind breaks (protect crops, reduce wind speed and wind erosion);
  maintaining vegetation cover (roots bind the soil, vegetation reduces surface run-off and absorbs water);
  addition of organic matter (improves soil structure).

## How soil is eroded

Soil erosion is the removal of soil, especially the fertile topsoil, faster than new soil forms. Two
agents do the work.

**Water erosion**

1. Raindrops hit bare soil and splash particles loose.
2. When rain falls faster than it can soak in, water flows over the surface as **surface run-off**,
   carrying loose particles with it.
3. On slopes, run-off speeds up and gathers into small channels (rills), which can deepen into gullies.
4. The soil is carried downhill and into rivers.

**Wind erosion**

1. Dry, loose, fine soil with no plant cover is exposed to strong winds.
2. The wind lifts and carries the lightest particles, which are often the most fertile.
3. Flat, open land with no shelter is most at risk, especially in dry seasons and droughts.

## Causes: why soil becomes vulnerable

Every cause works by doing one of three things: removing the plant cover that protects soil, weakening
the soil itself, or increasing the speed of water or wind across it.

| Cause | How it leads to erosion |
|---|---|
| Unsustainable agricultural practices | Overgrazing strips grass; overcultivation and monoculture exhaust organic matter so soil crumbles; ploughing up and down slopes creates channels for run-off |
| Deforestation | Removes roots that bind soil and the canopy that breaks the force of rain, so more rain hits the ground directly |
| Farming on steep slopes | Gravity speeds up run-off, giving it more energy to carry soil |
| Bare soil | Between crops, after harvest or after burning, nothing shields the soil from rain or wind |
| Wind and water erosion | The agents themselves: strong winds on dry land, heavy rain on bare ground |

Causes often combine. A forested hillside cleared for crops is affected by deforestation, steep slopes,
bare soil between harvests and heavy rain all at once.

## Impacts: from field to region

| Impact | Chain |
|---|---|
| Silting of rivers | Eroded soil washes into rivers → sediment settles on the river bed → channel holds less water, so flooding is more likely; reservoirs fill with silt; muddy water harms fish |
| Desertification | Repeated loss of topsoil in dry areas → land can no longer support vegetation → it becomes desert-like |
| Mass movement: landslides, rockslides, mudslides | Slopes without roots to bind soil become saturated in heavy rain → soil and rock slide downhill, burying fields, roads and homes |
| Loss of habitats and biodiversity | Soil and vegetation removed → organisms that live in or depend on them lose their habitat |
| Reduction in crop yield | Topsoil holds most nutrients and organic matter → its loss leaves thin, infertile soil → crops grow poorly |
| Malnutrition and famine | Lower yields → less food → people, especially children, do not get enough to eat; severe shortages become famine |
| Displacement of people | Land can no longer support farming, or homes are destroyed by mass movement → families are forced to move |

The impacts build on one another: erosion → lower yields → malnutrition → displacement. A strong answer
can follow the chain from soil to people.

## Six strategies, explained

The syllabus states what each strategy does; your job is to explain **how**.

- **Terracing** cuts a slope into a series of flat steps, each with a raised edge. Water is held on each
  step and soaks in rather than rushing downhill, so it **reduces movement of soil and surface run-off**.
  It turns steep land into farmland, but takes great effort to build and maintain.
- **Contour ploughing** ploughs furrows across the slope, following the contour lines, rather than up and
  down it. Each furrow acts as a small barrier that traps water, so it **reduces surface run-off**.
- **Bunds** are low banks of earth or stones built along the contours of a field. They hold back water so
  it soaks in, **reducing surface run-off**, and on open land they also **reduce wind speed and wind
  erosion** at ground level.
- **Wind breaks** are rows of trees, shrubs or hedges planted across the direction of the wind. They **protect
  crops, reduce wind speed and wind erosion**. Their roots also hold soil, and they can provide fuel or
  fruit, but they take up land and years to grow.
- **Maintaining vegetation cover**, for example by leaving crop stubble after harvest, planting cover
  crops between seasons or not overgrazing: **roots bind the soil**, and **vegetation reduces surface
  run-off and absorbs water**.
- **Addition of organic matter**, such as manure or compost, **improves soil structure**: it binds
  particles into larger crumbs that are harder for wind and water to carry away, and helps water soak in.

## Worked example: data on soil loss

*Invented data* from test plots on the same slope:

| Treatment | Soil lost (tonnes per hectare per year) |
|---|---|
| Bare soil ploughed up and down the slope | 40 |
| Contour ploughing | 22 |
| Terracing | 8 |

```
reduction with contour ploughing = (40 - 22) / 40 x 100 = 45%
reduction with terracing         = (40 - 8) / 40 x 100  = 80%
```

*Explanation:* terracing cuts the slope into flat steps, so water has almost no downhill speed and soaks
in, whereas contour furrows only slow the run-off. Terracing is more effective but far more costly and
labour-intensive to build.

## Matching a strategy to a place

*Question (original):* Suggest and explain **two** strategies to reduce soil erosion on a flat, dry, windy
plain where cattle graze. [4]

*Model answer:* Plant wind breaks of trees across the direction of the prevailing wind [1]; they reduce
wind speed at ground level, so less soil is lifted [1]. Maintain vegetation cover by rotating the cattle
between fields so grass is not overgrazed [1]; the roots bind the soil and the grass shelters it from the
wind [1].

*Why it scores:* terracing and contour ploughing would score nothing here, because they deal with run-off
on slopes. Always check whether the place is steep or flat, wet or windy.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing weathering (rock breakdown) with soil erosion (removal of soil).
- Saying contour ploughing reduces wind erosion. The syllabus links it to surface run-off.
- Describing a strategy without explaining how it works.
- Suggesting terracing for flat land or wind breaks as the main answer for run-off on a steep slope.
- Listing impacts without linking them to people, for example lower yields leading to malnutrition.
- Writing "desertification is the spread of deserts" with no mention of soil or land degradation.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a cause: say what happens to the soil or its cover.
- **Describe** an impact: give the effect and who or what it affects.
- **Describe and explain** a strategy: what is built or done, then how it stops wind or water moving soil,
  using the syllabus mechanism.
- **Suggest**: match the strategy to the slope, climate and land use given.
