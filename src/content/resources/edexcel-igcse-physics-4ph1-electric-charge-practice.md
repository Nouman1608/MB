## Section A

**1.** From this list, identify the two electrical conductors: copper, glass, graphite, nylon, rubber. **[2]**

**2.** State the type of particle that moves when an insulator is charged by friction. **[1]**

**3.** State what happens when two negatively charged objects are brought close together. **[1]**

**4.** An acetate strip becomes positively charged when rubbed with a duster. State what has happened to the electrons. **[2]**

**5.** Give one use of electrostatic charge other than in photocopiers. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A student investigates charging by friction. She rubs a polythene rod with a cloth and hangs it from a thread so that it can turn freely.

**(a)** She brings a second polythene rod, rubbed with the same cloth, close to one end of the hanging rod. Describe and explain what she observes. **[2]**
**(b)** She then brings the cloth close to the hanging rod. Describe and explain what she observes. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why the experiment works poorly on a humid day. **[1]**

**7.** A student holds a metal rod in his hand and rubs it with a cloth. The rod does not become charged. Explain why. **[2]**

**8.** After a balloon is rubbed on a woollen jumper, it sticks to a ceiling.

**(a)** The balloon gains electrons. State the charge on the balloon and on the jumper. **[2]**
**(b)** The ceiling is uncharged. Explain why the balloon is attracted to it. **[3]**

**9.** A person walks across a nylon carpet and then touches a metal door handle. They feel a small electric shock.

Explain, in terms of electrons, why this happens. **[3]**

**10.** Fuel is pumped into an aircraft through a long hose.

**(a)** Explain how a large electrostatic charge can build up during fuelling. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this charge is dangerous. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe and explain one precaution used to remove the danger. **[2]**

**11.** In an inkjet printer, drops of ink pass between two deflecting plates, one positive and one negative.

**(a)** A drop is given a negative charge. State which plate it moves towards, and why. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the printer makes different drops land in different places on the paper. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how a photocopier uses electrostatic charge to produce a copy of a page. **[5]**

**13.** Two students test two rods, X and Y, with a hanging, negatively charged polythene rod. Rod X attracts the polythene rod. Rod Y repels it.

**(a)** State the charge on rod Y. **[1]**
**(b)** A student concludes that rod X must be positively charged. Evaluate this conclusion. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Copper [1]; graphite [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Electrons [1]. **[1]**

**3.** They repel (push each other apart) [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Electrons have been transferred from the acetate [1] to the duster [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any one: inkjet printers; electrostatic spray painting [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** The hanging rod moves away (repulsion) [1], because both rods have gained electrons from the cloth, so they have the same (negative) charge and like charges repel [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The hanging rod moves towards the cloth (attraction) [1], because the cloth lost electrons and is positive, and unlike charges attract [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Water on the surfaces of the rods and cloth lets charge leak away, so little charge stays on them [1]. **[1]**

**7.** Metal is a conductor, so electrons can move through it [1]; any charge transferred flows through the rod and the student's body to earth, so the rod is left uncharged [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Balloon: negative [1]. Jumper: positive [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The negatively charged balloon repels electrons in the ceiling surface slightly away [1], so the part of the ceiling nearest the balloon becomes positive [1]. Unlike charges attract, and the attraction to the nearby positive region is greater than the repulsion from the more distant negative region [1]. **[3]**

**9.** As the person's shoes rub on the carpet, electrons are transferred, so the person becomes charged [1]. The metal handle is a conductor connected to earth [1], so when it is touched, electrons flow suddenly between the person and earth, which is felt as a shock [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Fuel rubs against the inside of the hose as it flows (friction) [1], transferring electrons so that the hose, nozzle or aircraft becomes charged [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The charge may jump to earth as a spark [1], which can ignite fuel vapour and cause an explosion or fire [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The aircraft is connected to earth with a conducting cable before fuelling (or a conducting hose is used) [1], so electrons flow to or from earth continuously and no large charge builds up [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** Towards the positive plate [1], because unlike charges attract (and it is repelled by the negative plate) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each drop is given a different amount of charge [1]; a larger charge means a larger force and a larger deflection, so the drops land in different positions [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 5:
- the drum is given an electrostatic charge over its whole surface [1]
- an image of the page is projected onto the drum [1]
- where light from the white parts of the page falls, the drum's coating conducts and the charge leaks away [1]
- the dark parts of the image remain charged [1]
- toner powder with the opposite charge is attracted to the charged areas only [1]
- the toner is transferred to charged paper and heated so that it melts and sticks [1]

**[5]**

**13. (a)** Negative [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The conclusion is not justified: attraction would happen if X were positive [1], but a neutral (uncharged) rod would also be attracted, because electrons in it move and leave the near side positive, so attraction alone cannot show X is positive [1]. **[2]**
