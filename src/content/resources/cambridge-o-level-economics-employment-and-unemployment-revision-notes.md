## 4.7.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Employment | Paid work, for an employer or self-employed |
| Unemployment | Without a job, willing and able to work, and looking for one |
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Full employment | All who want a job at the prevailing wage and are capable of doing one can get it; a small amount of frictional unemployment still exists |

Outside the labour force: full-time students, the retired, and anyone not looking for work.

## 4.7.2 Changing patterns of employment

| Trend | Reasons to give |
|---|---|
| Bigger tertiary share | Rising incomes raise demand for services; farm and factory machinery needs fewer workers; firms buy more services |
| Bigger formal economy | Larger registered firms replace small unrecorded traders; more education; stricter registration and tax collection |
| Rising female participation | Attitudes shift; girls stay longer in school; equal-treatment laws; households that need two wages |
| Lower state share | A turn to markets: privatisation, closing state firms, private firms taking over supply |

The level of employment rises with growth in output and falls in a recession.

Small reminder: a country has 2,400,000 employed and tertiary workers are 52% of employment. Tertiary workers = 0.52 × 2,400,000 = 1,248,000.

Women's share: a labour force of 800,000 includes 320,000 women, a share of 320,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = 40%. Later the labour force is 900,000 with 405,000 women, a share of 45%. Women's numbers rose by 85,000, while the total labour force rose by 100,000, so the share rose by 5 percentage points.

## 4.7.3 Measuring unemployment

**Rate (%) = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100**

Working order:
1. Work out the labour force by adding the employed and the unemployed.
2. Put the jobless figure over that total.
3. Convert to a percentage and write the % sign.

Reminder: 846,000 employed, 54,000 unemployed. Labour force = 900,000. Rate = 54,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 6.0%.

Working backwards: unemployment 96,000 is 6% of the labour force. Labour force = 96,000 ÷ 0.06 = 1,600,000, so employed = 1,504,000.

| | Claimant count | Labour force survey |
|---|---|---|
| Who | People claiming benefit | People in a sample who say they are jobless and looking |
| Strength | Quick, cheap, existing records | Includes non-claimants; same definition across countries |
| Weakness | Misses non-claimants; may include people who work while claiming | Costly; sampling error; relies on honest answers |

Reminder: claimant count 90,000. Survey rate 8% of a labour force of 1,250,000 gives 0.08 × 1,250,000 = 100,000. The survey is 10,000 higher.

## 4.7.4 Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Example | Remedy type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Search and matching time between jobs | A worker leaves one firm and spends weeks applying | Better job information |
| Structural | Industry declines; skills or location mismatch | Handloom weavers when a mill automates | Training, relocation help |
| Cyclical | Fall in total demand | Lay-offs in a recession | Higher demand |

Cyclical chain: spending falls, sales fall, output falls, labour demand falls, jobs are cut.

Reminder: labour force 1,000,000; frictional 20,000, structural 45,000, cyclical 35,000. Total unemployed = 100,000, so the rate is 100,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = 10.0%. Without the cyclical part, 65,000 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = 6.5%. Cyclical share = 35,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = 35%.

Diagram in words: draw a production possibility curve. A recession with high cyclical unemployment puts the economy at a point inside the curve, because labour is idle. Recovery moves the point out towards the curve. Only growth in resources or technology shifts the curve itself.

## 4.7.5 Consequences

| For | Main points |
|---|---|
| Individual | Lower income; stress; skills fade; possible time to retrain |
| Firms | Lower sales and profit; pool of labour for hiring; lost trained staff |
| Economy | Wasted capacity (a point inside the production possibility curve); smaller tax receipts; more benefit spending; inequality; social problems |

Reminder: 20,000 more people unemployed, each costing 4,000 in lost tax and benefit and 12,000 in lost output. Fiscal cost = 20,000 × 4,000 = 80,000,000. Output lost = 20,000 × 12,000 = 240,000,000.

## 4.7.6 Policies

| Type of unemployment | Policy | Effectiveness depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Fiscal and monetary policy to raise demand | Confidence, time lags, spare capacity, borrowing |
| Structural | Education, training, relocation help, subsidies, incentives to locate in depressed areas | Years to work; willingness to retrain or move; cost |
| Frictional | Job centres, online vacancy information | Cannot remove all search time |

Cost per job reminder: 12,000,000 spent on a scheme that finds 2,400 jobs costs 12,000,000 ÷ 2,400 = 5,000 per job.

### Evaluation prompts

- It depends on the type of unemployment, so one policy rarely fits all.
- In the short run a demand boost helps; in the long run skills and mobility matter more.
- A policy is judged by size of effect, speed, cost and who pays.
- A rich country and a poor country may face different mixes of types.

### Model paragraphs to adapt

Fiscal policy, cyclical unemployment: A government can raise spending on roads. This gives builders work and raises their incomes, so they spend more in shops, and shops hire more staff. Unemployment falls if the problem is a lack of demand. However, the effect may come slowly, it raises borrowing, and it does little if the unemployed lack the skills the new jobs need.

Training, structural unemployment: A free course in machine repair gives former mill workers skills that firms are seeking. It reduces occupational immobility, so vacancies are filled. It works only if firms are hiring in that field, and the benefits arrive after months or years.

Consequence for a firm: When many households lose their jobs, they cut spending, so a clothing shop sells less. Falling revenue reduces profit and may force the owner to cut hours or close.

### Evaluating in four moves

1. Name the type of unemployment.
2. Pair it with a matching policy and explain the chain.
3. Weigh speed, size, cost and side effects.
4. Reach a judgement that depends on the type, the country and the time frame.

## Must-know distinctions

- Unemployment rate versus number unemployed. The number can rise while the rate falls if the labour force grows faster.
- Claimant count versus survey. One counts benefit claimants, the other counts reported job-seeking.
- Frictional versus structural. Frictional is short search time with suitable jobs available; structural is a mismatch that can last years.
- Structural versus cyclical. Structural is a change in what the economy needs; cyclical is a shortage of demand.
- Employment versus labour force. Labour force includes the unemployed.

## Quick self-test

1. Write the formula used to find the unemployment rate.
2. A country has 2,850,000 employed and 150,000 unemployed. Calculate the rate.
3. Why can full employment still include some unemployment?
4. Give two reasons for a larger tertiary share of employment.
5. Give one disadvantage of the claimant count.
6. Employment rises by 30,000 and the labour force by 50,000. What happens to unemployment?
7. Which type of unemployment follows a recession?
8. Name one policy for structural unemployment and one limit.
9. A scheme costs 9,000,000 and creates 1,500 jobs. Calculate the cost per job.
10. Name one consequence of unemployment for firms.

### Answers

1. Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
2. Labour force = 3,000,000. Rate = 150,000 ÷ 3,000,000 × 100 = 5.0%.
3. Some people are always between jobs (frictional), so the labour market never matches perfectly.
4. Higher incomes raise spending on services; machines reduce the farm workforce.
5. It misses people who are unemployed but not claiming.
6. Unemployment rises by 20,000, since 50,000 − 30,000 = 20,000.
7. Cyclical.
8. Retraining; it takes years and trainees must be willing and able.
9. 9,000,000 ÷ 1,500 = 6,000.
10. Households spend less, so sales and profit fall.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by employment instead of the labour force.
- Defining unemployment without "willing and able" and "looking for work".
- Describing the three types without a cause for each.
- Offering a demand boost for a structural problem.
- Stating consequences for "society" but not for the individual or firms separately.
- Listing policies with no explanation of how they work.
- Never judging effectiveness: no mention of time lags, cost or size.
- Missing units, such as the percentage sign.
