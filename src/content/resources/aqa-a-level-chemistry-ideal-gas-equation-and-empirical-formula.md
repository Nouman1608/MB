## 3.1.2.3 The ideal gas equation

### The equation and its units

**pV = nRT**

| Symbol | Quantity | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| p | pressure | pascal, Pa |
| V | volume | cubic metre, m³ |
| n | amount of gas | mol |
| R | gas constant | J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| T | temperature | kelvin, K |

You do not need to memorise R; the specification says you will not be expected to recall it. The
examples on this page use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

The equation only works if every value is in SI units. Convert first, then substitute.

| You are given | Convert to SI |
|---|---|
| kPa | multiply by 10³ to get Pa |
| MPa | multiply by 10⁶ to get Pa |
| dm³ | multiply by 10⁻³ to get m³ |
| cm³ | multiply by 10⁻⁶ to get m³ |
| °C | add 273 to get K |

A useful check: 1 J = 1 Pa m³, so the units cancel to leave mol.

### Rearranging

From pV = nRT you can make any variable the subject:

- n = pV / RT
- V = nRT / p
- p = nRT / V
- T = pV / nR

Combine it with n = m / M when a mass is involved. This gives M = mRT / pV, the form used for a
volatile liquid.

### Worked example 1: amount and mass of a gas

A sealed glass bulb of volume 0.750 dm³ contains chlorine, Cl₂, at 18 °C and 96.0 kPa. Calculate the
mass of chlorine in the bulb. R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; Mr of Cl₂ = 71.0.

1. Convert: p = 96.0 × 10³ Pa; V = 0.750 × 10⁻³ m³; T = 18 + 273 = 291 K.
2. n = pV / RT = (96.0 × 10³ × 0.750 × 10⁻³) / (8.31 × 291) = 72.0 / 2418 = 0.02977 mol.
3. Mass = n × Mr = 0.02977 × 71.0 = **2.11 g** (3 significant figures, matching the data).

### Worked example 2: finding a temperature

0.0400 mol of nitrogen is held in a 1.20 dm³ container at 75.0 kPa. Calculate the temperature of the gas.

1. Convert: p = 75.0 × 10³ Pa; V = 1.20 × 10⁻³ m³.
2. T = pV / nR = (75.0 × 10³ × 1.20 × 10⁻³) / (0.0400 × 8.31) = 90.0 / 0.3324.
3. T = **271 K**, which is about −2 °C.

### Worked example 3: a volume, converted back

Calculate the volume, in dm³, occupied by 4.40 g of propane, C₃H₈ (Mr = 44.0), at 310 K and
2.00 × 10⁵ Pa.

1. n = 4.40 / 44.0 = 0.100 mol.
2. V = nRT / p = (0.100 × 8.31 × 310) / (2.00 × 10⁵) = 1.288 × 10⁻³ m³.
3. Convert back: 1.288 × 10⁻³ m³ × 10³ = **1.29 dm³**.

The equation returns m³; convert at the end if needed.

### Practical: the Mr of a volatile liquid

The method links AT a (measuring mass, volume and temperature), AT b (heating) and AT k (safe handling
of flammable liquids).

1. Heat a gas syringe in an oven or steam jacket to a steady temperature above the liquid's boiling
   point. Record the starting volume reading.
2. Weigh a small hypodermic syringe containing the liquid. Inject a few drops through the self-sealing
   cap into the gas syringe. Reweigh the small syringe at once. The mass injected is the difference.
3. The liquid vaporises. Record the new volume reading, the temperature and the room pressure.
4. Use M = mRT / pV with the volume of vapour (final reading minus starting reading).

### Worked example 4: processing the data

0.188 g of a liquid gives 73.5 cm³ of vapour at 120 °C and 98.5 kPa. R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

1. Convert: T = 393 K; p = 98.5 × 10³ Pa; V = 73.5 × 10⁻⁶ m³.
2. M = mRT / pV = (0.188 × 8.31 × 393) / (98.5 × 10³ × 73.5 × 10⁻⁶) = 84.8 g mol⁻¹.
3. Mr = **84.8** (no units).

Uncertainty (PS 3.3). If the balance reads to ±0.001 g, the mass by difference uses two readings, so
its conservative uncertainty is ±0.002 g, or 1.06% of 0.188 g. The vapour volume is also a difference:
final syringe reading minus initial reading. If each reading is ±0.5 cm³, the conservative uncertainty
in the difference is ±1.0 cm³, or 1.36% of 73.5 cm³. Using the conservative rule that percentage
uncertainties add in a product or quotient, the mass and volume contributions alone give about 2.4%.
This is not a total uncertainty: pressure and temperature uncertainties have not been supplied. If a
question specifies independent random-reading errors and quadrature, use that stated convention instead.
Do not infer an overall uncertainty or a narrow Mr interval from only one syringe-reading uncertainty.

Errors that push the answer the wrong way:

- Liquid that does not fully vaporise gives a smaller volume, so the calculated Mr is too **high**.
- Liquid that evaporates from the needle before reweighing makes the recorded mass too large, so Mr is
  too **high**.
- Vapour escaping past a leaky cap gives a smaller volume, so Mr is again too **high**.
- Using the syringe reading without subtracting the starting volume gives a larger volume, so Mr is too
  **low**.

## 3.1.2.4 Empirical and molecular formula

### Definitions

- **Empirical formula:** the ratio of atoms of each element in a compound, written in its simplest
  whole-number form.
- **Molecular formula:** how many atoms of each element are actually present in the compound's formula
  unit, such as one molecule.

You reach the molecular formula by scaling every subscript of the empirical formula by the same whole
number. For a molecule:

**multiplier = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass**

The multiplier can be 1. Then the two formulae are the same.

### Method in steps

1. Write the mass (or percentage) of each element. Find any missing element by difference.
2. Turn each into moles of atoms using its Ar.
3. Scale the mole values so the smallest becomes 1.
4. Clear fractions: × 2 for a .5; × 3 for .33 or .67; × 4 for .25 or .75.
5. Write the formula with the whole numbers.

Keep at least three significant figures in step 2. Rounding early can turn 1.33 into 1.3 and hide the
true ratio.

### Worked example 5: percentage composition to molecular formula

A compound contains 40.68% carbon, 5.08% hydrogen and 54.24% oxygen by mass. Its Mr is 118.
Find its molecular formula. (Ar values to use: O = 16.0, C = 12.0, H = 1.0)

| | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| % by mass | 40.68 | 5.08 | 54.24 |
| ÷ Ar | 3.39 | 5.08 | 3.39 |
| ÷ smallest | 1.00 | 1.50 | 1.00 |
| × 2 | 2 | 3 | 2 |

Empirical formula = **C₂H₃O₂**. Empirical formula mass = 24.0 + 3.0 + 32.0 = 59.0.
Multiplier = 118 ÷ 59.0 = 2. Molecular formula = **C₄H₆O₄**.

Linking the two sections: the liquid in worked example 4 is 14.1% carbon, 2.4% hydrogen and 83.5%
chlorine by mass (Cl = 35.5). Moles are 1.175 : 2.4 : 2.352, which scale to 1 : 2 : 2, so the empirical
formula is CH₂Cl₂ (mass 85.0). The gas data gave Mr = 84.8, so the multiplier is 1 and the molecular
formula is also **CH₂Cl₂**.

Two common routes (AT a and AT k):

- **Make the oxide.** Heat a weighed metal in a crucible in air until the mass stops changing, then
  weigh the oxide. Raise the lid briefly from time to time so oxygen can enter while little solid escapes.
- **Reduce the oxide.** Heat a weighed oxide in a stream of a reducing gas such as hydrogen until only
  the metal is left, then weigh the metal. Let it cool in the gas stream so it does not reoxidise.

In both, **heat to constant mass**: heat, cool, weigh, and repeat until successive readings agree within
the balance's resolution. This is evidence that further measurable mass change has stopped under the
conditions used; it supports, but does not by itself prove, completion of the intended reaction.

### Worked example 6: an oxide of lead

A lead oxide is reduced by heating in hydrogen. Lead compounds are toxic, so the work is done in a fume
cupboard. Results:

| Reading | Mass / g |
|---|---|
| empty tube and boat | 30.412 |
| tube, boat and oxide | 32.469 |
| tube, boat and lead (constant mass) | 32.277 |

(Ar: Pb = 207.2, O = 16.0)

1. Mass of lead = 32.277 − 30.412 = 1.865 g. Mass of oxygen = 32.469 − 32.277 = 0.192 g.
2. Moles: Pb = 1.865 / 207.2 = 0.009001; O = 0.192 / 16.0 = 0.01200.
3. Ratio Pb : O = 1 : 1.333. Multiply by 3: 3 : 4.
4. Empirical formula = **Pb₃O₄**.

Evaluation (PS 2.3). The oxygen mass comes from two readings of ±0.001 g, so ±0.002 g out of 0.192 g
is about 1.0%. If the oxide is not fully reduced, the final mass is too high, the oxygen mass too low,
and the ratio of O to Pb comes out too small.

## Common errors

- Putting kPa, cm³ or °C straight into pV = nRT. Each one gives an answer out by a factor of 10³, 10⁶
  or more.
- Forgetting to change m³ back into the cm³ or dm³ the question wanted.
- Rounding 1.33 or 1.50 to 1 in an empirical formula ratio.
- Using the oxide's mass as the oxygen's mass.
- Giving only the empirical formula when the question wants the molecular formula.
- Giving Mr with units of g mol⁻¹. Mr has no units; molar mass M does.
- Quoting more figures than the weakest measurement justifies (MS 1.1).
