These questions cover sub-topic 12.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699). Paper 4
itself contains only 35-mark essays; the Section A questions here are concept checks that build
the knowledge those essays need.

## Section A

**1.** Define *laicisation*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** problems in using church attendance figures as evidence of secularisation. **[4]**

**3.** Explain what Weber meant by the *disenchantment of the world*. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between an *inclusive* and an *exclusive* definition of religion, and explain how
the choice affects conclusions about secularisation. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by *secularisation from within*, with an example. **[3]**

**6.** Outline the argument of Voas and Crockett (2005) and explain how it challenges Davie. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** ways in which structural differentiation may reduce the social significance
of religion. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why David Martin argued that there is no single pattern of secularisation. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** "The evidence for a decline in religious belief is too unreliable to prove that
secularisation is taking place." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**10.** "Secularisation is a Western European experience, not a global process." Evaluate this
view. **[35]**

**11.** "Religious pluralism inevitably weakens religious belief." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Secularisation at the level of society [1]: institutions such as law, education and welfare
come to operate independently of religious authority (Dobbelaere) [1].

**2.** Any two, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for explaining why it matters):
- Attendance measures practice, not belief; people may believe without attending.
- People may attend for non-religious reasons (social status, a school place, family duty), so
  figures overstate religiosity.
- Organisations count differently, and some figures are estimates, so comparisons over time or
  between churches are unreliable.
- Figures cover mainstream churches and may miss growth in newer or informal groups.

**3.** Weber argued that rational, calculable thinking spreads in modern societies [1], so events
are explained by natural causes rather than magic or the supernatural [1]; the world comes to be
seen as something that can be understood and controlled, leaving less room for religious
explanation [1].

**4.** Exclusive definitions identify religion by belief in God or the supernatural [1]. Inclusive
definitions identify it by the functions it performs, such as creating solidarity [1]. Exclusive
definitions make decline easier to show because fewer activities count as religious [1];
inclusive definitions can treat national rituals or other belief systems as religion, making
religion look persistent [1].

**5.** Religious organisations remain active and attended [1] but adapt their teaching to secular
values, so religion itself becomes less distinctive [1]. Example: Herberg's (1955) argument that
American churches celebrated "the American way of life" more than distinctive doctrine [1].

**6.** Voas and Crockett analysed British survey data and found belief falling alongside
attendance [1], summarised as "neither believing nor belonging" [1]. Davie had argued that belief
persists while belonging declines [1], so their findings suggest a genuine decline in religion
rather than a change in its form [1].

**7.** Any two, 2 marks each:
- The state takes over education, so religion no longer controls how children are socialised.
- Welfare and health care move from religious charities to state services, reducing dependence on
  religious organisations.
- Law and politics operate on secular principles, so religious leaders lose formal power.

**8.** Martin argued that secularisation depends on each country's history [1], especially the
relationship between church and state, such as a single established church versus competing
denominations [1]; so the extent and form of decline varies from country to country rather than
following one universal path [1].

**9.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Reliability problems: membership counting varies between organisations; survey results change
  with question wording; census questions may record cultural identity.
- Validity problems: practice does not equal belief; Davie's believing without belonging and
  vicarious religion.
- Definitions: exclusive definitions build decline into the measure (link to 11.1).
- Golden-age problem: the 1851 Census of Religious Worship suggests the past was less devout than
  assumed.

**Against the view:**
- Different types of data (attendance, membership, belief surveys, rites of passage) point in the
  same direction in Western Europe; consistency across imperfect measures strengthens the case.
- Voas and Crockett (2005): belief is declining too.
- Bruce: generational decline appears in repeated surveys of different age groups.
- Societal-level evidence (laicisation) does not depend on counting individuals.

**Evaluation:** separate the levels; evidence is strongest for institutional decline in Western
Europe, weaker for private belief, weakest as a global claim. Reward answers that judge which
weaknesses actually matter rather than listing them.

**10.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Most evidence of decline comes from Western Europe; the thesis was built on its history.
- Norris and Inglehart (2004): religion persists where existential insecurity is high, and more
  religious societies have higher birth rates.
- Religious revival and political religion elsewhere (11.3 case studies such as the Iranian
  revolution).
- Berger's later view (1999) that much of the world is as religious as ever.

**Against the view:**
- Laicisation and rising "no religion" identity are not confined to Western Europe.
- Secularisation from within in the USA (Herberg) shows decline can take hidden forms outside
  Europe.
- Modernisation is a global process; if its causes spread, its effects may follow.

**Evaluation:** Martin's view that secularisation takes different patterns; distinguish societal
from individual levels; conclude whether Europe is the model or the exception.

**11.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Berger (1967): pluralism removes the single sacred canopy, causing a crisis of credibility.
- Bruce: social and cultural diversity turns faith into a personal option, which is easier to
  abandon.
- Competing claims encourage relativism: if all faiths may be true, none is certainly true.

**Against the view:**
- Religious market theory (Stark and Bainbridge): competition improves the "product" and
  stimulates participation, with the USA as the classic example.
- Pluralism may intensify belief through cultural defence or cultural transition (links to 12.3).
- Berger later revised his view.

**Evaluation:** "inevitably" is the key word; outcomes depend on context, such as whether
pluralism comes with state neutrality or conflict. A conclusion that pluralism weakens certainty
but not necessarily participation is a supported judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use 12.3 material in a 12.1 essay?** Yes, as counter-argument, provided you explain how
privatisation or resacralisation answers the 12.1 question.

**How many studies should a 35-mark essay use?** Enough to support each argument; explaining what
a study shows about the question earns more than naming many studies.
