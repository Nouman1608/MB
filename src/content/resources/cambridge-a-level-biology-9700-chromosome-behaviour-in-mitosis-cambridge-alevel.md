This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 5.2, Chromosome behaviour in mitosis, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content. The two learning outcomes ask you to describe the behaviour of chromosomes in plant and animal cells during the mitotic cell cycle, together with the nuclear envelope, the cell surface membrane and the spindle, naming prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase; and to interpret photomicrographs, diagrams and microscope slides to identify the stages.

The [mitotic cell cycle study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-the-mitotic-cell-cycle/) summarises the four stages and estimates the time spent in each from cell counts. This page follows each structure through the whole of mitosis, explains the evidence that tells similar stages apart, and covers how slides are prepared and drawn. Afterwards, use the [chromosome behaviour revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-chromosome-behaviour-in-mitosis-cambridge-alevel-revision-notes/) and the [chromosome behaviour practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-chromosome-behaviour-in-mitosis-cambridge-alevel-practice/).

## 5.2.1 Following each structure through mitosis

Most textbooks describe mitosis stage by stage. Examiners often ask about one structure at a time, so it helps to learn the same events structure by structure.

### Chromosomes

| Stage | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Interphase | Uncoiled as chromatin; not visible as separate structures; DNA replicated in S phase |
| Prophase | Coil and condense, becoming short, thick and visible; each seen as two sister chromatids joined at the centromere |
| Metaphase | Line up at the equator of the spindle, each attached by its centromere to microtubules from both poles |
| Anaphase | Centromeres divide; sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles, centromere first |
| Telophase | Chromatids, now chromosomes, arrive at the poles and begin to uncoil |

In anaphase the chromatids appear V-shaped, because the centromere is pulled ahead and the arms trail behind.

### Nuclear envelope

| Stage | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Prophase | Breaks down at the end of prophase into small vesicles; the nucleolus disappears |
| Metaphase and anaphase | Absent, so the spindle can reach the chromosomes |
| Telophase | Re-forms around each group of chromosomes; nucleoli reappear |

### Spindle

| Stage | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Prophase | Microtubules assemble into a spindle between the two poles; in animal cells the centrioles move to opposite poles and organise it |
| Metaphase | Microtubules attach to the centromere of each chromosome |
| Anaphase | Microtubules attached to centromeres shorten, pulling the chromatids apart |
| Telophase | The spindle breaks down |

Plant cells have **no centrioles**, yet they still form a spindle from microtubules.

### Cell surface membrane (and cell wall)

| Cell type | Behaviour during cytokinesis |
|---|---|
| Animal | The cell surface membrane is drawn inwards around the equator, forming a furrow that deepens until the cell divides in two |
| Plant | The rigid wall prevents furrowing. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse to form a cell plate; their membranes become the new cell surface membranes and their contents lay down a new wall between the daughter cells |

Cytokinesis usually begins during late anaphase or telophase.

## Counting during mitosis

**Worked example 1.** A plant cell has 6 chromosomes in G1.

| Stage | Chromosomes visible | Chromatids |
|---|---|---|
| Prophase | 6 (each double) | 12 |
| Metaphase | 6 on the equator | 12 |
| Anaphase | 12, moving as two groups of 6 | 12 (each now a chromosome) |
| Telophase | 6 at each pole | 6 at each pole |

If an image shows 6 V-shapes moving to each pole, the cell is in anaphase and the parent cell had 6 chromosomes.

## 5.2.2 Telling similar stages apart

| Confusion | Evidence to look for |
|---|---|
| Late prophase or metaphase? | Metaphase chromosomes form a single neat line across the middle; in prophase they are scattered |
| Metaphase or early anaphase? | In anaphase there are two lines or groups moving apart, with V-shaped chromatids |
| Late anaphase or telophase? | In telophase the groups are at the poles and clumped; nuclear envelopes may be visible; in plants a cell plate may be forming |
| Telophase or two interphase cells? | Interphase nuclei are round with a nucleolus and no visible chromosomes; telophase groups are still dense and irregular |
| Interphase or early prophase? | In early prophase thin threads become visible inside a nucleus that is still intact |

**Worked example 2: identify and justify.** A student describes four onion root tip cells.

- **Cell P:** an intact nucleus with a dark nucleolus and no threads. → **Interphase**: envelope and nucleolus present, no visible chromosomes.
- **Cell Q:** two dense groups of chromosomes at opposite ends, a faint line across the middle of the cell. → **Telophase**: groups at the poles and an early cell plate.
- **Cell R:** short, thick chromosomes in a band across the middle, no nuclear envelope. → **Metaphase**: chromosomes on the equator.
- **Cell S:** V-shaped structures in two groups part-way to the poles. → **Anaphase**: chromatids moving apart, centromere first.

A one-word answer often scores only if the reason is also given. Name the stage, then state the feature you used.

## Preparing and observing a root tip squash

Root tips are used because the region just behind the tip contains many dividing cells.

1. Cut the tip from a growing root, such as garlic or onion.
2. Warm it in dilute hydrochloric acid. This breaks down the material holding cells together, so they separate when squashed, and it stops further division.
3. Stain with a stain that binds to DNA, so that chromosomes show up darkly.
4. Place on a slide under a coverslip and press gently to spread the cells into a single layer.
5. Observe at low power to find the dividing region, then at high power to identify stages.

Wear eye protection when handling acid and stain.

## Drawing cells in mitosis

- Use a sharp pencil and single, continuous lines.
- Draw only what you see, at an appropriate size, with no shading.
- Show the outline of the cell, and the chromosomes as their actual shape and position.
- Label with ruled lines that do not cross, and add the magnification if required.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Describe the behaviour of the chromosomes and the spindle during mitosis in an animal cell. **[6]**

> In prophase the chromosomes condense and become visible, each made of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. *(Chromosomes, prophase.)*
>
> The centrioles move to opposite poles and the spindle forms. *(Spindle, prophase, animal-specific detail.)*
>
> In metaphase the chromosomes line up on the equator, attached to spindle microtubules by their centromeres. *(Both structures, metaphase.)*
>
> In anaphase the centromeres divide and the microtubules shorten, pulling sister chromatids to opposite poles. *(Cause and effect, anaphase.)*
>
> In telophase the chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. *(Chromosomes, telophase.)*
>
> The spindle breaks down. *(Spindle, telophase.)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying the nuclear envelope breaks down at the start of prophase; it is at the end.
- Writing "chromosomes are pulled apart" in anaphase instead of "sister chromatids separate".
- Placing chromosomes "in the middle of the nucleus" in metaphase; there is no nuclear envelope then.
- Stating that plant cells cannot form a spindle because they lack centrioles.
- Describing cytokinesis in plant cells as the membrane pinching in.
- Naming a stage on an image without the feature that identifies it.
