These notes cover Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 sub-topic **2.3 Isotopes** (2026–2028). Outcomes 1 and 2 are Core; points marked *(Extended only)* come from the Supplement. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [isotopes study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-isotopes/); questions are in the [isotopes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-isotopes-practice/).

## Definition to learn exactly

**Isotopes** are different atoms of the same element that have the **same number of protons** but **different numbers of neutrons**.

So isotopes have:

- the same proton number (same element)
- different mass numbers
- the same number of electrons, as atoms.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Isotope | An atom of an element with a particular number of neutrons |
| Proton number | Number of protons; the bottom number in a symbol |
| Mass number | Protons + neutrons; the top number in a symbol |
| Abundance | How common an isotope is in a natural sample, as a percentage, a ratio or a number of atoms |
| Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ | The average mass of an element's atoms, weighted by the abundance of each isotope |
| Ion | A charged particle; the charge is written at the top right of the symbol |

## Reading a symbol

For ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻:

| Part | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Top left | 37 | Mass number |
| Bottom left | 17 | Proton number |
| Top right | − | Charge of 1− |
| Protons | 17 | Bottom number |
| Neutrons | 37 − 17 = 20 | Top − bottom |
| Electrons | 17 + 1 = 18 | Gained one electron |

## Isotopes or not?

- Same bottom number, different top number → **isotopes**.
- Different bottom numbers → different elements, **not isotopes**, even if the top numbers match (¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N).
- Same atom with a charge (³⁹₁₉K and ³⁹₁₉K⁺) → atom and ion, **not isotopes**.

Hydrogen's three isotopes: ¹₁H (0 neutrons), ²₁H (1 neutron), ³₁H (2 neutrons).

## Same chemical properties *(Extended only)*

Three-link chain:

1. same number of **electrons**
2. → same **electronic configuration**
3. → same **chemical properties**, because reactions involve electrons.

"Same number of protons" alone does not earn the mark.

## Relative atomic mass *(Extended only)*

Aᵣ = Σ(isotope mass × abundance) ÷ total abundance

| Data given as | Divide by |
|---|---|
| Percentages | 100 |
| Numbers of atoms | Total number of atoms |
| Ratio, such as 3 : 1 | Sum of the ratio, 4 |

**Quick examples**

- Lithium: 7.5% Li-6, 92.5% Li-7 → (45 + 647.5) ÷ 100 = 6.925 → **6.9**.
- Potassium: 93.3% K-39, 6.7% K-41 → (3638.7 + 274.7) ÷ 100 = 39.134 → **39.1**.
- Ratio 9 : 1 of X-20 to X-22 → (180 + 22) ÷ 10 = **20.2**.

**Checks**

- Aᵣ lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses.
- It lies nearer the most abundant isotope.
- Percentages should add up to 100.

## Setting out a calculation *(Extended only)*

Use the same four lines every time:

```
1  check abundances add up to the total you will divide by
2  Aᵣ = (mass × abundance + mass × abundance + ...) ÷ total
3  work out each product, then add them
4  divide, then round the final answer only
```

Example with three isotopes of an invented element X: 60% of mass 50, 30% of mass 52, 10% of mass 53.

```
1  60 + 30 + 10 = 100
2  Aᵣ = (50 × 60 + 52 × 30 + 53 × 10) ÷ 100
3  3000 + 1560 + 530 = 5090
4  5090 ÷ 100 = 50.9
```

Sense check: 50.9 lies between 50 and 53 and is nearest 50, the most abundant isotope. If your answer falls outside the range of the isotope masses, an abundance or a multiplication has gone wrong.

## Exam traps

- Leaving "same element" or "same number of protons" out of the definition.
- Saying isotopes have different numbers of electrons.
- Calling an atom and its ion isotopes.
- Swapping the top and bottom numbers.
- A plain average of isotope masses, ignoring abundance.
- Dividing by 100 when abundances are numbers of atoms.
- Rounding too early; round only the final answer, usually to 1 decimal place.

## Self-test

1. Define isotopes.
2. How many neutrons are in ³⁴₁₆S?
3. How many electrons are in ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺?
4. Are ¹²₆C and ¹³₆C isotopes? Explain.
5. Are ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca isotopes? Explain.
6. Name the three isotopes of hydrogen by mass number and give the number of neutrons in each.
7. *(Extended only)* Why do chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 have the same chemical properties?
8. *(Extended only)* An element is 75% isotope of mass 7 and 25% isotope of mass 6. Calculate Aᵣ to 1 decimal place.
9. *(Extended only)* A sample contains 3 atoms of copper-63 for every 1 atom of copper-65. Calculate Aᵣ to 1 decimal place.
10. *(Extended only)* Why is the answer to question 9 closer to 63 than to 65?
11. What do the top and bottom numbers in ¹⁶₈O represent?
12. *(Extended only)* Why is a simple average of isotope masses wrong?

**Answers**

1. Different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
2. 34 − 16 = 18.
3. 20 − 2 = 18.
4. Yes: both have 6 protons, but 6 and 7 neutrons.
5. No: they have different numbers of protons (18 and 20), so they are different elements.
6. Hydrogen-1 (0), hydrogen-2 (1), hydrogen-3 (2).
7. They have the same number of electrons, so the same electronic configuration, so they react in the same way.
8. (7 × 75 + 6 × 25) ÷ 100 = 675 ÷ 100 = 6.75 = 6.8 (1 d.p.).
9. (63 × 3 + 65 × 1) ÷ 4 = 254 ÷ 4 = 63.5.
10. Copper-63 is three times as abundant as copper-65.
11. Top: mass number (16). Bottom: proton number (8).
12. It ignores how common each isotope is.
