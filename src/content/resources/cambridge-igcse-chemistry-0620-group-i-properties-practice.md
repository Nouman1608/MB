**1.** Any two from: relatively soft / can be cut with a knife [1]; low density / float on water [1]; low melting points.

**2. (a)** Decreases [1].

**(b)** Increases [1].

**3.** **C** [1].

**4.** Hydrogen [1]; hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the tube [1]; squeaky pop [1].

**5.** LiOH [1].

**6. (a)** Any three from: floats [1]; melts into a ball [1]; moves around the surface / fizzes / gets smaller and disappears [1].

**(b)** 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g): correct formulae and balancing [1]; state symbols [1].

**(c)** Blue or purple [1]; sodium hydroxide is formed, which is an alkali / the solution contains OH⁻ ions [1].

**7.** Lithium fizzes steadily / slowly [1]; sodium fizzes rapidly and melts [1]; potassium reacts violently and the hydrogen ignites, so reactivity increases down the group [1].

**8. (a)** Any value below 39 °C, e.g. 25–35 °C [1].

**(b)** Solid [1], if the predicted melting point is above 20 °C (the trend gives a value only a little below rubidium's 39 °C) [1]. (Accept liquid if the predicted value is below 20 °C and the reasoning is consistent.)

**(c)** Reacts very vigorously / explosively, more than rubidium [1]; caesium hydroxide [1]; hydrogen [1].

**10. (a)** Potassium chloride [1].

**(b)** 2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl: correct formulae [1]; balanced [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the oxide instead of the hydroxide as a product of the reaction with water.
- Forgetting to name hydrogen, or giving the test for oxygen (glowing splint) instead.
- Reversing the melting point trend.
- Writing unbalanced equations, such as Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂.
- Giving vague predictions without comparing to the element above.
- Listing "shiny" as unusual: most metals are shiny; the unusual properties are softness, low density and low melting point.
