This page serves **both routes**. The anthology question is Section A of Paper 2a on Route A and
Section A of Paper 2b on Route B; on each it is worth 30 marks and deserves about 45 minutes. Section
A below trains comparison skills with short tasks; Section B gives full 30-mark questions.

## Section A: comparison skills

Read Poem 1 and Poem 2, then answer Questions 1 to 6.

> **Poem 1: Grandmother's Field**
>
> She knew the field by its arguments:
> where the clay held water, where the wind
> refused to settle. Each spring she walked it,
> boundary to boundary, like reading a letter
> from someone she had quarrelled with and loved.

> **Poem 2: New Flat, Fourteenth Floor**
>
> From here the city is a map
> I have not learned to read.
> Lights come on in other people's windows.
> I name them, one by one,
> as if naming made them mine.

**1.** Identify one theme the two poems share. **[1]**

**2.** In Poem 1, explain the effect of the simile "like reading a letter / from someone she had
quarrelled with and loved". **[4]**

**3.** In Poem 2, how does the poet use the image of a map to present the speaker's relationship
with the city? **[3]**

**4.** Write one sentence that compares the two speakers' relationships with place, using a
comparative connective. **[2]**

**5.** Compare the endings of the two poems. What does each final line suggest? **[4]**

**6.** Poem 1 is written in the third person and Poem 2 in the first person. Explain how this
difference affects the reader's response. **[4]**

**7.** A student writes: *"Both poems are about places. Poem 1 is about a field and Poem 2 is about a
city."* Explain why this would score poorly and rewrite it as an analytical comparison. **[3]**

## Section B: exam-style questions (use your own studied *People and Places* poems)

**8.** Compare how poets present a person's sense of belonging to a place in **two** poems from
*People and Places*. **[30]**

**9.** Compare how poets present memory and the past in **two** poems from *People and Places*.
**[30]**

**10.** Compare how poets use the structure of their poems to present change in **two** poems from
*People and Places*. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Any one: the relationship between a person and a place; belonging; knowing or learning a place
[1].

**2.** Up to four marks. The simile presents the field as something that communicates, like a letter,
so the grandmother "reads" the land [1]. "Quarrelled with and loved" captures a relationship that is
both difficult and affectionate [1], suggesting that working the land involves struggle as well as
attachment [1]. The comparison makes the field feel like a family member, deepening the sense of a
lifelong bond [1].

**3.** Up to three marks. A map is a guide to a place [1], but the speaker has "not learned to read"
it, suggesting unfamiliarity or exclusion [1]. The image presents the city as something to be decoded,
so belonging is not yet achieved [1].

**4.** One mark for a valid comparison and one for a correct comparative connective. Example:
*"Whereas the grandmother knows her field intimately, the speaker of Poem 2 is still a stranger to the
city."* [2]

**5.** Up to four marks, two per poem. Poem 1 ends with "quarrelled with and loved", so the final word
settles on love and suggests that the bond with the land outlasts the struggle [2]. Poem 2 ends with
"as if naming made them mine": "as if" admits that naming does not create belonging, so the ending is
hopeful but uncertain [2].

**6.** Up to four marks. The third person in Poem 1 lets the reader observe the grandmother with
admiration from outside, perhaps through a grandchild's eyes [1], so her knowledge seems
long-established and almost legendary [1]. The first person in Poem 2 places the reader inside the
speaker's loneliness [1], so the uncertainty feels immediate and personal [1].

**7.** One mark for explaining the weakness: it states subject matter without comparing attitudes or
methods [1]. Two marks for a rewrite that compares attitude and method. Example: *"Both poets explore
how people come to know a place, but Poem 1 presents belonging as earned through years of walking the
field, whereas Poem 2's map imagery shows a speaker who has not yet learned to 'read' the city."* [2]

### Section B (approach: marks depend on your chosen poems)

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors, not point by point. The guidance
below describes a high-level response.

**8. Belonging.** A strong answer:

- chooses two poems that treat belonging differently, for example one in which the place gives the
  speaker identity and one in which the speaker feels excluded (AO1);
- states a comparative argument in the introduction;
- compares methods in every paragraph: voice and perspective, imagery of the place, tone, and form
  or structure (AO2);
- uses short, precise quotations from both poems in each paragraph;
- uses context only where it explains a choice, such as a poem written about migration (AO1);
- reaches a personal judgement on what the poems suggest belonging depends on (AO3).

**9. Memory.** A strong answer:

- compares how each poem moves between past and present, for example through tense shifts, flashback
  or a closing return to the present (AO2);
- analyses imagery that carries memory, such as objects, sounds or places;
- explores attitudes to the past: nostalgia, regret, celebration or loss (AO1);
- keeps the comparison integrated, using connectives and paired evidence (AO3).

**10. Structure and change.** A strong answer:

- identifies what changes in each poem (a person, a place, a relationship) and links structure
  directly to that change;
- analyses specific structural choices: stanza length, line length, enjambment, caesura, a volta, the
  relationship between opening and ending (AO2);
- compares how far each poem resolves the change, and why;
- avoids simply describing the form, always explaining its effect (AO2, AO3).

At the lower levels, answers describe each poem separately with little analysis of method; mid-level
answers compare clearly with some explained effects; top-level answers sustain an argued, integrated
comparison across language, form and structure.

## How to use this page

Do Section A in about 20 minutes. Then attempt one Section B question in 45 minutes with your
anthology open, as in the exam. Afterwards, check that every paragraph mentions both poems; if any
paragraph covers only one, rewrite it as an integrated comparison.
