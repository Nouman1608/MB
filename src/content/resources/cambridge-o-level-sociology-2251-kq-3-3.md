Key question 3.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How has globalisation affected
inequalities between societies?* Questions 3.1 and 3.2 look mainly at inequality *within* a society;
3.3 widens the lens to the gaps *between* richer and poorer societies. The syllabus names three areas:
migration, global ecological issues and sociological explanations for global inequality. Strong Paper
1 Question 3 answers explain the mechanism that links each issue to inequality and show that effects
are uneven, with winners and losers in every society.

The [O Level guide to tackling inequality and globalisation](/resources/o-level-sociology-reducing-inequality-and-globalisation/)
gives an overview of 3.2 and 3.3 together. This guide develops 3.3 with further examples and the
theoretical explanations in more depth.

## What the syllabus covers

- **3.3.1 The impact of migration on societies:** immigration/emigration; push and pull factors;
  increasing cultural diversity; economic consequences; negative perceptions of migrants.
- **3.3.2 The impact of global ecological issues on societies:** the influence of transnational
  companies; climate change; pollution; urbanisation.
- **3.3.3 Sociological explanations for global inequalities:** Marxist views of capitalist
  exploitation; colonialism; feminist ideas of patriarchy.

**Globalisation** is the growing interconnection of the world's societies through trade, investment,
communication, migration and culture. It is not new, but faster transport and digital technology have
greatly increased its speed and reach.

## Migration

**Immigration** means arriving in a country to settle; **emigration** means departing from one's
own country to settle elsewhere. The
same person is an emigrant from one society and an immigrant to another, so every movement affects two
societies at once.

### Push and pull factors

Everett Lee's model (1966) explains migration as the result of **push factors** in the place of origin
and **pull factors** in the destination, filtered by **intervening obstacles** such as cost, distance,
visas and family ties.

| Push factors | Pull factors |
|---|---|
| Unemployment and low wages | Jobs and higher wages |
| Conflict and persecution | Safety and political freedom |
| Environmental disaster, drought, rising seas | Better services, healthcare and education |
| Lack of education and opportunity | Family and community already settled |

The obstacles explain why the poorest people often cannot migrate at all: international migration
requires money, documents and contacts. This is one reason migration can widen inequality within
sending societies, as better-off families gain access to foreign earnings.

### Economic consequences

**For receiving societies:** migrants fill labour shortages in sectors such as construction, care,
health and agriculture. After the Second World War, Britain recruited workers from the Caribbean and
South Asia to fill such shortages; the arrival of the ship *Empire Windrush* in 1948 has become a
symbol of this period. Migrants pay taxes and start businesses, but may be concentrated in low-paid
and insecure work.

**For sending societies:** **remittances** (money sent home) support family consumption, education
and housing, and are an important source of income for some countries. Against this, the emigration
of doctors, nurses and engineers, the **brain drain**, weakens services in societies that paid for
their training.

### Cultural diversity and negative perceptions

Migration increases **cultural diversity** in receiving societies: new languages, religions, food,
music and hybrid identities. It can also produce **negative perceptions**: migrants may be stereotyped
as taking jobs, lowering wages or increasing crime, and become scapegoats during economic difficulty.
Media coverage can amplify these fears. Negative perceptions feed discrimination in housing and work,
reproducing inequality inside the receiving society as well.

## Global ecological issues

### Transnational companies

**Transnational companies (TNCs)** operate in many countries, and some have revenues larger than the
economies of small states. They bring jobs, investment and technology, but critics argue they move
production to places with low wages and weak regulation, leaving environmental and human costs behind.

- In December 1984, a gas leak at the Union Carbide pesticide plant in **Bhopal**, India, killed
  thousands of people and left many more with long-term illness.
- Oil spills in the **Niger Delta**, Nigeria, linked to companies including Shell, have damaged
  farmland and fishing for local communities.

The pattern is that profits flow to shareholders, often in richer countries, while risks fall on
communities with the least power to resist.

### Climate change

The countries that have emitted the most greenhouse gases over time are mainly industrialised ones,
yet the worst effects often fall on poorer societies, which can least afford sea walls, irrigation
or relocation. Rising seas threaten the existence of Tuvalu and the Maldives, both barely above sea
level; farming communities face drought,
floods and failed harvests. This is sometimes described as a question of **climate justice**. Climate
change can also act as a push factor for migration.

### Pollution

Pollution is concentrated where regulation is weakest. Electronic waste from richer countries has been
shipped to places such as the Agbogbloshie site in Accra, Ghana, where workers, including children,
burn cables to recover metals, exposing themselves to toxic smoke. Air pollution in fast-growing
industrial cities affects health and life expectancy, especially for poorer residents who cannot move
away.

### Urbanisation

**Urbanisation** is the growth in the proportion of a population living in towns and cities. It is
fastest in lower-income countries, driven by rural poverty and the pull of urban jobs. Cities offer
opportunities, but rapid growth often outpaces housing and services, producing large informal
settlements such as Dharavi in Mumbai, with crowded housing and limited sanitation.

## Explaining global inequality

### Marxist views of capitalist exploitation

For Marxists, the gap between rich and poor nations is what capitalism produces once it spreads
across the whole world. Andre Gunder
Frank's **dependency theory** argued that rich "metropolis" nations developed by exploiting poor
"satellite" nations, a process he called the "development of underdevelopment". Immanuel
Wallerstein's **world-systems theory** divided the world economy into a **core** of wealthy states, a
**periphery** supplying cheap labour and raw materials, and a **semi-periphery** in between. Wealth
flows towards the core through trade and the profits of TNCs.

### Colonialism

From the sixteenth century to the twentieth, European states seized large parts of the world as
colonies, and in 1884–1885 their representatives met in Berlin to settle the terms on which Africa
would be carved up between them.
Colonial economies were organised to export raw materials and cash crops to the colonising country,
local industries were often undermined, and borders were drawn with little regard for the people living
within them. Sociologists argue that these patterns, along with debts and trade relationships formed
in that period, continue to shape inequality after independence.

### Feminist ideas of patriarchy

Feminists add gender to the picture: in their view, inequality between societies is also
inequality between men and women. Women do much of the
world's unpaid farming and domestic work, yet own a small share of land and property. Global factories
in export industries such as clothing and electronics often employ young women at low wages because
they are seen as cheap and compliant. Arlie Hochschild (2000) described **global care chains**:
women from poorer countries migrate to care for children in richer ones, while their own children are
cared for by relatives or lower-paid workers at home.

## Worked example: an evaluation plan (practice)

*Evaluate the view that transnational companies increase inequalities between societies.*

- **For:** profits flow to shareholders in richer countries; low wages and weak safety standards in
  poorer ones; environmental disasters such as Bhopal; Marxist and dependency theorists see TNCs as
  the main channel of exploitation; feminists note reliance on low-paid women's labour.
- **Against:** TNCs create jobs, pay taxes and transfer skills and technology; some poorer countries
  have grown rapidly through export manufacturing; inequality also has other causes, such as
  colonial history, conflict and climate.
- **Judgement:** TNCs bring benefits, but the terms on which they operate tend to favour richer
  societies, so they more often widen than narrow global gaps.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about inequality *within* a society when the question asks *between* societies.
- Confusing immigration and emigration.
- Treating migration as wholly good or wholly bad; show both effects for both societies.
- Describing climate change without explaining why it falls unequally.
- Naming colonialism without explaining its lasting mechanisms.

## Exam technique

- **Explain three** (parts c and d): issue, mechanism, effect on inequality, example.
- **Discuss** (part e): one explanation or issue in depth, with evidence.
- **Evaluate** (part f): weigh benefits of globalisation against its costs and conclude.
