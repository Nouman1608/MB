## Section A

**1.** Explain why a large multicellular plant needs a transport system. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** differences between the structure of a xylem vessel and a sieve tube element. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the positions of xylem and phloem in a vascular bundle of the stem of a herbaceous dicotyledonous plant. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the importance of lignin in xylem vessels. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the rate of water uptake measured by a potometer is not exactly the same as the rate of transpiration. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A student used a potometer with a capillary tube of internal diameter 1.0 mm. She placed a fan at different distances from the shoot and recorded how far the air bubble moved in 5.0 minutes.

| Fan distance | Bubble movement in 5.0 min / mm |
|---|---|
| No fan | 15 |
| 50 cm | 28 |
| 25 cm | 41 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of water uptake with the fan at 25 cm, in mm³ min⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the trend in the results. **[2]**

**(c)** State **two** variables the student should keep constant. **[1]**

**7.** Describe and explain how water moves from the soil into the xylem of a root. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how water moves up the xylem, and why the diameter of a tree trunk becomes very slightly smaller during a sunny day. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **three** features of marram grass leaves that reduce water loss. **[3]**

**10.** Explain **two** adaptations of a water lily to living in fresh water. **[2]**

**11.** Describe how sucrose is loaded into the phloem at a source. **[4]**

**12.** A ring of bark, including the phloem but not the xylem, is removed from around a tree trunk. After some weeks the bark just above the ring swells. Explain this observation. **[2]**

**13.** On a hot, dry, windy day the stomata of a plant close. Explain the advantage of this and one disadvantage. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The plant has a small overall SA:V and long distances between roots and leaves, so diffusion would be far too slow [1]. Roots need sugars made in the leaves, and leaves need water and mineral ions absorbed by the roots [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two [1 each]: xylem vessels are dead, sieve tube elements are living; xylem vessels have no end walls, sieve tube elements have sieve plates; xylem walls are lignified, sieve tube walls are not; xylem has no cytoplasm, sieve tubes have a thin layer of cytoplasm (with no nucleus). **[2]**

**3.** The bundles form a ring near the outside of the stem [1]. In each bundle the xylem is on the inner side and the phloem on the outer side, with cambium between [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Lignin strengthens the walls so the vessels do not collapse under the tension of the transpiration stream (and supports the plant) [1]. It waterproofs the walls, keeping water inside the vessels [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Some of the water taken up is used by the plant, for example in photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid, rather than lost as vapour [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Volume = πr²l = π × 0.50² × 41 = 32.2 mm³ [1]. Rate = 32.2 ÷ 5.0 = **6.4 mm³ min⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** The closer the fan, the faster the water uptake (and transpiration) [1]. More air movement removes the humid air next to the leaf, keeping the water vapour concentration gradient between the air spaces and the air steep, so water vapour diffuses out of the stomata faster [1].

**(c)** Any two for [1]: light intensity; temperature; humidity; the same shoot (same leaf area); time allowed to adjust. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* The diameter is given, so halve it before using πr²; using 1.0 mm as the radius makes the answer four times too large.

**7.** Root hair cells have a lower water potential than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis [1]. It crosses the cortex by the apoplast pathway, through cell walls, and the symplast pathway, through cytoplasm and plasmodesmata [1]. At the endodermis the waterproof band in the cell walls blocks the apoplast pathway, so water passes into the cytoplasm [1]. Mineral ions are actively pumped into the xylem, lowering its water potential, so water moves into the xylem by osmosis [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Evaporation of water from the leaves pulls water up the xylem (transpiration pull) [1]. Water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds (cohesion) and to the xylem walls (adhesion), so a continuous column is pulled up [1]. On a sunny day transpiration is fast, so the column is under greater tension, which pulls the xylem walls inwards slightly and narrows the trunk [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any three [1 each, feature with explanation]: leaves roll up, enclosing humid air and reducing the exposed surface; stomata are on the inner surface and sunk in pits, trapping humid air so the gradient is reduced; hairs on the inner surface trap moist air; a thick waxy cuticle on the outer surface reduces evaporation through the epidermis. **[3]**

**10.** Any two [1 each]: stomata on the upper surface of the floating leaves, in contact with air for gas exchange; large air spaces keep leaves afloat and allow oxygen to reach submerged parts; little lignified support tissue because water supports the plant; thin or no waxy cuticle because water loss is not a problem. **[2]**

**11.** Companion cells use ATP to actively pump hydrogen ions out into the surrounding cell walls [1]. This creates a concentration gradient of hydrogen ions [1]. Hydrogen ions diffuse back into the companion cells through co-transporter proteins, carrying sucrose with them [1]. Sucrose then moves from the companion cells into the sieve tube elements through plasmodesmata [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Removing the phloem stops translocation of sucrose down the trunk past the ring [1]. Assimilates build up in the phloem above the ring, and the tissue swells (water follows by osmosis) [1]. **[2]**

**13.** Advantage: less water vapour is lost, reducing the risk of wilting [1]. Disadvantage: less carbon dioxide enters, so the rate of photosynthesis falls [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the diameter as the radius in a potometer calculation.
- Describing the apoplast pathway as through cells rather than cell walls.
- Saying transpiration is caused by osmosis in the leaf.
- Naming xerophyte features without explaining how each reduces water loss.
- Describing sucrose as actively pumped directly into the sieve tubes, missing the hydrogen ion co-transport.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the speed of the bubble equal the speed of water in the xylem?**
No. The bubble measures the volume of water taken up per minute. The speed in the xylem depends on the total cross-sectional area of the vessels, which is different from the capillary tube.

**Why does sucrose loading need hydrogen ions?**
The co-transporter protein only moves sucrose into the companion cell when hydrogen ions move in with it, so the active pumping of hydrogen ions out of the cell provides the energy indirectly.

**What evidence shows the xylem is under tension?**
Trunk diameter falls slightly when transpiration is fast, and when a xylem vessel is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out.
