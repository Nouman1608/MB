## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the challenges facing future food security and health.
- Evaluate strategies for making food production and health care more sustainable and resilient.
- Recognise uncertainty about the future and reason with scenarios.
- Weigh trade-offs between food, health and the environment.

## Yield gaps and future land needs

A **yield gap** is the difference between the yield farmers actually achieve and the yield possible on the same land with the best available practices, water and inputs. Yield gaps are often largest where smallholders lack credit, fertiliser, irrigation or advice. Closing them is the main route to sustainable intensification, because it raises production without clearing more land.

### Worked example 1: three futures for one region

An invented region consumes 10 million tonnes of cereal a year, grown at an average yield of 2 tonnes per hectare, so it farms 10 / 2 = **5 million hectares**. Demand is projected to rise by 50% to 15 million tonnes.

| Scenario | Demand (million t) | Yield (t per ha) | Land needed (million ha) | Change in farmland |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: no change in yield | 15 | 2.0 | 7.5 | +2.5 |
| B: yield gap partly closed | 15 | 3.0 | 5.0 | 0 |
| C: waste cut, smaller yield gain | 13.5 | 2.5 | 5.4 | +0.4 |

In scenario C, cutting loss and waste reduces the demand that must be grown by 10% (15 x 0.9 = 13.5), and a modest yield rise does the rest. The arithmetic shows why yield, waste and diet are linked: each one changes how much land, water and forest must be given up. It also shows the scale of the challenge in scenario A, where farmland would have to grow by half.

## Land sparing or land sharing?

Conservation scientists debate two ways to fit farming and nature together:

- **Land sparing:** farm a smaller area as intensively and efficiently as possible, and leave the rest as protected habitat.
- **Land sharing:** farm a larger area in a wildlife-friendly way, with hedgerows, mixed crops, fewer chemicals and lower yields, so nature and farming coexist on the same land.

Sparing tends to protect species that need large undisturbed habitats, but intensive farming can pollute water and degrade soil, and higher yields may simply encourage more clearance if profits rise. Sharing supports biodiversity and soil health on farms, but needs more land for the same output. Many geographers conclude that the right mix depends on the landscape and the species at stake. The debate is useful for evaluating agroecology and sustainable intensification against each other.

## Malthus and Boserup: two frames for the future

- **Thomas Malthus** (1798) argued that population tends to grow faster than food supply, so growth would eventually be checked by famine, disease or other limits.
- **Ester Boserup** (1965) argued that population pressure stimulates innovation and the intensification of farming, so rising demand drives higher production.

The record since the mid-twentieth century has mostly supported Boserup at the global scale: food output has grown faster than population, largely through higher yields. But Malthusian concerns return in new forms: soil degradation, groundwater depletion, climate change and biodiversity loss suggest environmental limits that innovation may not overcome everywhere. Use the two views as competing hypotheses and test them against evidence for a named region.

## What makes a food system resilient

Resilience is the ability to absorb a shock, such as a drought, a price spike or a pandemic, and keep people fed. Features that build it:

- **Diversity** of crops, varieties, suppliers and income sources, so one failure does not remove everything.
- **Reserves**, such as household grain stores or national strategic stocks, to bridge a bad year.
- **Redundancy** in supply routes, so a blocked port or road can be bypassed.
- **Safety nets** that support poor households before they sell their assets.
- **Risk transfer**, for example index-based crop insurance that pays out when rainfall falls below a set level, avoiding costly farm-by-farm inspections.

Some features trade efficiency for security. A perfectly efficient system with no stocks and one supplier is cheap in good years and fragile in bad ones.

## Future health: preparing systems

Health futures depend on systems as much as on technology.

- **Universal health coverage**, a target within Sustainable Development Goal 3, aims for everyone to receive essential health services without financial hardship. Without it, illness pushes households into poverty, which in turn worsens nutrition.
- **Ageing populations** shift demand towards long-term care for chronic disease, requiring more community care rather than only hospitals.
- **Pandemic preparedness** relies on surveillance, laboratory capacity, stockpiles and the ability to scale up vaccine production and distribution fairly.
- **Changing disease ranges** as warming allows vectors to survive at higher altitudes and latitudes will require surveillance in places that have not needed it before.
- **Digital health**, such as telemedicine and mobile-phone health messaging, can reach remote areas, but depends on connectivity and digital skills, so it can widen gaps as well as close them.

### Worked example 2: comparing two future strategies

An invented low-income country with high child stunting and rising diabetes considers two programmes with the same budget.

| Criterion | Programme X: biofortified staple crops | Programme Y: urban diabetes clinics |
|---|---|---|
| Problem targeted | Hidden hunger in rural children | Non-communicable disease in urban adults |
| Reach | Large, via ordinary food | Smaller, needs clinic visits |
| Speed of benefit | Several harvests to scale up | Immediate for patients treated |
| Equity | Favours poorer rural households | Favours urban residents near clinics |
| Sustainability | Farmers can save and replant some varieties | Needs continuing staff and drug costs |

A judgement might favour X for long-term equity and reach, while noting that Y addresses a growing burden that X does not touch, so a double burden ultimately needs both. Laying criteria out like this keeps an evaluation balanced.

## Annotated model answer

*To what extent is technology the answer to future food security?* **(sample paragraph)**

"Technology can close yield gaps, as improved varieties, irrigation and precision fertiliser use raise output without clearing more land, supporting Boserup's view that innovation can keep pace with demand **[argument for, linked to theory]**. However, technology reaches farmers only if they can afford it, so without credit and advice yield gaps persist among the smallholders who are most food-insecure **[access limit]**. Technology also does little for access and stability, which depend on income, prices and safety nets **[dimensions of food security]**. Technology is therefore necessary but insufficient: its contribution depends on institutions that spread its benefits **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating future food security as only a production problem.
- Assuming higher yields always protect nature, ignoring the land sparing and sharing debate.
- Presenting Malthus or Boserup as simply right or wrong.
- Forgetting that efficiency and resilience can conflict.
- Discussing future health only as new medicines, ignoring health systems.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** future strategies against criteria such as effectiveness, equity, cost, sustainability and resilience.
- **To what extent** needs a position plus conditions under which it holds.
- **Discuss** competing frames such as Malthus and Boserup with evidence on both sides.
- Treat projections as uncertain: compare scenarios rather than quoting a single figure.
