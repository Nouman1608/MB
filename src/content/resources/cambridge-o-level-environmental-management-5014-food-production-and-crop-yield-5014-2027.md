> **Syllabus edition note.** This guide follows the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 2.2 is about the central tension in farming: the world needs more food, but many ways of
producing it damage the land that future harvests depend on. The
[Topic 2 Land guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-land/) lists each strategy and
impact. This page groups the twelve strategies by the problem they solve, explains each impact as a
cause-and-effect chain, and works through a data question on irrigation.

## What the syllabus asks

- **2.2.1** Describe types of agriculture: arable, pastoral and mixed; subsistence and commercial;
  intensive; monoculture.
- **2.2.2** Define sustainable food production as "the production of sufficient food for the present
  generation using methods that ensure future generations can grow food from the same land".
- **2.2.3** Describe and explain twelve strategies to increase food production and crop yield.
- **2.2.4** Describe and explain nine impacts of unsustainable agricultural practices on the environment
  and people.

## Types of agriculture

The types are not alternatives to choose between: one farm can belong to several. A large wheat farm
selling its grain is **arable**, **commercial**, **intensive** and a **monoculture** all at once.

| Pair or type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Arable | Growing crops | Rice, wheat, vegetables |
| Pastoral | Rearing animals | Cattle, sheep, goats |
| Mixed | Both crops and animals on one farm | Maize grown alongside goats |
| Subsistence | Food grown mainly to feed the farmer's family | A small family plot of maize and beans |
| Commercial | Food produced for sale | A large farm selling to supermarkets |
| Intensive | High inputs of labour, money, fertiliser or machinery per area to get high yields | Greenhouses; heavily fertilised fields |
| Monoculture | A single crop grown over a large area | Large plantations of one crop |

## Sustainable food production

The definition has two parts: **sufficient food for the present** and **future generations can grow food
from the same land**. A method is sustainable only if it does both. Doubling yields by exhausting the
soil fails the second test; protecting the soil while people go hungry fails the first.

## Twelve strategies, grouped by purpose

### Keeping soil fertile

- **Mixed cropping, intercropping and crop rotation.** Mixed cropping grows two or more crops together in the
  same field at the same time; intercropping does the same in a set pattern, such as alternate rows; crop
  rotation grows different crops in a field in successive seasons. Different crops take different nutrients and suffer from
  different pests, so nutrients are used more evenly and pest and disease cycles are broken. Including a
  legume, such as beans, adds nitrogen to the soil.
- **Inorganic NPK fertilisers.** Manufactured fertilisers supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, the
  ions crops remove from the soil, so yields rise quickly.
- **Organic fertilisers: crop residue, manure and mulch.** Leftover stalks and leaves, animal manure and a
  layer of mulch add nutrients slowly as they decompose and add organic matter, which improves soil
  structure and water retention.

### Supplying water

- **Improved methods of irrigation.** In **trickle/drip irrigation**, narrow perforated tubes laid along
  the rows release water slowly at the base of each plant, so the soil surface stays mostly dry and far
  less water evaporates or runs off than when a field is flooded. **Rainwater harvesting** captures rain
  from roofs, slopes or small dams and stores it in tanks or ponds until the dry season. **Automated
  watering systems** switch water on by timer, or when a sensor shows the soil is dry, so crops are not
  over- or under-watered.

### Protecting the crop

- **Chemical control of pests (pesticides).** **Insecticides** kill insects that eat crops, **herbicides**
  kill weeds that compete for light, water and nutrients, and **fungicides** kill fungi that cause disease.
- **Biological control of pests.** A natural predator, parasite or disease of the pest is introduced, for
  example insects that eat aphids, so pests are controlled without chemicals.

### Using technology

- **Mechanisation.** Tractors, ploughs and harvesters allow larger areas to be farmed faster, so more food
  is produced with less labour.
- **Genetically modified organisms (GMOs).** Defined as "changing the genetic material of an organism by
  removing, changing or inserting individual genes to give favourable characteristics", such as resistance
  to pests, disease or drought.
- **Controlled environments.** **Greenhouses** control temperature and protect crops from weather and
  pests. **Hydroponics** grows plants with their roots in a nutrient solution instead of soil;
  **aeroponics** grows them with roots in air, sprayed with a nutrient mist. Both allow crops to be grown
  where there is no fertile soil.

### Using land differently

- **Managed grazing: livestock rotation.** Animals are moved between fields so pasture can regrow before it
  is grazed again, preventing overgrazing.
- **Urban farming.** Growing food in towns and cities, on rooftops, in vertical farms or in gardens, close
  to the people who eat it, reducing transport.
- **Agroforestry.** Trees are grown among crops or pasture. The trees shelter crops from wind, their roots
  hold soil, their leaves add organic matter, and they provide fruit, nuts or timber.

## Nine impacts of unsustainable practices

Each needs a **cause**, a **process** and an **effect**.

1. **Overproduction of food: food wastage.** More food is produced than can be sold or eaten, so it is
   thrown away, wasting the land, water, fertiliser and energy used to grow it.
2. **Food shortages.** Land used for **cash crops** (coffee, cotton, soya beans, palm oil) or **biofuel
   crops** is no longer used to grow food for local people, so local food supplies fall and prices rise.
3. **Mismanagement of irrigation.** Over-watering raises the water table and causes **water logging**, so
   roots lack oxygen. In hot climates, irrigation water evaporates and leaves dissolved salts behind;
   salts build up in the soil (**salinisation**), and crops cannot take up water. Badly managed
   irrigation can also wash soil away (**soil erosion**).
4. **Overuse of pesticides.** A few pests survive each spraying and breed, so the population becomes
   resistant (**pesticide resistance**). Pesticides also kill the pests' natural predators, so pest numbers
   can rebound even higher (**pest resurgence**). Bees and other **pollinators** are killed, reducing
   pollination of crops and wild plants.
5. **Overuse of fertilisers.** Fertiliser that crops do not take up is washed through the soil
   (**leaching**) into rivers and lakes, causing **nutrient enrichment** and **eutrophication**: algae
   multiply, block light, die and are decomposed by bacteria that use up the oxygen, so fish and other
   animals die.
6. **Exhaustion of nutrients in soils.** Growing crops year after year without replacing **organic content
   and inorganic ions** leaves the soil infertile, so yields fall.
7. **Removal of natural vegetation.** **Deforestation**, **overcultivation** (farming land so intensively
   it has no time to recover) and **overgrazing** (too many animals for the pasture) leave soil bare.
8. **Monoculture and intensification.** Heavy use of chemicals pollutes soil and water, and replacing
   varied habitats with one crop causes **loss of biodiversity**. One pest or disease can also destroy a
   whole crop.
9. **Soil erosion: loss of topsoil and nutrients.** Bare or exhausted soil is removed by wind and water,
   taking its most fertile layer.

## Worked example: comparing irrigation methods

*Invented data* for one hectare of the same crop:

| Method | Water used (m³) | Yield (tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| Flood irrigation | 8000 | 4.0 |
| Trickle/drip irrigation | 5000 | 4.6 |

```
yield increase = (4.6 - 4.0) / 4.0 x 100 = 15%
water per tonne, flood = 8000 / 4.0 = 2000 m³ per tonne
water per tonne, drip  = 5000 / 4.6 = 1087 m³ per tonne (to the nearest whole number)
```

*Explanation:* drip irrigation puts water directly at the roots, so less evaporates or runs off; the soil
is not waterlogged, so roots get oxygen; and less water evaporating from the surface means less
salinisation. Its limitation is the cost of pipes and the maintenance needed to stop holes clogging.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the types of agriculture as exclusive: one farm can be commercial, intensive and arable.
- Defining sustainable food production with only one of its two parts.
- Naming a strategy without explaining how it increases yield.
- Confusing pesticide resistance with pest resurgence.
- Describing eutrophication as "fertiliser poisons fish". The fish die from lack of oxygen.
- Saying salinisation is caused by sea water. It is caused by evaporation of irrigation water leaving
  salts.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a type of agriculture: what is produced, for whom and how intensively.
- **Describe and explain** a strategy: what is done, then how it raises yield.
- **Describe and explain** an impact: cause → process → effect on the environment or people.
- **Calculate**: show working; use the original value as the base for percentage change.
