Research methods are the tools sociologists use to collect evidence, and in Cambridge International
AS & A Level Sociology (9699) they make up the second half of Paper 1. Sections 2.1 and 2.3 of the
syllabus ask you to know the main types of data and methods, the stages of designing a study, and
the issues (bias, validity, reliability, representativeness and ethics) used to judge the value of
any piece of research. Question 2(b) on the paper asks you to evaluate a named research method, so
the strengths and limitations below are core exam material.

Section 2.2 (positivism, interpretivism, mixed methods and whether sociology is a science) is
treated in the [Paper 1 overview guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-socialisation-identity-methods/);
this guide concentrates on 2.1 and 2.3.

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 1, section 2 *Methods of
research*:

- **2.1 Types of data, methods and research design** — primary and secondary data; quantitative and
  qualitative data; strengths and limitations of official statistics, personal documents, digital
  content and media sources; quantitative methods (questionnaires, structured interviews,
  experiments, content analysis); qualitative methods (overt and covert participant and
  non-participant observation, unstructured, semi-structured and group interviews); stages of
  research design (research strategy, research questions and hypotheses, sampling frames, sampling
  techniques, pilot studies, operationalisation, conducting research and interpreting results).
- **2.3 Research issues** — theoretical, practical and ethical considerations influencing the
  choice of topic, choice of method and conduct of research; how findings may be biased by the
  values and actions of the sociologist and by choices made in funding, designing and conducting
  research; validity, reliability, objectivity, representativeness and ethics as concepts for
  assessing research.

## What is the difference between primary, secondary, quantitative and qualitative data?

| | Quantitative (numbers) | Qualitative (words, images, meanings) |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** (collected by the researcher) | Closed-question questionnaire, structured interview, experiment | Unstructured interview, participant observation |
| **Secondary** (already exists) | Official statistics, existing survey data | Diaries, letters, social media posts, news articles |

Quantitative data suits measuring patterns and testing hypotheses; qualitative data suits
understanding meanings. Many studies combine both.

## How useful are secondary sources?

- **Official statistics** are cheap, large-scale and allow comparison over time, but they reflect
  the definitions and recording practices of the agencies that produce them. J. Maxwell Atkinson's
  *Discovering Suicide* (1978) showed coroners deciding which deaths counted as suicide, so the
  statistics are partly a social construction.
- **Personal documents** (diaries, letters, autobiographies) give rich insight into meaning, but
  may be unrepresentative and written for an audience.
- **Digital content** (posts, blogs, forums, search data) is plentiful and often unprompted, but
  raises questions of authenticity, who is online, and whether public posts can ethically be used
  without consent.
- **Media sources** reveal how issues are framed but tell you about the media's agenda as much as
  about the event; the Glasgow University Media Group's *Bad News* (1976) used content analysis of
  television news to show this.

## What are the strengths and limitations of quantitative methods?

| Method | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | Large samples, cheap, standardised so reliable, easy to compare | Low response rates; closed questions impose the researcher's categories; respondents may lie |
| **Structured interview** | Higher response rate than postal questionnaires; interviewer can clarify | Interviewer effect; still limited depth; more costly |
| **Experiment** (laboratory or field) | Control of variables; can show cause and effect | Artificial; Hawthorne effect; ethical limits; Rosenthal and Jacobson's field experiment *Pygmalion in the Classroom* (1968) is hard to replicate |
| **Content analysis** | Systematic, replicable study of media and documents | Coding categories reflect the researcher's judgement; counts frequency not meaning |

## What are the strengths and limitations of qualitative methods?

- **Overt participant observation** — the group knows who you are, which is ethical, but they may
  change their behaviour. William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943) relied on a key
  informant, "Doc", to gain access.
- **Covert participant observation** — gives access to closed or deviant groups and natural
  behaviour, but involves deception and risk. Laud Humphreys' *Tearoom Trade* (1970) is the classic
  ethical controversy.
- **Non-participant observation** — the researcher watches without joining in; less risk of
  "going native" but less insight into meaning.
- **Unstructured interviews** — high validity, rapport and depth; Ann Oakley (1981) argued that a
  non-hierarchical style produces more honest answers. Limitations: time, cost, small samples,
  difficulty comparing answers.
- **Semi-structured interviews** — a set of core questions plus follow-ups, combining comparability
  with flexibility.
- **Group interviews** — reveal how views are formed in interaction, but dominant voices and peer
  pressure can distort answers.

## What are the stages of research design?

1. **Research strategy** — deciding the overall approach (survey, case study, ethnography).
2. **Research questions and hypotheses** — a hypothesis is a testable prediction, used mainly in
   positivist designs.
3. **Operationalisation** — turning a concept into something measurable (for example measuring
   "religiosity" by attendance, prayer frequency and belief).
4. **Sampling frame** — the list from which the sample is drawn (electoral register, school roll).
5. **Sampling technique** — random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball or opportunity.
6. **Pilot study** — a small trial run to test questions, timing and access.
7. **Conducting research** — collecting the data, managing access and ethics.
8. **Interpreting results** — analysing, checking for bias and drawing conclusions.

In an exam, linking each stage to a concept from 2.3 (a pilot study improves validity; a
stratified sample improves representativeness) is what lifts a description into analysis.

## What influences a sociologist's choice of topic and method?

**Theoretical factors** — positivists prefer reliable, quantitative methods; interpretivists prefer
valid, qualitative ones. **Practical factors** — time, cost, access, the researcher's skills and
personal characteristics. **Ethical factors** — informed consent, confidentiality, avoiding harm,
deception and the vulnerability of participants (children, prisoners). Choice of *topic* is also
shaped by values, funding and what is fashionable or newsworthy.

## How can research be biased?

The syllabus singles out bias from the **sociologist's values and actions** and from **funding,
design and conduct**:

- Howard Becker's "Whose side are we on?" (1967) argued that research inevitably takes sides, so
  sociologists should be open about their sympathies.
- Funders such as governments, charities or corporations may shape which questions are asked and
  which results are published.
- Leading questions, poor sampling and the interviewer effect introduce bias in design and conduct.
- Interpretation itself can be selective, especially in qualitative work where the researcher
  chooses which quotations to report.

## How do you assess the value of a research method?

| Concept | Meaning | Higher in |
|---|---|---|
| **Validity** | Does the data give a true picture of what it claims to measure? | Unstructured interviews, participant observation |
| **Reliability** | Would the same method give the same results if repeated? | Questionnaires, structured interviews, official statistics |
| **Objectivity** | Freedom from the researcher's own values | Standardised methods (though contested) |
| **Representativeness** | Is the sample typical of the wider population? | Large, randomly or stratified-sampled surveys |
| **Ethics** | Is the research morally acceptable? | Overt methods with informed consent |

## Frequently asked questions

**How should I answer a 2(b) "evaluate" question on a method?** Explain strengths and limitations
using the 2.3 concepts by name, and apply each to the research context in the question, for example
why covert observation of a youth gang raises both validity gains and safety risks.

**Is covert observation ever acceptable?** Many sociologists accept it where the group would never
consent and the knowledge gained is important, but it breaks informed consent and must protect
participants' identities.

**What is the difference between a sampling frame and a sample?** The frame is the full list of the
population; the sample is the part selected from it.

**Do I need to know 2.2 for a methods question?** Often yes: positivism and interpretivism explain
*why* researchers prefer certain methods, so link back to them.
