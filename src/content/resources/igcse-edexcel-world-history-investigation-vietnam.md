Historical Investigation A4, "The Vietnam Conflict, 1945–75", is one of five investigation options
in Section A of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It traces Vietnam's
struggle for independence from France, the division of the country in 1954, the growing American
commitment that led to full-scale war from 1965, the turning point of the Tet Offensive, Nixon's
withdrawal and the communist victory in 1975, together with the war's impact on Vietnamese civilians
and on American opinion. The exam is source-based.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics for A4:

1. **The struggle against France for independence, 1945–54** — the origins of the First Indochina War and
   the Vietminh's aims, Giap's tactics, the search for a diplomatic solution, and the French defeat at
   Dien Bien Phu and its consequences.
2. **US policy and intervention, 1954–64** — the Geneva Conference and the US response, Eisenhower and the
   Domino Theory, the formation of South Vietnam, life under Ho Chi Minh and Ngo Dinh Diem, the NLF and
   the Ho Chi Minh Trail, the Strategic Hamlet Programme, the fall of Diem (1963) and the Gulf of Tonkin
   incident (1964).
3. **Confrontation and escalation, 1964–68** — the Second Indochina War, Johnson, McNamara and Westmoreland,
   the jungle war, Search and Destroy, Operation Rolling Thunder, the televised war, Khe Sanh, the Tet
   Offensive and the Battle of Hue (1968).
4. **Nixon and Ford, 1969–75** — failed peace talks, Cambodia and Laos and the "secret bombing", relations
   with China, Kissinger and Le Duc Tho, Vietnamisation and withdrawal by 1973, the renewed North
   Vietnamese offensive, Ford's response, and the fall of Saigon (April 1975).
5. **The impact on civilians and attitudes in the USA** — effects on Vietnamese civilians, Hearts and Minds,
   My Lai, the Phoenix Programme, defoliation and bombing; university protests, media coverage, opposition
   and pro-war demonstrations; the Fulbright Hearings (1971).

## Why did Vietnam fight France, 1945–54?

Vietnam had been part of French Indochina. During the Second World War Japan occupied it, and the
communist-led **Vietminh** under **Ho Chi Minh** fought for independence. On 2 September 1945 Ho declared
Vietnam independent, but France returned to reclaim its colony. Talks failed, and war broke out at the
end of 1946.

The Vietminh's aims were national independence and a communist state. Their commander **General Giap**
used **guerrilla tactics** — ambushes, hit-and-run raids and the support of villagers — before moving to
larger conventional battles once his forces were strong enough. After the communist victory in China
(1949), Chinese supplies flowed to the Vietminh, while the USA paid an increasing share of France's war
costs. At **Dien Bien Phu** (March–May 1954) the French built a fortified base in a valley; Giap's troops
dragged artillery onto the surrounding hills and forced the garrison to surrender on 7 May 1954. Defeat
ended France's will to fight.

## Why did the USA become involved, 1954–64?

The **Geneva Conference** (1954) divided Vietnam temporarily at the **17th parallel**, with elections for
reunification due in 1956. The USA did not sign the final declaration. President **Eisenhower** explained
the **Domino Theory**: if Vietnam fell to communism, its neighbours would follow. The USA backed **Ngo
Dinh Diem**, who became president of the new **Republic of Vietnam** (South Vietnam) in 1955 and refused
to hold the 1956 elections, which Ho was expected to win.

In the North, Ho's land reform redistributed land but involved brutal campaigns against landlords. In
the South, Diem's government was corrupt, dominated by his family and Catholic in a mainly Buddhist
country; his persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation of a monk in
1963. The **National Liberation Front** (NLF, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong) was formed in
1960 to overthrow Diem, supplied from the North along the **Ho Chi Minh Trail** through Laos and
Cambodia. The **Strategic Hamlet Programme** (from 1962) moved peasants into fortified villages to cut
them off from the NLF, but it was resented and largely failed. Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup
in November 1963. In August 1964 the **Gulf of Tonkin incident** — reported attacks on US destroyers —
led Congress to pass a resolution giving President Johnson power to take military action.

## How and why did the war escalate, 1964–68?

**Lyndon Johnson**, advised by Defence Secretary **Robert McNamara**, launched **Operation Rolling Thunder**
(from March 1965), a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, and sent the first US combat
troops the same month. Under General **William Westmoreland**, troop numbers rose to over half a million
by 1968. The strategy of **Search and Destroy** used helicopters to find and kill enemy forces, measuring
success by "body counts". In the **jungle war**, the NLF used tunnels, booby traps and ambushes and could
melt into the civilian population.

Vietnam was the first **televised war**; Americans saw combat footage on the evening news. In January
1968 North Vietnamese forces besieged the US base at **Khe Sanh**, drawing American attention, and on
30–31 January launched the **Tet Offensive**, attacking cities across the South, including the US embassy
in Saigon. The battle for the old capital **Hue** lasted about a month. Militarily Tet was a defeat for the
communists, but it shattered official claims that the war was being won; Johnson announced in March
1968 that he would not seek re-election.

## How did Nixon and Ford end US involvement, 1969–75?

**Nixon** promised "peace with honour". **Vietnamisation** handed fighting to the South Vietnamese army
while US troops were withdrawn. Peace talks in Paris made little progress, and Nixon **widened the war**:
the **secret bombing** of Cambodia from 1969, an invasion of Cambodia in 1970 and an attack into Laos in
1971. His visit to **China** (1972) put pressure on Hanoi. **Henry Kissinger** and **Le Duc Tho** negotiated
in secret; after the North's offensive of spring 1972 and heavy US bombing in December 1972, the **Paris
Peace Accords** were signed in January 1973, and the last US combat troops left by March 1973.

The North launched a renewed offensive. Congress refused President **Ford**'s requests for more aid to
the South, and the **final offensive** of March–April 1975 swept south; **Saigon fell on 30 April 1975**
and Vietnam was reunited under communist rule.

## What was the war's impact on civilians and on the USA?

Civilians suffered massively. US forces used napalm and **defoliants** such as Agent Orange to destroy
jungle cover and crops; bombing destroyed villages; millions became refugees. The **Hearts and Minds**
campaign sought civilian support, but atrocities undermined it — at **My Lai** (March 1968) US soldiers
killed hundreds of unarmed villagers, and the cover-up was exposed in 1969. The **Phoenix Programme**
targeted the NLF's civilian network through capture and assassination, killing many people.

In the USA, opposition grew from **university protests**, draft-card burnings and marches; at Kent State
in May 1970 the National Guard shot four students dead. **Media coverage** of Tet and My Lai deepened
doubts, though **pro-war demonstrations** showed that many Americans still supported the war. In the
**Fulbright Hearings** (1971), the Senate Foreign Relations Committee heard testimony from veterans,
including John Kerry of Vietnam Veterans Against the War.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did the USA lose?**
Weigh guerrilla tactics and the Ho Chi Minh Trail, the weakness of the South
Vietnamese government, US tactics that alienated civilians, and declining support at home.

**Was Tet a victory or a defeat for the communists?**
Militarily a defeat; politically a turning point
because it destroyed American confidence.

**What was Vietnamisation?**
Nixon's policy of replacing US troops with South Vietnamese forces while
withdrawing Americans.
