This guide covers subtopic **1.13 Percentages** in Topic 1, Number, of Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024, 2025 to 2027 series. A percentage is a number out of 100, and percentage problems appear in finance, statistics and many applied questions. The [Number revision notes](/resources/o-level-mathematics-number-revision-notes/) give a brief summary. This page covers each skill in the subtopic using the multiplier method, which handles most problems in a single step.

Companion pages: the [Percentages revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-percentages-revision-notes/) and the [Percentages practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-percentages-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks

The syllabus lists five outcomes:

1. Calculate a given percentage of a quantity.
2. Express one quantity as a percentage of another.
3. Calculate percentage increase or decrease.
4. Calculate with simple and compound interest. Problems may include repeated percentage change, and **formulas are not given**.
5. Calculate using reverse percentages, for example finding the cost price from the selling price and the percentage profit.

Percentage calculations may include deposit, discount, profit and loss (as an amount or a percentage), earnings and percentages over 100%.

## Percentage of a quantity

Convert the percentage to a decimal (divide by 100) and multiply.

```
45% of 360 = 0.45 x 360 = 162
35% of 240 = 0.35 x 240 = 84
```

For simple percentages, use these shortcuts: 50% is half, 25% is a quarter, 10% is one tenth, 1% is one hundredth. Build up other values from these: 15% = 10% + 5%.

## One quantity as a percentage of another

Write the first quantity as a fraction of the second, then multiply by 100.

```
18 out of 24:  18/24 x 100 = 75%
26 out of 40:  26/40 x 100 = 65%
```

The two quantities must be in the same units. For "90 as a percentage of 60", the answer is 90/60 × 100 = 150%. A percentage can be greater than 100% when the first quantity is bigger than the second.

## Percentage increase and decrease

The **multiplier method** is quick and reliable.

- To increase by p%, multiply by 1 + p/100.
- To decrease by p%, multiply by 1 - p/100.

| Change | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| increase by 15% | 1.15 |
| increase by 7.5% | 1.075 |
| decrease by 12% | 0.88 |
| decrease by 12.5% | 0.875 |
| increase by 100% | 2 |
| decrease by 100% | 0 |

**Worked example.** Increase 60 by 15%.

```
60 x 1.15 = 69
```

**Worked example.** Decrease 80 by 12.5%.

```
80 x 0.875 = 70
```

### Finding a percentage change

```
percentage change = (change ÷ original) x 100
```

Always divide by the **original** value.

**Worked example.** The price of a jacket goes from $45 to $54. Find the percentage increase.

```
change = 54 - 45 = 9
9 ÷ 45 x 100 = 20%
```

**Worked example.** A population falls from 250 to 190. Find the percentage decrease.

```
change = 60
60 ÷ 250 x 100 = 24% decrease
```

## Profit, loss, discount, deposit and earnings

These are all percentage of an amount problems with a context.

- **Profit or loss**: selling price minus cost price. Percentage profit = (profit ÷ cost price) × 100.
- **Discount**: the amount taken off the price. A 20% discount means paying 80%.
- **Deposit**: the first payment. A 15% deposit on $1 840 is 0.15 × 1840 = $276, leaving $1 564 to pay.
- **Earnings**: wages, salary, commission or tax. A commission of 3.5% of $12 400 is 0.035 × 12 400 = $434.

**Worked example.** A shopkeeper buys a radio for $400 and sells it for $340. Find the percentage loss.

```
loss = 400 - 340 = 60
60 ÷ 400 x 100 = 15% loss
```

## Simple interest

With simple interest, the interest each year is the same, based on the original sum (the principal). The formula is not given in the exam, so remember it.

```
I = P x R x T ÷ 100
```

Here I is the interest, P is the principal, R is the yearly rate in percent and T is the time in years.

**Worked example.** Find the simple interest on $2 500 invested at 4% per year for 3 years, and the total amount.

```
I = 2500 x 4 x 3 ÷ 100 = $300
total = 2500 + 300 = $2 800
```

## Compound interest and repeated percentage change

With compound interest, the interest is added each year and then earns interest itself. Use a repeated multiplier.

```
amount = P x (1 + R/100)^n
```

where n is the number of years. This formula is also not given.

**Worked example.** Find the value of $2 500 invested at 4% compound interest for 3 years, and the interest earned.

```
multiplier = 1.04
amount = 2500 x 1.04^3 = 2500 x 1.124864 = $2 812.16
interest = 2812.16 - 2500 = $312.16
```

Compare the result with the simple interest of $300. The extra $12.16 comes from interest on interest.

The same idea works for any repeated percentage change, such as a car losing 18% of its value each year.

**Worked example.** A car costs $24 000 and loses 18% of its value each year. Find its value after 2 years.

```
multiplier = 0.82
value = 24 000 x 0.82^2 = 24 000 x 0.6724 = $16 137.60
```

### Successive changes are not added

An increase of 20% followed by a decrease of 20% does **not** return to the start.

```
1.2 x 0.8 = 0.96
```

The overall result is a 4% decrease, because the second change is calculated on the bigger amount.

## Reverse percentages

In a reverse percentage problem you know the amount after the change and need the original. Write the multiplier, then **divide** by it.

**Worked example.** After a 20% increase, a price is $54. Find the original price.

```
original x 1.2 = 54
original = 54 ÷ 1.2 = $45
```

**Worked example.** A radio is sold for $330 at a profit of 10% of the cost price. Find the cost price.

```
selling price = 110% of cost = 1.1 x cost
cost = 330 ÷ 1.1 = $300
```

**Worked example.** After a 12% discount a coat costs $66. Find the original price.

```
original x 0.88 = 66
original = 66 ÷ 0.88 = $75
```

The most common error is to find 12% of $66 and add it on. That gives the wrong answer because the 12% was taken from the original price, not the sale price.

## Two discounts and tax

When there are two stages, apply one multiplier for each stage.

**Worked example.** A coat costs $120. It is reduced by 20%, and then the sale price is reduced by a further 10%. Find the final price and the overall percentage reduction.

```
120 x 0.8 x 0.9 = $86.40
overall multiplier = 0.8 x 0.9 = 0.72
overall reduction = 28%   (not 30%)
```

**Worked example.** A meal costs $85 before a 12% service charge is added. Find the total, and then find the original price if the total were $95.20.

```
total = 85 x 1.12 = $95.20
reverse: 95.20 ÷ 1.12 = $85
```

## Percentage points and comparing changes

A rise from 20% to 25% is an increase of 5 **percentage points**, but it is a 25% increase in the rate itself (5 ÷ 20). The two statements mean different things, so read the wording carefully. When a question asks you to compare two deals, work out each final amount, not just the percentages.

## Percentages over 100

A value that is 130% of another is 1.3 times as big. To find 240% of 50, multiply: 2.4 × 50 = 120. A reverse problem with "130% of the original is 91" gives original = 91 ÷ 1.3 = 70.

## Exam technique

- Use the multiplier method and write the multiplier. This shows the method clearly.
- In reverse percentage questions, divide by the multiplier.
- For percentage change, divide by the original, not the new value.
- Write the formulas for interest from memory, since they are not given.
- For money, give answers to the nearest cent unless told otherwise.
- Do not round the multiplier or intermediate values.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the new value when finding percentage change.
- Using 12% of the sale price to reverse a discount.
- Adding percentage changes: +20% then -20% is not 0%.
- Treating compound interest as simple interest, or the other way round.
- Writing 1.5 as the multiplier for a 5% increase (it is 1.05).
- Forgetting that the deposit is a percentage of the full price.
- Rounding the multiplier too early.
