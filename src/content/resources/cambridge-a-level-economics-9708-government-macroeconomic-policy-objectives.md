This guide covers section **5.1 Government macroeconomic policy objectives** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content and opens Topic 5, which then examines the three types of policy available: fiscal (5.2), monetary (5.3) and supply-side (5.4). A Level section 10.1 returns to the objectives in more depth.

The [Macroeconomic objectives and fiscal policy guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-macroeconomic-objectives-and-fiscal-policy/) covers 5.1 briefly before moving on to fiscal policy. This page concentrates on the objectives themselves: what each one means in practice, why governments pursue it, and how to judge from data which objectives are being met. Afterwards, use the [policy objectives revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-government-macroeconomic-policy-objectives-revision-notes/) and the [policy objectives practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-government-macroeconomic-policy-objectives-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

| Outcome | Wording in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| 5.1.1 | use of government policy to achieve macroeconomic objectives: price stability, low unemployment, economic growth (policy conflicts and trade-offs are not required) |

Read the brackets carefully. At AS you explain each objective and how policy can be used to pursue it. You do **not** need to analyse how pursuing one objective may make another harder to achieve. That analysis belongs to A Level, Topic 10. An AS answer that spends its time on conflicts is answering a different question.

## The three objectives

### Price stability

**What it means.** A low and steady rate of inflation, so the general price level is predictable. It does not mean zero inflation or falling prices. Many governments and central banks express it as a numerical target, often a low positive rate, with a tolerance band around it.

**How it is measured.** The annual percentage change in the consumer price index (section 4.6).

**Why governments pursue it.**

- Households and firms can plan, sign contracts and invest with confidence when they can predict prices.
- The purchasing power of savings, pensions and fixed incomes is protected.
- Export prices stay competitive with those of trading partners.
- Fewer resources are wasted on frequent price changes and on managing money.

### Low unemployment

**What it means.** As few people as possible who are able, willing and seeking work are without a job. Zero is not realistic: some **frictional** and **seasonal** unemployment exists even in a strong economy, as people move between jobs or work varies through the year.

**How it is measured.** The unemployment rate from a labour force survey or a claimant count (section 4.5).

**Why governments pursue it.**

- Output is closer to the economy's potential, so fewer resources are wasted.
- Households have higher incomes and living standards.
- Government spends less on benefits and collects more tax.
- The social costs of long spells without work, such as lost skills and poorer health, are reduced.

### Economic growth

**What it means.** A sustained rise in **real GDP**, the economy's output adjusted for inflation. Governments aim for growth that can be sustained, which requires the economy's productive capacity to grow as well as its spending.

**How it is measured.** The annual percentage change in real GDP (section 4.4).

**Why governments pursue it.**

- More goods and services are available, raising average living standards.
- Rising incomes raise tax revenue without higher tax rates, funding public services.
- Firms' expectations of rising sales encourage investment and job creation.

## How policy is used to pursue the objectives

Section 5.1.1 is about the **use of government policy**. Governments have three types of policy, each covered in its own section.

| Policy type | Main instruments | Works mainly through |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal policy (5.2) | Government spending and taxation | Aggregate demand; capital spending and tax incentives can also raise capacity |
| Monetary policy (5.3) | Interest rates, money supply, credit regulations | Aggregate demand |
| Supply-side policy (5.4) | Measures that raise the quantity or quality of resources and productivity | Long-run aggregate supply |

Fiscal and monetary policy can each be **expansionary**, raising AD, or **contractionary**, reducing it.

### Matching policy direction to the problem

| Problem | Objective missed | Policy direction aimed at it |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation above target because AD grows too fast | Price stability | Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to slow the growth of AD |
| Rising unemployment as AD falls in a downturn | Low unemployment | Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy to raise AD |
| Slow growth with spare capacity | Economic growth | Expansionary demand-side policy to raise actual output |
| Slow growth of productive capacity | Economic growth | Supply-side policy to shift LRAS to the right |
| Unemployment caused by a mismatch of skills | Low unemployment | Supply-side measures such as training |

The table shows why diagnosing the **cause** comes first. Unemployment from weak demand calls for expansionary policy; unemployment from a skills mismatch calls for a supply-side response. Using the wrong tool may not help.

## Judging performance from data

Data-response questions often give a table of indicators and ask which objectives are being met.

### Worked example: Country K (invented figures)

| Indicator | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 0.5% | 2.8% | 3.4% |
| Inflation (CPI) | 1.2% | 2.1% | 4.6% |
| Unemployment rate | 8.1% | 6.5% | 4.9% |

The government's inflation target is 2%, with a tolerance of one percentage point either side.

**Reading the table.**

- **Year 1:** growth is weak and unemployment high. The growth and unemployment objectives are not being met. Inflation of 1.2% is within the target band (1% to 3%). The data point to weak aggregate demand, so expansionary policy would be the likely response.
- **Year 2:** growth has improved, unemployment has fallen by 1.6 percentage points and inflation is close to target. All three objectives are moving in the right direction.
- **Year 3:** growth and unemployment have improved further, but inflation of 4.6% is above the band. The price-stability objective is now being missed, so policy aimed at that objective would slow the growth of AD.

**Building a picture of levels.** Growth rates compound. If real GDP was 100 (index) before Year 1, it reaches 100 x 1.005 = 100.5 after Year 1, 100.5 x 1.028 = 103.3 after Year 2 and 103.3 x 1.034 = 106.8 after Year 3: a total rise of 6.8%, slightly more than the sum of the three rates (6.7%).

**Avoiding a common error.** A fall in the **inflation rate** is not a fall in prices, and a fall in the **growth rate** is not a fall in output. In Country K, had growth slowed from 3.4% to 1.0% in Year 4, output would still be rising.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why a government aims to achieve price stability. **[4]**

> Price stability means a **low and steady rate of inflation** *(1: meaning)*. When prices are predictable, firms can **plan and invest with confidence**, because they can estimate future costs and revenues *(1: first reason, explained)*. It also **protects the real value of savings and fixed incomes**, such as pensions, which would otherwise buy less each year *(1: second reason, explained)*. If domestic prices rise no faster than those of trading partners, the country's **exports remain price competitive** *(1: third reason, explained)*.

Each reason earns its mark by saying **why** stability helps, not just naming a benefit.

## Common mistakes

- Defining price stability as zero inflation or constant prices.
- Aiming for zero unemployment. Some frictional and seasonal unemployment is normal.
- Defining growth with nominal GDP. The objective is growth in real GDP.
- Writing about conflicts between objectives in an AS answer where the question does not ask for them.
- Recommending a policy without diagnosing the cause of the problem.
- Reading a falling rate as a falling level.

## Exam technique

- **Define** each objective in measurable terms: price stability (low, stable CPI inflation), low unemployment (low unemployment rate), growth (sustained rise in real GDP).
- **Explain** why an objective matters with a reason and its effect on households, firms or government.
- **Identify** from data which objectives are met by comparing figures with any target given.
- When asked how policy can achieve an objective, name the policy type, its direction (expansionary or contractionary) and the curve it shifts, then link to the outcome.
