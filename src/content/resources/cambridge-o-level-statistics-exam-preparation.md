Cambridge O Level Statistics 4040 is assessed by two externally assessed papers of identical shape:
each lasts 2 hours 15 minutes, carries 100 marks, and contributes 50% of the qualification. Each paper
mixes short questions worth up to 8 marks each with four longer questions worth around 15 marks each.
Electronic calculators are required. The papers are examined in the November series only, and the
qualification is linear -- candidates cannot resit an individual component, only the whole
qualification. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Data and Its Collection](/resources/o-level-cambridge-statistics-data-and-its-collection/) and
[Summary Representation of Data](/resources/o-level-cambridge-statistics-summary-representation-of-data/).

## The linear rule raises the cost of an underprepared paper

Because individual components cannot be resat, a weak performance on either paper can only be
addressed by retaking the entire qualification in a later November series. **Exam-preparation
priority**: plan revision so that both papers are ready at the same time. There is no strategy in
which one paper is "the one to focus on" -- and because the papers are examined only in November, the
practical cost of an underprepared sitting is a full year, not a few months.

## Two hours fifteen for a hundred marks is a fast pace for a calculation paper

135 minutes across 100 marks is a little over 1.3 minutes per mark, and statistics questions require
setting out working, not just producing answers. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise complete
papers against the clock, not individual questions at leisure. The specific skill worth building is
recognising within thirty seconds which method a question wants -- most lost time on a statistics
paper is spent deciding what to do rather than doing it.

## The four long questions are where the paper is won

Four questions of around 15 marks each account for roughly 60 of a paper's 100 marks. These are
multi-part questions in which later parts typically build on earlier ones. **Exam-preparation
priority**: practise long questions as complete units rather than answering isolated parts, and pay
particular attention to carrying a value forward correctly. A mistake in part (a) that is carried
consistently into part (b) usually still earns the method marks in (b); abandoning the question does
not.

## Method marks make an incomplete answer worth attempting

In a calculation subject, marks are available for a correct method even when the final figure is
wrong, and they are only available if the method is visible. **Exam-preparation priority**: build the
habit of writing the formula or the substitution before the answer, every time -- including on short
questions where it feels unnecessary. A bare wrong number scores nothing; the same wrong number with
its working shown often scores most of the marks.

## Calculator fluency is a distinct skill from statistical understanding

Calculators are required, and a candidate who is slow with their own machine loses time on every
computational question. **Exam-preparation priority**: use the same calculator throughout the course
and know its statistical functions -- mean, standard deviation, and how to enter frequency data --
without hunting through menus. Equally, know which quantities the calculator will *not* give you, so
that you are not searching for a function that does not exist while the clock runs.

## Worked routine: setting out a calculation for method marks

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - name the quantity you are finding:
Write "Mean =" or "Standard deviation =" before anything else, so
the intention is unambiguous even if the arithmetic goes wrong.

Step 2 - write the formula or the substitution:
Show the values going in. This is the line that carries the method
mark.

Step 3 - give the result with its unit and sensible accuracy:
An unrounded string of decimals is not a better answer; match the
accuracy of the data unless the question specifies otherwise.

Step 4 - sanity-check against the data:
Is the mean inside the range of the values? Is a probability
between 0 and 1? Is the standard deviation smaller than the range?
A five-second check catches most transcription errors.

Step 5 - if a later part uses this value, carry it forward:
State clearly that you are using your earlier answer, and continue
even if you suspect it is wrong.
```

Step 4 is the cheapest marks-protection available on this paper: an impossible answer is nearly always
a keying error, and it is visible in seconds if you look for it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: prepare both papers to the same standard, since components cannot be resat
  individually; practise complete papers against the clock; work long questions as whole units;
  learn your own calculator's statistical functions thoroughly.
- **During either paper**: write the formula or substitution before every answer, including on short
  questions.
- **On the four long questions**: carry values forward explicitly rather than abandoning a question
  after an early error.
- **After every calculation**: sanity-check the result against the data before moving on.

## Self-test

1. What is the practical consequence of the qualification being linear and November-only?
2. Roughly what share of each paper's marks sits in the four long questions?
3. Why should working be shown even on a short question you are confident about?
4. Name two sanity checks that catch a keying error immediately.

**Answers:** 1. An individual paper cannot be resat -- only the whole qualification, in a later
November series -- so an underprepared sitting costs a full year rather than a few months, and both
papers must be ready simultaneously. 2. About 60 of 100 marks, since the four long questions are worth
roughly 15 marks each. 3. Because method marks are only awarded when the method is visible; a bare
wrong number scores nothing, while the same number with working shown usually scores most of the
marks. 4. The mean must lie within the range of the data, and a probability must lie between 0 and 1
(a standard deviation smaller than the range is a third).
