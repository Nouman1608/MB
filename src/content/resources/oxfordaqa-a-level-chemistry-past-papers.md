This page is a guide to OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) past papers. It covers the series for which we could read the board's Report on the examination: January 2024 (all five papers), June 2024 (CH02) and January 2026 (CH01). For each one you get a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from the board's reports. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report belongs to OxfordAQA.

One thing to know before you start: we have not printed any grade thresholds on this page. We could not retrieve a grade-boundary document for any 9620 series, and we do not estimate or recall numbers. Each series section below says so. For the boundaries themselves, use the board's own results and grade-boundary information.

## Where to get the official papers

- [OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) qualification page](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/qualifications/international-as-a-level-chemistry/)
- [OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification (PDF)](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-specification.pdf)

The qualification page is the starting point for the assessment details and the exam materials OxfordAQA publishes for this subject. Recent papers, mark schemes and reports are released through the board, and some materials may require a centre login.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which shows what each mark needs. Then read the Report on the examination for that paper: it explains why answers that looked reasonable still lost marks and names the questions that separated stronger and weaker candidates. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. The specification this page follows is for teaching from September 2019, with the first International AS exams in May/June 2020. It is a modular qualification of five papers. CH01 (Inorganic 1 and Physical 1) and CH02 (Organic 1 and Physical 1) are the two AS papers, each 90 minutes and 70 marks, and each worth 20% of the full A-level. CH03 (Inorganic 2 and Physical 2) and CH04 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) are the A-level papers, each 90 minutes and 80 marks, worth 21% each. CH05 (Practical and Synoptic) is 85 minutes and 60 marks, worth 18%. The CH05 paper assesses the ten required practicals through a written exam. Papers set before the 2019 specification are not covered here.

## January 2026

The January 2026 report we could read is for CH01, the AS inorganic and physical paper. Its headline is that candidates could reach every mark on the paper and did better on numerical questions than on those needing an explanation. Wrong or unbalanced equations were common across the paper, a few scripts lost marks to hard-to-read handwriting, and the examiners reminded candidates to show working so that method marks are available when a final answer is wrong.

**Where marks were lost**

- CH01, bonding and structure: explanations of molecular shape that relied on symmetry instead of electron-pair repulsion, and not saying that equal repulsion between the bonding pairs sets the shape. Explanations of metallic and ionic lattices that talked about the wrong kind of particle or force, or never mentioned a giant lattice or the opposite charges involved.
- CH01, practical and titration questions: adding water to a volumetric flask without the careful, final-drop detail; choosing the wrong titres to average; saying a method was simply "more accurate" when asked about uncertainty; and weak answers on washing and drying a precipitate (not using deionised water).
- CH01, periodicity and atomic structure: very few candidates explained periodicity as a repeating pattern across periods; ionisation energy was defined without the key words (energy, gaseous, atom); electron configurations were incomplete; and mass spectrometry answers missed that detector current relates to abundance.
- CH01, energetics and calculations: enthalpy change confused with energy change, the sign of a result misjudged, a final division by the wrong number, and calculations that skipped unit conversions or the required number of significant figures.
- CH01, halogens and intermolecular forces: unbalanced or non-ionic equations, a wrong environmental consequence, and explanations of temporary dipoles that did not link one molecule to its neighbour.

## June 2024

For June 2024 we could read the report for CH02, the AS organic and physical paper. The examiners found that candidates generally did well, and were strongest at recalling test-tube reagents and at equilibrium-mole calculations. They asked for care with structures and curly arrows, for data not to be rounded until a calculation is finished, and for definitions of terms such as the types of isomerism to be learned exactly.

**Where marks were lost**

- CH02, mechanisms and drawing: arrows that started from an atom rather than a bond or lone pair, incorrect partial charges added to mechanisms, and structures missing bonds or carrying too many hydrogens.
- CH02, alkenes and isomerism: explaining the hardest points of E/Z isomerism (the need for two different groups on each carbon, and the priority rules based on atomic number), and naming and drawing a diene in skeletal form.
- CH02, polymers and forces: answers about the unreactive nature of poly(alkene) chains that talked about intermolecular forces, and hydrogen bonds drawn to the wrong atom.
- CH02, carboxylic acids and spectroscopy: leaving out heating under reflux, giving an incorrect range for an infrared absorption, and not knowing how the fingerprint region confirms identity.
- CH02, equilibrium and rate: confusing the effect of a change on yield with its effect on rate, describing the reaction as "shifting" instead of the equilibrium, and not explaining lower pressure in terms of how often successful collisions happen.

## January 2024

January 2024 is the series where we could read a report for every paper, so it is the best one on this page for working through all five 9620 papers. Across them the examiners asked for accurate definitions, equations balanced for atoms and charge, clear working, careful rounding, and a real understanding of why each required practical is done as it is.

**Where marks were lost**

- CH01: definitions of relative atomic mass and metallic bonding with a key word missing; electron-pair answers that were not specific about where the electrons came from; half-equations with electrons on the wrong side; and a mass-spectrometry style calculation where the unit and the idea of "one mole" were muddled. Practical questions on titration and making up a solution were weak, and balanced redox equations proved difficult.
- CH02: confusion between how a fraction of crude oil is defined and how it is separated; incomplete mechanisms for carbocation and radical reactions; naming slips (the ending of a diene, the numbering of an aldehyde); the molecular formula written as a structural formula; and rate-based explanations given where equilibrium position was being asked about.
- CH03: observations for a Period 3 reaction that were not well known; thermodynamic cycles where the number of atoms involved was missed; explaining ligand substitution only through entropy rather than enthalpy, entropy and free energy together; hydrated-ion charges and complexes written incorrectly; cell notation answers that omitted phase boundaries or inert electrodes; and buffer calculations treated as if only a weak acid were present.
- CH04: curly arrows, recrystallisation and the reason for each step in an acylation preparation; naming (the retained "e" in a diacid name, "hydroxy" not "hydroxyl"); the effect of hydrolysis on a polyamide explained by temperature instead of bond polarity; graph scales and tangents; spectroscopy answers about symmetry that did not mention dipoles cancelling; and a zwitterion's solubility explained without the idea of an ionic structure.
- CH05: why reagents are brought to temperature before an experiment; graph scales and plotting; the order of a reaction; identification tests for aldehydes and ketones where one test was confused with another; standard conditions; and explanations of insolubility that were not precise enough. The multiple-choice part was described as fairly easy, with a handful of questions that only about half of candidates answered correctly.

## What the reports keep saying

The three series above repeat a small set of points. They are worth building into your revision.

- Learn definitions word for word. Missing words such as "average", "gaseous" or "mole" cost marks in more than one series.
- Balance every equation for atoms and charge, and write ionic equations when asked for them.
- Show full working. Keep unrounded values until the end, and give the answer to the number of significant figures the question asks for.
- Know the reason behind each required practical, not only the steps. Questions on purity, washing, drying, rinsing apparatus and uncertainty came up repeatedly.
- Start every curly arrow at a lone pair or a bond, and draw every bond, including O-H.
- Keep rate and equilibrium apart. Explain a change in yield through equilibrium position and a change in speed through collision frequency.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OxfordAQA 9620 past papers?**
OxfordAQA publishes the specification and exam materials from the qualification page for International AS and A-level Chemistry. Some materials may need a centre login.

**Which 9620 papers make up the AS and the full A-level?**
The AS is CH01 and CH02. The full A-level adds CH03, CH04 and CH05, with CH05 being the practical and synoptic paper.

**Does this page give grade thresholds?**
No. We could not retrieve a grade-boundary document for any 9620 series, so we have printed no numbers. Use the board's own grade-boundary information.

**What do the 9620 examiners most often criticise?**
Common points are missing words in definitions, equations that are not balanced for atoms and charge, curly arrows that start in the wrong place, and weak answers about why a practical step is done.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
