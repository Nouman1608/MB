Key question 6.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How is crime dealt with in different
societies?* It closes the Crime topic and is mostly about *effectiveness*: how well punishments and
policing achieve what they are meant to achieve. The skill this guide builds is judging a punishment
or policing strategy **against a stated aim**, using examples from more than one society, because
"effective" means nothing until you say effective at what.

## The aims of punishment (6.3.1)

| Aim | Looks | Meaning | Question it asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protection of citizens | Forward | Keep the public safe from harm | Will this make people safer? |
| Deterrence | Forward | Discourage crime through fear of punishment, both by the offender (**individual deterrence**) and by others who see the punishment (**general deterrence**) | Will this put people off offending? |
| Retribution | Backward | Make the offender suffer in proportion to the harm done ("just deserts") | Is this deserved? |
| Incapacitation | Forward | Remove the offender's ability to offend | Can they offend while this lasts? |
| Rehabilitation | Forward | Change the offender so they no longer want or need to offend | Will they stop offending afterwards? |

Retribution is the only aim that looks **backward** to the crime. The others look **forward** to
preventing future crime. This difference explains why people disagree about punishment: a sentence
can be deserved yet fail to reduce reoffending, or reduce reoffending yet seem too lenient.

**Sociological views of punishment:**

- **Durkheim** argued that punishment expresses society's shared values and strengthens solidarity.
  He contrasted **retributive justice**, typical of simpler societies, with **restitutive justice**,
  aimed at restoring things to how they were, which he linked to complex modern societies.
- **Marxists** argue that punishment mainly controls the working class while the crimes of the
  powerful are rarely punished.
- **Michel Foucault** (*Discipline and Punish*, 1975) described a shift from **sovereign power**,
  displayed through public physical punishment, to **disciplinary power**, which controls people
  through surveillance. He used Jeremy Bentham's design for a prison, the **panopticon**, in which
  prisoners never know if they are being watched, so they control themselves.
- **John Braithwaite** (1989) distinguished **disintegrative shaming**, which labels and excludes the
  offender, from **reintegrative shaming**, which condemns the act but welcomes the offender back.

## Matching punishments to aims (6.3.2)

The most useful revision tool for 6.3.2 is to ask which aim each punishment serves best.

| Punishment | Aims it serves | Main doubt about effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Prison | Incapacitation; retribution; protection | High reoffending in many systems; spreads criminal skills; labels offenders |
| Rehabilitation programmes | Rehabilitation | Need resources and willing offenders |
| Community sentencing | Retribution (repaying society); rehabilitation | May be seen as soft, weakening deterrence |
| Financial penalties | Retribution; deterrence | Same fine hurts the poor more than the rich |
| Capital punishment | Retribution; incapacitation | Irreversible if the person was innocent; deterrent effect is disputed |
| Corporal punishment | Retribution; deterrence | Widely condemned as a human rights abuse |
| Exile | Protection; incapacitation | Moves the problem elsewhere |
| Ostracism | Retribution; deterrence through shame | May push the person further into deviance |
| Electronic tagging, curfews, restraining orders | Protection; incapacitation (partial) | Depend on monitoring; can be breached |
| Probation | Rehabilitation; supervision | Depends on caseloads and support |
| Police cautions | Individual deterrence; avoiding labelling | May seem to let offenders off |
| Social media and vigilante groups | Retribution; deterrence through public shaming | No due process; innocent people can be targeted |

**Examples from different societies:**

- **Exile** has a long history: Napoleon was exiled to Elba and later to St Helena. Today, forms of
  banishment include deportation of foreign offenders.
- **Ostracism** is used in some close-knit religious communities; for example, Amish communities
  practise shunning of members who break community rules.
- **Rehabilitation-focused prisons**: Norway's Bastøy prison, on an island, lets prisoners live in
  houses and work on a farm, reflecting a belief that prison should prepare people for life after
  release.
- **Restorative justice**: New Zealand introduced family group conferences for young offenders in
  1989, bringing offenders, victims and families together, an approach close to Braithwaite's
  reintegrative shaming.
- **Online vigilantism**: after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing, amateur online investigators wrongly
  named innocent people as suspects, showing the risk of punishment without due process.

## Policing and law enforcement (6.3.3)

**Targeting and stop and search.** Police concentrate on people and places thought to be linked to
crime. It can find weapons and drugs, but if it is used unfairly it damages trust. In Britain the
Scarman Report (1981) followed the Brixton disturbances, which came after an intensive
stop-and-search operation, and the Macpherson Report (1999) found the Metropolitan Police to be
institutionally racist.

**Zero tolerance.** James Q. Wilson and George Kelling's **broken windows** theory (1982) argued that
leaving minor disorder unchecked signals that no one cares, inviting more serious crime. Zero-tolerance
policing of minor offences in New York in the 1990s is often linked to the theory, though critics
argue crime was also falling for other reasons and that the approach targeted poorer and minority
communities.

**Surveillance, including digital surveillance.** CCTV, number-plate recognition, phone data and
facial recognition aid detection and may deter. Foucault's panopticon helps explain the effect:
people who think they may be watched control themselves. Doubts include **displacement** (crime moves
to unwatched places), privacy and the risk that surveillance is used against particular groups.

**Crime prevention.** **Situational crime prevention** (Ronald Clarke) reduces opportunities: better
locks, lighting and design. Oscar Newman's **defensible space** (1972) argued that housing design that
lets residents watch over shared areas reduces crime. **Social crime prevention** tackles causes, such
as youth clubs, education and employment schemes.

**Community and military style policing.** **Community policing** relies on public cooperation and
local knowledge. **Military style policing** relies on force, equipment and rapid response. Richard
Kinsey, John Lea and Jock Young (1986) argued that when police lose public cooperation they drift
towards military-style policing, which alienates communities further and leads to even less
information from the public: a vicious circle.

## Worked example: an annotated essay plan

**Question:** Evaluate the view that harsher punishments are the best way to reduce crime.

1. **Introduction:** the view rests on **deterrence** and **incapacitation**; the alternative stresses
   rehabilitation and prevention. *Names the aims.*
2. **For:** long prison terms incapacitate; capital and corporal punishment are used by some states
   for their deterrent message; retribution satisfies victims. *Links punishments to aims.*
3. **Against:** deterrence depends more on the certainty of being caught than on severity; prison
   can increase reoffending through labelling and criminal contacts; Bastøy and Halden show a
   rehabilitative alternative; Braithwaite's reintegrative shaming. *Uses evidence from different
   societies.*
4. **Policing alternative:** situational and social crime prevention; community policing. *Shows
   crime can be reduced without punishment.*
5. **Conclusion:** harsh punishment may serve retribution and incapacitation, but rehabilitation and
   prevention are more likely to reduce crime overall. *Judges against the aim in the question.*

## Common mistakes

- Calling a punishment "effective" without naming the aim it is effective at.
- Treating deterrence as one thing: separate individual and general deterrence.
- Using examples from only one society when the key question asks about "different societies".
- Confusing **incapacitation** (preventing offending) with **rehabilitation** (changing the offender).
- Describing stop and search without considering its effect on trust and on statistics (6.2).

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Define** each aim of punishment in one precise sentence.
- **Explain** the effectiveness of a punishment by linking it to an aim and giving a reason.
- **Discuss** a policing strategy by weighing its benefits against its costs.
- **Evaluate** by comparing punishments or strategies across societies and reaching a judgement.
