## Questions

**1.** Greenfield Print Shop sells 1,800 posters at a price of 5.00. Its total fixed costs are 2,700 and its variable cost is 1.40 per poster.

**(a)** Calculate total revenue. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate total costs. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate profit. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate average (total) cost per poster. **[1]**

**2.** What is meant by economies of scale? **[2]**

**3.** A firm produces 2,500 units. Its average cost is 6.40 and its total fixed costs are 4,000. It sells each unit at 7.20.

Calculate total costs, total variable costs, and profit. **[4]**

**4.** Identify which internal economy of scale each case illustrates.

**(i)** A growing chain hires a specialist finance director and a head of logistics. **[1]**
**(ii)** A large firm is offered a lower interest rate by its bank. **[1]**
**(iii)** A firm sells in many regions, so a poor year in one does not threaten it. **[1]**
**(iv)** A firm buys materials in very large orders and receives a discount. **[1]**

**5.** Riverbank Dairy pays 2.50 per carton when it orders fewer than 500 cartons and 2.20 per carton when it orders 500 or more. It orders 800 cartons.

**(a)** Calculate how much the firm saves on the order compared with paying 2.50 per carton. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the saving as a percentage of the original price per carton. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this lowers the firm's average cost. **[2]**

**6.**

**(a)** What is meant by external economies of scale? **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a cluster of similar businesses in the same area could lower a firm's costs. **[4]**

**7.**

**(a)** State two types of diseconomies of scale. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how communication problems could raise a growing firm's average cost. **[3]**

**8.** Orion Ltd's long run average cost (LRAC) at different outputs is shown below.

| Output | 5,000 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 20,000 | 25,000 | 30,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRAC | 12.00 | 9.00 | 7.50 | 7.00 | 7.60 | 8.50 |

**(a)** Calculate total cost at 10,000 units. **[1]**
**(b)** State the output at which the firm is most efficient. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage fall in LRAC between 5,000 and 20,000 units. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage rise in LRAC between 20,000 and 30,000 units. **[2]**
**(e)** Draw, or describe in words, an annotated LRAC diagram for this firm. **[4]**

**9.** A pottery has total fixed costs of 1,800 a month and a variable cost of 4 per pot.

**(a)** Calculate average (total) cost at outputs of 150, 300 and 450 pots. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why average cost falls as output rises. **[3]**

**10.** "A growing firm will always enjoy lower average costs."

Evaluate this statement. **[9]**

**11.** Harbour Textiles sells all its output at 9.00 per metre. At 50,000 metres its average cost is 7.20. It expands to 80,000 metres, where its LRAC is 8.10.

**(a)** Calculate profit at 50,000 metres. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate profit at 80,000 metres and the percentage change in profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why profit fell although output and revenue rose. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** TR = 1,800 x 5.00 = **9,000** [1]
**(b)** TVC = 1,800 x 1.40 = 2,520 [1]; TC = 2,700 + 2,520 = **5,220** [1]
**(c)** Profit = 9,000 - 5,220 = **3,780** [1]
**(d)** ATC = 5,220 / 1,800 = **2.90** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula, then the substitution. When a per-unit variable cost is given, work out total variable costs before adding fixed costs, because adding 1.40 straight to 2,700 is a common slip.

**2.** A fall in average cost [1] as a firm increases its scale of production (output) in the long run [1].
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs two separate ideas. "Costs fall" alone is incomplete; say average (per unit) cost and say what causes the fall.

**3.** TC = 2,500 x 6.40 = **16,000** [1]; TVC = 16,000 - 4,000 = **12,000** [1]; TR = 2,500 x 7.20 = 18,000 [1]; profit = 18,000 - 16,000 = **2,000** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Here the average cost is given, so the route to total cost is multiplication, not division. State TR as a step, since profit cannot be reached without it.

**4.** (i) **Managerial** [1]; (ii) **financial** [1]; (iii) **risk bearing** [1]; (iv) **purchasing (bulk buying)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Match the economy to the clue in the wording, such as "specialist" or "interest rate". Naming two types in one answer risks losing credit.

**5. (a)** Old price cost = 800 x 2.50 = 2,000 [1]; new cost = 800 x 2.20 = 1,760, so saving = **240** [1]
**(b)** 0.30 / 2.50 = **12%** [1]
**(c)** Buying in bulk gives a lower price per carton [1], so the cost of inputs per unit falls and average cost falls [1]
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) link the discount to cost per unit sold, not only to the total saving. A full chain runs from large order to lower unit price to lower average cost.

**6. (a)** A fall in average cost [1] that arises because the industry or area has grown, not because of the individual firm's own size [1]
**(b)** Any developed chain, for example: businesses in the same industry locate close together [1]; a pool of workers trained in the relevant skills develops, or specialist suppliers set up nearby [1]; the firm spends less on training or pays less for supply and delivery [1]; so its average cost falls [1]
*Examiner insight:* Pick one type, such as skilled labour or access to suppliers, and develop it. Saying only "the firm gets lots of benefits" is a list without an explanation.

**7. (a)** Any two from bureaucracy; communication problems; lack of control; distance between top management and workers [1] [1]
**(b)** The firm has many layers or sites, so messages pass through several people [1]; instructions are delayed or misunderstood, so errors and repeated work occur [1]; these wasted resources raise total cost per unit, so average cost rises [1]
*Examiner insight:* A diseconomy answer is complete only when it ends with average cost rising. Stopping at "communication is poor" describes the problem but not its economic effect.

**8. (a)** 10,000 x 9.00 = **90,000** [1]
**(b)** **20,000 units** (lowest LRAC, 7.00) [1]
**(c)** (12.00 - 7.00) / 12.00 [1] = **41.7%** [1]
**(d)** (8.50 - 7.00) / 7.00 [1] = **21.4%** [1]
**(e)** Labelled axes, output along the bottom and cost per unit up the side [1]; curve slopes downward to 20,000 units, labelled internal economies of scale [1]; lowest point at 20,000 units labelled most efficient output, LRAC 7.00 [1]; curve slopes upward beyond it, labelled diseconomies of scale [1]

```
Cost per unit
 |\
 |  \  economies of scale
 |    \_                    /  diseconomies of scale
 |       \__            __/
 |          \__      _/
 |             \____/   <- 20,000 units, LRAC 7.00
 +---------------------------> Output
```
*Examiner insight:* A diagram earns credit for what is labelled, so a bare curve gains little. For percentage change, divide by the starting value, not the end value.

**9. (a)** At 150: TC = 1,800 + 600 = 2,400, ATC = **16.00** [1]; at 300: TC = 3,000, ATC = **10.00** [1]; at 450: TC = 3,600, ATC = **8.00** [1]
**(b)** Fixed costs of 1,800 stay the same [1]; they are shared across more pots, so fixed cost per pot falls (12.00, 6.00, 4.00) [1]; variable cost stays 4 per pot, so total average cost falls [1]
*Examiner insight:* This is a short-run spreading of fixed costs, because the premises are unchanged. Do not label it an economy of scale, which needs all inputs to be changeable in the long run.

**10.** Sample points, one mark each where developed (nine needed):
- Definition: economies of scale are a fall in average cost as scale rises. [1]
- A named internal economy with a chain, for example bulk buying gives a lower price per unit. [1]
- A second internal economy with a chain, for example technical, financial or managerial. [1]
- External economies may also lower costs if the industry grows in the area. [1]
- A named diseconomy, for example bureaucracy or communication problems. [1]
- Chain showing why it raises average cost, such as slower decisions and extra admin staff. [1]
- The lowest point of the LRAC curve: beyond the most efficient output, growth raises average cost. [1]
- Evaluation: it depends on how far the firm is from its lowest point, its industry and its management. [1]
- A supported judgement, for example "not always: lower costs are likely while the firm is small, but past its most efficient output they rise". [1]
*Examiner insight:* The statement contains "always", which invites a counter-argument. Name the conditions under which it holds and fails, then finish with a conclusion that picks a side.

**11. (a)** TR = 50,000 x 9.00 = 450,000 [1]; TC = 50,000 x 7.20 = 360,000 [1]; profit = **90,000** [1]
**(b)** TR = 80,000 x 9.00 = 720,000; TC = 80,000 x 8.10 = 648,000; profit = **72,000** [1]; change = 72,000 - 90,000 = -18,000 [1]; -18,000 / 90,000 = **-20%** [1]
**(c)** Output beyond the most efficient point causes diseconomies of scale [1], such as lack of control or bureaucracy, so average cost rose from 7.20 to 8.10 [1]; costs rose faster than revenue (TC up by 288,000, TR up by 270,000), so profit fell [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use the numbers in the explanation. Show that total cost grew more than total revenue, and name a cause of the higher cost.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using average cost where total cost is needed, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to add fixed costs to variable costs.
- Dividing a change by the new value instead of the original value.
- Defining economies of scale without the word average or without the long run.
- Treating the spreading of fixed costs in the short run as an economy of scale.
- Calling an external benefit internal, such as an area's new port.
- Naming a diseconomy but not explaining why average cost rises.
- Drawing an LRAC curve with no labelled axes or lowest point.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
