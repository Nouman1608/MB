## What you must be able to do

- Use **precise atomic masses** and the **precise molecular mass** to determine the **molecular formula** of a compound.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| m/z | Mass-to-charge ratio of an ion |
| Molecular ion, M⁺• | The ion formed when the whole molecule loses one electron |
| Molecular ion peak | The peak at the highest m/z for the whole molecule; m/z equals Mᵣ |
| Precise mass | Mass of an isotope or molecule given to several decimal places |
| Nominal mass | Whole-number mass |
| Fragment ion | A positive ion formed when the molecular ion breaks up |
| Base peak | The tallest peak in the spectrum |

## The molecular ion

```
M(g) + e⁻ → M⁺•(g) + 2e⁻
```

- m/z of the molecular ion = Mᵣ of the molecule (charge +1).
- A **low-resolution** spectrum gives whole numbers only.
- A **high-resolution** spectrum gives m/z to four or more decimal places.

## Precise atomic masses

| Isotope | Precise mass | Compared with whole number |
|---|---|---|
| ¹H | 1.0078 | slightly above |
| ¹²C | 12.0000 | exactly |
| ¹⁴N | 14.0031 | slightly above |
| ¹⁶O | 15.9949 | slightly below |

These are example values. Use the numbers printed in the question.

## Method

1. Read the **precise m/z** of the molecular ion.
2. Round to the **nominal mass**.
3. List **candidate formulae** with that nominal mass, using only the elements stated.
4. Calculate the **precise mass** of each candidate.
5. Choose the one that matches the measured value. State why the others do not.

## Worked pattern: nominal mass 58

| Formula | Precise mass |
|---|---|
| C₃H₆O | 58.0417 |
| C₂H₂O₂ | 58.0054 |
| C₂H₆N₂ | 58.0530 |
| C₄H₁₀ | 58.0780 |

Measured 58.0420 matches C₃H₆O.

## Worked pattern: nominal mass 60

| Formula | Precise mass |
|---|---|
| C₂H₄O₂ | 60.0210 |
| CH₄N₂O | 60.0323 |
| C₃H₈O | 60.0573 |

Measured 60.0210 (C, H and O only) matches C₂H₄O₂.

## Why precise masses differ

- More hydrogen atoms push the mass slightly **above** the whole number.
- More oxygen atoms pull the mass slightly **below** the whole number.
- Carbon-12 contributes an exact whole number.

## From formula to structure

A molecular formula does not decide the structure. Combine mass spectrometry with other data.

| Formula | Possible structures | How to decide |
|---|---|---|
| C₃H₆O₂ | Propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate | Fizzes with carbonate: acid |
| C₃H₆O | Propanal, propanone | Silver mirror with Tollens': propanal |
| C₄H₈ | But-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene, cyclobutane | Bromine water; NMR or other data |

## Fragment ions

- Only **positively charged** fragments are detected.
- Neutral fragments and radicals leave undetected.
- Mass lost = m/z (M⁺•) − m/z (fragment).

| m/z | Possible fragment |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺, CHO⁺ |
| 43 | C₃H₇⁺, CH₃CO⁺ |
| 57 | C₄H₉⁺ |

| Loss from M | Lost species |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃• |
| 17 | OH• |
| 29 | C₂H₅• or CHO• |

## Calculating a precise mass

Add the contribution of each atom:

```
C₃H₆O = 3(12.0000) + 6(1.0078) + 15.9949 = 36.0000 + 6.0468 + 15.9949 = 58.0417
```

Use all the decimal places given until the final answer.

## Worked examples to copy

**Example 1.** A compound of C, H and O has a molecular ion at m/z 46.0417. Candidates: C₂H₆O and CH₂O₂.

1. C₂H₆O: 2(12.0000) + 6(1.0078) + 15.9949 = 46.0417.
2. CH₂O₂: 12.0000 + 2(1.0078) + 2(15.9949) = 46.0054.
3. The measured value matches C₂H₆O, which could be ethanol or methoxymethane.

**Example 2.** A fragment ion appears at m/z 29 and the molecular ion is at m/z 58.

1. 58 − 29 = 29, a loss of C₂H₅• or CHO•.
2. The fragment at 29 is therefore likely to be C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺.
3. Use other data to decide between them.

**Example 3.** Why does a low-resolution spectrum not identify a formula?

1. Several formulae have the same whole-number mass.
2. Only a high-resolution spectrum separates them by their decimal places.

## Exam traps

- Using Aᵣ values such as H = 1.0 instead of the precise masses supplied.
- Early rounding that makes two candidates look equal.
- Considering elements the question did not state.
- Treating the base peak as the molecular ion.
- Claiming a structure when only a formula is proved.
- Listing neutral fragments as peaks.
- Writing the molecular ion as M⁺ with no explanation of its charge, or confusing m/z with mass in grams.
- Calculating the mass lost from the wrong peak.

## Self-test

1. What is the molecular ion?
2. What does the m/z of the molecular ion tell you?
3. Why can a low-resolution spectrum not distinguish C₃H₆O from C₄H₁₀?
4. Which isotope has an exact whole-number mass?
5. Calculate the precise mass of C₂H₆O using 12.0000, 1.0078 and 15.9949.
6. A molecular ion has m/z 46.0054 (C, H and O only). Is it C₂H₆O or CH₂O₂?
7. Why is the precise mass of an oxygen-rich molecule slightly below a whole number?
8. What is the base peak?
9. A molecular ion is at m/z 74 and a fragment at 45. What mass has been lost?
10. Why are some fragments not detected?
11. Give two possible structures for C₃H₆O₂.
12. What additional test could separate them?

### Answers

1. The ion formed when a molecule loses one electron, M⁺•.
2. The relative molecular mass of the compound.
3. Both have a nominal mass of 58, and the whole-number masses are identical.
4. Carbon-12.
5. 24.0000 + 6.0468 + 15.9949 = 46.0417.
6. CH₂O₂ (calculated 46.0054; C₂H₆O is 46.0417).
7. Each oxygen-16 atom has a mass of 15.9949, which is a little below 16.
8. The tallest peak in the spectrum.
9. 29, consistent with C₂H₅• being lost.
10. Neutral species have no charge so are not accelerated or detected.
11. Propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate (also ethyl methanoate).
12. Sodium carbonate: the acid fizzes and the esters do not.
