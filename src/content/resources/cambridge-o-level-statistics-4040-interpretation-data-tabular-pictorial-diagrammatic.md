This guide covers section 2.4 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "Interpretation of data presented in tabular, pictorial or diagrammatic form". Here the table or diagram is already drawn. You must read values from it accurately, recover hidden figures such as totals, compare groups fairly, and write conclusions that the data actually support. This is where the assessment objective on interpretation and communication (AO2) is tested most directly in topic 2.

A short worked comparison of two box-and-whisker diagrams is in the [Venn, box-and-whisker and stem-and-leaf guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-venn-box-and-whisker-and-stem-and-leaf-diagrams/). This page works through every form in turn, with the calculations that interpretation questions need. Afterwards, use the [interpretation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-interpretation-data-tabular-pictorial-diagrammatic-revision-notes/) and the [interpretation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-interpretation-data-tabular-pictorial-diagrammatic-practice/).

## What this unit covers

| Skill in 2.4 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Reading | Extract values from tables, keys, scales, angles, sections and leaves |
| Recovering | Find totals or frequencies not printed, from angles, radii or percentages |
| Comparing | Compare groups using suitable measures, allowing for different group sizes |
| Concluding | State conclusions in context, and recognise claims the data cannot support |

## Reading pie charts

A pie chart gives shares, not counts. To get a count you need one known frequency or the total.

**Worked example 1.** In a pie chart of how pupils travel to school, the bus sector is 126° and represents 63 pupils. The car sector is 54°.

```
126° represents 63 pupils, so 1° represents 0.5 pupils
Total = 360 × 0.5 = 180 pupils
Car = 54 × 0.5 = 27 pupils
Share travelling by car = 54/360 = 15%
```

## Reading comparative pie charts

**Worked example 2.** Comparative pie charts show housing in two towns. Town A (4500 homes) has radius 6 cm; Town B has radius 4 cm. The "flats" sector is 100° in A and 144° in B.

```
Total for B = 4500 × (4/6)² = 4500 × 4/9 = 2000 homes
Flats in A = 100/360 × 4500 = 1250
Flats in B = 144/360 × 2000 = 800
Share of flats: A = 100/360 = 27.8%, B = 144/360 = 40%
```

Conclusion: a higher proportion of homes in Town B are flats (40% against about 28%), but Town A has more flats in number (1250 against 800). Both statements are true; the question tells you which is wanted ("proportion" or "number").

## Reading bar-type charts

- **Bar and dual bar charts**: read heights from the scale; for a dual chart, compare within each pair and across pairs.
- **Sectional bar charts**: the scale gives the **top** of each section. The size of a section is the reading at its top minus the reading at its bottom.
- **Percentage bar charts**: sections give percentages of each bar's own total. To find a count, you need the bar's total.

**Worked example 3.** A sectional bar chart for a library shows, for Monday, sections for fiction, non-fiction and children's books with tops at 18, 30 and 42 loans.

```
Fiction = 18
Non-fiction = 30 − 18 = 12
Children's = 42 − 30 = 12
```

A percentage bar chart for the same week shows that 35% of 260 loans were non-fiction: 0.35 × 260 = 91 loans.

## Reading stem-and-leaf diagrams

**Worked example 4.** Waiting times (minutes) for 16 customers:

```
0 | 4 7 8
1 | 1 3 3 6 9
2 | 0 2 5 8
3 | 1 8
4 | 3
5 | 7
Key: 1 | 3 means 13 minutes
```

```
Median = mean of 8th and 9th values = (19 + 20)/2 = 19.5 minutes
Range = 57 − 4 = 53 minutes
Number waiting more than 30 minutes = 4 (31, 38, 43, 57), which is 4/16 = 25%
```

Shape: most times lie between 4 and 28 minutes, with a long tail of a few much longer waits; the data are skewed towards the higher values. A comment like this, in words, is interpretation; the numbers alone are not.

## Interpreting box-and-whisker diagrams

Each part of a box-and-whisker diagram holds about a quarter of the data. That supports statements such as "about 25% of the values exceed the upper quartile".

**Worked example 5.** Two machines fill bags of rice. Box-and-whisker diagrams give these values (grams):

| | Minimum | Q1 | Median | Q3 | Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine A | 48 | 52 | 55 | 57 | 63 |
| Machine B | 45 | 50 | 56 | 61 | 66 |

```
IQR: A = 57 − 52 = 5 g;   B = 61 − 50 = 11 g
Range: A = 15 g;          B = 21 g
```

**Model comparison.** The medians are similar (55 g and 56 g), so on average the machines fill bags to about the same mass. Machine A is more consistent: its interquartile range is 5 g against 11 g for Machine B. A quarter of Machine B's bags weigh 61 g or more, while a quarter of Machine A's bags weigh 57 g or more, and a quarter of B's bags weigh 50 g or less against 52 g or less for A, so B's masses stray further from the middle in both directions. If bags must be close to a target mass, Machine A is the better choice.

The structure: compare an average, compare a spread, quote figures from both, and finish in context.

## Interpreting Venn diagrams and tables

Read the region that matches the wording exactly: "only", "both", "at least one", "neither". For tables, check the headings, the units and whether entries are counts or percentages before you use them. When comparing rows of different sizes, convert to percentages of each row first.

## Valid and invalid conclusions

A conclusion must follow from the data shown.

| Claim | Supported? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Town B has a higher proportion of flats" | Yes | From the angles |
| "Town B has more flats" | No | Town B's total is smaller; it has 800 against 1250 |
| "Machine A is better because its median is lower" | No | Medians are almost equal; spread is the real difference |
| "Longer revision causes higher marks" | Not from a diagram alone | A diagram shows association, not cause |
| "All customers wait about 19.5 minutes" | No | The median describes the middle, not every customer |
| "This will be true for every school" | Not without more evidence | The data come from one sample |

## Common errors

- Reading a sectional bar chart's top value as the size of the top section.
- Quoting a pie chart angle as a frequency.
- Comparing angles in comparative pie charts as if both charts had the same total.
- Giving a comparison with numbers but no words, or words but no numbers.
- Describing only the average, or only the spread.
- Making claims about cause, or about a wider population, that the data cannot support.

## Exam technique

- **Find** or **calculate**: show how each value was read or recovered.
- **Compare**: one statement on average and one on spread (or proportion), each with figures from both groups.
- **Comment** or **interpret**: say what the figures mean in the context of the question.
- **Is the claim justified?**: answer yes or no, then give the evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should a comparison of two box-and-whisker diagrams include?**
A comparison of the medians, a comparison of a spread (usually the interquartile range), figures from both diagrams, and a conclusion in context.

**How do I find a frequency from a pie chart?**
Multiply the sector's share (angle ÷ 360) by the total. If the total is not given, use a sector whose frequency is known to find what 1° represents.

**Can a diagram prove that one thing causes another?**
No. A diagram can show that two things occur together, but other explanations are possible.
