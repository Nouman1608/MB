## 3.11 Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Standard cost | Predetermined cost of one unit, set in advance from expected quantities and prices |
| Standard cost card | Build-up of the standard cost: quantity × price for materials, hours × rate for labour |
| Variance | Difference between a standard (or budgeted) figure and the actual figure |
| Variance analysis | Calculating variances, splitting them into price and quantity parts, and explaining causes |
| Favourable (F) | Increases profit compared with standard |
| Adverse (A) | Reduces profit compared with standard |
| Management by exception | Managers investigate only significant variances |
| Standard quantity / standard hours | Quantity or hours that **actual output** should have used |

## Variance formulas

| Variance | Formula | Usually answerable |
|---|---|---|
| Materials price | (SP − AP) × AQ | Buyer / purchasing |
| Materials usage | (SQ − AQ) × SP | Production supervisor |
| Total materials | SQ × SP − actual cost | -- |
| Labour rate | (SR − AR) × AH | Personnel / pay setting |
| Labour efficiency | (SH − AH) × SR | Production supervisor |
| Total labour | SH × SR − actual cost | -- |
| Sales price | (AP − SP) × actual units sold | Sales manager |
| Sales volume | (actual units − budgeted units) × standard contribution per unit | Sales manager |

S = standard, A = actual, P = price, Q = quantity, R = rate, H = hours. With costs written
"standard minus actual", a positive answer is F. With sales written "actual minus standard", a
positive answer is F.

Value the sales volume variance at standard contribution when fixed costs appear as a separate line
in the reconciliation. If the question gives a standard profit per unit to use, use that.

## Method in steps: calculating variances

1. Find actual unit price (actual cost ÷ actual kg) and actual rate (actual wages ÷ actual hours).
2. Find SQ = actual output × standard kg per unit, and SH = actual output × standard hours per unit.
3. Price and rate variances: difference in price × **actual** quantity or hours.
4. Usage and efficiency variances: difference in quantity × **standard** price or rate.
5. Check: price + usage = total materials; rate + efficiency = total labour.
6. Label every variance F or A.

## Method in steps: reconciliations

**Cost reconciliation**

1. Start with standard cost of **actual** output (plus budgeted fixed overheads if included).
2. **Add** adverse cost variances; **deduct** favourable ones.
3. End with actual cost. Check it against the actual figures given.

**Profit reconciliation**

1. Start with budgeted profit.
2. Sales volume variance, then sales price variance.
3. Materials price and usage, labour rate and efficiency, then any fixed overhead difference.
4. **Add** favourable variances; **deduct** adverse ones (two columns help).
5. End with actual profit, calculated separately as a check.

## Small worked reminder

Standard: 2 kg at £8 per kg per unit. Output 600 units. Actual: 1 150 kg costing £9 545.

```
AP = 9 545 ÷ 1 150 = £8.30          SQ = 600 × 2 = 1 200 kg
Price = (8.00 − 8.30) × 1 150 = £345 A
Usage = (1 200 − 1 150) × 8.00 = £400 F
Total = 9 600 − 9 545 = £55 F  (check: 400 F − 345 A)
```

A likely link: better material (price A) cut waste (usage F).

Sales: budget 1 500 units at £40, standard contribution £15. Actual 1 620 units at £38.

```
Sales price  = (38 − 40) × 1 620 = £3 240 A
Sales volume = (1 620 − 1 500) × 15 = £1 800 F
Net effect of the price cut = £1 440 A
```

The extra 120 units did not make up for the lower price, so on these figures the cut reduced
profit. A judgement should also consider market share and whether the higher volume will last.

## Interpreting a variance: what to write

For each significant variance, give three things:

1. **The figure and direction**, for example "labour efficiency £600 A: 40 hours more than standard".
2. **A cause that matches the direction**: slower trainees, machine breakdowns or poor material for
   adverse efficiency; experienced staff or new equipment for favourable.
3. **A consequence or action**: who is answerable, whether it is controllable, whether the standard
   needs updating, and what it means for profit.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Price v usage:** price uses actual quantity; usage uses standard price.
- **Rate v efficiency:** rate uses actual hours; efficiency uses standard rate.
- **Fixed budget v standard cost of actual output:** cost variances are always measured against
  the standard for what was actually produced.
- **Sales price v sales volume:** price uses actual units sold; volume uses standard contribution.
- **Cost v profit reconciliation:** adverse variances are added to cost but deducted from profit.
- **Controllable v uncontrollable:** a national price rise or pay award is not the buyer's or
  supervisor's fault; the standard may need revising.
- **Significant v insignificant:** small variances may not justify the cost of investigation.

## Linked variances

| Decision | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Cheaper material | Price F, usage A, labour efficiency A |
| Better material | Price A, usage F, labour efficiency F |
| Cheaper, less skilled labour | Rate F, efficiency A, materials usage A |
| More skilled labour | Rate A, efficiency F, materials usage F |
| Lower selling price | Sales price A, sales volume F |
| Higher selling price | Sales price F, sales volume A |

## Advantages and disadvantages in brief

**Advantages:** control through responsibility; management by exception; standards feed budgets
and pricing; clear targets that can motivate; simpler inventory valuation; makes managers examine
methods.

**Disadvantages:** costly to set up and maintain; standards date quickly; suits repetitive
production, not one-off work or many services; tight or imposed standards demotivate; can
encourage behaviour that helps one variance and harms others; information arrives after the event;
ignores non-financial factors such as quality.

## Quick self-test

1. Define standard cost.
2. Standard: 4 kg at £3 per kg per unit. Output 500 units. Actual: 2 100 kg costing £6 090.
   Calculate the materials price and usage variances.
3. Standard: 0.5 hours at £12 per hour per unit. Output 1 000 units. Actual: 480 hours costing
   £6 000. Calculate the labour rate, efficiency and total variances.
4. What does your answer to question 3 show about total variances?
5. Budget: 2 000 units at £25; standard contribution £8. Actual: 1 900 units at £26. Calculate the
   sales price and sales volume variances.
6. Materials usage variance £450 A; standard price £5 per kg; standard quantity 3 000 kg. How many
   kg were used?
7. Who is usually answerable for the materials price variance?
8. Budgeted profit £18 000. Variances: sales volume £1 200 F, sales price £2 000 A, total materials
   £500 A, total labour £300 F. Calculate actual profit.
9. "A favourable variance is always good news." True or false? Give a reason.
10. Give one cause that explains labour rate A together with labour efficiency F.
11. In a cost reconciliation, is a favourable variance added or deducted?

### Answers

1. A predetermined cost for one unit of output, set in advance from expected quantities and prices.
2. AP = 6 090 ÷ 2 100 = £2.90. Price = (3.00 − 2.90) × 2 100 = **£210 F**. SQ = 2 000 kg; usage =
   (2 000 − 2 100) × 3 = **£300 A**. Total £90 A.
3. AR = 6 000 ÷ 480 = £12.50. Rate = (12 − 12.50) × 480 = **£240 A**. SH = 500; efficiency =
   (500 − 480) × 12 = **£240 F**. Total = 6 000 − 6 000 = **nil**.
4. A nil total can hide two significant variances that offset each other.
5. Price = (26 − 25) × 1 900 = **£1 900 F**. Volume = (1 900 − 2 000) × 8 = **£800 A**.
6. 450 ÷ 5 = 90 kg extra, so **3 090 kg**.
7. The buyer or purchasing manager (unless prices rose across the market).
8. 18 000 + 1 200 − 2 000 − 500 + 300 = **£17 000**.
9. False. A favourable materials price variance from cheap material may cause larger adverse usage
   and efficiency variances.
10. Using more skilled, higher-paid staff who work faster.
11. Deducted from standard cost.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating standard quantity or standard hours from budgeted output, not actual output.
- Using actual price in the usage variance, or standard quantity in the price variance.
- Valuing sales volume at selling price, ignoring the variable cost of the extra units.
- Missing F or A labels, or calling a cost saving adverse.
- Adding adverse variances to profit, or getting the direction wrong in a cost reconciliation.
- A reconciliation that does not end on the actual figure, with no check made.
- Giving a cause for a variance that does not match its direction (for example, "cheap material"
  for an adverse price variance).
- Treating each variance in isolation when the data show a clear link.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no judgement for the business in the question.
