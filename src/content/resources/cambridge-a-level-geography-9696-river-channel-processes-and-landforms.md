This guide covers **1.3 River channel processes and landforms** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is precise, and examiners use its exact terms:

- **Erosion:** abrasion/corrasion, solution, cavitation and hydraulic action.
- **Load transport:** traction, saltation, suspension and solution.
- **Deposition and sedimentation:** the Hjulström curve.
- **River flow:** velocity and discharge, patterns of flow (laminar, turbulent and helicoidal), and thalweg.
- **Channel types:** straight, braided and meandering.
- **Landforms:** meander (river cliffs, point bars, oxbow lakes), riffle and pool sequences, waterfalls, gorges, bluffs, floodplains, levées and deltas.

It is examined in Paper 1, in Section A data response questions and in the Hydrology and fluvial geomorphology structured question in Section B. ## Erosion: four processes, four mechanisms

- **Hydraulic action** is the force of moving water on the bed and banks. Water forced into cracks compresses air, and the pressure loosens and removes material. It is most effective on jointed or poorly consolidated banks during high discharge.
- **Abrasion (corrasion)** is erosion by the load: particles carried by the river scrape, grind and strike the bed and banks. It is usually the most effective erosion process in rivers carrying coarse load, and it drills **potholes** where pebbles are swirled in eddies.
- **Cavitation** happens in very fast, turbulent water. Pressure drops so low that bubbles of vapour form; when they collapse, they release tiny but intense shock waves against the rock surface. It is important below waterfalls and in rapids, and is rare in slow lowland rivers.
- **Solution** is the chemical removal of soluble minerals, especially from limestone and chalk, by slightly acidic river water.

The syllabus does not list attrition under erosion, though you may meet it as the process by which particles knock against each other and become smaller and rounder. Do not confuse cavitation with hydraulic action: one is bubble collapse, the other is water and air pressure in cracks.

## Transport and the Hjulström curve

Load moves in four ways: **traction** (large particles rolled or slid along the bed), **saltation** (smaller particles bounced in short hops), **suspension** (fine particles held up by turbulence) and **solution** (dissolved minerals carried invisibly). Traction and saltation together form the **bedload**.

The **Hjulström curve** plots particle size against velocity, with two lines. The upper line is the **critical erosion (entrainment) velocity**: the speed needed to pick a particle up. The lower line is the **settling (fall) velocity**: below it, the particle is deposited. Between the lines, a particle already moving stays in transport. Three features score marks:

- Sand-sized particles are entrained at the lowest velocities.
- Clay and silt need **higher** velocities than sand to be entrained, because they are cohesive and lie in a smooth layer that offers little resistance to the flow to grip.
- The gap between the lines is widest for fine particles, so once clay is suspended it stays in transport until the water is almost still. This explains why fine sediment settles in lakes, on floodplains after floods and at river mouths.

**Sedimentation** is the settling and build-up of deposited material. Particles are deposited in order of size as velocity falls, which is why deposits are **sorted**: coarse material near the channel or the head of a delta, fine material further away.

## River flow: velocity, discharge and flow patterns

**Velocity** is the speed of the water; **discharge** is the volume passing a point per second (cross-sectional area x mean velocity). In a cross-section, velocity is lowest against the bed and banks, where friction acts, and highest just below the surface near the middle of a straight reach. Downstream, velocity often increases slightly even though the gradient falls, because the channel becomes larger and smoother and loses proportionally less energy to friction.

Flow patterns:

- **Laminar flow** is smooth flow in parallel layers. It occurs only in very shallow, slow water over smooth surfaces and is rare in rivers.
- **Turbulent flow** is chaotic flow with eddies moving water up, down and across the channel. It is the normal state of rivers, and its upward eddies keep fine load in suspension.
- **Helicoidal flow** is a corkscrew motion around a bend. Surface water moves towards the outer bank, plunges down and returns along the bed towards the inner bank, carrying eroded sediment across the channel.

The **thalweg** is the line of fastest flow (and usually the deepest water) along a river. In a meander it swings towards the outside of each bend, then crosses the channel between bends.

## Channel types

| Type | Key features | Typical conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Straight | Low sinuosity over short distances; thalweg still wanders | Channels following fault lines, joints or engineered reaches |
| Meandering | Single sinuous channel with alternating bends | Moderate gradient, fine and cohesive banks, steady supply |
| Braided | Many channels separated by gravel bars and islands | High coarse load, variable discharge, weak banks, steep gradient |

Braiding develops when discharge falls after a flood or meltwater pulse and the river can no longer carry its coarse load, so it deposits bars that split the flow.

## Landforms

- **Riffle and pool sequences:** shallow riffles of coarse sediment alternate with deeper pools. Water accelerates over pools during high flows and scours them; riffles form where velocity drops and coarse sediment accumulates. The alternation helps the thalweg wander and start bends.
- **Meanders:** erosion on the outside of a bend forms a steep **river cliff**; deposition on the inside builds a gently sloping **point bar**, layered from coarse at the base to fine at the top as each flood adds sediment. Bends grow and migrate downstream over time.
- **Oxbow lakes:** erosion on the outer banks narrows the neck of a meander until a flood cuts through it; deposition seals the old bend, leaving a curved lake that gradually fills with sediment and vegetation.
- **Waterfalls and gorges:** where resistant rock overlies weaker rock, the weaker rock is eroded faster by hydraulic action, abrasion and cavitation in the plunge pool. The resistant cap is undercut and collapses, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided **gorge**.
- **Bluffs:** the steep valley sides at the edge of a floodplain, cut back by meanders migrating across the valley floor.
- **Floodplains:** wide, flat areas built by lateral erosion and migrating meanders, which leave point bar deposits, and by vertical deposition of fine sediment during floods.
- **Levées:** when a river overflows, velocity falls sharply at the channel edge, so the coarsest load is dropped first, building raised banks; finer material spreads further across the floodplain. Repeated floods raise the levées.
- **Deltas:** where a river enters a sea or lake, velocity falls and the load is deposited faster than waves and tides can remove it. Clay particles **flocculate** (clump together) in salt water and settle quickly.

## Worked example 1: sinuosity

*A fictional river reach measures 7.2 km along its channel. The straight-line distance between the same two points is 4.5 km. Calculate the sinuosity and classify the channel.*

Sinuosity = channel length / straight-line distance = 7.2 / 4.5 = **1.6**.
A common convention treats values above 1.5 as meandering, so this reach is meandering.

## Worked example 2: velocity and discharge from fieldwork

*Students timed a float over 10 m of a straight stream: the mean time was 25 seconds. The cross-sectional area was 3.0 m². Using a correction factor of 0.8 for surface velocity, calculate discharge.*

Surface velocity = 10 / 25 = 0.40 m/s.
Mean velocity = 0.40 x 0.8 = 0.32 m/s.
Discharge = 3.0 x 0.32 = **0.96 cumecs**.

The correction is used because floats move with the fast surface water, while water near the bed and banks is slowed by friction.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain the formation of levées." [5]*

> Levées form on rivers that regularly flood their floodplains **(context)**. When discharge exceeds bankfull, water spreads out of the channel, its depth falls and friction with the floodplain increases, so velocity drops sharply at the channel edge **(cause of deposition)**. Following the Hjulström curve, the coarsest load is deposited first, close to the banks, while finer silt and clay are carried further and settle across the floodplain **(sorting)**. Each flood adds another layer, so the banks are built up into raised ridges **(repetition over time)**. Between floods, deposition on the channel bed can raise the river above the level of its floodplain **(extension point)**.

## Common mistakes

- Using "erosion" for transport processes, or listing traction as a type of erosion.
- Describing cavitation as "air in cracks" (that is hydraulic action).
- Reading the Hjulström curve as if clay is the easiest particle to erode.
- Calling the inside of a meander a "slip-off slope" and never mentioning the point bar, which is the syllabus term.
- Explaining a waterfall without saying why the rock beneath erodes faster.
- Describing braided channels with no reference to load and variable discharge.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the formation of** a landform: write a sequence of processes in order, with the reason each one happens at that location.
- **Describe** a cross-section or map: quote measurements from the resource, such as depth or width, and use the correct landform names.
- **Compare** channel types: make direct comparisons of load, discharge and bank material.
- Diagrams are credited when they are labelled with processes, not just features, and when the text refers to them.
