## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between soil texture and soil structure. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the role of biological weathering in soil formation. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between leaching and soil erosion. **[2]**

**4.** State the role of nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil system. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why clay-rich and humus-rich soils hold more nutrients than sandy soils. **[3]**

**6.** Outline why ploughing a grassland soil releases carbon dioxide. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) Three fictional soil samples were analysed.

| Sample | Sand / % | Silt / % | Clay / % |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 70 | 15 | 15 |
| Y | 20 | 20 | 60 |
| Z | 40 | 40 | ? |

**(a)** Calculate the clay percentage of Sample Z. **[1]**
**(b)** Using a soil texture triangle, the samples are classed as sandy loam, clay and loam. Match each class to its sample. **[1]**
**(c)** Predict, with reasons, which sample will have the highest infiltration rate and which will hold the most nutrients. **[4]**

**8.** (Data response) Infiltration was measured with a ring at three fictional sites. Each value is the mean of three tests.

| Site | Fall in water level / mm | Time / minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Ungrazed meadow | 72 | 12 |
| Lightly grazed pasture | 40 | 12 |
| Gateway used by cattle | 6 | 12 |

**(a)** Calculate the infiltration rate at each site in mm per hour. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between the meadow and the gateway. **[3]**
**(c)** State two ways the method was, or could be, made fair. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how earthworms and fungi contribute to the formation of good soil structure. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why soils in a region where rainfall greatly exceeds evaporation are often acidic and low in nutrients. **[4]**

**11.** Explain the causes of the Dust Bowl of the 1930s and evaluate the response to it. **[6]**

**12.** "Soil is a non-renewable resource." Discuss this statement. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay particles, which is fixed by the parent material [1]; structure is the way particles are grouped into aggregates, which can be improved or damaged by management [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, breaking rock apart [1]; lichens and microorganisms release acids that chemically break down minerals, releasing nutrients and forming fine particles [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Leaching is the downward removal of dissolved nutrients and minerals by percolating water [1]; erosion is the removal of the soil particles themselves from the surface by water or wind [1]. **[2]**

**4.** They convert nitrogen gas from the air into ammonium, a form plants can use [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Clay particles and humus have large surface areas carrying negative charges [1] that attract and hold positively charged nutrient ions such as calcium and potassium [1]. Sandy soils have few charged surfaces and large pores, so nutrients are leached away quickly [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Ploughing breaks up aggregates and exposes organic matter to oxygen [1], so decomposers break it down faster and release carbon dioxide by respiration [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 100 − 40 − 40 = **20%** [1].
**(b)** X = sandy loam; Y = clay; Z = loam (all three needed) [1].
**(c)** X will have the highest infiltration rate [1], because its high sand content gives large pores through which water passes quickly [1]. Y will hold the most nutrients [1], because its 60% clay provides many negatively charged surfaces that hold nutrient ions [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Meadow: 72 ÷ 12 = 6.0 mm per minute = **360 mm per hour** [1]; pasture: 40 ÷ 12 × 60 = **200 mm per hour** [1]; gateway: 6 ÷ 12 × 60 = **30 mm per hour** [1].
**(b)** Repeated trampling by cattle at the gateway compacts the soil [1], squeezing out pore spaces and breaking aggregates [1], so water enters twelve times more slowly than in the meadow and more runs off the surface [1].
**(c)** Any two: tests were repeated three times and a mean taken [1]; the same ring and the same starting depth of water should be used at every site [1]; the ring should be pushed in to the same depth; tests should be done on the same day, not just after rain. **[8]**

**9.** Earthworm casts and mucus bind mineral particles and organic matter into crumbs [1]. Fungal threads (hyphae) wrap around and glue particles together into aggregates [1]. The pores between aggregates improve drainage, aeration and root growth [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Water percolates downwards through the profile most of the year [1], carrying away dissolved basic ions such as calcium, magnesium and potassium (leaching) [1]. As these are removed, hydrogen and aluminium ions dominate the exchange sites, so the soil becomes acidic [1]. Nutrients are carried below the root zone, so the upper soil is infertile [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Causes:
- Deep-rooted prairie grasses that held the soil were ploughed up for wheat [1].
- Ploughing broke up soil structure and left the surface bare between crops [1].
- Severe drought in the 1930s dried the exposed topsoil, so strong winds removed it in dust storms [1].
Evaluation of the response:
- The Soil Conservation Service (1935) promoted contour ploughing, shelter belts and returning land to grass, which reduced wind erosion [1].
- Its success depended on persuading many separate farmers to change methods at once [1].
- Judgement: the response was effective in reducing erosion, but the event showed that prevention through suitable land use is cheaper than recovery [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so use these points as a guide:
- Soil is renewed by weathering and the addition of organic matter, so it is technically renewable [1].
- But formation is very slow: a few centimetres can take centuries [1].
- Erosion on bare or ploughed land can remove soil many times faster than it forms [1].
- On a human time scale, therefore, lost topsoil cannot be replaced, so it behaves as non-renewable [1].
- Some properties, such as organic matter and structure, can be rebuilt in years with good management [1].
- Nutrient levels can be restored with manure, fertiliser and legumes [1].
- Degradation such as salinization or severe erosion to bedrock may be effectively permanent [1].
- Value systems differ: a technocentric view may rely on fertilisers and technology to maintain yields; an ecocentric view treats soil as irreplaceable natural capital [1].
- Judgement: soil is renewable in principle but should be managed as non-renewable, because rates of loss under poor management far exceed rates of formation [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert mm per minute to mm per hour.
- Giving a texture class without checking the percentages total 100.
- Confusing leaching with erosion.
- Explaining infiltration differences without the link from compaction to pore space.
- Treating drought as the only cause of the Dust Bowl.
