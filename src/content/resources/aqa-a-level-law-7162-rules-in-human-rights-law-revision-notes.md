Condensed notes on the rules and principles that run across the five rights in the specification. For
full explanation and a worked scenario, use the
[Rules in Human Rights law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-rules-in-human-rights-law/).

## The method in one line

**Right and Article** → **limited or qualified?** → **engaged?** → **interference or failure to
protect?** → **justified?** → **who claims, where, and for what remedy?**

## Sources of the rules

| Layer | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Convention text | Rights and permitted limits | Article 11.1 and 11.2 |
| Strasbourg case law | Scope and meaning | Handyside v UK (1976) |
| Human Rights Act 1998 | Domestic enforcement | s6 public authorities; s7 victims; s8 remedies |
| Statute | Specific powers | PACE 1984 (arrest and detention); Public Order Act 1986 (marches and assemblies) |
| Common law | Judge-made rights and liberties | Entick v Carrington (1765); Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993) |

- **Derbyshire (1993)**: a local authority cannot sue for defamation; free criticism of government.
- **Ex parte Simms (1999)**: **principle of legality**; Parliament must use clear words to override
  fundamental rights.

## The five rights

| Right | Article | Type | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life | 2 | Limited | Article 2.2 list; force "no more than absolutely necessary" |
| Liberty and security | 5 | Limited | Grounds in 5.1; safeguards in 5.2 to 5.5 |
| Private and family life | 8 | Qualified | 8.2: in accordance with law, legitimate aim, necessary |
| Expression | 10 | Qualified | 10.2: prescribed by law, legitimate aim, necessary |
| Assembly and association | 11 | Qualified | 11.2: prescribed by law, legitimate aim, necessary |

**Absolute** for contrast: Article 3; no justification is possible.

## Cross-cutting principles

| Principle | Meaning | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Negative obligation | State must not interfere without justification | All Articles |
| Positive obligation | State must protect the right, even against private parties | X and Y v Netherlands (1985): gap in criminal law, Art 8; Osman v UK (1998): real and immediate risk, Art 2 |
| Living instrument | Read "in the light of present-day conditions" | Tyrer v UK (1978): birching degrading |
| Practical and effective | Rights must not be "theoretical or illusory" | Airey v Ireland (1979): no real access to court |
| Autonomous meaning | Convention terms have their own meaning | Engel v Netherlands (1976): "criminal charge" |
| Margin of appreciation | Some discretion for states, varying with the issue | Handyside v UK (1976) |
| Proportionality | No more restriction than needed | Applied under the second paragraphs |
| Non-discrimination | Article 14, only with another Article | A v SSHD (2004): Arts 5 and 14 |

## Vertical and horizontal effect

- **Vertical**: rights bind the state and public authorities (HRA s6).
- **Indirect horizontal**: courts are public authorities, so they develop private law compatibly with
  the Convention.
- **Campbell v MGN (2004)**: misuse of private information developed between private parties by
  balancing Articles 8 and 10.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Limited right | Restricted only in the ways the Article lists |
| Qualified right | Restricted if lawful, for a legitimate aim and necessary in a democratic society |
| Absolute right | Never restricted or balanced |
| Positive obligation | Duty on the state to act to protect a right |
| Living instrument | Convention read according to present-day conditions |
| Margin of appreciation | Discretion allowed to states in applying the Convention |
| Principle of legality | Clear words needed for a statute to override fundamental rights |
| Horizontal effect | Influence of Convention rights on disputes between private parties |

## Exam traps

- Applying the qualified-rights test to Article 2 or Article 5.
- Missing a positive obligation where the harm was done by a private person.
- Arguing Article 14 on its own.
- Forgetting the common law route, such as the principle of legality.
- Treating Campbell v MGN as direct horizontal effect; the effect is indirect, through the courts.
- Stopping at engagement without testing justification.

## Self-test

1. Name the three types of Convention right and give an Article for each.
2. Which of the five rights in the specification are limited rights?
3. What three requirements must an interference with a qualified right satisfy?
4. What is a positive obligation? Give a case.
5. What did the European Court say in Tyrer v UK?
6. What principle comes from Airey v Ireland?
7. What does "autonomous meaning" mean, and which case is linked to it?
8. What did the House of Lords decide in Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers?
9. What is the principle of legality?
10. How do Convention rights affect disputes between private parties?
11. Why can Article 14 not be argued alone?
12. List the six steps for a human rights problem question.

**Answers:** 1. Absolute (Article 3), limited (Article 2 or 5), qualified (Article 8, 10 or 11). 2.
Article 2 and Article 5. 3. Prescribed by law (in accordance with the law), a legitimate aim in the second
paragraph, and necessary in a democratic society (proportionate to a pressing social need). 4. A duty on
the state to take steps to protect a right; X and Y v Netherlands (1985) or Osman v UK (1998). 5. The
Convention is a living instrument to be interpreted in the light of present-day conditions; birching was
degrading. 6. Rights must be practical and effective, not theoretical or illusory. 7. Convention words
have a meaning independent of national labels; Engel v Netherlands (1976). 8. A local authority cannot
sue in defamation, because free criticism of government must be protected. 9. Parliament must use clear
words to override fundamental rights; general words will not do (ex parte Simms). 10. Indirectly: courts
are public authorities and develop private law compatibly, as in Campbell v MGN (2004). 11. It prohibits
discrimination only in the enjoyment of the other Convention rights. 12. Identify the right; classify it;
check engagement; identify interference or failure to protect; test justification; enforcement and
remedy.
