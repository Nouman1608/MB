**This practice set covers topic 2.4, The product, in Specialist Option 2 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios that combine the topic with research methods. All shops, products and people are fictional.

Related: [The product study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-the-product/) · [The product revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-the-product-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** reasons why people wrap gifts. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by the central gaze cascade effect. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the difference between customer-focused and product-focused sales techniques, including the effect of each on the buyer–seller relationship. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how the need for cognitive closure helps to explain the disrupt-then-reframe technique. **[3]**

**5.** Outline **two** factors that increase post-purchase cognitive dissonance. **[2]**

**6.** Buying a product involves several decisions.

**(a)** Describe the Engel Kollat Blackwell model of buyer decision-making and post-purchase cognitive dissonance. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate explanations of buying the product, including a discussion of reductionism versus holism. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**7.** Nordbake, a biscuit company, wants its new dark chocolate biscuit to be seen as rich and intense.

**(a)** Using Becker et al. (2011), suggest **two** features of the packaging. **[2]**

**(b)** The company tests its design by asking 40 shoppers to rate the biscuits' taste from 1 (mild) to 10 (intense). Identify the level of measurement and give **one** weakness of this type of rating. **[3]**

**8.** A supermarket manager wants to increase sales of a new juice.

**(a)** Using research into shelf position, suggest where the juice should be placed and explain why. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** advantage of using eye-tracking rather than asking shoppers what they looked at. **[2]**

**9.** At Kestrel Cars, a salesperson offers customers a free coffee, says the model is "the most popular in town" and warns that "only two are left in this colour".

**(a)** Identify the **three** principles from Cialdini's six ways to close a sale used here. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** ethical concern about using these principles. **[2]**

**10.** Ravi has just bought an expensive television and now keeps thinking about the cheaper model he rejected.

**(a)** Using the Engel Kollat Blackwell model, identify the stage Ravi is at. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** ways Ravi might reduce his dissonance. **[2]**

**11.** A researcher wants to investigate whether age affects reasons for choosing a supermarket. She plans a questionnaire for shoppers outside three supermarkets.

**(a)** Write **one** closed question and **one** open question she could use. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** problem with the sample she would obtain, and how it could be improved. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for any two: to hide the gift and create surprise (1); to show it is a gift, not an ordinary purchase (1); to signal the giver's effort and care (1); to put the recipient in a positive mood (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. Products in the horizontal centre of a shelf display attract more visual attention (1). Attention to the central option increases just before the choice is made (1), so central products are more likely to be chosen, as shown with eye-tracking by Atalay et al. (2012) (1).

**3.** Up to 4 marks. Customer-focused techniques identify and meet the buyer's needs (1), building trust and a long-term relationship, so the buyer is more likely to return (1). Product-focused techniques emphasise the product's features and benefits (1); this informs the buyer but can seem impersonal, as the seller may not consider whether the product suits them, giving a weaker relationship (1).

**4.** Up to 3 marks. Disrupt-then-reframe creates brief confusion with an odd detail, then offers a positive reframe (1). People high in need for cognitive closure dislike ambiguity and want a quick, firm answer (1), so they accept the reframe as a way to end the confusion without thinking critically about the offer, as Kardes et al. (2007) proposed (1).

**5.** One mark each for any two: high cost (1); importance of the purchase (1); difficulty of reversing the purchase (1); attractive features of the rejected alternative (1).

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. The EKB model describes five stages (1): problem recognition, when the consumer notices a need (1); information search, internal from memory or external from reviews and friends (1); evaluation of alternatives against criteria such as price and quality (1); purchase; and post-purchase outcomes, when the buyer judges satisfaction (1). Post-purchase dissonance is discomfort when doubts about the choice conflict with having made it; it increases with cost, importance and attractive rejected alternatives and is reduced by seeking supporting information or returning the item (1). Credit Nordvall (2014) as the named study.

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Reductionism versus holism*: the EKB model breaks buying into separate stages, which makes it easy to apply and test; but real decisions loop back and overlap, and emotion, mood and social influence act on the whole process.
- *Limits*: the model suits planned, expensive purchases; impulse buys skip stages.
- *Cultural and demographic differences*: Sinha et al. (2002) studied Indian shoppers and found reasons for store choice varied with age and gender, so one model may not fit all groups.
- *Application*: retailers can target each stage, e.g. reviews at the information-search stage and reassurance after purchase to reduce dissonance and returns.
- *Idiographic versus nomothetic*: the model is general, but the time spent at each stage varies between people.
- *Validity*: much evidence comes from self-report of past purchases, which may be inaccurate.
- *Conclusion*: a useful framework rather than a precise account of every purchase.

**7.**

**(a)** One mark each: an angular shape (1); strongly saturated colours (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Ordinal (accept interval if justified as a rating scale treated as equal intervals) (1). Weakness: participants may interpret the points differently, e.g. one person's 7 is another's 5 (1), so scores are subjective and hard to compare between people (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. In the horizontal centre of the juice display (1), because the central gaze cascade effect means central products get more attention (1) and are more likely to be chosen (Atalay et al., 2012) (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Eye-tracking records where shoppers actually look, giving objective data (1); shoppers may not remember or be aware of where they looked, so self-reports could be inaccurate (1).

**9.**

**(a)** One mark each: reciprocity (free coffee) (1); social proof ("most popular") (1); scarcity ("only two left") (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. The principles can pressure customers into buying without careful thought (1), which may be manipulative, especially if claims such as "only two left" are untrue (1).

**10.**

**(a)** Post-purchase outcomes (1).

**(b)** One mark each: looking for positive reviews of the television he bought (1); focusing on its strengths or playing down the cheaper model (1). Credit returning the television.

**11.**

**(a)** One mark each. Closed: "Is price the main reason you shop here? Yes/No" (1). Open: "Why do you choose this supermarket?" (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Shoppers outside three supermarkets are an opportunity sample (1), which may not include people who shop online or at other times, and certain age groups may be over-represented at certain times of day (1). Improve by sampling at different times and days, or using quota sampling to include each age group (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 6(a), stopping at the purchase stage and leaving out post-purchase outcomes.
- In question 8(a), writing "eye level" instead of the centre of the display.
- In question 9(a), naming "persuasion" or "pressure" instead of the principles.
