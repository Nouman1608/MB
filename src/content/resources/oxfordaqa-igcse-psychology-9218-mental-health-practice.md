## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a compulsion?
A A sudden image of a loved one being hurt
B A fear of germs on door handles
C Counting to fifty before leaving a room
D A belief that a thought could cause harm **[1]**

**2.** Identify **two** characteristics of mental health named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Outline how cultural variations in beliefs can affect the recorded incidence of mental health problems. **[2]**

**4.** State **three** characteristics of PTSD used in diagnosis with the International Classification of Diseases. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **one** biological factor linked to PTSD. **[1]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**6.** Kofi lost his job eight months ago. Since then he has stopped seeing friends, struggles to get out of bed and has developed frequent headaches. Explain **two** effects of significant mental health problems on the individual, using Kofi as an example. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** effects that a rise in significant mental health problems could have on society. **[4]**

**8.** Nadia is afraid that she will cause a fire. She checks that every plug is switched off many times before going to bed and feels responsible for her family's safety. Explain Nadia's behaviour using the cognitive explanation of OCD. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a therapist could use cognitive behaviour therapy to treat Nadia. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate biological explanations of OCD. **[4]**

**11.** Describe Eftekhari's study of the effectiveness of prolonged exposure therapy and give **one** limitation of it. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate prolonged exposure therapy as a treatment for PTSD. **[4]**

**13.** Describe **and** evaluate dual representation theory as an explanation of PTSD. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1].

**2.** **Positive engagement with society** [1]; **effective coping with challenges** [1].

**3.** Cultures differ in whether they regard certain experiences, such as hearing voices or prolonged low mood, as a **mental health problem**, a spiritual matter or an ordinary reaction to life [1]. Where it is not seen as a problem, people are **less likely to seek diagnosis**, so it is less often recorded [1].

**4.** **Re-experiencing** the event in the present, such as flashbacks or nightmares [1]; **avoidance** of thoughts, places or people linked to the event [1]; a persistent sense of **current threat**, such as hypervigilance or an exaggerated startle response [1].

**5.** Any one: **genetic vulnerability**; an **over-active amygdala**; the role of the **hippocampus**; **stress hormones** affecting how the memory is stored [1].

**6.** **Damage to relationships** [1]: Kofi has stopped seeing friends, so his social support is weakening [1]. **Negative impact on physical wellbeing** [1]: his frequent headaches suggest his physical health is suffering (accept: difficulty coping with day-to-day life — struggling to get out of bed) [1].

**7.** Any two, 2 marks each, for example: **need for more social care** — more people require support services, increasing demand on health and social services [1] [1]; **implications for the economy** — lost working days and lower productivity, plus the cost of treatment [1] [1]. (Also accept **increased crime rates**, explained as greater demand on police and the courts.)

**8.** Nadia's **obsession** is an intrusive thought that she will cause a fire [1]. According to the cognitive explanation, she **overestimates the likelihood of danger** [1] and has **inflated responsibility**, believing it is up to her alone to keep her family safe [1]. Checking the plugs is a **compulsion** that briefly reduces her anxiety, which keeps the behaviour going [1].

**9.** The therapist helps Nadia **identify and challenge** her irrational beliefs, such as how likely a fire really is and whether she alone is responsible [1]. Using **exposure**, Nadia switches off the plugs once and goes to bed [1] while **response prevention** means she is supported **not to go back and check** [1]. She learns that her anxiety **falls on its own** without checking, so the compulsion weakens [1].

**10.** Any four, 1 mark each, including at least one strength and one limitation: a strength is that **concordance is higher in identical twins**, supporting a genetic influence [1]; drugs that **raise serotonin** reduce symptoms for many patients, supporting the neurochemical explanation [1]. A limitation is that identical twin concordance is **not 100%**, so environmental factors must also play a role [1]; it is unclear whether brain differences **cause** OCD or are a **result** of it [1].

**11.** Eftekhari evaluated a nationwide scheme in which therapists were **trained to deliver prolonged exposure therapy** [1] to **US military veterans** diagnosed with PTSD [1]. Patients' **PTSD and depression symptoms fell significantly** after treatment [1]. Limitation, any one: the sample was **veterans only**, so findings may not generalise to civilians; there was **no control group** for comparison [1].

**12.** Any four, 1 mark each, including at least one strength and one limitation: a strength is that it targets **avoidance**, which keeps PTSD going [1]; research such as **Eftekhari's study** found significant symptom reductions in a real clinical setting [1]. A limitation is that reliving the trauma is **very distressing**, so some patients **drop out** [1]; it requires the patient to recall the trauma in detail, which may not suit everyone, raising **ethical concerns** about distress [1].

**13.** *Description (up to 3):* the theory proposes that a traumatic event is stored in **two memory systems** [1]: a **verbally accessible memory** that holds the event in words, placed in time and context, which the person can choose to retrieve; and a **situationally accessible memory** holding raw images, sounds and bodily sensations [1]. After trauma the situational memory is intense but **not connected** to the verbal record, so a cue such as a smell or sound activates it **automatically**, and the person relives the event as a flashback with no sense that it belongs to the past [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* a strength is that it **explains flashbacks** and why they feel present rather than past [1]. It also explains **why exposure therapy works**: recounting the trauma helps link the sensory memory to its context [1]. A limitation is that the two memory systems **cannot be observed directly**, making the theory hard to test [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming effects on individuals without linking them to the person in the scenario.
- Calling Nadia's checking an obsession: the thought is the obsession, the checking is the compulsion.
- Describing CBT in general terms without using Nadia's actual trigger.
- Evaluating with only strengths or only limitations when the question says "evaluate".
- Describing dual representation theory without explaining why flashbacks feel as if they are happening now.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which named studies are in 9218 mental health?**
Kearns's study of the effectiveness of CBT for OCD and Eftekhari's study of the effectiveness of prolonged exposure therapy for PTSD. Learn the aim, method, findings and an evaluation point for each from your course materials.

**Do I need to know the exact ICD criteria?**
You need the main characteristics and diagnostic features of each disorder, such as obsessions and compulsions for OCD and re-experiencing, avoidance and current threat for PTSD.

**How do I evaluate a therapy?**
Give evidence of effectiveness, explain how the therapy targets the cause it is based on, and consider drawbacks such as distress, drop-out and whether it suits everyone.
