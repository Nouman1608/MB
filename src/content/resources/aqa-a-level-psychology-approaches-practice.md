## Section A — Short answer

**1.** Outline the key difference between classical and operant conditioning. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by "vicarious reinforcement" in social learning theory. **[2]**

**3.** State the three parts of personality proposed by the psychodynamic approach, in the order they
develop. **[3]**

**4.** Identify **one** defence mechanism and briefly describe how it protects the ego from anxiety. **[2]**

**5.** What is meant by "self-actualisation" in the humanistic approach? **[2]**

## Section B — Application

**6.** A young child sees an older sibling praised by their parents for sharing a toy, and the child
begins sharing toys more often afterwards, even though the child was never directly rewarded.
**(a)** Identify the approach that best explains this behaviour. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the four mediational processes, explain how the child's behaviour changed. **[6]**

**7.** A researcher wants to compare the cognitive approach and the biological approach as
explanations for why some people are more vulnerable to depression than others.
**(a)** Outline what each approach would highlight as the likely cause. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way the two approaches differ in terms of the nature–nurture debate. **[3]**

## Section C — Extended writing

**8.** "Discuss the extent to which the humanistic approach can be considered a genuinely scientific
approach to psychology, using at least one other approach as a point of comparison." **[12]**

**9.** Compare the behaviourist approach and the psychodynamic approach in terms of determinism and
the type of evidence each relies on. **[8]**

## Worked answers

**1.** Classical conditioning is learning through **association** — an unconditioned stimulus is
repeatedly paired with a neutral stimulus until the neutral stimulus alone produces the response **[1]**.
Operant conditioning is learning through **consequences** — behaviour is shaped by reinforcement
(positive or negative) and punishment **[1]**. The key difference is that classical conditioning
creates an automatic association between stimuli, while operant conditioning is driven by the outcome
that follows a voluntary behaviour **[1]**.

**2.** Vicarious reinforcement is when an observer sees a model's behaviour being rewarded **[1]**, and
this increases the likelihood that the observer will imitate that behaviour themselves, without having
been personally reinforced for it **[1]**.

**3.** Id (present from birth) **[1]**, Ego (develops in early childhood) **[1]**, Superego (develops
around age 5) **[1]**.

**4.** Repression — pushing a distressing memory or thought out of conscious awareness entirely so the
person is not consciously aware of it **[2]**. (Denial or displacement, correctly described, would
also gain full marks.)

**5.** Self-actualisation is reaching one's full potential **[1]**, the innermost layer of Maslow's
hierarchy of needs, which can only be reached once more basic needs (such as physiological, safety,
and esteem needs) have been met **[1]**.

**6. (a)** Social learning theory **[1]**.
**(b)** **Attention** — the child must have attended to and noticed the sibling's behaviour and its
consequence **[1–2]**. **Retention** — the child must have stored a mental representation of the
sharing behaviour and the praise that followed **[1]**. **Motor reproduction** — the child must be
physically capable of performing the sharing behaviour themselves **[1]**. **Motivation** — seeing the
sibling praised (vicarious reinforcement) gives the child an expectation of similar reward, motivating
them to imitate the behaviour **[1–2]**. Full marks require all four processes named and applied
specifically to the sibling scenario, not defined generically.

**7. (a)** The cognitive approach would highlight faulty or negatively biased information processing —
for example, distorted thinking patterns or maladaptive schemas that make some people interpret
events more negatively **[2]**. The biological approach would highlight genetic vulnerability or
neurochemical imbalance — for example, lower levels of serotonin, or genes inherited from a parent
with depression **[2]**.
**(b)** The cognitive approach leans more towards **nurture**, since thinking patterns and schemas are
seen as learned from experience over a person's life **[1–2]**, whereas the biological approach leans
towards **nature**, since it explains vulnerability through inherited genes and innate neurochemistry
that exist independently of a person's experiences **[1]**.

**8.** A strong answer defines "scientific" (objective, falsifiable, using controlled and replicable
methods) and applies this standard directly to the humanistic approach: it deliberately rejects
lab-based and reductionist methods in favour of studying the whole, subjective person, making its
central concepts (self-actualisation, congruence) difficult to operationalise or test directly — a
weakness from a strictly scientific standpoint, though humanistic psychologists would argue this
trade-off is justified by the richness and real-world relevance of the resulting insight. Comparing
this explicitly against the biological approach (highly scientific: uses brain scans, twin studies,
controlled experiments, produces falsifiable and replicable findings) earns the strongest marks,
since the question specifically asks for a point of comparison rather than an isolated evaluation of
humanistic psychology alone. A balanced conclusion should weigh the trade-off between scientific
rigour and holistic, meaningful insight rather than dismissing the humanistic approach outright.

**9.** Behaviourism is **hard determinist** — all behaviour is the product of prior reinforcement
history, with no role for choice — and relies on **controlled laboratory evidence**, largely from
animal studies (Pavlov's dogs, Skinner's rats and pigeons), generalised to humans. The psychodynamic
approach is also broadly **determinist** (psychic determinism — behaviour is driven by unconscious
conflicts the person cannot control), but relies on very different evidence: **case studies** of
individual patients (such as Freud's case studies), which are rich in qualitative detail but difficult
to generalise or test scientifically. Both approaches therefore agree that behaviour is not a matter
of free will, but disagree sharply on evidence type — one favours objective, replicable experimental
data, the other favours idiographic, in-depth case material that is harder to verify or generalise.
A strong answer states this shared determinism explicitly before contrasting the evidence types, since
the question rewards an explicit comparison on both named dimensions rather than two separate
descriptions.
