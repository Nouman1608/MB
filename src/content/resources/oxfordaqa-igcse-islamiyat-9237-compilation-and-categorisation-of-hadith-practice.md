## Answers

**How the parts are marked.** One mark for each correct answer in the 1- and 2-mark questions. In the 4-mark questions, one mark for each point identified and one for explaining it. The 6-mark questions give four AO1 marks on that pattern plus up to two AO2 marks: **one** for a general, accurate use of a source of authority, **two** for a specific use applied to the question. The 12-mark question is marked by levels:

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Thorough examination of a range of points; logical reasoning with detailed evidence; excellent, direct use of the passage |
| 3 | 7–9 | Clear examination of more than one point; reasoning with some detail; good use of the passage |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some accurate examination; reasoning not always supported; general use of the passage. **Maximum Level 2 if only one point is made** |
| 1 | 1–3 | Limited examination with little reasoning. **Maximum Level 1 if the passage is not used** |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit |

**1.** The chain of narrators who passed the report down to the Prophet (pbuh) [1].

**2.** Imam Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: Hasan; Da'if; Maudu'.

**4. (a)** Da'if (weak) [1].

**(b)** One reason identified [1] and explained [2].

- **Broken chain** [1]: it is not known who passed the report on [1], so nobody can check whether he was honest and accurate [1].
- **Condition of Sahih not met** [1]: a Sahih Hadith needs a continuous chain [1], so a gap places the report below that standard [1].

**5.** For each of two features: feature identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Strict condition on narrators** [1]: narrators had to be shown to have met their teachers [1].
- **Arranged by subject** [1]: books and chapters on faith, prayer and Zakah make it easy to find rulings [1].
- **Chapter headings** [1]: they contain the compiler's legal conclusions [1].
- **Careful selection** [1]: he kept only Hadith he judged sound from the very large number he had heard [1].
- **High reputation** [1]: most Sunni Muslims regard it as the most authentic book after the Qur'an [1].

**6.** For each of two ways: way identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Comparing with the Qur'an** [1]: a Hadith that contradicts clear Qur'anic teaching is doubted [1].
- **Comparing with other Hadith** [1]: a report that opposes stronger, well-known reports is suspect [1].
- **Checking sense and history** [1]: a report that is impossible or contradicts known facts is rejected [1].
- **Examining the style** [1]: exaggerated promises and threats raise suspicion about authenticity [1].

**7.** AO1 **[4]**: for each of two reasons, reason identified [1] and explained [1]. AO2 **[2]**: one source of authority used generally [1] or specifically and applied [2].

Reasons (AO1):

- **Fabrication** [1]: some people invented reports for political or sectarian reasons [1].
- **Importance of Hadith** [1]: Muslims use them to decide what is lawful and unlawful, so accuracy matters [1].
- **Human error** [1]: honest narrators may forget or confuse details [1].
- **Distance in time** [1]: collections were made two centuries after the Prophet's (pbuh) death [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- Bukhari 3461: "whoever tells a lie on me intentionally will surely take his place in the Fire" [2].
- The same Hadith also says "Convey (my teachings) to the people even if it were a single sentence", so passing on correctly is a duty [2].

**8.** AO1 **[4]**: as in question 7. AO2 **[2]**: as in question 7.

Differences (AO1):

- **Meeting** [1]: Bukhari required that a narrator had met the person he narrated from, while Muslim accepted that they were contemporaries [1].
- **Arrangement** [1]: Muslim grouped versions of one Hadith together, while Bukhari arranged by subject with chapter headings expressing legal opinions [1].
- **Introduction** [1]: Muslim wrote a long introduction explaining his method [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- Bukhari 3461: the warning against lying explains why both scholars set strict standards [2].
- Both used the Isnad, which Ibn al-Mubarak is reported to have described as part of the religion [2].

**9.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **For the statement:** the Isnad was the first and most distinctive test; it showed who passed the report and whether they were honest and able; a broken or doubtful chain could remove a Hadith at once; the warning in the Hadith motivated careful checking of every narrator.
- **Against the statement:** the Matn also had to be tested, because a chain can look sound while the text has errors or conflicts with the Qur'an; scholars compared versions of reports; the compilers' work on arrangement and selection mattered too; the duty to "convey" means scholars also had to teach and spread Hadith.
- **Balanced view:** the Isnad and Matn work together, and a Sahih Hadith must pass both.

*Use of the passage*

- "Convey (my teachings) to the people" shows the duty to pass on his words.
- "Even if it were a single sentence" shows that small details matter.
- "Whoever tells a lie on me intentionally" explains why testing was needed.
- "Take his place in the Fire" shows how serious a false Hadith is.

Level 4 answers use the passage directly, discuss both Isnad and Matn with examples, and reach a reasoned judgement.
