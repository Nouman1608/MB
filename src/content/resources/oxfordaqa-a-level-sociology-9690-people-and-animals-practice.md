## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by speciesism. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two kinds of domesticated animal named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the sociozoologic scale. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two social causes of the emergence of zoonotic diseases. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which companion and therapy animals are part of human social life. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Varossa.

| Indicator (fictional) | 1980 | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wild elephants (thousands) | 40 | 18 | 24 |
| Land in protected areas (%) | 4 | 12 | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in Varossa's wild elephant population between 1980 and 2000. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain why the data could be used to support protected areas, and why a sociologist might be cautious about this conclusion. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> The fictional Rimba National Park was created to protect rhinos and lions. About 3,000 herders who had grazed cattle there for generations were moved to land outside the park, where grass is poorer. Rangers now patrol the boundary and fine anyone caught grazing inside. The park earns money from foreign tourists on safari, but few jobs go to the herders. Some young herders have been arrested for entering the park to collect firewood.

**(a)** Identify two costs of the park for the herders. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse the arguments for and against this type of conservation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why the slaughter of animals for food is largely hidden from consumers. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three arguments in the debate over animal experimentation. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how Bryant's idea of the zoological connection shows that animals matter to sociology. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that animal welfare reforms are enough to protect animals. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent are relationships between humans and animals socially constructed? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Discrimination against beings because of the species they belong to [1], for example treating animal suffering as less important simply because the animal is not human (a term coined by Ryder and popularised by Singer) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: working animals [1]; livestock [1]; pets or companion animals. **[2]**

**3.** A social ranking of animals, described by Arluke and Sanders [1], according to how well they fit human purposes, from "good" animals such as pets to "bad" animals such as vermin [1]. The same species can move position by setting, showing animal status is socially constructed [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Deforestation and urban expansion push people into contact with wildlife [1], allowing viruses carried by animals such as bats to pass to humans [1]. Intensive farming crowds animals together and wildlife trade brings species into markets [1], so diseases can spread between species and then to people, as with Nipah virus in Malaysia [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Pets are treated as family members, with names, celebrations and medical care [1], providing companionship and emotional support (Serpell) [1]. Assistance dogs and therapy animals in hospitals or schools are used to support people [1], although evidence on health benefits is mixed and the animals' welfare is debated [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** (18 - 40) / 40 x 100 [1] = a 55% fall [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Elephant numbers rose from 18,000 to 24,000 between 2000 and 2025 while protected land rose from 12% to 15% [1], suggesting protected areas may help species recover [1]. However, the data show correlation, not causation, as recovery may reflect other changes such as anti-poaching laws or reduced ivory demand [1], and they say nothing about the human costs of protected areas, such as evictions [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: loss of grazing land [1]; moved to poorer land [1]; fines for grazing; arrests for collecting firewood; few jobs from tourism. **[2]**
**(b)** For: protecting the park keeps rhinos and lions safe from grazing pressure and conflict [1], and tourist income can fund conservation and the national economy [1]. Against: moving 3,000 herders is fortress conservation, which Brockington criticised [1], because the rural poor bear the costs while foreign tourists enjoy the benefits [1]. Fines and arrests for firewood turn customary use into crime [1], and few jobs for herders reduce local support, which can make conservation harder to sustain [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Industrial slaughter divides killing into many separate tasks out of public view [1], which Pachirat calls the politics of sight, making violence acceptable to consumers and even workers [1]. Elias showed that carving moved behind the scenes as manners changed [1], so meat is now sold in forms that separate it from the living animal, reducing moral discomfort [1]. **[4]**

**9.** For: animal research has contributed to medical treatments and vaccines [1], so supporters argue it saves human lives [1]. Against: rights theorists such as Regan argue animals cannot be used as means [1], whatever the benefit to humans [1]. Middle position: the 3Rs (Russell and Burch) aim to replace, reduce and refine animal use [1], and bans such as the EU's on cosmetic testing (2013) show some uses are judged unjustifiable [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Bryant (1979) argued sociology had ignored animal-related human behaviour [1].
- Work: farming, fishing and veterinary jobs involve animals [1].
- Crime: poaching, wildlife trafficking and cruelty [1].
- Family: pets as family members [1].
- Religion: sacrifice and dietary rules [1].
- Leisure and status: hunting, racing, breed ownership [1].
- Analysis: animals shape and are shaped by social institutions, not only nature [1].
- Judgement: Bryant shows a gap in sociology, though later writers such as Arluke and Sanders developed the theory more fully [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Welfare accepts human use but aims to reduce suffering [1].
- The Five Freedoms set standards for farmed animals [1].
- Anti-cruelty laws date from the 1820s in Britain [1].
- The 3Rs reduce suffering in experiments [1].
- Application: own-society example of a welfare law or practice [1].
- Rights theorists (Regan) argue welfare reforms still treat animals as means [1].
- Reforms may make use more acceptable and so entrench it [1].
- The scale of industrial farming limits what welfare rules achieve [1].
- Pachirat: hidden slaughter means consumers rarely see welfare failures [1].
- Wild animals face habitat loss that welfare laws do not address [1].
- Welfarists reply that reforms reduce real suffering now [1].
- Judgement: welfare reforms help but do not address scale, habitat loss or the question of use [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Arluke and Sanders: the sociozoologic scale ranks animals socially [1].
- The same species is vermin, tool or pet depending on setting [1].
- Own-society application: dogs as guards, pets or unclean in different groups [1].
- Religious rules on sacred or forbidden animals vary between societies [1].
- Cruelty is socially defined, as debates over bullfighting or festivals show [1].
- Descartes' view of animals as machines was a historical construction [1].
- Elias and Pachirat: hidden slaughter shapes how meat is seen [1].
- Noske: the animal-industrial complex defines animals as raw material [1].
- Pets increasingly treated as family members [1].
- Counter: animals' capacity to suffer is biological, not constructed (Bentham, Singer) [1].
- Zoonoses show animals affecting humans regardless of meaning [1].
- The Columbian Exchange shows animals as active agents in history [1].
- Biodiversity loss and extinction are physical realities [1].
- Own-society application: a local conservation or working-animal example [1].
- Analysis: meanings are constructed, but they are built around real animal bodies and needs [1].
- Judgement: the status and treatment of animals is largely constructed, but their capacities and effects are not [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer separates the meaning of animals (constructed) from their biology and effects (not constructed) and weighs the two.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing welfare with rights.
- Naming diseases without explaining the social drivers.
- Treating protected areas as good or bad without considering who pays.
- Ignoring correlation and causation in data questions.
- Using no own-society example in an extended answer that asks for one.
