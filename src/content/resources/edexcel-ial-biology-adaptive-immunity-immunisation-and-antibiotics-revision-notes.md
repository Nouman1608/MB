## 6.10 Lymphocytes at a glance

| Cell | Made from | Key job | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| B effector (mostly plasma cells) | Selected B cell | Secrete antibody | Lots of rough ER; one antibody type |
| B memory | Selected B cell | Wait; divide on second exposure | Long-lived; no antibody now |
| T helper | Selected T cell | Release cytokines | Activates B cells, T killer cells and phagocytes |
| T killer | Activated T cell | Destroy infected or abnormal cells | Needs direct contact; perforin damages the membrane |
| T memory | Selected T cell | Make T helper and T killer cells fast | Long-lived |

**Sequence in steps**

1. Antigen-presenting cell shows the antigen.
2. Matching T helper cell binds (clonal selection) and divides (clonal expansion).
3. T helper releases cytokines.
4. B cell with matching receptor divides, forming plasma cells and B memory cells.
5. T killer cells are activated and attack infected cells.
6. Memory cells remain.

**Humoral** means antibodies in body fluids (B cells). **Cell-mediated** means whole cells doing the killing (T cells).

**Primary against secondary response**

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Lag before antibody appears | Long | Short |
| Peak antibody | Low | Much higher |
| Cause | Few matching cells to find and clone | Many memory cells already selected |
| Symptoms | Often | Rarely |

**Small reminder.** If a primary peak is 6 AU and a secondary is 90 AU, the secondary is 90 / 6 = 15 times higher.

## 6.11 Four types of immunity

| | Active (own antibodies, memory cells) | Passive (antibodies given) |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Catching and recovering from an infection | Placenta (fetus); breast milk |
| **Artificial** | Vaccination | Injected antibodies (antivenom, tetanus immunoglobulin) |

**Must-know distinctions**

- Active: slow, long-lasting, memory cells present.
- Passive: immediate, short-lived, no memory cells, antibodies are broken down.
- Vaccine: antigen without disease. Booster: second dose giving a secondary response.
- Use passive treatment when protection is needed straight away, such as after a snake bite or an infected wound.

## 6.12 Evolutionary race: evasion mechanisms

Chain of reasoning: a pathogen variant escapes a defence, so it survives and reproduces, so its alleles spread, so the defence becomes less effective, so hosts with better responses survive, and the cycle repeats.

| Mechanism | Example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Antigenic variation | Influenza virus | Memory cells do not recognise the changed antigen |
| Destroying T helper cells | HIV | Loss of coordination of B and T cells |
| Surviving inside macrophages | Mycobacterium tuberculosis | Avoids digestion |
| Latency | Viruses in a dormant state | Few antigens shown |
| Capsule | Some bacteria | Antigens covered, so phagocytes bind less well |
| Antibiotic resistance | MRSA | Selection by antibiotic use |

When you answer, state the example, the mechanism and the effect on the immune response (three linked points).

## 6.13 Antibiotics

| | Bacteriostatic | Bactericidal |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Stops growth and reproduction | Kills bacteria |
| Example | Tetracycline (blocks bacterial protein synthesis) | Penicillin (stops cell wall formation, causing lysis) |
| Viable count | Stays level | Falls |
| Needs immune system? | Yes, to clear bacteria | Less dependent |

- Antibiotics target bacterial features: cell wall, ribosomes. They do not act on viruses.
- Resistance: random mutation, then selection by antibiotic, then spread of the allele to offspring and other bacteria by plasmids.
- Turbidity includes dead cells, so only viable counts show killing.

## 6.14 Core Practical 14 in steps

1. Disinfect bench; wash hands.
2. Spread a standard volume of safe, non-pathogenic bacteria on sterile nutrient agar to make a lawn.
3. Place different antibiotic discs with sterile forceps, plus a control disc in sterile water.
4. Tape the lid in two places only; invert; incubate (for example 25 °C, 24 to 48 hours).
5. Measure the diameter of each clear zone; repeat.
6. Autoclave or disinfect everything.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | Type of antibiotic |
| Dependent variable | Diameter or area of clear zone |
| Controlled | Bacterial density, agar depth, disc size, concentration, temperature, time |
| Why invert? | Condensation drips onto the lid, not onto the agar |
| Why not seal fully? | To avoid anaerobic conditions in which harmful bacteria might grow |
| Zone area | π r² with r = half of the diameter |

**Small reminder.** Zone diameter 18 mm: r = 9 mm; area = π × 9² = 254 mm² (to 3 significant figures). Subtract the disc area if you want the clear area only; a 6 mm disc has area π × 3² = 28.3 mm², so 254 − 28.3 = 226 mm².

**Evaluation points.** Larger zone means more inhibition, but molecule size affects diffusion; the zone does not show bacteriostatic or bactericidal action; repeats allow a mean.

## 6.15 Hospital-acquired infections

| Contributory cause | Practice that follows |
|---|---|
| Hands carry bacteria | Hand washing, alcohol gel, bare below the elbow |
| Vulnerable patients | Isolation, screening, cleaning |
| Devices and wounds | Aseptic technique; remove devices early |
| Overuse of antibiotics | Prescribe only when needed; test sensitivity; narrow-spectrum choice where possible; reserve certain antibiotics; audit |

**Infection rate** = infections / patient-days × 1000. **Percentage change** = (new − old) / old × 100.

## Worked reminders

**Classify from counts.** A culture begins at 2.0 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³. After 8 hours, antibiotic X gives 2.1 × 10⁶ and antibiotic Y gives 4.0 × 10³.

```
X: (2.1 - 2.0) / 2.0 x 100 = +5 %, so growth has stopped: bacteriostatic
Y: (4.0 x 10^3 - 2.0 x 10^6) / 2.0 x 10^6 x 100 = -99.8 %: bactericidal
```

**Compare antibiotics from zones.** Mean zone diameters (disc 6 mm): antibiotic S 20 mm and antibiotic V 15 mm.

```
Area S = pi x 10^2   = 314 mm2
Area V = pi x 7.5^2  = 177 mm2
Clear area beyond the disc: S = 286 mm2, V = 148 mm2
S / V = 286 / 148 = 1.9 times
```

Notice that the diameters differ by a factor of 1.33 but the areas by 1.9. This is because area depends on the square of the radius.

**Rate in a hospital.** 48 infections in 16 000 patient-days is 3.0 per 1000 patient-days. After new rules, 27 infections in 15 000 patient-days is 1.8 per 1000. The fall is (3.0 − 1.8) / 3.0 × 100 = 40 %.

## More must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Antibody and cytokine | Antibodies are proteins made by plasma cells that bind antigens. Cytokines are signalling molecules released by T helper cells (and other cells) that stimulate other immune cells |
| T helper and T killer | T helper coordinates by signalling. T killer destroys the infected cell directly |
| Clonal selection and clonal expansion | Selection picks the lymphocyte with the matching receptor. Expansion is its division by mitosis into a clone |
| Vaccination and antibody injection | Vaccination gives antigen, so you make antibodies and memory cells. Antibody injection gives ready-made antibodies |
| Resistance and tolerance | Resistance is a property of bacteria that survive antibiotics. It is not a change in the patient |
| Lytic and latent viruses | A lytic virus makes new virions and bursts the cell. A latent virus stays dormant in the host cell, showing few antigens |

## Short model answers

**Explain why the second response to a vaccine is faster.** B memory cells were made in the first response [1]. They are already selected for the antigen and are more numerous than the original matching cells [1]. They divide and form plasma cells quickly [1], so antibody appears sooner and reaches a higher level [1].

**Explain how resistance spreads in a population of bacteria.** A mutation gives a resistant allele [1]. Sensitive bacteria are killed by the antibiotic, leaving resistant individuals to reproduce with less competition [1]. Offspring inherit the allele, and plasmids can carry it into other bacteria [1].

## Quick self-test

1. Name the cell type that releases cytokines to activate B cells and T killer cells.
2. Which B cell stays in the body, without secreting antibody, to respond later?
3. Classify the immunity of a baby given antibodies in breast milk.
4. Why does passive immunity not last?
5. Antibody titre rises from 4 AU to 60 AU in 8 days. Calculate the mean rate of increase.
6. By what factor does a titre of 60 AU exceed one of 4 AU?
7. State one feature of influenza that allows evasion of memory cells.
8. Give the difference between bacteriostatic and bactericidal antibiotics.
9. A clear zone has diameter 15 mm. Calculate the zone area in mm² to 3 significant figures.
10. A ward falls from 45 infections to 27. Calculate the percentage reduction.
11. Give one way that antibiotic prescription is controlled to slow resistance.

### Answers

1. T helper cell.
2. B memory cell.
3. Natural passive.
4. No memory cells are formed and the antibodies are broken down.
5. (60 − 4) / 8 = 7 AU per day.
6. 60 / 4 = 15 times.
7. Surface antigens change by mutation (antigenic variation).
8. Bacteriostatic stops growth; bactericidal kills.
9. π × 7.5² = 177 mm².
10. (45 − 27) / 45 × 100 = 40 %.
11. Prescribe only when needed, test which antibiotic the bacteria are sensitive to, or reserve some antibiotics for resistant infections.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a plasma cell a memory cell, or saying that T cells make antibodies.
- Saying that antibodies "kill" pathogens, without saying they bind, neutralise or mark them for phagocytes.
- Treating passive immunity as producing memory cells.
- Using "adapt" or "become immune" for bacteria where mutation followed by selection is needed.
- Saying that a larger clear zone proves that the antibiotic kills bacteria.
- Forgetting the control disc, or not stating that the plate is inverted and taped in two places.
- Using the diameter in place of the radius when working out area.
- Describing hospital measures with no link to the cause they address.
- Comparing infection counts when the patient-days differ.
