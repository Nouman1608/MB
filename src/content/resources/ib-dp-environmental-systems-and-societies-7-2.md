## Primary and secondary energy

**Primary energy** is energy as it is found in nature: coal, crude oil, natural gas, uranium, sunlight, wind, flowing water, geothermal heat and biomass. **Secondary energy** is a carrier made from primary sources so that energy can be moved and used: electricity, petrol and diesel refined from crude oil, and hydrogen. Electricity is never a source in itself; its environmental impact depends on what generated it.

This distinction stops a common error. An electric car is only as low-carbon as the grid that charges it. A country that generates most of its electricity from coal cuts tailpipe pollution in its cities by switching to electric vehicles, but much less of its CO₂.

## Energy conversion and efficiency

Every conversion loses some energy as heat, in line with the second law of thermodynamics from Topic 1. Efficiency is:

```
Efficiency (%) = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100
```

### Worked example 1: a thermal power station

A fictional coal-fired station burns fuel containing 2,000 MJ of chemical energy and runs at 38% efficiency.

```
Electrical output = 2,000 × 0.38 = 760 MJ
Waste heat        = 2,000 − 760  = 1,240 MJ
```

Most of the energy in the coal leaves as heat in cooling water and flue gases. A **combined heat and power** (CHP) plant uses that heat to warm nearby buildings. If a CHP plant delivers 80% of its input as useful electricity plus heat, the same fuel gives 2,000 × 0.80 = 1,600 MJ of useful energy, more than double the useful output, for the same emissions.

In a systems diagram, show the input, the useful output and the heat output as separate flows. The heat flow is the largest in many thermal stations.

## Capacity and capacity factor

A turbine's **rated capacity** is its maximum power output. Its **capacity factor** is its actual output over a year divided by what it would produce running at full power all year. Wind and solar have lower capacity factors than fossil or nuclear plants because their output depends on the weather and the time of day.

### Worked example 2: a wind farm

A fictional wind farm has 20 turbines of 3 MW each and a capacity factor of 32%. A year has 8,760 hours.

```
Rated capacity  = 20 × 3 = 60 MW
Annual output   = 60 × 8,760 × 0.32 = 168,192 MWh ≈ 168 GWh
Households supplied (at a fictional 4,000 kWh each per year)
                = 168,192,000 ÷ 4,000 ≈ 42,000 households
```

If you multiply capacity by hours without the capacity factor, you overstate the output by about three times. Examiners use this to test whether you can read energy data critically.

## Intermittency and how grids cope

Solar and wind are **variable** (intermittent): output changes with weather and daylight and cannot simply be turned up when demand rises. Fossil fuel, nuclear, geothermal, biomass and reservoir hydro plants are **dispatchable** to different degrees, meaning operators can control their output.

Ways to keep a grid with a large share of variable renewables stable:

- **Storage:** pumped hydro (pump water uphill when power is cheap, release it through turbines when needed), batteries, and stored heat.
- **Interconnectors:** cables between regions or countries, so a calm area can import from a windy one.
- **A diverse mix:** wind often blows when the sun is weak, so the two together vary less than either alone.
- **Demand management:** shifting flexible use, such as charging vehicles, to times of high supply.

In an evaluation, intermittency is a limitation you can partly answer, not a reason to reject renewables outright. State the cost of the answers too: storage and interconnectors need investment and materials.

## Measuring energy security

Energy security can be given a number with the **import dependency**:

```
Import dependency (%) = net energy imports ÷ total primary energy supply × 100
```

### Worked example 3

A fictional country has a primary energy supply of 1,200 PJ a year. It produces 300 PJ itself and imports the rest.

```
Net imports       = 1,200 − 300 = 900 PJ
Import dependency = 900 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 75%
```

High dependency is not the only risk. Ask also: how many suppliers are there, do supplies cross unstable regions, how much is stored, and how diverse is the domestic mix? A country importing 75% from many stable partners may be more secure than one importing 40% from a single neighbour.

## How national choices differ

Real examples show the factors from the Topic 7 guide at work.

- **Iceland** generates almost all of its electricity from hydroelectric and geothermal sources, because its geology and rivers make both cheap and plentiful. Availability of resources is the deciding factor.
- **France** generates most of its electricity in nuclear power stations, a choice built up through government policy from the 1970s to reduce dependence on imported oil. Energy security and political decisions led.
- **Germany** closed its last three nuclear power stations in April 2023, after public opposition to nuclear power grew. Cultural attitudes and politics outweighed the low operating emissions of nuclear power, and the country relied on expanding renewables alongside fossil fuels.

These cases let you argue that there is no single best energy source: the right mix depends on resources, history, values and politics.

## Efficiency, conservation and the rebound effect

- **Energy efficiency:** the same service from less energy, such as LED lighting, heat pumps or better insulation.
- **Energy conservation:** using less of the service, such as lowering a thermostat or travelling less.

### Worked example 4: lighting

Replacing a 60 W bulb with a 9 W LED giving similar light, used for 1,000 hours a year:

```
Saving = (60 − 9) W × 1,000 h = 51,000 Wh = 51 kWh per bulb per year
```

Savings can be partly cancelled by the **rebound effect**: when a service becomes cheaper, people use more of it, for example leaving efficient lights on longer or driving an efficient car further. In 1865 the economist William Stanley Jevons argued that more efficient steam engines increased Britain's total coal use, an idea now called the Jevons paradox. This is why efficiency policies are often paired with pricing or conservation measures.

## Common mistakes

- Calling electricity an energy source. It is a secondary carrier.
- Multiplying rated capacity by hours without the capacity factor.
- Saying renewables "cannot supply a grid" with no mention of storage, interconnection or diversity.
- Treating energy security as the same as self-sufficiency.
- Assuming efficiency savings are always fully realised.
- Leaving out units: MW (power) and MWh or GWh (energy) are different quantities.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** efficiency, output or dependency: write the formula, convert units first, give the answer with units.
- **Explain** a country's mix: link each source to a named factor, such as geology, policy or public attitude.
- **Evaluate** an energy strategy: emissions, cost, reliability, security, social acceptance and wider environmental impact, then a judgement for the country in the question.
- **To what extent** questions on a transition: consider the timescale, who gains and who loses, and the value systems behind each option.
