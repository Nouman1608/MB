These notes condense section **3.1.5.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). The [Effect of temperature study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-temperature-on-reaction-rate/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-effect-of-temperature-on-reaction-rate-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- The meaning of **rate of reaction**.
- The **qualitative effect of temperature** on rate.
- Use the **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** to explain why a small rise in temperature can cause a large rise in rate.
- Required practical 3: investigate how rate changes with temperature.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time |
| Units | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| Collision frequency | Number of collisions per second |
| Activation energy, Eₐ | Minimum energy for a successful collision |
| Initial rate | Rate at the start, before concentrations have changed |
| Rate ∝ 1 ÷ time | When a fixed amount of change is timed, the rate is proportional to the reciprocal of the time |

## The two effects of increasing temperature

| Effect | Size |
|---|---|
| Particles move faster, so more collisions per second | Small |
| A greater proportion of particles have energy at least equal to Eₐ, so more collisions are successful | Large, and the main reason |

## The full explanation (use all points)

1. Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy.
2. The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve moves to the right, with a lower peak and a larger high-energy tail.
3. Eₐ does not change, and lies in the tail where the curve is low.
4. The area to the right of Eₐ increases by a large proportion.
5. A greater proportion of collisions have energy at least equal to Eₐ.
6. The frequency of successful collisions increases, so the rate increases.

Diagram: two curves, the same area, T₂ to the right and lower, Eₐ marked, areas to the right of Eₐ shaded.

## Practical: sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO₂(g) + S(s) + H₂O(l)

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Measure | Time for a cross viewed through the mixture to disappear |
| Rate | Proportional to 1 ÷ time |
| Independent variable | Temperature |
| Control variables | Concentrations, volumes, flask, cross, observer |
| Safety | SO₂ is toxic; use a fume cupboard or ventilation; eye protection |

Data example: 20 °C 85 s, 30 °C 43 s, 40 °C 22 s, 50 °C 11 s, 60 °C 6 s.
Relative rates (1000 ÷ t): 11.8, 23.3, 45.5, 90.9, 166.7. The rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise.

### Limitations and improvements

- Judging when the cross disappears is subjective, so use a light sensor or the same observer.
- Temperature drifts during the reaction, so use a water bath and record a mean.
- Equilibrate both solutions to the same temperature before mixing.
- Short times have a large percentage timing uncertainty (±0.5 s on 6 s is 8%; on 85 s it is 0.6%).
- Repeat and average.

## Worked reminders

**Rate from data.** The time falls from 85 s to 43 s. The relative rate is 85 ÷ 43 = **1.98 times** as great.

**Collision effect.** From 298 to 308 K the collision frequency rises by only about 1.7%, yet rates often double, so the proportion with E ≥ Eₐ must be the main factor.

**What does not change.** Eₐ, ΔH and the number of molecules.

**Units of rate.** If a concentration falls by 0.020 mol dm⁻³ in 40 s, rate = 0.020 ÷ 40 = **5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.

**Graph shape.** Relative rate against temperature is a curve that gets steeper, not a straight line.

## Common mistakes

- Giving only the increase in collision frequency as the reason.
- Saying heating lowers the activation energy.
- Omitting "equal to or greater than".
- Not mentioning the tail of the distribution.
- Using rate ∝ time instead of 1 ÷ time.
- Not controlling concentration and volume in the practical.
- Using a graph without labelled axes and units.
- Saying "human error" for an experimental limitation.

## Self-test

1. Define rate of reaction.
2. State the units of rate when concentration is in mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds.
3. State the two reasons why rate increases with temperature, and say which matters more.
4. Explain, using the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, why a small increase in temperature can cause a large increase in rate.
5. State what happens to the activation energy when the temperature is raised.
6. In a thiosulfate experiment the cross disappears after 60 s at 25 °C and 30 s at 35 °C. By what factor does the rate increase?
7. Explain why rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time in this experiment.
8. State two control variables in the practical.
9. Suggest why SO₂ is a hazard.
10. A concentration falls by 0.030 mol dm⁻³ in 50 s. Calculate the mean rate.
11. Suggest one improvement to the measurement of the end point.
12. Explain why both solutions are brought to the same temperature before mixing.

### Answers

1. The change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time.
2. mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
3. Particles collide more often (small effect); a greater proportion of particles has energy at least equal to Eₐ (the main effect).
4. The distribution moves right and the tail grows; Eₐ lies in the tail where the area is small, so the area beyond Eₐ increases by a large proportion; more collisions are successful.
5. Nothing: it is unchanged.
6. 60 ÷ 30 = **2** times.
7. The same amount of sulfur must form to hide the cross every time, so rate = fixed amount ÷ time.
8. Any two of: concentration and volume of thiosulfate, concentration and volume of acid, the flask, the cross, the observer.
9. It is toxic and can trigger asthma attacks.
10. 0.030 ÷ 50 = **6.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
11. Use a light sensor and data logger, or the same observer each time.
12. If they are at different temperatures, the mixture temperature is unknown and changes at the start, so the rate is not measured at the stated temperature.
