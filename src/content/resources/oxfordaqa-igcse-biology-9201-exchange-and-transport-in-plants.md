| Statement | Content |

| b | Root hairs increase the surface area of roots; the flattened shape and internal air spaces increase the surface area to volume ratio of leaves |
| c | Stomata obtain carbon dioxide and remove oxygen produced in photosynthesis; they also help control the rate of water loss |
| d | Plants mainly lose water vapour from leaves, mostly through stomata; evaporation is faster in hot, dry, windy conditions; stomata can close to prevent wilting |

| f | Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to stem and leaves (the transpiration stream); phloem carries dissolved sugars from leaves to the rest of the plant (translocation); structure is related to function |

## The route of a carbon dioxide molecule into a leaf

Carbon dioxide enters by **diffusion**: net movement from a higher to a lower concentration. Follow one molecule:

```
air outside the leaf → stoma (pore) → air spaces inside the leaf
→ cell wall and membrane of a mesophyll cell → chloroplast, where it is used
```

Why does it keep moving in? Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide inside the cells, so the concentration inside the
leaf stays **lower** than in the air outside. That keeps the **concentration gradient** in place. Oxygen made in
photosynthesis diffuses the other way: its concentration is higher inside the leaf, so it leaves through the
stomata.

The **internal air spaces** matter because gases diffuse much faster through air than through cells, and the
spaces bring air close to almost every cell. The **flattened shape** means no cell is far from a surface.

## Roots and root hairs

Most of the water and mineral ions a plant needs are absorbed by its roots. **Root hairs** are long, thin
extensions of root cells that grow out into the soil. Thousands of them greatly increase the **surface area**
in contact with soil water, so absorption is faster.

**Worked example 1: the effect of root hairs** (invented figures). A section of root without hairs has a surface
area of 2.0 mm². Root hairs on the same section add 14.0 mm².

```
total surface area = 2.0 + 14.0 = 16.0 mm²
increase factor    = 16.0 ÷ 2.0 = 8 times
```

If uptake is proportional to surface area, this section can absorb water about eight times as fast as it could
without hairs. When a plant is transplanted carelessly, root hairs are torn off, and it may wilt even in moist
soil.

## Stomata and guard cells: a balancing act

Stomata have two jobs that pull in opposite directions:

- **open** to let carbon dioxide in (and oxygen out) for photosynthesis;
- help to **control water loss**, because water vapour also escapes through open stomata.

The size of each stoma is controlled by the two **guard cells** that surround it. When the plant has plenty of
water, the guard cells swell and the stoma opens. If the plant loses water faster than the roots can replace it,
the guard cells lose water and the stoma **closes**, which prevents **wilting**. The cost is that carbon dioxide
can no longer enter freely, so photosynthesis slows.

## Where is water lost? An experiment

The specification says plants mainly lose water vapour from their leaves, and most of the loss takes place through
the stomata. A classic experiment shows this.

**Worked example 2** (invented data). Four similar leaves are hung up for 24 hours. Petroleum jelly, which is
waterproof, is spread on some surfaces. In this species, most stomata are on the lower surface.

| Leaf | Surface covered | Mass at start (g) | Mass after 24 h (g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | None | 2.40 | 1.56 |
| B | Upper only | 2.36 | 1.60 |
| C | Lower only | 2.44 | 2.30 |
| D | Both | 2.38 | 2.35 |

Because the leaves start at different masses, compare **percentage loss**:

```
A: (2.40 − 1.56) ÷ 2.40 × 100 = 35%
B: (2.36 − 1.60) ÷ 2.36 × 100 = 32% (2 s.f.)
C: (2.44 − 2.30) ÷ 2.44 × 100 = 5.7% (2 s.f.)
D: (2.38 − 2.35) ÷ 2.38 × 100 = 1.3% (2 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: covering the lower surface (C) cut water loss far more than covering the upper surface (B).
The lower surface has most of the stomata, so most water vapour leaves through stomata. Leaf D still lost a
little, showing a small loss through the rest of the surface.

### Why hot, dry and windy conditions increase loss

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Hot | Water evaporates faster from the cells inside the leaf |
| Dry air | The concentration gradient of water vapour between the leaf and the air is steeper |
| Windy | Water vapour is blown away from the leaf surface, keeping the gradient steep |

## Two transport systems

Flowering plants have **separate** transport systems.

### Xylem and the transpiration stream

**Xylem** transports **water and mineral ions** from the roots to the stem and leaves. The movement of water from
the roots, through the xylem and out of the leaves is the **transpiration stream**. As water evaporates from the
leaves, more is pulled up the xylem to replace it, like a continuous column.

**Worked example 3: speed of water in the xylem.** A celery stalk is stood in coloured water. After 25 minutes the
dye has risen 15 cm up the xylem.

```
speed = distance ÷ time = 15 cm ÷ 25 min = 0.6 cm per minute
in one hour: 0.6 × 60 = 36 cm
```

Moving the stalk to a warm, windy place would increase water loss from the leaves, so the dye would rise faster.

### Phloem and translocation

**Phloem** carries **dissolved sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant**, including the **growing regions**
(such as shoot tips and young leaves) and the **storage organs** (such as potato tubers, where sugar is converted
to starch). This process is **translocation**. Unlike the transpiration stream, translocation can move material
up or down the plant, depending on where it is needed.

### Structure related to function

| Tissue | Structure | How it suits the function |
|---|---|---|
| Xylem | Long hollow tubes of dead cells joined end to end, with no end walls; walls strengthened with lignin | Water flows through without obstruction; strong walls support the plant and do not collapse |
| Phloem | Columns of living cells with pores in the end walls (sieve plates), with companion cells alongside | Sugar solution passes from cell to cell through the pores; the living cells can move sugars in either direction |

## Common mistakes

- Saying carbon dioxide is "breathed in" by leaves. It diffuses in through stomata.
- Mixing up the cargo: xylem carries **water and mineral ions**, phloem carries **dissolved sugars**.
- Saying the transpiration stream goes in both directions; only translocation does.
- Comparing raw mass losses when leaves start at different masses; use percentage change.
- Forgetting the cost of closing stomata: less carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
- Writing that guard cells are the pores. The stoma is the pore; guard cells surround it.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** an adaptation for exchange: feature → larger surface area or shorter distance → faster diffusion or
  absorption.
- **Interpret** water-loss data: calculate percentage change, compare, and link to where stomata are.
- **Explain** wilting: water lost faster than replaced → guard cells close stomata → less water lost.
- **Compare** xylem and phloem: use paired points (contents, direction, process, living or dead).
