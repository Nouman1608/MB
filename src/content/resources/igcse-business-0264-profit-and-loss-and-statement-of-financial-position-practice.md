Money is in dollars. All businesses and people are invented. Where a question asks for capital employed, use total assets - current liabilities.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term profit. **[2]**

**2.** State two reasons why profit is important to a private sector business. **[2]**

**3.** Bramblecott Tools sold 2,400 hammers at 15 each in one year. Its cost of sales was 21,600 and its expenses were 9,300.

**(a)** Calculate its revenue. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its gross profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate its profit. **[2]**

**4.** Wychwood Prints had revenue of 90,000, gross profit of 38,000 and profit of 11,500. Calculate its cost of sales and its expenses. **[4]**

**5.** Classify each item as a non-current asset, a current asset, a non-current liability or a current liability. **[5]**

- A delivery van
- Paper held ready to be sold
- Money owed to a supplier for ink
- A bank loan repayable over five years
- Cash in the till

**6.** Salterton Kites has the following items on its statement of financial position: premises 85,000; machinery 22,000; inventory 14,000; trade receivables 6,500; cash 3,500; trade payables 9,000; overdraft 4,000; bank loan repayable in five years 40,000.

**(a)** Calculate total assets. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate total liabilities. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate working capital. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate capital employed. **[2]**

**7.** Explain one way in which profit can be a source of finance for a business. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the difference between a non-current liability and a current liability. Give one example of each. **[3]**

**9.** Mossgiel Bakery had revenue of 64,000, cost of sales of 38,400 and expenses of 28,000 last year.

**(a)** Calculate the profit or loss. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one action the owner could take to improve the result. **[3]**

**10.** Kilnwick Cafe has two years of figures.

| Line | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 120,000 | 138,000 |
| Cost of sales | 48,000 | 62,100 |
| Expenses | 60,000 | 66,000 |

**(a)** Calculate gross profit and profit for each year. **[4]**
**(b)** Analyse why profit fell although revenue rose. **[4]**

**11.** Sandiway Signs has the following statement of financial position. Non-current assets: machinery 96,000. Current assets: inventory 11,000, trade receivables 18,000, cash 1,000. Current liabilities: trade payables 22,000, overdraft 26,000. Non-current liabilities: bank loan 50,000.

**(a)** Calculate working capital. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate total liabilities. **[2]**
**(c)** The owner wants to buy a second machine for 30,000, paid for by increasing the overdraft. Evaluate whether this is a good decision. **[6]**

**12.** Blythewood Gardens asks a bank for a long-term loan of 40,000. Last year it had revenue of 210,000, cost of sales of 126,000 and expenses of 70,000. Its statement of financial position shows non-current assets of 150,000, inventory of 12,000, trade receivables of 4,000, cash of 20,000, trade payables of 18,000, an overdraft of 12,000 and an existing bank loan of 60,000.

**(a)** Calculate Blythewood Gardens' profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify whether the bank should lend the 40,000. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Profit is revenue minus total costs [1]. A negative result is a loss, so profit is the surplus left after all costs are paid [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that only says "money the business makes" is too vague. Answers usually need the idea of revenue less costs.

**2.** Any two from: reward for risk-taking; source of finance; measure of success; attracts investors. One mark for each reason stated [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating two separate reasons from the syllabus list is enough here. Repeating one idea in two ways is unlikely to earn the second mark.

**3. (a)** Revenue = 2,400 x 15 = **36,000** [1]
**(b)** Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales = 36,000 - 21,600 [1] = **14,400** [1]
**(c)** Profit = gross profit - expenses = 14,400 - 9,300 [1] = **5,100** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the subtraction you are doing. If one number is wrong, visible working still shows that you know the method.

**4.** Cost of sales = revenue - gross profit = 90,000 - 38,000 [1] = **52,000** [1]. Expenses = gross profit - profit = 38,000 - 11,500 [1] = **26,500** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Questions with missing figures test whether you can rearrange the formulae. Do not use revenue in the second calculation, since expenses come from gross profit.

**5.** Delivery van: non-current asset [1]. Paper held ready to be sold: current asset (inventory) [1]. Money owed to a supplier: current liability (trade payables) [1]. Bank loan repayable over five years: non-current liability [1]. Cash in the till: current asset [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Look at how long the business will keep the asset, or how long until the debt is due. A van is kept for years, whereas money owed to a supplier is usually due soon.

**6. (a)** Non-current assets = 85,000 + 22,000 = 107,000. Current assets = 14,000 + 6,500 + 3,500 = 24,000 [1]. Total assets = **131,000** [1]
**(b)** Current liabilities = 9,000 + 4,000 = 13,000. Total liabilities = 13,000 + 40,000 [1] = **53,000** [1]
**(c)** Working capital = current assets - current liabilities = 24,000 - 13,000 [1] = **11,000** [1]
**(d)** Capital employed = total assets - current liabilities = 131,000 - 13,000 [1] = **118,000** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Group the items before adding. A subtotal for each category makes later parts easier and shows your method clearly.

**7.** Profit that is kept in the business (retained profit) can be used to finance the business [1]. For example, it can pay for new machinery or more inventory [1]. The business does not have to borrow, so it avoids paying interest [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A three-mark explanation usually needs a point, a development and a consequence. Naming "retained profit" alone is only the start.

**8.** A non-current liability is owed for longer than a year, whereas a current liability is due within a year [1]. Example of a non-current liability: a bank loan [1]. Example of a current liability: trade payables or an overdraft [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Make the comparison explicit with a word such as "whereas". Two separate definitions, with no contrast, can read as incomplete.

**9. (a)** Gross profit = 64,000 - 38,400 = 25,600 [1]. Profit = 25,600 - 28,000 [1] = **-2,400, a loss of 2,400** [1]
**(b)** One action: reduce expenses [1], for example by moving to cheaper premises or cutting advertising [1]. Expenses would need to fall by more than 2,400, less than 9% of 28,000, for the bakery to reach a profit [1]. Raising prices is another valid action if it is developed in the same way. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative answer should be labelled as a loss. For the action, tie it to the figures in the case rather than giving a generic idea.

**10. (a)** Year 1 gross profit = 120,000 - 48,000 = **72,000** [1]; profit = 72,000 - 60,000 = **12,000** [1]. Year 2 gross profit = 138,000 - 62,100 = **75,900** [1]; profit = 75,900 - 66,000 = **9,900** [1]
**(b)** Revenue rose by 18,000 (15%) [1]. Cost of sales rose by 14,100, about 29%, so it grew nearly twice as fast as revenue [1]. Gross profit therefore rose by only 3,900 [1]. Expenses rose by 6,000, more than the gain in gross profit, so profit fell by 2,100 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An analyse answer builds a chain from one figure to the next. Describing that revenue rose and profit fell, with no link between them, is only a description.

**11. (a)** Current assets = 11,000 + 18,000 + 1,000 = 30,000. Current liabilities = 22,000 + 26,000 = 48,000 [1]. Working capital = 30,000 - 48,000 = **-18,000** [1]
**(b)** Total liabilities = 48,000 + 50,000 [1] = **98,000** [1]
**(c)** For: a second machine may raise output and revenue, and machinery is a non-current asset the business keeps [1]. It may meet demand the present machine cannot, so profit could rise [1]. Against: working capital is already negative at -18,000 [1]. An overdraft is a current liability, so borrowing 30,000 this way takes current liabilities to 78,000 and working capital to -48,000 [1]. A long-term asset is being funded by short-term borrowing, with interest and a risk that the bank asks for repayment [1]. Judgement: the purchase is a poor decision on these terms [1]. The owner should consider a longer-term loan or leasing, or first improve working capital by collecting the 18,000 owed by customers. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluate answer weighs both sides and then commits to a decision with a reason. Use the figures from the case, and do not end with "it depends" alone.

**12. (a)** Gross profit = 210,000 - 126,000 = 84,000 [1]. Profit = 84,000 - 70,000 = **14,000** [1]
**(b)** For lending: the business makes a profit of 14,000, which shows it earns more than it spends [1]. Current assets are 36,000 against current liabilities of 30,000, so working capital is positive at 6,000, and cash of 20,000 is available [1]. Against lending: it already has a bank loan of 60,000, and total liabilities are 90,000, so a further 40,000 adds to the debt [1]. Annual profit of 14,000 is small compared with a 40,000 loan and working capital of 6,000 gives only a thin cushion [1]. Judgement: the bank should lend only with caution, for example a smaller amount or a security over the non-current assets of 150,000 [1]. The reason is that the business is profitable and can meet short-term bills, but its existing debt is already high and the profit is modest [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A justify answer needs a clear decision and a reason that uses evidence from both statements. A reply that lists figures without saying what they mean for the lender is incomplete.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving gross profit when the question asks for profit.
- Showing only a final number. Write the formula and the substitution.
- Placing an overdraft among non-current liabilities, or inventory among non-current assets.
- Using total liabilities instead of current liabilities when finding working capital.
- Forgetting that a negative profit is a loss, and not labelling it.
- Listing the reasons profit is important without linking them to the business in the question.
- Describing figures in a case study without analysing why they changed or what they mean.
- Writing only one side in an evaluate or justify question, or finishing with no decision.
- Treating profit as cash. A business with profit can still have negative working capital.
