## 2.7.1 What PED measures

Price elasticity of demand is the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. A large response means demand is elastic. A small response means demand is inelastic.

The key word is *responsiveness*. PED is not how much people want the good.

## 2.7.2 Calculating and interpreting PED

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price

% change = (new value - old value) / old value x 100
```

A demand curve slopes downward, so price and quantity move in opposite directions and PED comes out negative. Answers often quote the figure without the minus sign and judge its size.

**Worked example 1 (inelastic).** A small pizzeria (invented) raises the price of a pizza from 2.00 to 2.40. Weekly sales fall from 500 to 440 pizzas.

- % change in price = (2.40 - 2.00) / 2.00 x 100 = +20%
- % change in quantity = (440 - 500) / 500 x 100 = -12%
- PED = -12 / +20 = **-0.6**

The size is below 1, so demand is **price inelastic**: quantity fell by a smaller percentage than price rose.

**Worked example 2 (elastic).** A juice stall (invented) cuts the price of a smoothie from 4 to 3. Sales rise from 200 to 290 a day.

- % change in price = (3 - 4) / 4 x 100 = -25%
- % change in quantity = (290 - 200) / 200 x 100 = +45%
- PED = +45 / -25 = **-1.8**

The size is above 1, so demand is **price elastic**.

### Reading the value

| PED (ignoring sign) | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Buyers take the same quantity whatever the price |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | The quantity percentage is smaller than the price percentage |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary | Quantity and price move by equal percentages |
| Between 1 and infinity | Elastic | The quantity percentage is larger than the price percentage |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | A tiny price rise removes all demand |

**Using PED backwards.** If PED is -0.5 and a shop raises price by 8%, quantity demanded changes by -0.5 x 8 = -4%. From 2,000 units a week that is 2,000 x 0.96 = 1,920 units.

### Diagrams (price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis)

```
Perfectly inelastic   Perfectly elastic   Unitary elastic
P |      |            P |                  P | \
  |      |              |__________          |  \_
  |      |              |                    |     ‾‾--__
  +------ Q             +------------ Q      +----------- Q
 vertical line          horizontal line     curve bowing in
 (PED = 0)              (PED = infinity)    towards the origin
```

- A vertical demand curve means PED is 0.
- A horizontal demand curve means PED is infinite.
- A steep curve shows inelastic demand and a shallow curve shows elastic demand, when both are drawn on the same scales.
- A curve that bows in towards the origin (a rectangular hyperbola) shows unitary elasticity at every point.

A straight downward-sloping demand curve does not have one PED. Near the top (high price, low quantity) a given change in price is a small percentage and a given change in quantity is a large percentage, so demand is elastic there. Near the bottom it is inelastic.

## 2.7.3 Determinants of PED

1. **Availability and closeness of substitutes.** If a buyer can switch easily, a price rise sends them elsewhere, so demand is elastic. A good with few substitutes has inelastic demand.
2. **Necessity or luxury.** People keep buying necessities when price rises. They cut back on luxuries.
3. **Proportion of income spent.** If the good takes a small share of income, a price rise barely matters and demand is inelastic. A large item makes buyers more sensitive.
4. **Time.** Over a longer period buyers find substitutes and change habits, so demand is more elastic in the long run.
5. **How broadly the good is defined.** Demand for fuel is less elastic than demand for one brand of fuel, because rival brands are substitutes only for the brand.
6. **Habit or addiction.** Buyers who feel they must have the product respond little to price.

Link each determinant to the good: with no close substitutes for a medicine, buyers cannot switch, so demand is inelastic.

## 2.7.4 PED and total spending (revenue)

Total spending by buyers equals the revenue of sellers: price x quantity. Whether revenue rises or falls after a price change depends on PED.

| PED | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Inelastic | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |
| Unitary | No change | No change |
| Elastic | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |

The reason: on elastic demand the percentage change in quantity beats the percentage change in price, so revenue moves with quantity. On inelastic demand, price wins.

**Worked example 3.** A demand schedule for a product in Market K (invented) follows the pattern quantity = 120 - 10 x price.

| Price | Quantity | Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 20 | 200 |
| 8 | 40 | 320 |
| 6 | 60 | 360 |
| 4 | 80 | 320 |
| 2 | 100 | 200 |

Price falls from 10 to 8: % change in price = -20%, % change in quantity = +100%, so PED = -5 (elastic) and revenue rises from 200 to 320.
Price falls from 8 to 6: PED = +50 / -25 = -2 (elastic) and revenue rises to 360.
Price falls from 4 to 2: % change in price = -50%, % change in quantity = +25%, so PED = -0.5 (inelastic) and revenue falls from 320 to 200.

Revenue is greatest at a price of 6, where demand is about unitary. Cutting price below 6 lowers revenue.

In a diagram, revenue is the rectangle under the demand curve: height equals the price and width equals the quantity. After a price cut on elastic demand, the new rectangle is larger. After a price cut on inelastic demand, the new rectangle is smaller.

## 2.7.5 Why PED matters

**Consumers.** If demand is inelastic, a price rise leaves buyers paying more in total for almost the same amount, so spending on necessities takes a larger share of their budget. If demand is elastic, buyers can protect themselves by switching, and competition between sellers helps them.

**Producers.** Firms use PED to set prices and forecast revenue.
- Inelastic demand: raising price increases revenue, and costs may fall because less is sold.
- Elastic demand: cutting price (or running a sale) increases revenue. Raising price loses revenue.
- Firms try to make demand less elastic, for example by building brand loyalty.

**Government.** Governments use PED when they set indirect taxes.

**Worked example 4.** Country A (invented) imposes a tax on a good, and the price rises from 5.00 to 5.50 (+10%). The tax is 0.50 per unit and sales before the tax are 8,000.
- If PED = -0.3: quantity falls 3%, to 7,760. Tax revenue = 7,760 x 0.50 = **3,880**.
- If PED = -2.0: quantity falls 20%, to 6,400. Tax revenue = 6,400 x 0.50 = **3,200**.

A tax raises more revenue on inelastic demand, but it also cuts consumption less. If the aim is to discourage a harmful product, inelastic demand makes the tax a weak tool, and the government may need other measures too, such as information campaigns or regulation.

## 2.8.1 What PES measures

Price elasticity of supply is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. Supply curves slope upward, so PES is positive.

## 2.8.2 Calculating and interpreting PES

```
PES = % change in quantity supplied / % change in price
```

**Worked example 5 (inelastic).** A tomato farm (invented) sees price rise from 1.50 to 1.80 a kilo (+20%). Output offered rises from 4,000 to 4,600 kilos (+15%). PES = 15 / 20 = **0.75**, so supply is inelastic.

**Worked example 6 (elastic).** A pottery workshop (invented) sees price rise from 200 to 220 (+10%). It raises output from 50 to 65 vases a month (+30%). PES = 30 / 10 = **3.0**, so supply is elastic.

**Using PES backwards.** If PES = 0.4 and price rises 25%, quantity supplied rises 0.4 x 25 = 10%. From 600 units that is 660.

### Diagrams

```
Perfectly inelastic   Perfectly elastic   Unitary
P |      |            P |                  P |        /
  |      |              |__________          |      /
  |      |              |                    |    /
  +------ Q             +------------ Q      +/---------- Q
 vertical (PES = 0)     horizontal          straight line through
                        (PES = infinity)    the origin (PES = 1)
```

- A vertical supply curve means PES is 0 (a fixed stock).
- A horizontal supply curve means PES is infinite.
- A straight supply curve that starts on the price axis is elastic. One that starts on the quantity axis is inelastic. One through the origin has PES of 1 everywhere.

## 2.8.3 Determinants of PES

1. **Spare capacity.** A firm with idle machines and workers can raise output at once.
2. **Stocks of finished goods.** A firm holding stock can release it when price rises. A good that cannot be stored (fresh fruit) has low PES.
3. **Time.** Supply is more elastic in the long run, because firms can build capacity, hire and train.
4. **Ease of switching factors of production.** Mobile inputs (workers with general skills, flexible machinery) let a firm move into the more profitable good.
5. **Supply of raw materials.** If inputs are in short supply or take a long time to grow, supply is inelastic.
6. **Length of the production process.** Crops that take a season to grow cannot respond within it.

## 2.8.4 Why PES matters

**Worked example 7.** Demand in two markets (invented) starts at the pattern quantity = 200 - 10 x price, so the starting price in each is 10 and quantity 100. Demand then rises to quantity = 230 - 10 x price. In Market X supply is inelastic (quantity = 100 + 2 x (price - 10)). In Market Y supply is elastic (quantity = 100 + 20 x (price - 10)).

| | New price | New quantity | % change price | % change quantity | PES |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Market X | 12.5 | 105 | +25% | +5% | 0.2 |
| Market Y | 11.0 | 120 | +10% | +20% | 2.0 |

The same rise in demand gives a large price rise and small quantity rise in X, and the reverse in Y.

**Consumers.** Where supply is inelastic, a rise in demand pushes prices up sharply for little extra output. Where supply is elastic, prices stay steadier.

**Producers.** Firms with elastic supply can take advantage of a price rise by expanding output quickly. Those with inelastic supply cannot, and their rivals may win the extra sales. Producers use PES when planning spare capacity and stock.

**Government.** Governments need PES when they want more output. A subsidy to a sector with inelastic supply raises output only slightly and lowers price only slightly, so much of the money may simply raise producers' profit. A government that wants to ease a shortage of a good with inelastic supply, such as housing, may need measures that increase supply over time, because a short-run price signal will not work quickly.

## Common errors

- Putting the formula upside down (price over quantity).
- Using the new value as the base for a percentage: use the starting value.
- Saying a steep demand curve means demand is "strong". It means inelastic.
- Saying revenue falls when price rises without checking whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
- Writing "elastic" when the figure is exactly 1: that is unitary.
- Using a PES determinant in a PED answer, or the reverse.
- Describing the significance of PED without saying who is affected, how and why.
