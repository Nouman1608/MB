These questions cover section 2.5, Competition, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All firms, markets and figures are invented. Revise first with the [2.5 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-competition-ocr-gcse-economics/) or the [2.5 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-competition-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/). ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by competition between producers. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** reasons why producers compete. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** methods of non-price competition a hairdresser could use. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** Define a monopoly. **[1]**
**(b)** State **one** barrier to entry that could protect a monopoly. **[1]**

**5.** A market has total annual sales of £150 million. The four largest firms sell £45m, £30m, £20m and £15m.

**(a)** Calculate the market share of the largest firm. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why firms in an oligopoly often avoid competing on price. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Two bakeries in a village each sell 400 loaves a week at £2.50. Each loaf costs £1.50 to make. Bakery X cuts its price to £2.20 and wins 100 customers from Bakery Y.

**(a)** Calculate each bakery's weekly profit before the price cut. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate each bakery's weekly profit after X's price cut. **[2]**
**(c)** Bakery Y then matches the £2.20 price and customers split evenly again. Calculate each bakery's profit and the total weekly saving for consumers. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why Bakery X's price cut may have been a mistake. **[2]**

**8.** A discount supermarket opens in a town that already has two large supermarkets. Analyse how this is likely to affect prices in the town. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways a monopoly differs from a competitive market. **[4]**

**10.** In an invented country, broadband firm A has 38% of the market, firm B 27% and firm C 20%. Many small firms share the rest.

**(a)** Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain whether this market is an oligopoly. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why prices in this market might be higher than in a competitive market. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how a patent creates a barrier to entry, and **one** way this could still benefit consumers. **[4]**

**12.** Many new online clothing retailers have entered the market in recent years. Evaluate the impact of this competition on producers and consumers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Competition is rivalry between firms selling to the same customers [1], each trying to win sales through lower prices or better products [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention the customers firms compete for and how they compete.

**2.** Any two of: to make a profit [1]; to gain market share [1]; to survive; to grow. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "To beat rivals" restates the question; name the aim behind it.

**3.** Any two of: better customer service [1]; advertising on social media [1]; a more convenient location or longer opening hours; higher-quality products; a more attractive salon. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Discounts or lower prices are price competition and do not score here.

**4. (a)** A market with one producer, or one firm so dominant it faces little competition [1].
**(b)** Patents, licences, very high set-up costs, control of a key resource, brand loyalty or economies of scale (any one) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A barrier must explain why rivals cannot enter, not just why the firm is large.

**5. (a)** 45 ÷ 150 × 100 = **30%** [1].
**(b)** 45 + 30 + 20 + 15 = £110m [1]; 110 ÷ 150 × 100 = **73.3%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the whole market's sales, not by the four firms' total.

**6.** Firms are interdependent, so a price cut is likely to be matched by rivals [1], leaving each with the same share of the market but lower profit, so they compete through branding or advertising instead [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The idea of rivals matching the cut is essential.

**7. (a)** Each: (2.50 − 1.50) × 400 = **£400** [1].
**(b)** X: 500 × (2.20 − 1.50) = **£350** [1]. Y: 300 × (2.50 − 1.50) = **£300** [1].
**(c)** Each: 400 × (2.20 − 1.50) = **£280** [1]. Consumers save 0.30 × 800 = **£240** a week [1].
**(d)** Once Y matched the cut, X lost the extra customers [1] and its profit fell from £400 to £280, so both bakeries ended up worse off [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Profit per loaf falls from £1.00 to £0.70 with any cut, so sales must rise a lot for profit to rise.

**8.** The discount supermarket offers lower prices [1], so some customers switch away from the existing supermarkets [1]. To keep their customers, the existing firms are likely to cut some prices or run more offers [1], so the general level of grocery prices in the town falls [1]. The new store also adds to supply in the local market, adding to the downward pressure on prices [1]. However, the existing supermarkets may instead compete on quality, range or service, so some prices may not fall much [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis is a chain, and the last mark comes from a reasoned limit to the effect.

**9.** A monopoly has one dominant firm, whereas a competitive market has many firms [1], so the monopoly has much more control over price [1]. Barriers to entry in a monopoly are high [1], whereas in a competitive market new firms can enter easily, which keeps prices down [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each difference earns two marks when both sides of the comparison are given.

**10. (a)** 38 + 27 + 20 = **85%** [1].
**(b)** Yes: three firms hold 85% of the market [1], so a few large firms dominate, which is the meaning of an oligopoly [1].
**(c)** With few rivals and high barriers to entry, the large firms face less pressure from competition [1], so they can keep prices above the level a market with many firms would reach [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figure from (a) to support the judgement in (b).

**11.** A patent gives a firm the legal right to be the only producer of an invention for a period [1], so other firms cannot copy it and enter the market [1]. However, the profit the firm expects from this protection encourages it to spend on research [1], so consumers benefit from new products, such as medicines, that might otherwise not exist [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The benefit must be linked to the patent, not just to "big firms".

**12.** Consumers: more retailers mean more choice [1] and pressure on prices, as firms undercut each other [1]; online firms also compete on delivery speed and easy returns, raising service quality [1]. Producers: existing retailers face lower profit margins [1], and some high-street shops may close, with job losses [1]; efficient firms that cut costs may grow and gain market share [1]. Judgement: consumers are likely to be the main winners [1], but the effect on producers depends on whether they can cut costs or offer something rivals cannot, such as a strong brand [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the context: online, delivery, high-street closures. A general answer that could fit any market caps the marks for application.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an oligopoly a monopoly.
- Giving price cuts as non-price competition.
- Dividing by the wrong total in market-share calculations.
- Price-cutting answers that forget rivals can respond.
- Evaluations that cover consumers only.
