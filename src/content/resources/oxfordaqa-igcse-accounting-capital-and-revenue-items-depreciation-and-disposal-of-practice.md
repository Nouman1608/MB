## Questions

**1.** Valdane Dairy buys a refrigerated lorry, pays for diesel for it, and pays for a cold-storage unit to be fitted to the lorry body.

**(a)** State whether each payment is capital expenditure or revenue expenditure. **[3]**
**(b)** The accountant wrongly adds 1,800 of diesel to the cost of the lorry. State the effect on profit and on the value of the lorry. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each receipt as capital income or revenue income: (i) the owner pays 12,000 into the business bank account; (ii) milk sales; (iii) sale of an old delivery trolley for 450; (iv) rent of 3,000 for letting a spare garage. **[4]**

**3.** Explain two reasons why non-current assets depreciate. **[4]**

**4.** Fordyce Packaging buys a machine for 27,500. It expects a life of 5 years and a residual value of 2,500, and uses the straight line method.

**(a)** Calculate the annual depreciation charge. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the provision for depreciation and the net book value at the end of Year 3. **[2]**

**5.** Equipment costs 40,000 and is depreciated at 15% a year using the reducing balance method. Calculate the depreciation charge for each of Years 1, 2 and 3, and the net book value at the end of Year 3. **[6]**

**6.** Fixtures cost 12,000 and are depreciated at 10% of cost a year. At the start of Year 2 the provision for depreciation is 1,200.

**(a)** Prepare the provision for depreciation account for Year 2. **[4]**
**(b)** Show the fixtures in the Year 2 statement of financial position, with cost, provision and net book value. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why depreciation is described as a non-cash expense, and state its effect on the value of an asset. **[4]**

**8.** A vehicle that cost 22,000 has a provision for depreciation of 14,000. It is sold for 6,200 paid into the bank. Prepare the disposal account and say where the balance is transferred. **[6]**

**9.** A machine cost 16,000 on 1 January Year 1. It is depreciated at 12.5% of cost a year, with depreciation charged for each month of ownership. On 31 March Year 4 it is sold for 10,100.

**(a)** Calculate the provision for depreciation at 31 December Year 3. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the depreciation for Year 4 up to the date of sale. **[1]**
**(c)** Prepare the disposal account. **[3]**
**(d)** State the net book value at the date of sale and use it to confirm the profit or loss. **[1]**

**10.** Fordyce Packaging bought a machine for 40,000 on 1 January Year 1 and depreciates it at 20% reducing balance, charging a full year in the year of purchase and a full year in the year of sale. The machine is sold on 31 December Year 3 for 17,000. The profit before depreciation and the disposal result is 52,000.

**(a)** Calculate the provision for depreciation at the start of Year 3. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the depreciation charge for Year 3. **[2]**
**(c)** Prepare the disposal account. **[4]**
**(d)** Calculate the profit for Year 3 after depreciation and the disposal result. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Lorry: capital expenditure [1]. Diesel: revenue expenditure [1]. Cold-storage unit: capital expenditure, as it adds to the asset and brings it into use [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Profit is overstated by 1,800 because an expense has not been charged [1]. The lorry is overstated by 1,800 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit when each classification is stated outright; a reason alone, without the word capital or revenue, often leaves the point unmade.

**2.** (i) Capital income [1]. (ii) Revenue income [1]. (iii) Capital income, as it is the sale of a non-current asset [1]. (iv) Revenue income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Sale proceeds of a non-current asset are a common trap: calling them revenue income because money was received loses the mark.

**3.** Wear and tear: use physically damages the asset, so it earns less and eventually fails [1] [1]. Obsolescence: newer technology makes the asset out of date even if it still works [1] [1]. (Passage of time is also acceptable.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain questions usually reward a reason plus a link to the asset; "it gets old" with no cause is thin.

**4. (a)** (27,500 − 2,500) ÷ 5 [1] = **5,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Provision 3 × 5,000 = **15,000** [1]. NBV 27,500 − 15,000 = **12,500** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the residual value being deducted makes your method visible even if arithmetic slips.

**5.** Year 1: 15% × 40,000 = **6,000**, NBV 34,000 [1]. Year 2: 15% × 34,000 = **5,100** [1], NBV 28,900 [1]. Year 3: 15% × 28,900 = **4,335** [1], NBV **24,565** [1]. Percentage applied to the net book value each year, not to cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing out each opening NBV shows the method, so one early slip need not cost every later figure.

**6. (a)**

| Provision for depreciation | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Year 2 balance c/d | 2,400 | |
| Year 2 balance b/d | | 1,200 |
| Year 2 depreciation (10% × 12,000) | | 1,200 |
| Totals | 2,400 | 2,400 |

Opening balance b/d on the credit side [1]. Charge of 1,200 on the credit side [1]. Closing balance 2,400 [1]. Totals agree [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Fixtures cost 12,000 [1]; provision 2,400, net book value **9,600** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The provision is a credit balance carried forward; marks are lost when the closing balance is shown on the wrong side.

**7.** No cash is paid out when depreciation is charged; the cash left when the asset was bought [1]. It is an expense, so it reduces profit [1]. Each charge adds to the provision [1], so the net book value of the asset falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers mention both what does not happen (no bank payment) and what does (lower profit and lower asset value).

**8.**

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vehicle at cost | 22,000 | Provision for depreciation | 14,000 |
| | | Bank | 6,200 |
| | | Loss on disposal | 1,800 |
| Total | 22,000 | Total | 22,000 |

Cost on the debit side [1]. Provision on the credit side [1]. Bank on the credit side [1]. Balancing loss of **1,800** on the credit side [1]. Totals of 22,000 [1]. Transferred to the income statement [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cost and provision go in separately; entering only the net book value of 8,000 changes the layout and risks losing the credit for each.

**9. (a)** Annual charge 12.5% × 16,000 = 2,000 [1]. Three years: **6,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 2,000 × 3/12 = **500** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Provision at sale 6,500.

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine at cost | 16,000 | Provision for depreciation | 6,500 |
| Profit on disposal | 600 | Bank | 10,100 |
| Total | 16,600 | Total | 16,600 |

Cost 16,000 on the debit side [1]. Provision 6,500 and bank 10,100 on the credit side [1]. Profit of **600** on the debit side [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** NBV = 16,000 − 6,500 = 9,500; proceeds of 10,100 are 600 more, which is a profit of **600** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part-year depreciation needs to be in the provision before the disposal account is opened; leaving out the 500 changes the profit.

**10. (a)** Year 1: 20% × 40,000 = 8,000 [1]. Year 2: 20% × 32,000 = 6,400 [1]. Provision **14,400** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** NBV 25,600 [1]. 20% × 25,600 = **5,120** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Provision at sale 14,400 + 5,120 = 19,520.

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine at cost | 40,000 | Provision for depreciation | 19,520 |
| | | Bank | 17,000 |
| | | Loss on disposal | 3,480 |
| Total | 40,000 | Total | 40,000 |

Cost on the debit side [1]. Provision of 19,520 on the credit side [1]. Bank 17,000 on the credit side [1]. Loss of **3,480** [1]. **[4]**
**(d)** 52,000 − 5,120 − 3,480 = **43,400** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In a multi-part question each part feeds the next, so show every intermediate figure; a clear route lets a slip early on be separated from correct method later.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a repair or fuel cost capital because the amount is large.
- Treating the proceeds from selling a non-current asset as sales or revenue income.
- Giving "wear and tear" with no explanation of what it does to the asset.
- Leaving the residual value out of the straight line calculation.
- Using cost instead of the opening net book value in the reducing balance calculation.
- Putting the provision balance on the debit side of the provision account.
- Entering the net book value rather than cost and provision in the disposal account.
- Showing a loss on the debit side, or a profit on the credit side.
- Forgetting part-year depreciation before a disposal when the question asks for it.
