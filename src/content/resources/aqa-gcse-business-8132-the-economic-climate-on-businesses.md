This guide goes deeper into section 3.2.3 The economic climate on businesses of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. It sits in Topic 2, Influences on business, which can be assessed on either written paper. The combined page, [Ethical and environmental considerations and the economic climate](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-ethical-and-environmental-considerations-and-the-economic-climate/), sets out the basics. This page concentrates on the skill examiners test most here: explaining why the same economic change helps one business and hurts another.

## What the specification asks for

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Interest rates: "how fluctuating interest rates can affect businesses that rely on overdrafts and loans for finance" and "how fluctuating interest rates can affect consumer and business spending" | "demonstrate and understand how businesses might be affected by changes in the rate of interest" |
| Level of employment | "identify how and why businesses might be affected by changes in levels of employment" |
| Consumer spending | "discuss how demand for products and services may change as incomes fluctuate" |

The specification adds: "Economic theory relating to how/why interest rates change is not required." You need effects, not causes.

## The economic climate as an external influence

The economic climate is the state of the economy in which a business operates: how expensive borrowing is, how many people have jobs, and how much money households have to spend. A business cannot control any of these, so it must respond. Answers score well when they say which functional area responds and how:

- **Finance:** interest costs, cash flow, whether to borrow.
- **Marketing:** prices, promotions, product range.
- **Operations:** how much to produce and how much stock to hold.
- **Human resources:** how many staff to employ and what to pay.

## Interest rates

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money, shown as a percentage of the amount borrowed, and the reward for saving. "Fluctuating" means it can rise or fall.

### Effect on businesses that rely on overdrafts and loans

Overdrafts and many business loans charge interest at a rate that can change. When rates rise:

- interest payments increase, so profit falls;
- more cash goes out each month, which can squeeze cash flow;
- borrowing for new equipment or premises becomes less attractive, so expansion may be delayed.

When rates fall, the reverse happens. The more a business borrows, the more it is affected. A business funded mainly by the owner's own money or retained profit feels the change far less.

### Effect on consumer spending

Higher interest rates affect households in three ways:

1. Loans and credit cards cost more, so people buy fewer items on credit, such as cars, furniture and kitchens.
2. Households with mortgages on variable rates pay more each month, leaving less to spend.
3. Saving pays more, so some people save rather than spend.

The businesses hit hardest sell expensive items that customers often buy on credit. Businesses selling everyday essentials are affected less.

### Effect on business spending

Businesses also spend less when rates rise. They delay buying machinery or vehicles, and they reduce orders from suppliers. So a manufacturer of equipment for other businesses, such as delivery vans or printing machines, can see orders fall even if it borrows nothing itself.

### Who gains from higher rates?

A business with cash in the bank earns more interest. A business with no borrowing and customers who pay in cash is protected. This is why "higher interest rates are bad for business" is too general to earn many marks.

## Worked example 1: two businesses, one rate rise

Figures invented. The interest rate on business borrowing rises by 2 percentage points.

**Draycott Motors** sells new cars. It has a £400,000 loan to fund the cars on its forecourt. Most customers buy using finance.

- Extra interest = 2% of £400,000 = £8,000 a year.
- Customers face higher repayments on car finance, so some delay buying. If sales fall from 300 to 270 cars and each earns £1,200 profit before interest, profit falls by 30 × £1,200 = £36,000.
- Total hit to profit: £8,000 + £36,000 = £44,000.

**Thornbury Laundrette** has no borrowing and £20,000 in savings. Customers pay cash for a basic service.

- Extra interest earned = 2% of £20,000 = £400 a year.
- Demand for laundry changes little, because it is an essential service.

The same change cost one business £44,000 and gave the other £400. That contrast is what an evaluation should show.

## Level of employment

The level of employment is the number of people in work. When it changes, businesses are affected through demand and through labour.

| | When employment rises | When employment falls (unemployment rises) |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | More people earning, so spending rises | Fewer people earning, so spending falls, especially on non-essentials |
| Recruitment | Harder to find staff; more competition for skilled workers | More applicants for each vacancy |
| Wages | Pressure to raise pay to attract and keep staff | Less pressure on pay |
| Staff turnover | Staff may leave for better offers | Staff more likely to stay |

Be precise about "how and why". How: a fall in sales or a change in labour costs. Why: because income from wages has changed, or because the number of available workers has changed. A business may gain on one side and lose on the other. A restaurant in an area of rising unemployment may find recruitment easier but lose diners.

## Consumer spending and changing incomes

When incomes rise, households can spend more. When incomes fall, they cut back. But demand does not change equally for every product.

- **Luxuries and expensive items** (holidays, restaurant meals, designer clothes): demand changes a lot as incomes change, because people can postpone or cut them.
- **Essentials** (basic food, energy, toiletries): demand changes little, because people still need them.
- **Budget alternatives** (value ranges, discount stores, repair services): demand may rise when incomes fall, as people trade down from dearer options.

To discuss, compare the type of product with the change in income, then suggest a response: launch a cheaper range, run promotions, cut stock, or move to premium lines when incomes rise.

## Worked example 2: falling incomes

Figures invented. Oakhurst Garden Rooms sells garden offices at £14,000. It sold 90 in a year. Incomes in its region fall and sales drop by 20%.

- Lost sales = 20% of 90 = 18 garden rooms.
- Lost revenue = 18 × £14,000 = £252,000.

A shed retailer nearby selling £600 sheds might see little change, or even a rise, as some customers choose a cheaper option. Oakhurst could respond by offering a smaller model at a lower price or payment by instalments, although instalments would be less attractive if interest rates were also high.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name the economic change and the business affected.
- **Explain:** change → effect on costs or demand → effect on profit, applied to the business.
- **Analyse:** follow the chain through more than one link, for example rates rise → customers spend less on credit → fewer car sales → stock builds up → more cash tied up.
- **Discuss or evaluate:** weigh the size of the effect for this business. Ask: how much does it borrow, what does it sell, who are its customers? Then decide.

## Common mistakes

- Explaining why interest rates change. The specification says this is not required.
- Saying every business suffers when rates rise. Savers and cash businesses can gain.
- Confusing employment levels with the business's own staff numbers.
- Treating all products the same when incomes fall.
- Forgetting the labour-market side of employment changes.
- Writing about the economy in general without applying it to the business in the case.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know why interest rates change for AQA GCSE Business?**
No. The specification states that economic theory about how or why interest rates change is not required. Focus on the effects on businesses.

**Which businesses are most affected by a rise in interest rates?**
Those with large overdrafts or loans, and those selling expensive items that customers buy on credit, such as cars and furniture.

**Can rising unemployment ever help a business?**
Yes. It can make recruitment easier and reduce pressure on wages, and businesses selling budget alternatives may see demand rise.
