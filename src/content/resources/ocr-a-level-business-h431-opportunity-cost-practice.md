## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by opportunity cost. **[2]**

**2.** Kirkby Print has £40,000 to spend. A website upgrade would add £6,800 of profit a year, a new guillotine £6,200, staff training £5,900, and repaying a loan charging 9% interest would save £3,600 a year. Kirkby chooses the website upgrade. Identify the opportunity cost of this decision. **[2]**

**3.** Which **one** of the following should **not** be treated as an opportunity cost when deciding whether to continue a project?

A: Interest that could be earned on the cash the project needs.

B: £20,000 already spent on research that cannot be recovered.

C: Contribution from other work the staff could do instead.

D: The rent that could be earned by letting the space to another firm. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why the opportunity cost of using spare warehouse space is likely to be low. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** opportunity cost of a managing director spending three months leading a new product launch. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Selwood Ceramics fires pottery in a kiln that is available for 3,000 hours a year. All 3,000 hours are used to make vases. Each vase takes 5 kiln hours, sells for £120 and has variable costs of £45. A lighting company offers an order for 250 lamp bases at £90 each. Each lamp base has variable costs of £40 and takes 6 kiln hours.

**(a)** Calculate the total contribution from the lamp-base order. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of accepting the order. **[3]**

**(c)** Advise Selwood Ceramics whether to accept the order. **[6]**

**7.** Marcus owns Linden Cycle Café, a café and bicycle repair shop. The accounts show a profit of £31,000 for the year. Marcus works full time in the business and draws no salary beyond the profit. He has £120,000 of his own money invested in it. He could earn £29,000 a year as a café manager elsewhere, and a savings account would pay 4.5% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the profit after taking account of these opportunity costs. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether Marcus should continue running Linden Cycle Café. **[8]**

**8.** Harwell Engineering has £500,000 in cash. The directors are choosing between spending it on automated machinery, which would cut costs by £90,000 a year, and keeping it as a reserve because demand in its industry is uncertain. Analyse the impact of opportunity cost on this decision. **[6]**

**9.** Corbin Theatre Company has spent £60,000 on the set for a play that is selling poorly. If the play continues for the rest of the season, it is expected to earn a contribution of £15,000. If it closes now, the theatre could host a touring comedy show earning a contribution of £25,000. The set cannot be sold or reused.

**(a)** Identify the opportunity cost of continuing the play. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the £60,000 spent on the set should not affect the decision. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate the usefulness of opportunity cost to a business making a strategic decision, such as whether to open in a new country. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The benefit of the next best alternative that is given up [1] when a business chooses one use of a scarce resource such as money, time or capacity [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The next best alternative is the new guillotine [1], so the opportunity cost is **£6,200 of profit a year** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The space is not being used for anything else, so little or no benefit is given up by using it [1]; the opportunity cost would rise only if another use, such as letting it to another firm, became available [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The time the managing director would otherwise spend on other strategic work, such as negotiating with major customers or planning expansion [1], so those decisions are delayed and income that could have been won is lost [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Contribution per lamp base 90 − 40 = £50 [1]; 250 × 50 = **£12,500** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Hours needed 250 × 6 = 1,500 [1]; vases displaced 1,500 ÷ 5 = 300 [1]; contribution per vase 120 − 45 = £75, so opportunity cost 300 × 75 = **£22,500** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** The order earns £12,500 but costs £22,500 of contribution forgone [1], so accepting would reduce profit by **£10,000** [1]. On the figures, Selwood should decline [1]. Factors that could change this: the lighting company might offer regular, larger orders in future, which would matter if vase demand is falling [1]; Selwood might be able to buy or rent a second kiln, so the order would not displace vases [1]. Judgement: decline unless the kiln has spare hours or the lighting company offers a higher price; the price would need to rise to at least £130 a lamp base, where contribution of £90 × 250 = £22,500 matches what is lost [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Return forgone 0.045 × 120,000 = £5,400 [1]; 31,000 − 29,000 − 5,400 = **−£3,400** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** On the accounts, the café is profitable, earning £31,000 [1]. But once Marcus's forgone salary and interest are counted, he is £3,400 a year worse off than if he sold up, invested and took a job [1].
Reasons to continue: the figures for the job and interest are estimates and may not be available [1]; there are non-financial benefits such as independence and doing work he enjoys [1]; if the business is growing, future profit may exceed the opportunity costs [1]; the business itself may be worth more than £120,000 if sold as a going concern later [1].
Reasons to stop: if profit is flat, he is consistently giving up income and taking on risk for less reward [1].
Judgement: a gap of £3,400 is small, so Marcus should continue if the business is growing or he values running it; he should review the decision if profit stays flat for another year [1]. **[8]**

**8.** If Harwell buys the machinery, the opportunity cost is the security of holding £500,000 in reserve, which could protect the business if demand falls [1]; if it holds the cash, the opportunity cost is the £90,000 a year of cost savings given up [1]. Holding cash may look costless because nothing is spent, but recognising the £90,000 forgone shows it is not [1]. The machinery would pay back in about 5.6 years (500,000 ÷ 90,000) [1], so the cash would be committed for a long period in an uncertain market [1]. Opportunity cost therefore turns the decision into a comparison of a known saving against the value of flexibility, which depends on how likely a fall in demand is [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** The comedy show, which would earn a contribution of **£25,000** [1], so continuing the play gives up £10,000 more than it earns [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The £60,000 is a sunk cost: it has already been spent [1] and cannot be recovered whichever option is chosen [1], so only future costs and benefits, £15,000 against £25,000, should affect the decision [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Usefulness: it forces managers to compare the new country with the alternatives, such as expanding at home, rather than judging it alone [1]; if a home expansion would earn more for the same investment, the overseas move is a poor choice even if profitable [1]. It reminds them that management time spent on the overseas venture is taken from the existing business [1], which may suffer [1].
Limitations: the value of the next best alternative is an estimate, especially for long-term strategic choices [1]; a strategic decision may open future options that are hard to value, such as access to a fast-growing market [1]; non-financial benefits, such as spreading risk across countries, are difficult to compare with profit [1].
Evaluation: opportunity cost is most useful when the alternatives are clear and can be valued, as with a choice between two investments [1]. Judgement: for a strategic decision it is a valuable way of framing the choice, but it should be used alongside investment appraisal and qualitative factors rather than as the deciding figure [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding all the rejected alternatives together.
- Treating money already spent as relevant to a decision.
- Accepting an order because its contribution is positive without checking spare capacity.
- Forgetting the owner's own time and capital when judging whether a small business is worth running.
- Naming the alternative without giving its value.
