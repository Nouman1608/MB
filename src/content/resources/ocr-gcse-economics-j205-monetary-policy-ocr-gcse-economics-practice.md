These questions cover section 3.6 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Monetary policy. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [monetary policy study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-monetary-policy-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [monetary policy revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-monetary-policy-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by monetary policy. **[1]**

**2.** Name the institution that sets Bank Rate in the UK. **[1]**

**3.** Explain how a cut in interest rates is likely to affect borrowing by households. **[2]**

**4.** A household has a variable-rate mortgage of £135,000. Its rate falls from 4.25% to 3.75%. Calculate the change in interest paid per month. **[2]**

**5.** Which one of the following is the most likely result of a rise in interest rates? **[1]**

- A. Firms invest more in new equipment.
- B. Households save more.
- C. Demand-pull inflation increases.
- D. Households borrow more to buy cars.

**6.** Explain one reason why a central bank might raise interest rates. **[2]**

**7.** A family borrows £18,000 for one year to buy a car. Calculate the extra interest they pay if the rate is 9.0% rather than 6.5%. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Delta Ltd is considering three projects, each funded by borrowing.

| Project | Cost | Expected profit per year (before interest) |
|---|---|---|
| A | £200,000 | £13,000 |
| B | £120,000 | £6,600 |
| C | £60,000 | £4,500 |

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage return on each project. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate Delta's total investment if the interest rate is 5%, and if it rises to 7%. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what your answer to (b) shows about monetary policy. **[1]**

**9.** In Country Z, the central bank cuts its rate from 4.0% to 2.5%. Total mortgage debt is £1,200bn, of which 40% is on variable rates.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly fall in interest paid by variable-rate borrowers if the cut is passed on in full. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse how the cut could affect economic growth and employment. **[3]**

**10.** Rosa, who is retired, has savings of £15,000. Her savings rate falls from 3.5% to 2.0%, while inflation is 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in her yearly interest income. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate her real return at the new rate, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why Rosa may cut her spending. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate the effects of a rise in interest rates on consumer spending and saving. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether monetary policy is effective in achieving price stability. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The use of interest rates (and sometimes the money supply) by the central bank to influence the economy and meet objectives such as low inflation [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The Bank of England (its Monetary Policy Committee) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Loans and mortgages become cheaper to repay [1], so households are more willing to borrow to buy homes, cars and other large items, and borrowing rises [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Change = 135,000 × 0.005 = £675 a year [1]; 675 ÷ 12 = **£56.25 less per month** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. Saving becomes more rewarding. The other options describe effects of a rate cut. **[1]**

**6.** Inflation is above the target because demand is growing too fast [1]. A rise makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so spending falls and demand-pull pressure on prices eases [1]. **[2]**

**7.** 18,000 × 0.09 = £1,620; 18,000 × 0.065 = £1,170 [1]. Extra = **£450** [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** A: 13,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **6.5%** [1]. B: 6,600 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **5.5%** [1]. C: 4,500 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **7.5%** [1].
**(b)** At 5%, all three are profitable: **£380,000** [1]. At 7%, only C is: **£60,000** [1].
**(c)** Higher interest rates reduce investment, because fewer projects earn more than the cost of borrowing [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Variable debt = 40% × £1,200bn = £480bn [1]. Fall in interest = 480 × 0.015 = **£7.2bn a year** [1].
**(b)** Variable-rate borrowers have £7.2bn more a year to spend, and cheaper credit encourages new borrowing [1]. Higher consumer spending and investment raise total demand, so firms increase output and growth rises [1]. Firms need more workers to produce the extra output, so employment rises and unemployment falls [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** 15,000 × 0.035 = £525; 15,000 × 0.02 = £300; fall = **£225** [1].
**(b)** 1.02 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **−1.0%** [1].
**(c)** Her income from savings has fallen and is now losing purchasing power, so she has less to spend [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Spending: borrowers on variable rates pay more interest, so their disposable income falls [1] and they cut spending on non-essentials [1]. Credit for cars, furniture and holidays becomes dearer, so credit-funded spending falls [1]. Saving: savers earn more, so saving becomes more attractive and households may save a larger share of income [1]. Against: households on fixed-rate mortgages are unaffected until their deal ends [1], and savers with no debts receive more interest and may spend more [1]. Banks may raise savings rates by less than loan rates, weakening the saving effect [1]. Judgement: spending is likely to fall and saving to rise, but the effect will be gradual and depends on how much debt is on variable rates and how confident households are [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For: higher rates reduce borrowing and spending, so demand-pull inflation falls [1]. The central bank can act quickly and in small steps, and its independence helps keep expectations of inflation low, which itself restrains pay claims and price rises [1]. Against: there are long time lags before rate changes affect prices [1]. When inflation is cost-push, from world energy or food prices, higher rates cannot remove the cause [1], and reducing demand enough to offset it may cause a recession and higher unemployment [1]. Judgement: monetary policy is effective against demand-pull inflation over time, but less effective against cost-push shocks, where the central bank may have to accept inflation above target for a while [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing monetary policy with fiscal policy.
- Saying the government sets interest rates.
- Treating every borrower as affected immediately.
- Jumping from "rates rise" to "inflation falls" without the steps.
- Forgetting that rate changes help one group and hurt another.
