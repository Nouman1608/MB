These notes cover **"bystanderism"**, a content point in the "social responsibility" topic of the psychology of human relationships option (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2019). The option as a whole is in the [human relationships revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-of-human-relationships-revision-notes/); the full explanations and an essay plan are in the [bystanderism study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-bystanderism/).

## Recall in one minute

- **Bystander effect:** an individual is less likely, or slower, to help when others are present.
- **Five-stage decision model:** notice, interpret, take responsibility, decide how, act.
- **Inhibiting processes:** pluralistic ignorance (stage 2), diffusion of responsibility (stage 3), audience inhibition (stage 5).
- **Cost-reward:** help when costs of helping are low and costs of not helping are high.
- **Social identity:** we help those we see as in-group members, and the in-group boundary can shift.
- **Real emergencies:** the effect is weaker in dangerous situations, and in real conflicts someone usually intervenes.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bystanderism | how witnesses behave when someone needs help |
| Bystander effect | lower or slower helping by an individual when other bystanders are present |
| Diffusion of responsibility | responsibility is spread among bystanders, so each feels less obliged to act |
| Pluralistic ignorance | each bystander reads others' calm as a sign that there is no emergency |
| Audience inhibition | fear of embarrassment or judgement when acting in front of others |
| Arousal: cost-reward model | distress creates arousal; bystanders weigh the costs of helping and not helping |
| Salient identity | the group membership most active in a person's mind at the moment |
| Meta-analysis | statistical combination of the results of many studies |

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Method | Finding | Process or point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Darley and Latané (1968) | participants heard a seizure over an intercom, believing they were alone or with others | alone, far more likely and faster to seek help | diffusion of responsibility |
| Latané and Darley (1968) | smoke entered a room while students filled in questionnaires, alone, with passive confederates or in groups of three | alone, most reported; with passive confederates, very few did | pluralistic ignorance |
| Latané and Rodin (1969) | a woman fell and cried out in the next room | alone helped most; with a passive confederate least; friends helped more than strangers | relationships between bystanders |
| Piliavin et al. (1969) | staged collapses on New York subway trains | frequent, fast help; less help for a victim who appeared drunk | cost-reward |
| Levine et al. (2005) | fallen jogger in a club, rival or plain shirt, after club or general fan identity was made salient | more help for in-group shirts; in-group widened when fan identity was salient | social identity |
| Fischer et al. (2011) | meta-analysis of bystander studies | effect reduced in dangerous emergencies | danger and arousal |
| Philpot et al. (2020) | CCTV of real conflicts in Lancaster, Amsterdam and Cape Town | someone intervened in nine of ten incidents; more bystanders, more likely someone intervened | real-world validity |

## Evaluation toolkit

- **Laboratory studies:** high control and replicable; artificial emergencies; deception and distress.
- **Field studies:** realism; no informed consent; less control over who is present.
- **Samples:** many classic studies used male American students; Levine et al. used male football fans.
- **Individual versus group:** individual helping falls with group size, but the chance that someone helps can rise.
- **Culture:** Philpot et al. found a similar pattern in three countries; earlier work was mostly American.
- **Alternative explanations:** the decision model, cost-reward and social identity can be combined rather than treated as rivals.

## Model paragraph (contrast)

> The decision model and the social identity account explain bystanderism differently. Latané and
> Darley's model treats bystanders as individuals making a series of judgements, with the number of
> other people affecting how they interpret the event and how responsible they feel. Levine et al.
> (2005), by contrast, show that what matters is the relationship between bystander and victim: the same
> fans helped a jogger in a rival club's shirt when their wider identity as football fans was salient.
> The decision model therefore explains why crowds can inhibit helping, while the social identity
> account explains why some crowds help.

## Exam traps

- **"The bystander effect explains..."** It is the finding; name the process.
- **Wrong process.** Smoke-filled room shows pluralistic ignorance; intercom shows diffusion of responsibility.
- **Ignoring ethics.** Deception, distress and lack of consent are central evaluation points.
- **One-sided.** Include Fischer et al. or Philpot et al. to qualify the classic findings.
- **Unsure figures.** State the direction of a result rather than guessing percentages.

## Self-test

1. Define the bystander effect.
2. Name the five stages of Latané and Darley's decision model.
3. Which process does the smoke-filled room study demonstrate, and at which stage?
4. What is diffusion of responsibility?
5. In Latané and Rodin (1969), who helped more: pairs of friends or pairs of strangers?
6. What are the two kinds of cost in the arousal: cost-reward model?
7. What did Levine et al. (2005) change between their two studies?
8. What does Levine et al. (2005) show about helping?
9. What did Fischer et al. (2011) find about dangerous emergencies?
10. In how many of the incidents studied by Philpot et al. (2020) did someone intervene?
11. How can individual helping fall while the chance of help rises in larger groups?
12. Give one ethical issue in bystander research.

**Answers**

1. An individual is less likely, or slower, to help when other bystanders are present.
2. Notice the event; interpret it as an emergency; take responsibility; decide how to help; act.
3. Pluralistic ignorance, at the interpretation stage.
4. Responsibility is shared among those present, so each person feels less personal obligation to act.
5. Pairs of friends.
6. The costs of helping and the costs of not helping.
7. Which identity was made salient: Manchester United supporter in the first, football fan in general in the second.
8. People help those they see as in-group members, and the in-group can be widened by changing which identity is salient.
9. The bystander effect was reduced.
10. About nine out of ten.
11. Each individual is less likely to act, but with more people present there are more potential helpers, so the chance that at least one acts can still rise.
12. For example: deception about the emergency; distress caused to participants; no informed consent in field studies.
