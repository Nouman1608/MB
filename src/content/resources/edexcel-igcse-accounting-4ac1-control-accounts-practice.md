## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain two purposes of control accounts. **[4]**

**2.** State whether each item is entered on the debit side or the credit side of a trade receivables control account, or is not entered at all.

**(a)** Returns inwards. **[1]**
**(b)** Cash sales. **[1]**
**(c)** A customer's dishonoured cheque. **[1]**
**(d)** Irrecoverable debts written off. **[1]**
**(e)** Increase in the provision for irrecoverable debts. **[1]**
**(f)** Credit sales. **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a contra (set-off) entry between the two control accounts. **[2]**

**4.** Customers owed 9,200 at the start of the month. Credit sales were 23,600, receipts from customers 21,450, discount allowed 380 and returns inwards 470. Calculate the trade receivables control account balance at the end of the month. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a provision for irrecoverable debts is not entered in the trade receivables control account. **[2]**

**8.** Hollins Pet Supplies' trade receivables control account shows a balance of 31,460. The total of the list of customer balances is 30,945. These errors are found:

1. The sales returns book was overcast by 90.
2. A sales invoice for 315 was posted to the customer's account as 135.
3. A customer's credit balance of 85 was included in the list as a debit balance.
4. A customer's balance of 595 was left off the list.

**(a)** Prepare a statement showing the corrected control account balance. **[2]**
**(b)** Prepare a statement showing the corrected total of the list. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how control accounts help a business to:

**(a)** prevent fraud **[2]**
**(b)** locate errors. **[2]**

**10.** For each error, state whether it affects the payables control account, the list of supplier balances, or neither.

**(a)** The purchases day book was undercast by 400. **[1]**
**(b)** A payment to R. Hale was debited to R. Hall's account. **[1]**
**(c)** A supplier's balance of 1,620 was copied onto the list as 1,260. **[1]**
**(d)** A credit purchase invoice was lost and never recorded. **[1]**

**11.** L. Marsh buys goods from the business on credit and also supplies it with goods on credit. At the month end Marsh owes the business 620 and the business owes Marsh 450. They agree to set off the amounts.

**(a)** State the entries in the two control accounts. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the amount Marsh still owes after the set-off. **[1]**

**12.** The owner of a small business with six credit customers says control accounts are a waste of time. Evaluate this view. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each explained. Checking accuracy [1]: the control account balance is compared with the total of the individual accounts, so agreement suggests the ledger is correct [1]. Fraud prevention [1]: a different person prepares the control account from the ledger clerk, so false entries are likely to be noticed [1]. Also acceptable: locating errors to one ledger; quick totals for the financial statements. **[4]**

**2.** (a) Credit [1]. (b) Not entered [1]. (c) Debit [1]. (d) Credit [1]. (e) Not entered [1]. (f) Debit [1]. **[6]**

**3.** When a person is both a customer and a supplier, the amount owed to them is set off against the amount they owe [1]; the receivables control account is credited and the payables control account debited with the smaller amount [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Debits 9,200 + 23,600 = 32,800 [1]. Credits 21,450 + 380 + 470 = 22,300 [1]. Balance = **10,500** debit [1]. **[3]**

**5.** A provision is an estimate of possible losses on the debts as a whole [1]; no individual customer's account is changed, and the control account must agree with the total of the individual accounts [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Balance per control account 31,460, add sales returns overcast 90 [1], corrected balance **31,550** [1].
**(b)** Total per list 30,945 [1]; add invoice understated 315 − 135 = 180 [1]; less credit balance listed as debit 2 × 85 = 170 [1]; add omitted balance 595; corrected total **31,550** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The returns book error affects only the control account, because individual returns were posted correctly from the credit notes.

**(b)** If the trial balance does not agree, comparing each control account with its ledger list shows which ledger holds the error [1], so only that ledger needs checking instead of every account [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Debit the trade payables control account with 450 [1]; credit the trade receivables control account with 450 [1].

**12.**
- With only six customers, the list of balances is short and easy to check, so the arithmetical check is less valuable [1].
- But a control account still gives the trade receivables figure quickly and supports separation of duties against fraud [1].
- If the business grows, setting up control accounts later is harder, and errors are already easier to locate with them [1].
- Conclusion: the view has some force for a very small business, but the low cost and fraud protection mean preparing a control account is still worthwhile [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Including cash sales, cash purchases or provisions.
- Dishonoured cheques on the credit side.
- Netting off opening debit and credit balances.
- Misplacing the closing balance on the smaller side of the account.
- Correcting the control account for an error in an individual account, or vice versa.
- Adjusting a wrong-side posting by the amount once instead of twice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where does a refund to a customer go in the receivables control account?**
On the debit side. The refund removes the customer's credit balance, so it increases what the customer owes back towards nil.

**Does a posting to the wrong customer's account affect the control account?**
No. Both accounts are in the receivables ledger, so the total of the list is unchanged and still agrees with the control account.
