These questions practise key questions 6.2 and 6.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251). Set A
covers patterns and explanations; Set B covers punishment and policing. Both follow the six-part
(a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for each Paper 2 question.

---

## Set A — Patterns and explanations (6.2)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *chivalry thesis* **[2]**
- (ii) *relative deprivation* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of crimes of the powerful. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why some ethnic minority groups may be over-represented in crime
statistics. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the culture of masculinity may lead to crime. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the labelling explanation of crime and deviance. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that strain theory is the best explanation of crime. **[14]**

## Set B — Dealing with crime (6.3)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *retribution* **[2]**
- (ii) *incapacitation* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of non-custodial sentences. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why prison may fail to reduce crime. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** strengths of community policing. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the effectiveness of digital surveillance in preventing crime. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that rehabilitation is the most effective aim of punishment. **[14]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** The idea that police, courts and other agencies treat female offenders more leniently
than men, so women's crime is under-recorded (Pollak, 1950) (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Feeling deprived in comparison with others, such as people in the same society or
those shown in the media, even if not in absolute poverty (2) (Lea and Young, 1984).

**(b) [2]** Any two: tax evasion by corporations; breaking health and safety laws; price fixing;
environmental pollution by companies; bribery or corruption by officials; state crimes.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each: institutional racism in policing; police targeting and stop
and search in minority areas; material and relative deprivation; stereotyping by police, courts and
media (Gilroy, 1982); marginalisation and lack of opportunity; status frustration.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each: violence used to prove toughness and gain respect; risk-taking
and excitement valued (Miller's focal concerns, 1958); gang membership as a source of masculine
status; pressure to be a provider may lead to property crime when legitimate work is unavailable;
misogyny linked to violence against women.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- Becker (1963): an act becomes deviant only when labelled so.
- Agents of control have the power to apply labels, often to less powerful groups.
- The label becomes a **master status**; the person may accept it, leading to a **self-fulfilling
  prophecy** and **deviant career**.
- Moral panics (Stanley Cohen, 1972) show labelling on a wider scale.
- Evaluation: explains why some groups are criminalised and how reactions amplify deviance; but
  does not explain the first deviant act, can be deterministic and may neglect victims.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*Strain theory's strengths:*
- Merton (1938): society stresses success but blocks legitimate means for some, leading to
  innovation — explains working-class property crime.
- Links crime to social structure, not individual wickedness.
- Developed by subcultural theory (A. Cohen, 1955).

*Weaknesses and alternatives:*
- Does not explain non-utilitarian crime such as vandalism or violence for thrills (Katz, 1988).
- Ignores crimes of the powerful (Box, 1983) — Marxists explain these better.
- Assumes everyone shares the same goals.
- Labelling theory explains how reactions shape statistics.
- Feminism explains gender differences that strain theory ignores.

*Conclusion:* strain theory explains some patterns well but is not the best single explanation.

## Answers — Set B

**(a)(i) [2]** Punishment as deserved payback, so that the offender suffers in proportion to the harm
caused (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Removing an offender's ability to commit further crime, for example by imprisonment
(2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: electronic tagging; curfews; restraining orders; probation; community service;
fines; police cautions.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each: prisoners learn criminal skills and contacts; prison labels
people, making it harder to find work after release; many prisons lack rehabilitation programmes;
family ties are broken; overcrowding and violence; deterrence is weak if offenders do not expect to
be caught.

**(d) [6]** Three strengths, 2 marks each: builds trust so that people report crime; local knowledge
helps prevent crime; officers can work with schools and youth groups; reduces tension compared with
military-style policing; improves the legitimacy of the police.

**(e) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Effective:* CCTV, phone data and facial recognition aid detection and deterrence; online
  monitoring detects cyber crime; evidence for prosecutions.
- *Limitations:* crime may move elsewhere (displacement); offenders hide faces or use encryption;
  privacy concerns and risk of misuse by the state; surveillance may target some groups more than
  others; expensive.

**(f) [14]**
- *For rehabilitation:* addresses causes of offending such as addiction or lack of skills; can lower
  reoffending (Norway's rehabilitative prisons such as Halden, opened 2010); cheaper in the long run;
  respects human rights.
- *Against / alternatives:* public and victims may want retribution; incapacitation protects the
  public from dangerous offenders; deterrence may prevent crime by others; rehabilitation programmes
  need resources and willing offenders.
- *Conclusion:* the most effective aim depends on the offence and offender; many systems combine
  aims.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use examples from different countries?** Yes — the syllabus key question asks how crime is
dealt with "in different societies", so comparison is credited.

**How should I structure (f)?** A short introduction, arguments for, arguments against or
alternatives, and a conclusion that answers the question.

**Do I need to name theorists?** Accurate names such as Merton, Becker and Box strengthen answers,
but clear explanation matters most.
