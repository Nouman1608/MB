**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 7, "Colonialism and crisis in Rwanda and the Congo (circa 1875 to 2003)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies, each evaluating perspectives on an argument. This study includes colonial atrocities, genocide and mass violence. Handle it with care: use precise terms, avoid ethnic stereotypes, and remember that the victims and survivors are real people. The six inquiry topics are: colonialism in the Congo Free State and Belgian Congo; German and Belgian colonialism in Rwanda; the post-independence civil war in Rwanda; Mobutu and Zaire; the Rwandan genocide; and the Second Congo War.

## 1. Colonialism in the Congo Free State (1885 to 1908) and Belgian Congo (1908 to 1960)

- **Political impact.** After the Berlin Conference, Leopold II ruled the Congo Free State as a personal possession, not as a Belgian colony. He governed through agents and the *Force Publique*. After an international campaign, led by figures such as E. D. Morel and Roger Casement, the Belgian state took over in 1908. The Belgian Congo was ruled through what is often called the colonial trinity of state, Catholic missions and large companies. Africans had no political rights.
- **Economic impact.** Ivory and then wild rubber were gathered under quotas, with hostages taken, villages punished and hands cut off as proof of killing. Estimates of deaths from violence, disease and famine vary widely but run into millions. Later, the Union Minière mined copper in Katanga and the colony produced cotton, palm oil, diamonds and uranium.
- **Experiences of Indigenous peoples.** Forced labour, taxes, population movement to mines, and mission education that was basic. A small class of *évolués* gained education but not equality.
- **Emergence and growth of nationalism.** Political parties were allowed late: ABAKO under Joseph Kasa-Vubu, and the Mouvement National Congolais of Patrice Lumumba (1958). Riots in Léopoldville in January 1959 shocked Belgium, which agreed to independence on 30 June 1960 with little preparation.

**Argument to weigh:** "The Belgian takeover in 1908 made little difference." Support: forced labour and company power continued. Challenge: the worst excesses of the rubber system were reduced and missions, schools and clinics expanded.

## 2. German and Belgian colonialism in Rwanda (1885 to 1962)

- **Pre-colonial background.** Rwanda was a monarchy ruled by a Tutsi *mwami*. Under Kigeli IV Rwabugiri (about 1867 to 1895), the court extended its control, tightened systems of clientship (*ubuhake*) and forced labour (*uburetwa*), and deepened the difference in status between Tutsi elite and Hutu cultivators. This is the guide's "institutionalisation of identity". Hutu, Tutsi and Twa categories were fluid in some respects, but the changes made the distinction rigid and political.
- **German rule (from about 1897) and Belgian rule.** Germany ruled indirectly through the court. After the First World War, Belgium received a League of Nations mandate (1919), later a UN trusteeship. The Belgians centralised the administration, used Tutsi chiefs, expanded forced labour and introduced coffee growing. Colonial ideas, including the "Hamitic hypothesis" that Tutsi were racially superior outsiders, shaped policy.
- **Social engineering and identity cards.** Belgian censuses of the early 1930s introduced identity cards that recorded each person as Hutu, Tutsi or Twa. This turned a flexible social difference into a fixed legal category.
- **Experiences of Indigenous peoples.** Most Rwandans experienced taxes, forced labour and cash-crop demands. In the 1950s the Belgian administration and the Catholic Church shifted support to the Hutu majority, a change that contributed to the revolution of 1959.

## 3. Reasons for the post-independence civil war in Rwanda

- **Purges of the Tutsi.** The 1959 revolution overthrew Tutsi chiefs and was followed by violence and the flight of tens of thousands of Tutsi. Further killings followed in 1963 to 1964. The republic under Grégoire Kayibanda, then Juvénal Habyarimana (from a coup in 1973), favoured Hutu from the north and limited Tutsi participation.
- **Economic disparities and environmental crisis.** Rwanda was one of the most densely populated countries in Africa. Land shortage, falling coffee prices in the late 1980s and economic stress sharpened competition.
- **Rise of the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) and outbreak of civil war.** Tutsi exiles, many in Uganda, formed the RPF, which invaded in October 1990. War followed, and the government encouraged fear of a Tutsi return.
- **Arusha Accords and the assassination of Habyarimana.** The Arusha Accords (August 1993) were meant to share power and bring in a UN force (UNAMIR). Extremists opposed them. On 6 April 1994 the plane carrying Habyarimana was shot down; who did this remains disputed. The genocide began within hours.

## 4. Emergence and impact of Mobutu on Zaire

- **Congo Crisis (1960 to 1965).** Within days of independence the army mutinied, Belgium intervened, and Katanga under Moïse Tshombe seceded. The UN sent a large force. Prime Minister Lumumba sought Soviet help and was dismissed by Kasa-Vubu. Colonel Joseph-Désiré Mobutu took control in September 1960. Lumumba was killed in January 1961, with Belgian and Katangan involvement and US approval of his removal. Mobutu seized power in November 1965.
- **Maintenance of power.** Single-party rule under the Popular Movement of the Revolution, elimination of rivals, public hangings in the early years, and a cult of personality built around his authenticity campaign. The country was renamed Zaire in 1971.
- **Economic policies.** Nationalisations in the 1970s, then plundering of the state through personal and family accounts. Copper prices fell, infrastructure decayed and the economy shrank. Western governments backed Mobutu as an anti-communist ally during the Cold War.
- **First Congo War (1996 to 1997).** After the genocide, Hutu militants used camps in eastern Zaire. Rwanda and Uganda backed a rebellion led by Laurent-Désiré Kabila. Mobutu fled in May 1997.

## 5. Reasons for, and impact of, the Rwandan genocide (1994)

- **Interahamwe and media propaganda.** The Interahamwe militia and the army carried out killings. The radio station RTLM and the magazine *Kangura* spread hatred and dehumanising language. The interim government, a group of extremists often linked to the president's circle, organised killings.
- **Mass killings and arrival of the RPF.** Between April and July 1994, about 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi but also moderate Hutu and Twa, were killed in roughly 100 days (estimates vary). The RPF advanced and took Kigali on 4 July.
- **Response of the international community.** UNAMIR, led by General Roméo Dallaire, was cut back after the killing of ten Belgian peacekeepers, and the UN Security Council delayed action. France led Opération Turquoise in June. Later leaders, including Western ones, acknowledged failure.
- **Justice and reconciliation.** The International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (1994, at Arusha), national trials and the *gacaca* community courts (from 2002). Debates continue on fairness, on the ethnic policies of the RPF-led government, and on how to teach this history.

## 6. Reasons for, and impact of, the Second Congo War (1998 to 2003)

- **Armed intervention by neighbouring states.** In 1998 Kabila turned against his Rwandan and Ugandan allies. Rwanda and Uganda backed rebel groups, while Angola, Zimbabwe and Namibia sent troops to support Kabila. Kabila was assassinated in January 2001 and succeeded by his son Joseph. The Lusaka ceasefire (1999) and later agreements ended the main fighting by 2003.
- **Control of minerals.** Coltan, diamonds, gold and copper funded armies and enriched foreign companies and officials, as documented by a UN panel of experts (2001 to 2003).
- **M23 and other militia groups.** Many armed groups, including those that later became M23 (2012), grew from these networks. Treat M23 as part of the war's long legacy.
- **Atrocities.** Mass killing, sexual violence used as a weapon, and forced recruitment of children. Most of the millions of deaths in the war were from disease and hunger in areas disrupted by conflict.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "Belgian colonial policies were the main reason for the Rwandan genocide." To what extent do you agree?

1. Define "main reason": the claim says the colonial legacy outweighs later causes.
2. For: identity cards, favouring Tutsi chiefs then shifting to Hutu, forced labour, the "Hamitic" ideology.
3. Against: decisions by Rwandan leaders after 1962, the civil war from 1990, the extremist plan, media propaganda and the failure of the Arusha process.
4. Perspectives: some historians stress colonial structures; others stress the choices of the 1990s elite and the war.
5. Judgement: colonialism created the categories and grievances, but the genocide required deliberate planning and an immediate crisis in 1994.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the genocide as "tribal" or "ancient hatred".
- Treating Hutu and Tutsi as natural, unchanging groups.
- Ignoring the Congo: it is half of the study.
- Using unsupported death tolls.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the people named in the guide the only ones I can be asked about?** Yes: Kigeli IV Rwabugiri, Juvénal Habyarimana, Mobutu and Laurent Kabila, and groups such as the RPF, the Interahamwe and M23.

**How do I use the word genocide?** It is the standard term for the 1994 events and is recognised in law by the ICTR. Use it with evidence of intent and organisation.
