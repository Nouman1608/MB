These notes condense **3.1.5 The market mechanism, market failure and government intervention in markets** of AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, parts 3.1.5.1 to 3.1.5.9. The [3.1.5 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-market-mechanism-market-failure-and-government-intervention-aqa-aslevel-economics/) works through a tax and a maximum price with numbers; the [3.1.5 practice questions](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-market-mechanism-market-failure-and-government-intervention-aqa-aslevel-economics-practice/) test every part. All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Price mechanism | Interaction of demand and supply that allocates resources through prices |
| Signalling function | Price changes carry information to buyers and sellers |
| Incentive function | Higher prices reward producers for supplying more |
| Rationing function | Price limits demand to the quantity available |
| Market failure | A market leads to a misallocation of resources |
| Complete market failure | The market is missing altogether |
| Partial market failure | The market exists but produces the wrong quantity |
| Public good | Non-rival and non-excludable |
| Free-rider problem | People benefit without paying, so private provision fails |
| Quasi-public good | Has some, but not all, public-good characteristics |
| Externality | A cost or benefit to a third party, so private and social values diverge |
| Merit good | Under-consumed in a free market, judged better for people than they realise |
| Demerit good | Over-consumed in a free market, judged worse for people than they realise |
| Asymmetric information | One party to a transaction knows more than the other |
| Factor immobility | Factors cannot easily move between jobs or places |
| Government failure | Intervention leads to a misallocation of resources |

## Recall by part

**3.1.5.1** Signalling, incentive and rationing functions coordinate buyers and sellers and decide what, how and for whom to produce.

**3.1.5.2** Causes of failure listed in the specification: public goods, positive and negative externalities, merit and demerit goods, monopoly and other imperfections, and inequalities of income and wealth.

**3.1.5.3** Non-rival + non-excludable = free riders = no private provision (complete failure). Quasi-public when excludable (tolls) or rival (congestion). Technology can make goods excludable, as with encrypted television broadcasting.

**3.1.5.4** Negative externality: over-production. Positive externality: under-production. Show with a demand and supply diagram: a second supply curve including external costs (above the private one), or a second demand curve including external benefits (to the right). No MSC/MSB diagrams needed.

**3.1.5.5** Classification is a value judgement. Externalities in consumption plus imperfect information explain under-consumption of merit goods and over-consumption of demerit goods. Not every externality good is a merit or demerit good.

**3.1.5.6** Imperfect or asymmetric information; monopoly power; occupational and geographical immobility of factors.

**3.1.5.7** Ability to buy depends on income and wealth, so an inequitable distribution misallocates resources. Progressive taxes and government spending reduce inequality.

**3.1.5.8** Tools: indirect taxes, subsidies, price controls, state provision, regulation. Evaluate the case for and against intervention and compare tools.

**3.1.5.9** Sources: inadequate information, conflicting objectives, administrative costs. Results: new distortions and unintended consequences.

## Calculations to practise

```
Specific tax of t per unit: new Qs uses (P - t) in place of P
  Qd = 600 - 20P, Qs = -60 + 20P -> P = 16.5, Q = 270
  Tax 3: Qs = -120 + 20P -> P = 18, Q = 240
  Consumers pay +1.5, producers keep 15 (-1.5), revenue 3 x 240 = 720

Subsidy of s per unit: new Qs uses (P + s)
Maximum price below equilibrium: shortage = Qd - Qs at the ceiling
Minimum price above equilibrium: surplus = Qs - Qd at the floor
```

## Exam traps

- Calling a good public because the state provides it (health care and education are merit goods, not public goods).
- Mixing up rival and excludable.
- Showing a negative externality as a demand shift when it arises in production.
- Ignoring elasticity when judging a tax: inelastic demand means a small fall in quantity.
- Price controls set on the wrong side of equilibrium, which have no effect.
- No mention of government failure when the question asks you to evaluate intervention.
- Forgetting that merit and demerit labels are value judgements.

## Self-test

1. Name the three functions of prices.
2. Distinguish between complete and partial market failure.
3. Explain why the free-rider problem prevents private provision of a public good.
4. Give an example of a quasi-public good and say why it is not pure.
5. Does a factory polluting a river cause over- or under-production? Why?
6. How would you show a positive consumption externality on an AS demand and supply diagram?
7. Why is classifying a good as a merit good a value judgement?
8. Give an example of asymmetric information.
9. Qd = 400 - 10P and Qs = -50 + 20P. Find the equilibrium. A maximum price of 12 is set. Calculate the shortage.
10. Name the three sources of government failure in the specification.
11. Give one unintended consequence of a minimum price on alcohol.
12. Which two tools does the specification say are used to reduce inequality?

### Answers

1. Signalling, incentive, rationing.
2. Complete: no market exists (missing market). Partial: a market exists but misallocates resources.
3. Non-payers cannot be excluded, so people wait for others to pay; a firm cannot collect enough revenue to cover costs.
4. A toll road: it is excludable through the toll and becomes rival when congested.
5. Over-production: the firm's costs exclude the external cost, so its supply curve is too far to the right.
6. Draw a second demand curve to the right of private demand, labelled "including external benefits"; output where private demand meets supply is below the level the second curve gives.
7. It rests on a view that people should consume more than they choose, which is a normative opinion.
8. A used-car seller knowing about faults the buyer cannot see; a buyer of insurance knowing their own risk better than the insurer.
9. 450 = 30P, so P = 15 and Q = 250. At 12, Qd = 280 and Qs = 190: a shortage of 90.
10. Inadequate information, conflicting objectives, administrative costs.
11. Some drinkers may buy from illegal sellers, or spend less on food to keep buying alcohol.
12. Progressive taxes and government spending.
