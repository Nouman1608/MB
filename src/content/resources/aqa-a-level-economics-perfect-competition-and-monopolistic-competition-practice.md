## Questions

**1.** Explain why a firm in a perfectly competitive market is a price taker. **[3]**

**2.** A firm in Country D (illustrative) is a price taker facing a market price of £15. Its total costs are: 0 units £30; 1 unit £42; 2 units £52; 3 units £60; 4 units £69; 5 units £81; 6 units £97; 7 units £119. Fixed cost is £30.

**(a)** Find the profit-maximising output and the profit or loss. **[4]**
**(b)** Should the firm stay open in the short run? Justify your answer. **[1]**

**3.** Demand rises in a perfectly competitive market. Explain, with reference to the firm and the market diagrams, what happens in the short run and the long run. **[6]**

**4.** In a competitive market 80 identical firms each supply 15 units at a long-run price of £22. Demand rises, and at £22 the quantity demanded becomes 1,920 units. Calculate the original market output and the number of firms in the new long-run equilibrium. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how (a) freedom of entry and exit and (b) perfect knowledge affect the behaviour of firms in a perfectly competitive market. **[4]**

**6.** In the market for a cleaning chemical in Country E (illustrative), demand is P = 90 − 0.05Q and private supply is P = 15 + 0.025Q. Production causes pollution costing £6 per unit. The market is perfectly competitive.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium quantity and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the socially optimal quantity and the overproduction. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what this shows about the efficiency of perfect competition. **[1]**

**7.** Assess critically the proposition that perfectly competitive markets lead to an efficient allocation of resources. **[8]**

**8.** A florist in a monopolistically competitive market has demand P = 82 − q and total cost TC = 450 + 10q + 0.5q², so MC = 10 + q. Calculate its profit-maximising output, price and supernormal profit. **[4]**

**9.** Later, entrants cause the florist's demand to become P = 55 − 0.625q. Its lowest AC is at 30 units. Explain and show what happens in the long run. **[6]**

**10.** A small bakery (illustrative) sells 400 loaves a month at £25 with variable cost £15 per loaf. It spends £500 a month on a loyalty scheme and sales rise to 460 loaves.

**(a)** Calculate the change in monthly profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the gain may not last. **[2]**

**11.** Explain how perfect competition in a labour market can act as a yardstick for judging a town where one employer hires most workers. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The firm is one of very many producers, each small relative to the market [1]. Its output cannot change the market price [1], and with identical products buyers would go elsewhere if it charged more, so it accepts the going price [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the assumption and then linking it to the firm's inability to influence price usually gains more than listing assumptions alone.

**2. (a)** MC for units 1 to 7 is 12, 10, 8, 9, 12, 16, 22 [1]. MC rises through MR = £15 between 5 units (12) and 6 units (16), so output is **5 units** [1]. TR = 15 × 5 = £75 and TC = £81 [1]. Result: **loss of £6** [1].
**(b)** VC at 5 units is 81 − 30 = 51, so AVC = £10.20, below the price of £15, and the loss of £6 is less than fixed cost of £30. The firm stays open [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the MC column or the comparison of MC with price; a bare output figure leaves nothing to credit if it is wrong.

**3.** Short run: the market price rises as demand shifts right [1]. The firm's horizontal demand curve moves up, and output rises along MC to the new MC = MR point [1]. Price is above AC, so supernormal profit [1]. Long run: profit attracts entry as there are no barriers [1]. Market supply shifts right and price falls back to the lowest point of AC [1]. The firm returns to normal profit but the industry is larger [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A clear chain from profit to entry to supply to price, tied to both diagrams, shows the reasoning the question asks for.

**4.** Original output = 80 × 15 = **1,200 units** [1]. New firms = 1,920 ÷ 15 = **128** [1], so 128 − 80 = **48 entrants** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State what your number means (firms in total or entrants); an unlabelled 128 or 48 can be read either way.

**5.** (a) Free entry and exit means supernormal profit attracts new firms and losses cause exit [1], so profit returns to normal in the long run [1]. (b) Perfect knowledge means buyers know all prices, so a firm charging more loses its sales [1], and rivals can copy techniques at once, so no firm keeps a cost advantage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each assumption needs its consequence stated for firm behaviour, not just a definition.

**6. (a)** 90 − 0.05Q = 15 + 0.025Q gives 75 = 0.075Q, so **Q = 1,000** [1], **P = £40** [1].
**(b)** MSC = 21 + 0.025Q [1]. 90 − 0.05Q = 21 + 0.025Q gives Q = **920** [1]. Overproduction = 1,000 − 920 = **80 units** [1].
**(c)** At 1,000 units MSC is £46 but the value to buyers is £40, so resources are misallocated even though the market is perfectly competitive [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Build the social curve by adding the external cost to the supply equation, then say what the comparison means.

**7.** The proposition: P = MC in the long run and output at lowest AC, so resources go to their best use [1]. It depends on assumptions such as no externalities [1]. Negative externalities make MSC exceed MPC, so overproduction [1]. Public goods and merit goods would not be provided correctly [1]. Real buyers lack perfect knowledge [1]. Where economies of scale are large, tiny firms cannot reach the lowest cost [1]. Only normal profit leaves little for research, so dynamic efficiency may suffer [1]. Judgement: the proposition is a useful benchmark where spillovers are small, but it fails where the assumptions fail, and fairness is not addressed at all [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the points and reach a judgement about when the proposition holds; a list of limitations without a conclusion tends to stay descriptive.

**8.** MR = 82 − 2q [1]. MC = MR: 10 + q = 82 − 2q, so q = **24** [1]. P = 82 − 24 = **£58** [1]. TR = 1,392; TC = 450 + 240 + 288 = 978; profit = **£414** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Derive MR from the demand equation yourself; using the demand slope for MR gives the wrong output.

**9.** Supernormal profit attracts entrants with close substitutes [1]. Each firm's demand shifts left and becomes more elastic [1]. MR = 55 − 1.25q; MC = MR gives 10 + q = 55 − 1.25q, so q = **20** [1]. P = 55 − 12.5 = **£42.50**. AC = 450/20 + 10 + 10 = £42.50, so profit is normal (zero) [1]. The lowest AC is at 30, so excess capacity is **10 units** [1]. MC at 20 is £30, so P exceeds MC by **£12.50**, which is allocative inefficiency [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the tangency check (P equals AC) and then comment on the inefficiency, not only on the profit.

**10. (a)** Extra loaves = 60 [1]. Extra contribution = 60 × (25 − 15) = £600 [1]. Net change = 600 − 500 = **+£100** a month [1].
**(b)** Rivals can copy a loyalty scheme, so demand shifts back [1], and the £500 cost remains, so profit returns towards normal [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use contribution per unit; subtracting the full cost per loaf from price mixes in unchanged fixed costs.

**11.** In the competitive model there are many employers and many identical workers, so each firm takes the wage set by the market [1]. Real wages and employment in the town can be compared with this benchmark [1]. A dominant employer can hold pay below the competitive level [1]. The benchmark shows what is lost but does not say it is achievable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the yardstick to the town given; a general account of perfect competition does not use the context.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the competitive firm's demand curve as downward sloping.
- Using demand slope as the MR slope instead of doubling it.
- Giving an output without showing the MC and MR comparison.
- Calling any loss a reason to close immediately.
- Defining efficiency but not assessing the proposition.
- Placing the long-run monopolistically competitive firm at the lowest AC.
- Leaving units off money answers, and not saying whether a number is firms or entrants.
- Describing non-price competition with no cost or judgement.
