Topic 7 Ethics and Ownership covers why computing professionals must act ethically, why copyright law is needed, how the main software licence types differ, and the applications and impact of artificial intelligence. Cambridge International AS & A Level Computer Science 9618 tests it in Paper 1 with written answers applied to a scenario, so marks come from precise terms and points tied to the situation described, not from general opinion.

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Ethics | Moral principles that govern how a person or group behaves |
| Professional ethical body | An organisation (e.g. BCS, IEEE) that sets a code of conduct its members agree to follow |
| Code of conduct | A published set of standards of professional behaviour |
| Copyright | The legal right of the creator of an original work to control its copying, distribution and adaptation |
| Software licence | A legal agreement setting out how software may be used, copied and distributed |
| Plagiarism | Presenting someone else's work as your own |
| Artificial intelligence | Machines or software carrying out tasks that would normally need human intelligence, such as recognising speech or making decisions |

## Why do computing professionals need ethics?

- Computer systems affect the safety, money and privacy of large numbers of people, and professionals often know more than their clients or users, so the public must be able to trust them.
- The law often lags behind technology; many harmful actions are not illegal.
- A code of conduct gives a professional a standard to point to when refusing an unethical instruction from an employer.

**Why join BCS or IEEE?** Membership shows employers and clients that you have agreed to a published code of ethics and to keep your knowledge up to date. The body provides guidance, training and recognition (qualifications or chartered status), and can discipline members who break its code. The BCS Code of Conduct is organised under four headings: public interest; professional competence and integrity; duty to relevant authority (the employer or client); duty to the profession. Typical themes across both bodies:

| Theme | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Public interest | Put public health, safety, privacy and the environment first |
| Competence | Only take on work you are competent to do; keep skills up to date |
| Honesty and integrity | Do not make false claims about a system; report faults; avoid conflicts of interest and bribery |
| Duty to employer/client | Work in the client's interest, keep confidential information confidential |
| Duty to the profession | Uphold its reputation, support colleagues, do not take credit for others' work |

## What is the impact of acting ethically or unethically?

Use a three-step answer: **what was done → who is affected → consequence**.

| Acting ethically | Acting unethically |
|---|---|
| Users can trust the product and the company | Users can be harmed (e.g. unsafe software, stolen data) |
| Company reputation and sales are protected | Company may face lawsuits, fines and loss of customers |
| Employee keeps professional standing | Employee may be dismissed or expelled from the professional body |
| Problems are fixed before release | Problems found later cost more to fix and may cause harm |

## Why is copyright legislation needed?

- It protects the creator's work (code, documentation, images, music, video) from being copied, sold or adapted without permission.
- It lets creators earn money from their work, so it encourages people and companies to invest in developing software.
- It gives a legal remedy against piracy (illegal copying and distribution of software).
- Copyright protects the **expression** (the actual code), not the underlying idea.

## How do the software licence types compare?

| Licence | Cost | Source code | Copying / modifying | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free Software Foundation (free software, e.g. GPL) | May be free or sold | Must be available | Users may run, study, change and redistribute; changed versions must be released under the same terms | Developers who want everyone to keep the freedom to change the code |
| Open Source Initiative (open source) | Usually free | Available | Can be modified and redistributed under the terms of an OSI-approved licence, some of which allow use in closed products | Community development, peer review, businesses wanting to adapt code |
| Shareware | Free for a trial period or with limited features, then paid | Not available | Copying of the trial version is allowed for evaluation; the code cannot be modified | Trying software before buying |
| Commercial | Paid (one-off or subscription) | Not available | Copying and modification not allowed; use limited to the licence terms (e.g. number of users) | Software with support and guaranteed updates |

"Free" in Free Software Foundation means **freedom**, not zero price. November 2024 Paper 1 questions tested software licences.

## What are the applications and impact of AI?

**Applications** (examples to use): medical diagnosis from scans; autonomous vehicles; voice assistants and speech recognition; facial recognition; fraud detection in banking; recommendation systems in online shops and streaming; robots in manufacturing; chatbots for customer service.

| Impact | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Social | Faster diagnosis; help for people with disabilities; safer driving if reliable | Bias in decisions learned from biased data; loss of privacy and increased surveillance; unclear responsibility when an AI makes a wrong decision |
| Economic | Higher productivity; new jobs in AI development and maintenance; 24-hour service | Loss of some jobs, especially routine ones; high development cost; benefits may go to a few large companies |
| Environmental | Optimising energy use, traffic flow and farming to reduce waste | Large amounts of electricity (and cooling water) used to train and run AI models in data centres; electronic waste from hardware |

## Worked example: justifying a licence

*A charity has written an app for volunteers and wants other charities to be able to improve it, while making sure no company can turn it into a closed paid product.*

Choose a **Free Software Foundation licence** (such as the GPL): the source code is available so other charities can study and improve the app, and any modified version must be distributed with the same freedoms, so it cannot be made closed. A commercial licence would stop others changing it, and shareware hides the code.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Writing "it is illegal" when the question asks whether something is **ethical**; ethics and law are different.
- Treating free software as meaning "costs nothing".
- Saying copyright protects an idea or algorithm itself.
- Listing code-of-conduct themes without applying them to the scenario's people and facts.
- Giving AI impacts that are all social; a question asking for social, economic **and** environmental impact needs one of each.
- Repeating the example given in the question stem; examiners in June 2024 and June 2025 noted that it earns no credit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to quote the BCS or IEEE code word for word?**
No. Know the main themes (public interest, competence, integrity, duty to employer and profession) and apply them.

**What is the difference between free software and open source?**
Both give access to the source code. The Free Software Foundation stresses users' freedoms and its licences require changed versions to stay free; the Open Source Initiative stresses practical benefits and approves a range of licences, some of which allow the code to be used in closed products.

**Is shareware the same as freeware?**
No. Shareware is free only for a trial or with limited features and must then be paid for.
