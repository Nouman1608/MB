## Section A

**1.** (19.2.1) Explain two advantages of treating diabetes with recombinant human insulin rather than insulin extracted from pigs. **[2]**

**2.** (19.2.1) Explain why recombinant factor VIII is safer for people with haemophilia than factor VIII purified from donated blood. **[2]**

**3.** (19.2.2) State what is meant by genetic screening. **[1]**

**4.** (19.2.1) Explain why a lack of functional adenosine deaminase (ADA) causes severe combined immunodeficiency. **[2]**

**5.** (19.2.3) Suggest why gene therapy, which adds a functional allele, is more likely to succeed for a recessive condition than for a dominant one. **[2]**

**6.** (19.2.3) Explain why a child of a person who received gene therapy for SCID would not inherit the inserted allele. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** (19.2.2, 19.2.4) A man's father developed Huntington's disease at the age of 48. His mother does not carry the allele. The man is 25 and has no symptoms.

**(a)** Assuming his father was heterozygous, state the probability that the man has inherited the allele. **[1]**

**(b)** If the man is heterozygous, state the probability that each of his children would inherit the allele. **[1]**

**(c)** Outline two advantages to the man of being tested before he has children. **[2]**

**(d)** Suggest two reasons why he might decide not to be tested. **[2]**

**8.** (19.2.2) Cystic fibrosis is caused by recessive alleles of the CFTR gene.

**(a)** Outline the advantages of genetic screening for cystic fibrosis. **[3]**

**(b)** A carrier (Ff) has a child with a person who has cystic fibrosis (ff). State the probability that the child has cystic fibrosis. **[1]**

**9.** (19.2.2) A woman whose mother and aunt both had breast cancer is tested and found to carry a faulty BRCA1 allele.

**(a)** Outline the advantages to her of this result. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the result does not mean that she will definitely develop breast cancer. **[1]**

**10.** (19.2.3) A child has SCID caused by a lack of ADA.

**(a)** Outline how gene therapy could be used to treat the child. **[4]**

**(b)** In some early SCID gene therapy trials, children developed leukaemia. Explain how the treatment could cause this. **[2]**

**11.** (19.2.3) Explain why inherited eye diseases of the retina are well suited to treatment by gene therapy. **[3]**

**12.** (19.2.4) Discuss the social and ethical considerations of using genetic screening and gene therapy in medicine. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two [2]: it has the same amino acid sequence as human insulin, so it is less likely to cause an immune / allergic response; a large, reliable supply that does not depend on slaughtered animals; acceptable to people with religious or ethical objections to pig products; consistent purity.

**2.** Donated blood can carry pathogens such as HIV or hepatitis C, which were passed to people with haemophilia [1]. Recombinant factor VIII is made by cultured cells, so there is no risk of donor-borne infection [1].

**3.** Testing a person's DNA for alleles associated with a genetic disease [1].

**4.** Without ADA a toxic product of nucleotide breakdown accumulates [1] and kills developing lymphocytes (T and B cells), so neither cell-mediated nor antibody responses work [1].

**5.** In a recessive condition the disease results from a missing protein, so one added functional allele restores it [1]. In a dominant condition the faulty allele's product still causes harm even when a normal allele is present, so adding an allele does not help [1].

**6.** Only somatic cells (bone marrow / blood cells) were altered, not gametes / germ-line cells [1].

**7. (a)** 0.5 (1 in 2) [1].

**(b)** 0.5 (1 in 2) [1].

**(c)** Any two [2]: can make informed decisions about having children; can choose IVF with embryo testing; can plan his life, career and finances; a negative result removes uncertainty.

**(d)** Any two [2]: there is no cure, so knowing could cause anxiety or depression; risk of discrimination by employers or insurers; a positive result reveals information about relatives; he may prefer not to know.

**8. (a)** Any three [3]: carrier testing tells couples their risk before having children; embryos (with IVF) or fetuses can be tested; newborn screening allows treatment (physiotherapy, antibiotics) to start early; relatives can be offered testing.

**(b)** 0.5 (1 in 2) [1].

**9. (a)** Any three [3]: more frequent checks such as mammograms / MRI, so any tumour is found early when easier to treat; option of preventive surgery; informed lifestyle choices; female relatives can be offered testing.

**(b)** The allele increases risk but does not cause cancer on its own; other mutations and environmental factors are also needed [1].

**10. (a)** Bone marrow stem cells (or T cells) are removed from the child [1]. A normal ADA allele is inserted into them using a viral vector such as a retrovirus [1]. The modified cells are returned to the child [1]. They divide to produce lymphocytes that make ADA, so the immune system can work [1].

**(b)** The retrovirus inserts the allele into the host's chromosomes at a random position [1]; in some children it inserted next to / disrupted a gene controlling cell division, so cells divided uncontrollably [1].

**11.** Any three [3]: the eye is small and enclosed, so a small dose of vector reaches the target cells; retinal cells do not divide, so the allele is not diluted and the effect lasts; the eye has weak immune responses, so the vector is less likely to be destroyed; the untreated eye acts as a comparison.

**12.** Indicative content, one mark each, to a maximum of 6. Credit at least one benefit and one concern for full marks.
- Screening allows early diagnosis, monitoring or preventive treatment.
- Screening allows informed decisions about having children / embryo selection.
- Positive results for incurable conditions such as Huntington's disease cause anxiety.
- Results reveal information about relatives, who may not wish to know.
- Risk of discrimination by employers or insurers.
- Embryo selection: discarded embryos; fear of selection for non-medical traits.
- Gene therapy may cure rather than manage disease, reducing lifelong treatment.
- Gene therapy is expensive; unequal access.
- Vectors may cause immune reactions or cancer.
- Germ-line therapy would affect future generations who cannot consent.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not comparing recombinant proteins with the older source.
- Saying gene therapy removes or replaces the faulty allele.
- Getting dominant and recessive inheritance probabilities wrong.
- One-sided ethics answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**How are these questions organised?**
Section A has short questions; Section B uses scenarios and extended answers. The learning outcome is in brackets at the start of each question.

**Do I need to know the names of specific gene therapy products?**
No. Outline the method for SCID and inherited eye diseases: the vector, the target cells and how the functional allele restores the missing protein.
