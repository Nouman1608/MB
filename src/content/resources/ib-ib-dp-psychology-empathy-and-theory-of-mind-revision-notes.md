These notes cover **"development of empathy and theory of mind"**, a content point in the "developing an identity" topic of the developmental psychology option (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2019). The rest of the option is in the [developmental psychology revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-developmental-psychology-revision-notes/); full explanations and an essay plan are in the [empathy and theory of mind study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-empathy-and-theory-of-mind/).

## Recall in one minute

- **Theory of mind:** understanding that others have beliefs, desires and knowledge that can differ from one's own and from reality.
- **The test:** false-belief tasks; the child must predict behaviour from what the other person believes, not from what is true.
- **Timing:** explicit, verbal success typically emerges between about four and five, in many cultures.
- **Earlier signs:** non-verbal studies suggest some implicit understanding in infancy, but this is debated.
- **Empathy:** affective (sharing a feeling) develops early; cognitive (understanding why) depends partly on theory of mind.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Theory of mind | attributing mental states to oneself and others, and using them to predict behaviour |
| False belief | a belief that does not match reality |
| Unexpected-location task | a character's object is moved while they are away (Maxi; Sally-Anne) |
| Unexpected-contents task | a familiar container holds something unexpected (the sweet tube) |
| Violation of expectation | infants look longer at events that break what they expect |
| Mindblindness | Baron-Cohen's term for difficulty attributing mental states, proposed in relation to autism |
| Affective empathy | an emotional response matching or responding to another's emotion |
| Cognitive empathy | understanding what another person feels and why |
| Reactive crying | newborns crying in response to another newborn's cry |
| Emotional contagion | automatically "catching" another's emotion without understanding it |

## Theory of mind studies

| Study | Method | Finding | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premack and Woodruff (1978) | chimpanzee research | introduced the term "theory of mind" | shows the concept's origins in comparative research |
| Wimmer and Perner (1983) | Maxi and the chocolate, told with toys | correct predictions much more common in older children | verbal and memory demands |
| Perner, Leekam and Wimmer (1987) | sweet tube containing a pencil | many three-year-olds said another child would think "pencil" | simple, repeatable procedure |
| Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) | Sally-Anne with dolls; autistic, Down syndrome and typically developing children | most autistic children failed despite higher verbal mental age than the Down syndrome group | dolls; small sample; not all autistic children failed |
| Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001) | meta-analysis of false-belief studies | consistent shift between about three and five; task changes helped but did not remove the trend | large evidence base across countries |
| Callaghan et al. (2005) | same task in Canada, India, Peru, Samoa, Thailand | passing began at a similar age, about five, in all five | supports universality |
| Onishi and Baillargeon (2005) | non-verbal looking-time task with fifteen-month-olds | longer looking when the actor acted against her false belief | may reflect simpler rules; debated |

## Empathy: Hoffman's stages

| Stage | Approximate timing | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Global empathy | first year | distressed by others' distress |
| Egocentric empathy | second year | helps in ways that would comfort themselves |
| Empathy for another's feelings | from about two to three | responds to others' different feelings |
| Empathy for another's life condition | late childhood | responds to someone's general situation |

## Empathy studies

| Study | Finding | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| Sagi and Hoffman (1976) | newborns cried more to another newborn's cry than to a synthetic sound | may be contagion, not empathy |
| Zahn-Waxler et al. (1992) | mothers' records showed concern and helping increased through the second year | maternal report may be biased |
| Warneken and Tomasello (2006) | eighteen-month-olds helped an adult who dropped a pen accidentally, not one who dropped it deliberately | control conditions show understanding of goals |

## Evaluation toolkit

- **Task demands:** language, memory and attention can make children fail tasks they partly understand.
- **Cross-sectional designs:** most studies compare age groups rather than follow children over time.
- **Cultural evidence:** Callaghan et al. support universality; parenting and language still affect timing.
- **Interpretation of looking time:** longer looking shows surprise, not necessarily belief understanding.
- **Reports by parents:** convenient and natural, but open to bias.
- **Ethics:** studies with young children need parental consent, the child's assent and very brief, non-distressing tasks.

## Exam traps

- **Merging the concepts.** Define theory of mind and empathy separately, then link them.
- **Causal claims about autism.** The Sally-Anne findings show an association, not a cause.
- **Fixed ages.** Use "around" and "typically".
- **One task only.** Show that different tasks, and non-verbal methods, give different answers.
- **Description without development.** Every study should say what it shows about change with age.

## Self-test

1. Define theory of mind.
2. Why are false-belief tasks used to test it?
3. Describe the unexpected-contents task.
4. What did Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001) conclude?
5. Which five countries were in Callaghan et al. (2005), and what was found?
6. What method did Onishi and Baillargeon (2005) use?
7. Give one criticism of Onishi and Baillargeon's interpretation.
8. Why did Baron-Cohen et al. (1985) include children with Down syndrome?
9. Distinguish affective and cognitive empathy.
10. What is egocentric empathy?
11. What did the control condition show in Warneken and Tomasello (2006)?
12. Give one ethical consideration in this research.

**Answers**

1. Understanding that others have mental states, such as beliefs and desires, that can differ from one's own and from reality, and that guide their behaviour.
2. Passing requires the child to predict behaviour from someone's belief rather than from reality, which shows understanding that beliefs can be false.
3. A child sees a familiar sweet tube, says it contains sweets, is shown a pencil inside, and is asked what someone who has not looked will think is inside.
4. Across many studies and countries, children shift from failing to passing between about three and five, and task changes do not remove this trend.
5. Canada, India, Peru, Samoa and Thailand; children began passing at a similar age, about five, in all five.
6. A non-verbal violation-of-expectation method measuring how long infants looked at events.
7. Longer looking could reflect simpler expectations about where people usually search rather than understanding of beliefs.
8. As a comparison group with a learning disability, to show that failure was not simply due to lower mental age.
9. Affective empathy is sharing or responding to another's feeling; cognitive empathy is understanding what they feel and why.
10. Trying to help in ways that would comfort oneself, such as offering one's own toy.
11. Infants helped only when the adult needed help, not when the pen was dropped deliberately, suggesting they understood the adult's goal.
12. For example: parental consent and the child's assent; avoiding distress; keeping tasks short.
