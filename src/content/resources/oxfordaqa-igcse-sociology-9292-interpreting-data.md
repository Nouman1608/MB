This guide covers **3.1.3.4 Interpretation of data** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology
(9292). The specification lists "How to interpret data from quantitative sources, including tables,
charts and graphs" and "How to interpret data from qualitative sources, including extracts from field
notes and unstructured interviews." Research methods is assessed in Section C of **both** papers, and
in Paper 1 Section C includes "a structured question based on data about an unseen issue of global
sociological significance". The [research methods study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-research-methods/)
gives a short routine for data questions; this page develops the skills in full, with invented data so
that every calculation can be checked. Interpreting data draws on AO2: "Interpret sociological
evidence and data".

## Part 1: quantitative data

### Read before you calculate

Before looking at any number, read:

- **The title:** what is being measured, where and when.
- **The units:** numbers, percentages, thousands, rates per 1,000.
- **The categories:** rows, columns, axis labels, the key.
- **The source and date,** if given: who collected the data and how recent it is.

Many lost marks come from misreading units, such as treating "thousands" as single numbers.

### Tables

Tables give exact figures. Typical tasks are to identify the highest or lowest value, compare two
groups, calculate a difference and describe a pattern.

**Worked example (invented data).**

> **Item A** — Percentage of households with internet access, 2010–2020
>
> *Illustrative data, invented for this guide.*
>
> | Country | 2010 | 2015 | 2020 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | X | 20 | 35 | 60 |
> | Y | 70 | 82 | 91 |

- **Identify a pattern:** access rose in both countries between 2010 and 2020.
- **Quantify:** in Country X it rose from 20% to 60%, an increase of **40 percentage points**; in
  Country Y it rose from 70% to 91%, an increase of **21 percentage points**.
- **Compare:** the gap between the countries narrowed from 50 percentage points (70 − 20) in 2010 to
  31 percentage points (91 − 60) in 2020, but Country Y still had much higher access.

### Percentages and percentage points

These are different, and the difference is often tested.

- A **percentage-point** change is the simple difference between two percentages: 20% to 60% is a rise
  of 40 percentage points.
- A **percentage change** compares the change with the starting figure: (60 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = a
  **200%** increase.

For Country Y, the rise of 21 percentage points is a percentage increase of 21 ÷ 70 × 100 = **30%**.
Both statements are correct; they answer different questions, so use the term the question asks for.

### Raw numbers and rates

Raw numbers can mislead when populations differ in size.

> **Item B** — Recorded thefts in two towns in one year
>
> *Illustrative data, invented for this guide.*
>
> | Town | Recorded thefts | Population |
> |---|---|---|
> | A | 1,200 | 40,000 |
> | B | 900 | 20,000 |

Town A has more thefts, but the **rate per 1,000 people** is 1,200 ÷ 40,000 × 1,000 = **30** in Town A
and 900 ÷ 20,000 × 1,000 = **45** in Town B. Relative to its population, Town B has the higher rate.

### Averages

Tables sometimes give, or ask you to work out, an average. The **mean** adds the values and divides by
how many there are; the **median** is the middle value when they are placed in order. With invented
weekly incomes of 200, 250, 300, 350 and 2,000, the mean is 3,100 ÷ 5 = **620**, but the median is
**300**. One very high income pulls the mean up, so the median gives a better picture of a typical
person. This matters when interpreting data on wealth and income (3.2.1.3).

### Charts and graphs

- **Bar charts** compare categories: read each bar against the scale, not by eye.
- **Line graphs** show change over time: describe the overall **trend**, then any **fluctuations**,
  peaks or turning points, quoting years and values.
- **Pie charts** show shares of a whole: comment on the largest and smallest shares and remember
  that a pie chart does not show the total number.

Check where the scale starts: a vertical axis that does not begin at zero can make small differences
look large.

### Limitations of quantitative data

Questions often ask for a limitation. Strong answers name a specific weakness of the data in front of
them:

- **What it does not show:** causes, meanings, or groups not included.
- **Sample and representativeness:** how many people, and who.
- **Definitions:** how "access", "crime" or "literacy" was measured, and whether this changed.
- **Source:** who collected it, and for what purpose; official statistics may reflect recording
  practices.
- **Time:** a single year, or only a few points, may hide fluctuations.
- **Correlation, not causation:** two things changing together does not prove one caused the other.

## Part 2: qualitative data

The specification names "extracts from field notes and unstructured interviews". These give
**meanings** and **experiences** rather than numbers.

**Worked example (invented extract).**

> **Item C** — Field notes from observation of a youth club
>
> *Original extract, invented for this guide.*
>
> "Tuesday, 7 pm. About twenty young people present. The older boys take the pool table as soon as
> they arrive; younger ones watch. Two girls ask to play and are told 'after this game' three times.
> They give up and sit with their phones. When the youth worker comes over, the boys offer the girls
> the next game. As soon as she leaves, the game continues as before."

**Interpreting it:**

- **Identify themes:** status by age and gender; control of shared space.
- **Use short quotations as evidence:** "after this game" three times suggests polite exclusion.
- **Interpret meaning:** the boys' behaviour changes when the youth worker is present, which shows
  awareness of what adults expect.
- **Consider the research process:** the change in behaviour when an adult approaches suggests that
  the observer's presence could also affect behaviour (a form of the Hawthorne effect, which the
  specification lists under 3.1.3.3).
- **Limitations:** one evening, one club; the notes record the observer's interpretation; the young
  people's own explanations are missing, which an unstructured interview could add.

For an **unstructured interview extract**, follow the same steps, paying attention to the
interviewee's own words, feelings and explanations, and to whether the interviewer's questions or
presence may have shaped the answers.

## Linking data to sociology

Paper 1 Section C is set on "an unseen issue of global sociological significance". After describing
the data, link it to concepts you know: poverty, gender inequality in access to education, the
digital divide, urbanisation. Say what the data suggests, but avoid claiming that it proves a cause.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing percentage points with percentage change.
- Comparing raw numbers when populations differ.
- Describing a pattern without quoting figures and units.
- Reading a chart by eye rather than against the scale.
- Giving a generic limitation ("it might be biased") with no link to the data.
- Treating a qualitative extract as representative of everyone.
- Claiming correlation proves causation.
