Condensed recall for **3.2.2 Language, thought and communication** (AQA GCSE Psychology 8182,

## Language and thought

| | Piaget | Sapir-Whorf |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Language depends on thought | Thinking depends on language |
| Direction | Thought → language | Language → thought |
| Evidence for | Sinclair-de-Zwart: teaching conservation words did not teach conservation | Berinmo colour studies; Carmichael's labelled drawings changed recall |
| Weakness | Underplays how language helps organise and remember ideas | Strong version rejected: we can perceive things we cannot name |

- **Strong version** (determinism): language **determines** thought.
- **Weak version** (relativity): language **influences** thought. Better supported.

## Human vs animal communication

**Animal communication functions:** survival (alarm calls), reproduction (courtship), territory
(birdsong), food (bee dance).

**Von Frisch's bees:**
- **Round dance** = food is near; search close to the hive.
- **Waggle dance** = food is further away. **Angle** of waggle run relative to vertical = direction
  relative to the sun. **Length** of waggle = distance.

**Human language is different because of:** displacement (past, future, absent, imaginary),
creativity/productivity (unlimited new sentences), abstract ideas and plans.

## Non-verbal communication (NVC)

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| Eye contact | Regulates turn-taking, signals interest or attraction, shows emotion |
| Open posture | Uncrossed arms and legs; signals friendliness |
| Closed posture | Folded arms; can signal defensiveness |
| Postural echo | Mirroring someone's posture; signals rapport, increases liking |
| Touch | Shows support, affection or status |
| Personal space | Our "own" zone; varies by culture, status and gender (Hall's zones) |

## Explanations of NVC

**Darwin (evolutionary):** NVC is **innate** and evolved because it aided survival (fear face warns
others; bared teeth threaten).
*Evidence:* expressions appear very early in babies; blind-born children show similar expressions;
Ekman found basic expressions recognised in an isolated culture.

**Learned:** **Yuki (2007)** -- Japanese participants judged emotion more from the **eyes**,
Americans more from the **mouth**, in emoticons and faces, reflecting cultural norms about
controlling expression. Cultural differences in gestures and personal space also suggest learning.

**Best conclusion:** basic expressions may be innate; culture shapes how they are shown and read.

## Applying it to a scenario

For an application question, use a three-step chain: **name** the behaviour or theory, **say what it
means or communicates**, then **link it to a detail in the scenario**. For example: a pupil who
stands close to a new classmate may be following the personal-space norms of their home **culture**,
so the classmate steps back to restore their own comfortable distance. A correct term with no link to
the scenario usually earns only part of the marks.

## Exam traps

- Swapping Piaget and Sapir-Whorf.
- Angle of the waggle = **direction**, not distance.
- Yuki supports **learning**, not Darwin.
- Postural echo is mirroring, not "open posture".

## Self-test

1. What is the weak version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis?
2. What did Sinclair-de-Zwart's study suggest about language and thought?
3. In the waggle dance, what does the length of the waggle run show?
4. Give two of the functions of animal communication.
5. What is postural echo?
6. State one piece of evidence that NVC is innate.
7. What did Yuki find?

**Answers:** 1. Language influences (but does not determine) the way we think. 2. That thought comes
first: teaching words did not give children the understanding of conservation, supporting Piaget.
3. The distance to the food. 4. Any two: survival, reproduction, territory, food. 5. Unconsciously
mirroring another person's posture, which signals rapport. 6. Any one: babies show expressions very
early; blind-born children show similar expressions; basic expressions are recognised across
cultures. 7. Japanese participants used the eyes more, and American participants the mouth more, to
judge emotion in emoticons, suggesting NVC interpretation is learned through culture.
