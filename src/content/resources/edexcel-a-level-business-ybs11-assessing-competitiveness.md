This guide covers section **3.3.5 Assessing competitiveness** in Unit 3 (Business decisions and strategy) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. A business is competitive if it can match or beat its rivals on price, quality and service while earning enough to survive and grow. This section covers the financial and human resource measures used to judge that. The specification says the accounting ratios will not be supplied in the examination, so you must learn the formulas. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Business decisions and strategy study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-business-decisions-and-strategy/). Condensed notes are in the [Assessing competitiveness revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-assessing-competitiveness-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Assessing competitiveness practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-assessing-competitiveness-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Interpretation of financial statements:** the statement of comprehensive income (key information and stakeholder interest) and the statement of financial position (key information and stakeholder interest).
2. **Ratio analysis:** calculating gross profit margin, profit for the year margin, current ratio, acid test ratio, gearing and return on capital employed (ROCE); interpreting ratios to make decisions; limitations of ratio analysis.
3. **Human resources:** calculating and interpreting labour productivity, labour turnover and retention, and absenteeism; limitations; strategies to raise productivity and retention and cut turnover and absenteeism (financial rewards, employee share ownership, consultation and empowerment).

## The two financial statements

The **statement of comprehensive income** shows revenue, costs and profit over a period (a year). The **statement of financial position** shows what the business owns (assets), owes (liabilities) and is worth to its owners (equity) at one date.

Our example is Marlow Components. Figures are in $000.

| Statement of comprehensive income | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 4,000 | 4,400 |
| Cost of sales | 2,600 | 3,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **1,400** | **1,400** |
| Other operating expenses | 800 | 880 |
| **Operating profit** | **600** | **520** |
| Interest | 60 | 90 |
| **Profit for the year** | **540** | **430** |

| Statement of financial position | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | 2,400 | 3,000 |
| Inventory | 500 | 700 |
| Trade receivables | 400 | 560 |
| Cash | 100 | 40 |
| **Current assets** | **1,000** | **1,300** |
| Current liabilities | 600 | 1,000 |
| Non-current liabilities | 800 | 1,100 |
| **Net assets** | **2,000** | **2,200** |
| Share capital | 500 | 500 |
| Retained profits | 1,500 | 1,700 |
| **Total equity** | **2,000** | **2,200** |

**Stakeholder interest.** Owners and shareholders look at profit and return; managers at margins and cost control; lenders at profit to cover interest and at gearing; suppliers at liquidity (will they be paid?); employees at profit and stability; and competitors and the government at overall performance and tax.

## Profitability ratios

```
Gross profit margin         = gross profit / revenue x 100
Profit for the year margin  = profit for the year / revenue x 100
```

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 1,400 / 4,000 x 100 = **35.0%** | 1,400 / 4,400 x 100 = **31.8%** |
| Profit for the year margin | 540 / 4,000 x 100 = **13.5%** | 430 / 4,400 x 100 = **9.8%** |

Revenue grew 10%, but cost of sales grew 15.4% (2,600 to 3,000), so gross profit stayed flat at $1.4m and the gross margin fell by 3.2 percentage points. Overheads and interest then widened the fall in the profit for the year margin to 3.7 points. The cause may be dearer materials, price cutting to win sales, or inefficient production. Marlow has become less price-competitive in the sense that it keeps less from each sale.

## Liquidity ratios

```
Current ratio    = current assets / current liabilities
Acid test ratio  = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities
```

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | 1,000 / 600 = **1.67** | 1,300 / 1,000 = **1.30** |
| Acid test ratio | (1,000 - 500) / 600 = **0.83** | (1,300 - 700) / 1,000 = **0.60** |

Both ratios have fallen. An acid test below 1 means the business cannot cover its current liabilities from liquid assets alone, and 0.60 in Year 2 is a warning, since inventory and receivables have grown but cash has fallen to $40,000. The firm may rely on its overdraft or its suppliers' patience. Possible responses are tighter credit control, lower inventory, factoring, or longer-term finance.

## Gearing and return on capital employed

```
Capital employed = non-current liabilities + total equity
Gearing          = non-current liabilities / capital employed x 100
ROCE             = operating profit / capital employed x 100
```

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Capital employed | 800 + 2,000 = **2,800** | 1,100 + 2,200 = **3,300** |
| Gearing | 800 / 2,800 x 100 = **28.6%** | 1,100 / 3,300 x 100 = **33.3%** |
| ROCE | 600 / 2,800 x 100 = **21.4%** | 520 / 3,300 x 100 = **15.8%** |

Gearing has risen from 28.6% to 33.3% because Marlow borrowed to buy assets. A gearing below 50% is often called low-geared; a higher figure means more risk, since interest must be paid whatever the profit. Interest rose from $60,000 to $90,000, which is a sign of this. ROCE fell from 21.4% to 15.8%: the business employs $500,000 more capital but earns less operating profit. ROCE should be compared with interest rates and with rivals. If a bank loan costs 7%, 15.8% still leaves a margin, but the downward direction is a concern.

## Interpreting ratios to make decisions

A ratio has little meaning alone. Compare it with the business's own past, with rivals and with the industry average. Then ask what caused the change, what it implies for the business, and what the decision-makers should do. For Marlow, the evidence suggests an urgent need to control costs of sales (to restore margins), to manage working capital (inventory and receivables), and to be careful about further borrowing. A lender would be cautious about additional loans, while a supplier may tighten credit.

**Limitations of ratio analysis:** it uses past data, not the future; different firms use different accounting policies, so comparisons can mislead; ratios ignore non-financial factors (quality, brand, staff morale); a single year may be unusual or seasonal; inflation distorts comparisons across years; and a firm may "window dress" the year-end figures. Ratios are best used together and alongside qualitative evidence.

## Human resource measures

```
Labour productivity = output (or revenue) / number of employees
Labour turnover     = number of leavers / average number of employees x 100
Retention rate      = staff who stayed from the start / staff at the start x 100
Absenteeism         = days lost to absence / total working days available x 100
```

**Worked example.** Marlow employed an average of 250 people in Year 2. Revenue per employee = 4,400,000 / 250 = **$17,600**. Thirty employees left, so labour turnover = 30 / 250 x 100 = **12%**. Of 240 employees at the start of the year, 210 were still there at the end, so retention = 210 / 240 x 100 = **87.5%**. If staff could work 240 days each (250 x 240 = 60,000 days) and 2,700 days were lost to absence, absenteeism = 2,700 / 60,000 x 100 = **4.5%**.

High turnover and absence raise recruitment, training and temporary staffing costs, disrupt production and can lower quality, all of which hurt competitiveness. Whether 12% is high depends on the industry and on the comparison with rivals. **Limitations:** averages hide differences between departments; absence has legitimate causes; turnover is not always bad (the departure of poor performers may help); and productivity measured by revenue is affected by price changes.

**Strategies to raise productivity and retention and reduce turnover and absenteeism:**

- **Financial rewards:** performance-related pay, bonuses and profit-sharing motivate, but are costly and can cause a short-term focus.
- **Employee share ownership:** gives staff a stake in the success of the business, which builds loyalty, but its value depends on the share price and does little for lower-paid or short-term staff.
- **Consultation strategies:** staff councils and surveys give a voice and improve decisions, but are slow and raise expectations that must be met.
- **Empowerment strategies:** giving staff authority over how they do their work raises motivation and speeds decisions, but needs training and trust.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong profit figure: ROCE uses operating profit, while the net margin uses profit for the year.
- Forgetting that capital employed is non-current liabilities plus equity, not total assets.
- Putting current liabilities in the gearing numerator.
- Calling a ratio "good" or "bad" with no comparison.
- Leaving out inventory in the current ratio, or including it in the acid test.
- Treating labour turnover as always bad, or absenteeism as only a worker problem.

## Exam technique

**Calculate** answers need the formula, the figures and a result with a percentage sign or ratio form. For **analyse**, link the ratio to a cause and an effect on the business. **Assess** answers should use several ratios together, mention limitations, and judge competitiveness on balance, for instance "profitability is weakening, but liquidity is the more urgent threat".
