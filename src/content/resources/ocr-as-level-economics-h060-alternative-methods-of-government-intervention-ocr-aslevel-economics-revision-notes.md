These notes condense the specification's list of government interventions: "taxation, subsidies, government expenditure, price controls, buffer stock systems, public/private partnerships, legislation, regulation, tradable pollution permits, information provision, competition policy". They also cover the requirement to evaluate "the effectiveness of government intervention". The content sits in topic 2.8 Government intervention, Component 01, Microeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). Government failure, the other part of topic 2.8, has its own pages. ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Tax on spending; a specific tax is a fixed amount per unit, an ad valorem tax a percentage of price |
| Internalising an externality | Making the decision maker pay or receive the external cost or benefit |
| Subsidy | Payment per unit to producers that lowers their costs |
| Maximum price | Legal ceiling set below equilibrium |
| Minimum price | Legal floor set above equilibrium |
| Buffer stock | Agency buys and sells stocks to keep price within a band |
| Tradable pollution permit | Right to emit one unit, within an overall cap, that can be bought and sold |
| Public/private partnership | Private firm builds or runs a public project, paid by government over time |
| Legislation | Laws that ban or require actions |
| Regulation | Rules and standards enforced by an agency |
| Competition policy | Action against mergers and practices that reduce competition |

## Diagram rules

| Method | What moves |
|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Supply shifts up by the tax per unit |
| Subsidy | Supply shifts down by the subsidy per unit |
| Maximum price | Horizontal line **below** equilibrium: shortage |
| Minimum price | Horizontal line **above** equilibrium: surplus |
| Buffer stock | Band with floor and ceiling; agency buys at floor, sells at ceiling |
| Permits | Vertical supply of permits at the cap; permit price set by demand for permits |

## Worked reminders

Market: Qd = 120 − 4P and Qs = 6P − 30. Equilibrium P = £15, Q = 60.

- **£5 tax:** Qs = 6P − 60, so P = **£18**, Q = **48**. Buyers pay £3 more; sellers keep £13 (£2 less). Revenue = 5 × 48 = **£240**.
- **£2.50 subsidy:** Qs = 6P − 15, so P = **£13.50**, Q = **66**. Cost = 2.50 × 66 = **£165**.
- **Maximum price £12:** Qd = 72, Qs = 42: **shortage 30**.
- **Minimum price £18:** Qd = 48, Qs = 78: **surplus 30**.

**Tax incidence rule:** the side of the market that is less price elastic bears more of a tax.

**Permits rule:** with trading, the firm with the lowest cost of cutting pollution does most of the cutting, so the same cap is met at lower total cost.

## Strengths and limits

| Method | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Internalises cost; raises revenue | MEC hard to measure; regressive; little effect if demand inelastic |
| Subsidy | Raises output of beneficial goods | Opportunity cost; dependency; hard to size |
| Maximum price | Makes good cheaper for those who get it | Shortage, queues, black markets |
| Minimum price | Supports producer incomes or deters use | Surplus; cost of buying it up |
| Buffer stock | Stabilises prices and incomes | Storage cost; runs out of stock or money; band set too high |
| Permits | Certain cap; cheapest abatement | Setting the cap; monitoring; too many permits issued |
| Government expenditure | Direct provision of under-provided goods | Opportunity cost; possible inefficiency |
| Public/private partnership | Private expertise; spreads cost | Long, costly, inflexible contracts |
| Legislation and regulation | Clear and direct | Enforcement cost; no reward for exceeding standards |
| Information provision | Cheap; keeps choice | Often ignored |
| Competition policy | Lower prices, more choice | Slow; hard to judge cases |

## Five evaluation tests

1. **Effectiveness:** does it reach the target?
2. **Cost:** budget, administration, opportunity cost.
3. **Information:** can the right level be set?
4. **Side effects:** shortages, surpluses, black markets, relocation.
5. **Equity:** who gains and who pays?

## Exam traps

- Tax and subsidy shift **supply**, not demand.
- A maximum price above equilibrium, or a minimum price below it, has no effect.
- The tax revenue rectangle uses the **new** quantity.
- Subsidy cost = subsidy per unit × **new** quantity.
- Permits cap quantity; a tax sets a price. Do not mix them up.
- Name a specific weakness when evaluating, not "it may not work".
- Match the method to the cause of the market failure.

## Self-test

1. Which curve shifts when a specific tax is imposed, and in which direction?
2. Qd = 200 − 5P, Qs = 5P − 50. Find the equilibrium.
3. A £4 tax is added in question 2. Find the new price, quantity and tax revenue.
4. A maximum price of £20 is set in question 2's original market. Find the shortage.
5. Why does a maximum price lead to a shortage?
6. State two problems of running a buffer stock.
7. Firm X can cut emissions at £10 a tonne and Firm Y at £30. Which firm should do more cutting, and why?
8. Give one advantage of permits over a pollution tax.
9. Give one advantage of a tax over regulation.
10. What is a public/private partnership?
11. Who bears more of a tax when demand is more inelastic than supply?
12. Name the intervention that targets market power.

### Answers

1. Supply shifts up (to the left) by the amount of the tax.
2. 200 − 5P = 5P − 50, so **P = £25**, **Q = 75**.
3. Qs = 5(P − 4) − 50 = 5P − 70. 200 − 5P = 5P − 70, so **P = £27**, **Q = 65**; revenue = 4 × 65 = **£260**.
4. Qd = 100, Qs = 50: shortage **50**.
5. The price is held below equilibrium, so quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls.
6. Any two of: storage costs; perishable goods; agency may run out of stock or money; band set too high causes growing surpluses.
7. Firm X, because it can cut emissions more cheaply, so the cap is met at lower total cost; it can sell its spare permits to Y.
8. Permits fix the quantity of pollution with certainty.
9. A tax gives firms an incentive to keep cutting pollution and raises revenue.
10. A project in which a private firm builds or runs public infrastructure or services, paid by the government over many years.
11. Consumers.
12. Competition policy.
