This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **18.3 Selection**, the last part of topic 18, Variation and selection. It has six learning outcomes. Outcomes 1 to 3, on natural selection and selective breeding, are Core. Outcomes 4 to 6, on adaptation, antibiotic-resistant bacteria and the differences between natural and artificial selection, are Supplement content, so they are **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4).

The [variation and selection study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-variation-and-selection/) lists the steps for each process. This page shows how to apply them to unfamiliar organisms, which is what most selection questions demand, and how to write long answers that collect every marking point. Afterwards, use the [selection revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-selection-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [selection practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-selection-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## 18.3.1 Natural selection as a five-link chain

The syllabus lists five ideas, (a) to (e). A long answer earns a mark for each one you apply to the organism in the question. Think of them as a chain that must not break:

1. **Variation:** individuals in the population differ genetically, for example in fur colour.
2. **Over-production:** they produce more offspring than the environment can support.
3. **Struggle for survival:** there is competition for resources such as food, water, space and mates; predators and disease also kill many individuals.
4. **Better adapted individuals survive and reproduce:** those with a feature suited to the environment are more likely to survive long enough to breed.
5. **Alleles passed on:** they pass the alleles for that feature to their offspring, so the allele becomes more common in the population over many generations.

### Worked example 1: applying the chain

**Question:** A population of mice lives on pale sand. Lava from a volcano covers the area with dark rock. Owls hunt the mice by sight. After many years, most mice in the area have dark fur. Explain how natural selection led to this change. [5]

**Model answer:** There was variation in fur colour in the mouse population, caused by different alleles (1). The mice produced more offspring than could survive (1). Owls could see pale mice more easily on dark rock, so more pale mice were eaten; the mice also competed for food (1). Dark mice were better camouflaged, so they were more likely to survive and reproduce (1). They passed on the allele for dark fur to their offspring, so over many generations the allele became more common (1).

**Why it scores:** every one of the five ideas is applied to mice and owls. A general description of natural selection that never mentions fur, rock or owls would lose marks for application.

### What natural selection does not do

- It does not make individuals change during their life. A pale mouse cannot turn dark.
- It does not create the useful allele. The allele was already in the population because of earlier mutation (sub-topic 18.1).
- It does not act "in order to" help a species. The environment simply removes less suited individuals more often.

A well-known real case is the peppered moth in Britain: during the industrial revolution, the dark form became common in areas where soot darkened tree trunks, because pale moths were easier for birds to see there.

## 18.3.2 and 18.3.3 Selective breeding

Selective breeding is carried out by humans. The syllabus describes three steps, repeated:

1. **Select** individuals with the desirable feature.
2. **Cross** them to produce the next generation.
3. **Select offspring** that show the desirable feature, and breed from them.
4. **Repeat over many generations.**

### Why many generations?

- Not all offspring inherit the desired alleles from both parents, so some do not show the feature.
- Many features, such as milk yield or grain size, show continuous variation and are affected by several genes, so improvement happens gradually.
- Breeders want a variety in which all the offspring show the feature reliably.

### Worked example 2: applying to a given context

**Question:** A farmer wants sheep that produce more wool. Outline how she could use selective breeding to do this. [3]

**Model answer:** Choose the rams and ewes that produce the most wool (1). Breed these together (1). From their lambs, choose those that grow the most wool when adult and breed them; repeat this over many generations (1).

**Applying to other contexts:**

| Context | Desirable feature to select |
|---|---|
| Wheat in a windy region | short, strong stems that do not fall over |
| Tomatoes | resistance to a disease, larger fruit, better flavour |
| Dairy cattle | high milk yield (selected using the yield of a bull's mothers and daughters, because bulls produce no milk) |
| Dogs for guiding blind people | calm temperament, willingness to learn |
| Chickens | more eggs per year, faster growth |

The dairy example is a favourite "suggest" question: a bull cannot be chosen by its own milk yield, so breeders look at the yield of the cows related to it.

## 18.3.4 Adaptation as a process (Extended only)

> Adaptation is the **process**, resulting from natural selection, by which **populations** become more suited to their environment **over many generations**.

The three marked words are the ones examiners look for. Compare the terms:

| Term | What it is |
|---|---|
| Adaptive feature (18.2) | a feature of an individual |
| Adaptation (18.3) | a process happening to a population over time |

A sentence such as "the mouse adapted to the dark rock" is wrong; "the population became adapted over many generations" is right.

## 18.3.5 Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (Extended only)

This is natural selection with bacteria as the population and an antibiotic as the selecting factor.

1. In a population of bacteria there is variation. A random **mutation** gives some bacteria an allele for **resistance**.
2. An antibiotic is used. **Non-resistant bacteria are killed**; resistant bacteria survive.
3. The resistant bacteria have **less competition** for nutrients and space.
4. They **reproduce** rapidly by asexual reproduction and pass the resistance allele to their offspring.
5. Over many generations a **strain** of resistant bacteria develops, and the antibiotic no longer works against it.

Because bacteria reproduce so quickly, many generations pass in a short time, so resistance spreads fast. This links to topic 10: using antibiotics **only when essential** reduces the number of occasions on which resistant bacteria are selected, which limits the development of strains such as MRSA. Revise that link in the [diseases and immunity study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-diseases-and-immunity/).

## 18.3.6 Natural and artificial selection compared (Extended only)

| | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| What does the selecting | the environment (predators, climate, disease, competition) | humans |
| Features selected | those that help survival and reproduction | those that humans find useful or desirable |
| Speed | usually slow | usually faster, because only chosen individuals breed |
| Result | populations better suited to their environment | breeds and varieties suited to human needs; some may survive poorly in the wild |

An "outline the differences" question needs matched pairs: "in natural selection the environment selects, whereas in artificial selection humans select".

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out the over-production of offspring or the struggle for survival from a natural selection answer.
- Writing "the fittest survive" with no explanation of why or what feature helps.
- Saying the environment causes the mutation. The variation existed first.
- Ending a natural selection answer at "they survive"; the marks continue with "reproduce and pass on alleles".
- In selective breeding, forgetting "over many generations" or "select the offspring".
- (Extended) Calling an individual's feature an "adaptation" when the question asks about the process.
- (Extended) Saying bacteria "become immune" to antibiotics. Bacteria become resistant; immunity is a term for the body's defence against pathogens.

## Exam technique for 18.3 command words

- **Describe** natural selection: five ideas, each applied to the organism in the question.
- **Outline** selective breeding: select, cross, select offspring, repeat over many generations; name the feature.
- **Explain** a change in a population: use the five-link chain and end with alleles becoming more common.
- **Suggest**: choose a sensible feature and selecting factor for the context given.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is antibiotic resistance Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Describing the development of antibiotic-resistant strains as an example of natural selection is Supplement content, so it is Extended only.

**Why does selective breeding take many generations?**
Not every offspring inherits all the desired alleles, and many useful features are affected by several genes, so breeders must keep selecting and crossing until the feature appears reliably.

**Does natural selection change individual organisms?**
No. Individuals keep the features they inherited. Natural selection changes how common alleles are in a population over many generations.
