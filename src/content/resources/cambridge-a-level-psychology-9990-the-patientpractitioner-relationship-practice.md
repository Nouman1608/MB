**This practice set covers topic 3.1, The patient–practitioner relationship, in Specialist Option 3 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All clinics, patients and practitioners are fictional.

Related: [The patient–practitioner relationship study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-the-patientpractitioner-relationship/) · [The patient–practitioner relationship revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-the-patientpractitioner-relationship-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by a patient-centred (sharing) consultation style. **[2]**

**2.** Describe research into practitioner clothing as non-verbal communication. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the three stages of delay in seeking treatment identified by Safer et al. (1979). **[3]**

**4.** Compare Munchausen syndrome and malingering. **[4]**

**5.** Diagnosis and consultation style affect patients.

**(a)** Describe the study by Savage and Armstrong (1990). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate the study by Savage and Armstrong (1990), including a discussion of cultural differences. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** At Riverside Clinic, a doctor tells a patient: "Your FBC shows mild anaemia; we'll check your ferritin." The patient nods but leaves confused.

**(a)** Using research into verbal communication, explain why the patient is confused. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** ways the doctor could improve communication. **[2]**

**7.** A new screening test told Mr Lee he had diabetes, but later tests showed he did not. The same test told Ms Ortiz she was healthy, but she was later found to have diabetes.

**(a)** Identify the type of diagnostic error made for each patient. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why a doctor might accept more false positives for a serious but treatable illness. **[2]**

**8.** Lena has had a painless lump for three months. She has not told anyone and says she cannot afford time off work.

**(a)** Identify **two** components of the health belief model shown in this description. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** cue to action that might lead Lena to see a doctor. **[1]**

**9.** A psychologist plans to replicate McKinstry and Wang (1991) using photographs of nurses in different uniforms.

**(a)** Write a suitable directional hypothesis. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how she could control **one** extraneous variable in the photographs. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why her findings may lack ecological validity. **[2]**

**10.** In Savage and Armstrong (1990), 89% of patients returned the first questionnaire but only 58% returned the second. Explain why this matters for the conclusions drawn from the second questionnaire. **[3]**

**11.** A hospital records a man who has attended six different emergency departments in a year with dramatic symptoms that tests cannot confirm. He has many surgical scars and leaves when staff question him.

**(a)** Identify **two** supporting features of Munchausen syndrome in this description. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why a case study is appropriate for researching this condition. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. The practitioner invites the patient's views and concerns, using open questions (1), and decisions about diagnosis and treatment are shared, with the patient taking an active part (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. McKinstry and Wang (1991) showed patients photographs of doctors in different styles of dress, from formal to casual (1). Patients rated which they preferred and how confident they would be in each doctor (1). Formal dress was generally preferred, especially by older patients (1).

**3.** One mark per stage. Appraisal delay: time taken to decide a symptom is a sign of illness (1). Illness delay: time from deciding one is ill to deciding to seek help (1). Utilisation delay: time from deciding to seek help to actually attending (1).

**4.** Up to 4 marks. Similarity: both involve feigning or exaggerating illness (1), so both lead to inappropriate use of health services (1). Difference: in Munchausen syndrome the motive is to gain medical attention and the sick role (1), whereas a malingerer is motivated by an external reward, such as money or time off work (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Aim: to compare patient satisfaction with directing and sharing consultation styles (1). Field experiment in an inner London general practice with one GP (1). 359 patients were randomly allocated to a directing or a sharing style for the part of the consultation about treatment, advice and prognosis (1). Satisfaction questionnaires were completed immediately and one week later (1). Patients given the directing style reported higher satisfaction on almost all measures (1), especially those with physical problems and those given a prescription; there was no significant difference for psychological or chronic problems (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named issue, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Cultural differences*: patients in a 1990 London practice may have expected an authoritative doctor; in cultures or times with greater emphasis on patient autonomy, the sharing style may be preferred, so findings may not generalise across cultures.
- *Strengths*: random allocation and a real setting give internal and ecological validity; large sample.
- *Weaknesses*: one GP limits generalisation; response rate fell to 58%; satisfaction is self-reported and may be affected by politeness towards the doctor.
- *Individual and situational explanations*: preference varied with type of problem.
- *Application*: doctors may adapt style to the patient and problem.
- *Ethics*: patients did not choose their style, though both styles were normal practice.
- *Conclusion*: challenges the assumption that sharing is always best, though limited in scope.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. The doctor used medical terms ("FBC", "ferritin") (1); McKinlay (1975) found patients often do not understand such terms, so information is not communicated (1).

**(b)** One mark each: use plain language, e.g. "a blood test shows you are low in iron" (1); check understanding by asking the patient to explain it back (1). Credit written information.

**7.**

**(a)** One mark each. Mr Lee: false positive, diagnosed with an illness he does not have (1). Ms Ortiz: false negative, her illness was missed (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Missing a serious illness could delay treatment and be life-threatening (1), whereas a false positive can be checked with further tests, so its costs are smaller (1).

**8.**

**(a)** One mark each: low perceived severity, because the lump is painless (1); perceived barriers, because she cannot afford time off work (1).

**(b)** One mark for a plausible cue, e.g. the lump growing or becoming painful, or a health campaign about lumps (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. One for variables, one for direction, e.g. "Patients will give higher confidence ratings to nurses in a formal uniform than to nurses in casual clothes" (2).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Use the same model in every photograph (1), so that differences in ratings are due to clothing, not the person's appearance, age or expression (1). Credit same pose, background or lighting.

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Rating a photograph is not the same as being treated by a real nurse (1); in real care, voice, manner and behaviour also influence confidence (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. Fewer than two-thirds replied, so the second sample may not represent all patients (1). Those who replied may differ, e.g. be more satisfied or have more time (1), so differences between styles at one week may be biased and less valid (1).

**11.**

**(a)** One mark each: many surgical scars (1); leaving when questioned (evasive behaviour or self-discharge) (1). Moving between hospitals is an essential feature, so credit it only if the candidate identifies it as such.

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. The condition is rare, so few cases are available for large studies (1), and a case study gives detailed, idiographic information about the person's history and behaviour (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 4, describing both conditions separately without a clear comparison.
- In question 5(a), stating that patients preferred the sharing style.
- In question 9(a), writing a hypothesis without a direction.
