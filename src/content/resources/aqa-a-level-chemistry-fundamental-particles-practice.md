These questions cover section **3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for AS and A-level exams from June 2016 onwards. Everything here is AS content and sits in Paper 1. Where a calculation needs a relative mass for the electron, the question gives you 1/1840.

## Questions

**1.** Give the relative mass and the relative charge for each of three particles: the proton, the neutron and the electron. **[3]**

**2.** Describe where the protons, neutrons and electrons are found in an atom. **[2]**

**3.** One particle has a relative charge of 0 and a relative mass of 1. Another has a relative charge of −1.

**(a)** Name the two particles. **[2]**
**(b)** State roughly how many times heavier the first particle is than the second. **[1]**

**4.** An aluminium ion contains 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.

**(a)** Give the relative charge of the ion. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the relative mass of its nucleus. **[1]**
**(c)** A different ion has 16 protons and 18 electrons. State its relative charge. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the number of neutrons does not affect the charge of a particle. **[1]**

**5.** A lithium atom has 3 protons, 4 neutrons and 3 electrons. Calculate the percentage of the mass of the atom that is due to its electrons. Use 1/1840 for the relative mass of an electron and give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[3]**

**6.** In an invented experiment, 20 000 alpha particles are fired at thin metal foil. 19 984 pass through with almost no change of direction, 14 are deflected slightly, and 2 bounce back towards the source.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the particles that bounce back. **[1]**
**(b)** State what the 19 984 particles show about the structure of the atom. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what the 2 particles that bounce back show about the structure of the atom. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why an atom has no overall charge although its nucleus is positively charged. **[3]**

**8.** Before the nucleus was discovered, one model of the atom pictured a sphere of positive charge with the electrons scattered through it.

**(a)** State one observation from alpha particle scattering that this model could not explain. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the model could not explain it. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the nuclear model explains it. **[1]**

**9.** A helium atom has two protons and two electrons, but its mass is about four times that of a hydrogen atom, which has one proton. Explain how the discovery of the neutron resolved this problem. **[3]**

**10.** A magnesium atom has 12 protons, 12 neutrons and 12 electrons. It forms the ion Mg²⁺. Use 1/1840 for the relative mass of an electron.

**(a)** Calculate the relative mass of the atom, to 5 decimal places. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the relative mass of the ion, to 5 decimal places. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the difference between the two, and express it as a percentage of the mass of the atom, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(d)** State what your answer shows about using the mass of an atom for its ion. **[1]**

**11.** A student writes: "A copper atom has 29 electrons, so the electrons must make up a large part of its mass." A copper atom in this question has 29 protons, 34 neutrons and 29 electrons.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the mass of the atom that is due to its electrons, to 3 significant figures. **[3]**
**(b)** State how many electrons have the same total mass as one proton. **[1]**
**(c)** Use your answers to evaluate the student's statement. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe what the rest of the volume of the atom contains. **[2]**

**12.** A calcium atom has 20 protons, 20 neutrons and 20 electrons.

**(a)** State the total relative charge of the nucleus. **[1]**
**(b)** The atom loses two electrons. State the number of electrons in the ion, its relative charge and its relative mass to the nearest whole number. **[3]**
**(c)** Many scientists repeated alpha scattering experiments before the nuclear model was accepted. Explain why this was needed. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Proton has mass 1 and charge +1 [1]. Neutron has mass 1 and charge 0 [1]. Electron has mass 1/1840 and charge −1 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* each particle needs both values, and the sign on the electron's charge is part of the answer. A mass of zero for the electron is a different claim from a very small mass.

**2.** The protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the centre [1]. The electrons are outside the nucleus [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "in the shells" is a weaker answer than "outside the nucleus" unless you also say the protons and neutrons are in the nucleus; give a location for every particle.

**3. (a)** The first particle is a neutron [1] and the second is an electron [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** About **1840** times [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* a mass ratio needs a number. Say "about 1840 times", not "much heavier".

**4. (a)** Charge = 13 − 10 = **+3** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Relative mass of the nucleus = 13 + 14 = **27** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Charge = 16 − 18 = **2−** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Neutrons carry no charge, so they add mass but nothing to the total charge [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* write the charge as a number with its sign (3+ or +3). "3" alone loses the information that the ion is positive. Show protons minus electrons so the method is visible.

**5.** Mass of 3 electrons = 3 ÷ 1840 = 0.00163 [1]. Mass of atom = 7 + 0.00163 = 7.00163 [1]. Percentage = 0.00163 ÷ 7.00163 × 100 = **0.0233%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the nucleus mass is the protons plus the neutrons (3 + 4 = 7), and it is easy to forget the neutrons. Keep spare digits in the middle of a calculation and round only at the end.

**6. (a)** 2 ÷ 20 000 × 100 = **0.01%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Most of the atom is empty space [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Alpha particles are positive, so something positive repelled them [1]. Only a small, dense, heavy region could turn a particle back, so the positive charge and nearly all the mass must be packed into a tiny nucleus [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* link each observation to the conclusion it supports. Listing the three observations without saying what each shows describes the experiment and does not use it as evidence.

**7.** The nucleus contains protons, which are +1, and neutrons, which are 0, so it is positive overall [1]. The electrons outside the nucleus are −1 each [1]. In an atom the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the charges cancel [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the statement "equal numbers of protons and electrons" is the key idea. Without saying that the charges are equal and opposite, the answer only restates the question.

**8. (a)** Some alpha particles were deflected through large angles or bounced back [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** In the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread through the whole atom [1], so there is no concentrated charge to repel an alpha particle strongly enough to turn it back [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The nuclear model puts the positive charge and nearly all the mass inside a small nucleus, which can turn back a particle that meets it head-on [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain" asks for a reason. "The model was wrong" does not say why; state what the model predicted and how the observation differs.

**9.** Two protons account for a charge of +2 and a mass of only about 2 units [1]. The atom's mass is about 4, so about 2 more mass units are needed without extra charge [1]. The neutron has a mass similar to the proton and no charge, so two neutrons in the nucleus supply the missing mass [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* both properties are needed for the argument: no charge, so it does not change the +2, and a mass of 1, so it fills the gap. An answer that gives only one of the two usually leaves the argument incomplete.

**10. (a)** Nucleus = 12 + 12 = 24 [1]. Electrons = 12 ÷ 1840 = 0.00652, so the atom is **24.00652** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The ion has 10 electrons: 10 ÷ 1840 = 0.00543, so the mass is **24.00543** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Difference = 2 ÷ 1840 = 0.00109 [1]. Percentage = 0.00109 ÷ 24.00652 × 100 = **0.00453%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The change is so small that the atom and ion can be treated as having the same mass [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the difference is two electrons, so check that you used 10 and 12 electrons and not the charge by mistake. Quote the number of decimal places the question asks for.

**11. (a)** Mass of 29 electrons = 29 ÷ 1840 = 0.0158 [1]. Mass of atom = 63 + 0.0158 = 63.0158 [1]. Percentage = 0.0158 ÷ 63.0158 × 100 = **0.0250%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** **1840** electrons [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The electrons contribute about 0.025% of the mass, so the student's statement is not supported [1]. Number of electrons does not decide mass because each is so light, and nearly all the mass comes from the protons and neutrons [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The rest of the atom is mostly empty space [1], containing the moving electrons only [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* an evaluation should give a verdict backed by your own number. Quoting 0.0250% and then saying why it matters is stronger than a general statement that electrons are light.

**12. (a)** Only protons carry positive charge, so the nucleus has a charge of **+20** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The ion has 18 electrons [1]. Its charge is 20 − 18 = **2+** [1]. The relative mass is 20 + 20 = 40 plus a negligible 18 ÷ 1840, so **40** to the nearest whole number [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** New evidence may come from an error or a one-off result, so repeating by other scientists shows whether the result can be relied on [1]. A model is only accepted if it explains the repeated results and the earlier evidence [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers to part (c) say what repetition achieves (reliability, independent confirmation) rather than saying only that scientists "check". State the charge in part (b) with its sign.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting only one property of a particle when the question asks for charge and mass.
- Leaving the sign off the electron's charge, or writing an ion's charge without + or −.
- Writing the relative mass of the electron as 0, or as 1.
- Placing electrons in the nucleus.
- Forgetting the neutrons when calculating the mass of the nucleus.
- Rounding in the middle of a percentage calculation, then losing the answer.
- Describing the alpha scattering results but not stating what each result shows about the atom.
- Saying the old model was "wrong" without naming the evidence that did not fit.
- Writing "equal charges" instead of "equal and opposite charges" when explaining why atoms are neutral.
- Using neutron number to work out charge.
