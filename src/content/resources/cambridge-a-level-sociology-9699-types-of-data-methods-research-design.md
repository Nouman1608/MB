Section 2.1, *Types of data, methods and research design*, is the first of three methods sub-topics in Paper 1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It supplies the toolkit you use in the Paper 1 question that asks you to evaluate a research method, and it underpins every methods essay.

This guide adds depth to the [research methods, design and issues guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-research-methods-design-and-issues/), which covers 2.1 and 2.3 together. Revise with the [2.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-types-of-data-methods-research-design-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-types-of-data-methods-research-design-practice/).

## What the syllabus lists

- The differences between primary and secondary sources of data and between quantitative and qualitative data.
- The strengths and limitations of secondary sources: official statistics, personal documents, digital content and media sources.
- The strengths and limitations of quantitative methods: questionnaires, structured interviews, experiments and content analysis.
- The strengths and limitations of qualitative methods: overt and covert participant and non-participant observation, unstructured interviews, semi-structured interviews and group interviews.
- Stages of research design: research strategy, research questions and hypotheses, sampling frames, sampling techniques, pilot studies, operationalisation, conducting research and interpreting results.

## Two distinctions that organise everything

**Primary or secondary?** Primary data is collected by the researcher for the current study. Secondary data already exists, produced by someone else for another purpose. Re-analysing an existing survey is *secondary analysis*: the numbers are not new, but the questions asked of them are.

**Quantitative or qualitative?** Quantitative data is numerical and suits measuring how often, how many and how strongly. Qualitative data is words, images and observations, and suits understanding meanings and experiences.

The two distinctions cross: a diary is secondary and qualitative, an existing government survey is secondary and quantitative, and your own unstructured interviews are primary and qualitative. Content analysis can even turn qualitative material (news articles) into quantitative data (counts).

## Secondary sources in more depth

**Official statistics** are produced by governments and their agencies. Sociologists separate **hard** statistics, such as births and deaths, which are recorded fairly completely, from **soft** statistics, such as crime, which depend on whether events are noticed, reported and recorded. Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) used official suicide rates to argue that suicide varies with social integration. Jack Douglas (*The Social Meanings of Suicide*, 1967) replied that the rates reflect coroners' and families' interpretations, so they cannot simply be taken as facts.

**Personal documents** include diaries, letters, memoirs and photographs. John Scott (*A Matter of Record*, 1990) proposed four tests for any document:

1. **Authenticity**: is it genuine and complete?
2. **Credibility**: is the author sincere and accurate?
3. **Representativeness**: is it typical of the documents that once existed?
4. **Meaning**: can the researcher understand what it meant to its author and readers?

**Digital content** includes social media posts, forums, blogs and search data. It is plentiful, cheap and often unprompted, so it can capture opinion as it happens. But online users are not representative of a whole population, identities can be faked, and using public posts without consent raises ethical questions.

**Media sources** show how issues are framed, but they are produced to attract audiences, so they tell you about the media's selection of news as much as about events.

## Quantitative methods in more depth

**Questionnaires.** Closed questions with fixed answers are quick to code and compare; open questions allow respondents to answer in their own words but are harder to analyse. Scales, such as "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree", measure strength of opinion. Questionnaires can be posted, handed out or completed online; online versions are cheap and fast but reach only those who are online and willing. Low response rates make the sample less representative.

**Structured interviews** are questionnaires read out by an interviewer. Response rates are higher and questions can be clarified, but the interviewer's age, sex, ethnicity or manner can affect answers (the **interviewer effect**), and respondents may give socially acceptable answers (**social desirability**).

**Experiments** test cause and effect by changing one variable and holding others constant.
- *Laboratory* experiments give control but are artificial.
- *Field* experiments take place in real settings but give less control.
- The **Hawthorne effect**, named after the Western Electric factory studies led by Elton Mayo in the 1920s and 1930s, describes people changing behaviour because they know they are studied.
- Sociologists use experiments less than psychologists because social behaviour has many causes and manipulating people raises ethical problems.

**Content analysis** counts features of media or documents using a **coding frame**, for example the number of women and men quoted as experts in a month of news reports. It is systematic and can be repeated, but the categories reflect the researcher's choices, and counting how often something appears does not show what it means to the audience.

## Qualitative methods in more depth

| Method | What it involves | Example of use |
|---|---|---|
| Overt participant observation | Joining a group with its knowledge | Eileen Barker's *The Making of a Moonie* (1984) studied a new religious movement openly over several years |
| Covert participant observation | Joining a group in disguise | James Patrick's *A Glasgow Gang Observed* (1973) studied a violent gang without revealing his identity, and left when the risks grew |
| Non-participant observation | Watching without joining in, openly or discreetly | Observing classroom interaction from the back of a room |
| Unstructured interview | A guided conversation with no fixed questions | Exploring experiences of migration or illness |
| Semi-structured interview | Core questions with freedom to probe | Comparing teachers' views while allowing follow-ups |
| Group interview | Several people interviewed together, often called a focus group | How young people talk about social media together |

Key evaluation points: participant observation gives insight into meanings but risks **going native**, where the researcher over-identifies with the group; covert work gives natural behaviour but involves deception; group interviews show how opinions form in interaction, but dominant members can silence others.

## Research design, stage by stage

### Worked example: designing a study

*Topic: Does heavy social media use affect teenagers' self-esteem?*

1. **Research strategy.** Choose an overall approach. A positivist design might use a large questionnaire survey to look for a correlation; an interpretivist design might use semi-structured interviews to explore how teenagers experience comparison online. Many studies mix both.
2. **Research question or hypothesis.** A research question is open: "How do teenagers describe the effect of social media on how they see themselves?" A hypothesis is a testable prediction: "Teenagers who spend more than three hours a day on social media report lower self-esteem than those who spend less."
3. **Operationalisation.** Turn concepts into measurable indicators. "Heavy use" could be hours per day from screen-time records; "self-esteem" could be scores on an agreed self-esteem scale. Different operational definitions can produce different results.
4. **Sampling frame.** A list of the population, such as the registers of five secondary schools, used with permission. A frame that leaves out home-schooled teenagers or pupils absent long-term is incomplete.
5. **Sampling technique.**
   - *Random*: every pupil has an equal chance of selection.
   - *Systematic*: every tenth name on the list.
   - *Stratified*: the sample mirrors the population by year group and sex.
   - *Quota*: the researcher fills set numbers of each category without a list.
   - *Snowball*: participants recommend others, useful for hard-to-reach groups.
   - *Opportunity*: whoever is available, quick but often biased.
6. **Pilot study.** Test the questionnaire on a small group to find confusing questions, check timing and spot ethical problems.
7. **Conducting the research.** Obtain consent from pupils and, for under-age participants, from parents and schools; protect anonymity; keep conditions consistent.
8. **Interpreting results.** Analyse patterns, check for alternative explanations (a correlation does not prove that social media *causes* low self-esteem; unhappy teenagers may use it more), and consider how representative the sample was.

In an exam, linking each stage to a concept is what turns description into analysis: stratified sampling improves representativeness; a pilot improves validity and reliability; consent addresses ethics.

## Exam technique for 2.1

- In a question asking you to evaluate a method, give strengths *and* limitations unless told otherwise, and apply each one to the research context in the question.
- Use the concepts from 2.3 by name: validity, reliability, representativeness, objectivity and ethics.
- Link methods to theory: positivists favour reliable, quantitative methods; interpretivists favour valid, qualitative ones (developed in 2.2).
- Avoid generic points such as "it is expensive" unless you say why cost matters for this study.

## Common mistakes

- Calling all secondary data quantitative.
- Treating a sampling frame as the same as the sample.
- Writing "observation" without saying participant or non-participant, overt or covert.
- Confusing a research question with a hypothesis.
- Listing strengths of a method without linking them to a research concept or context.
