## 4.5.1: the meaning of unemployment

A person is unemployed if they are able and willing to work at the going wage rate, are actively looking and have no job. People in work, people who cannot work and people who choose not to look (full-time students, carers, the retired) are not unemployed. The employed plus the unemployed make up the **labour force**.

**Worked example 1.** Country A has a working-age population of 26.0 million. There are 18.4 million employed and 1.1 million unemployed.

```
Labour force       = 18.4 + 1.1 = 19.5 million
Unemployment rate  = 1.1 ÷ 19.5 × 100 = 5.6%
Participation rate = 19.5 ÷ 26.0 × 100 = 75.0%
Not in labour force = 26.0 − 19.5 = 6.5 million
```

The base is the labour force, not the whole population.

## 4.5.2: measures and measurement difficulties

There are two common approaches. A **labour force survey** asks a sample of households about their situation, using an agreed definition of looking for work. A **claimant count** counts people who register for unemployment benefit. The survey allows comparison across countries and years. The claimant count is quick but depends on benefit rules.

**Worked example 2.** In Country A the survey gives 1.1 million unemployed and the claimant count gives 0.78 million, 70.9% of the survey figure. The gap may arise because some jobless people are ineligible for benefit or have not registered.

| Difficulty | Effect on the measured rate |
|---|---|
| Discouraged workers stop searching, so leave the labour force | Understates unemployment |
| Hidden unemployment: people who would work if jobs existed | Understates it |
| Underemployment: part-timers who want full-time hours count as employed | Understates it |
| Informal or illegal work by people who claim benefit | Overstates it |
| Sampling error and differing definitions between countries and years | Either way |
| One national average hides regions, age groups and ethnic groups | Hides detail |

**Worked example 3: a rate that falls without more jobs.** In the following year employment in Country A stays at 18.4 million, but 0.2 million jobless people give up searching. Unemployment is now 0.9 million.

```
Labour force = 18.4 + 0.9 = 19.3 million
Rate         = 0.9 ÷ 19.3 × 100 = 4.7%   (down from 5.6%)
```

Those 0.2 million people still have no job. Counting them as unemployed would give 1.1 million out of 19.5 million, which is 5.6% again. Ask who has been left out.

## 4.5.3: causes and types

| Type | Cause | Illustration (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Frictional | Time taken to match job-seekers with vacancies; imperfect information | A graduate searches for six weeks |
| Structural | A lasting mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs on offer, after an industry declines | A coal-mining town loses its mine; jobs exist on the coast needing different skills |
| Cyclical (demand-deficient) | A fall in aggregate demand, so firms cut output and labour | A downturn cuts demand for cars |
| Seasonal | Demand for labour varies with the time of year | Ski instructors in summer |
| Technological | Machines or software replace workers | Self-service tills replace cashiers |

Technological unemployment is structural when displaced workers lack the new skills. Frictional unemployment is short. Structural unemployment can last years because retraining takes time and housing costs block moves.

**Worked example 4.** A region has 600 unemployed people: 90 frictional, 210 structural, 150 cyclical, 100 seasonal and 50 technological. These sum to 600, so the shares are 15%, 35%, 25%, 16.7% and 8.3%. A boom would mainly remove the cyclical 25%, not the structural 35%.

**Cyclical unemployment in AD/AS words.** With price level on the vertical axis and real output on the horizontal axis, aggregate demand shifts left from AD1 to AD2 along an upward-sloping SRAS curve. The equilibrium moves to lower output, below the level where all willing workers are employed. Firms need fewer workers, so employment falls.

## 4.5.4: consequences of unemployment

**Worked example 5.** In Country A each unemployed worker would produce 30,000 cu (currency units) a year. The government pays 6,000 cu in benefits and loses 4,500 cu in tax per person. With 1.1 million unemployed:

```
Lost output      = 1.1m × 30,000 = 33,000 million cu
Benefits paid    = 1.1m × 6,000  =  6,600 million cu
Tax forgone      = 1.1m × 4,500  =  4,950 million cu
Fiscal cost      = 6,600 + 4,950 = 11,550 million cu
```

| Group | Consequences |
|---|---|
| Unemployed people | Lower income, poverty, stress, loss of skills, harder to find work as the spell lengthens |
| Firms | Weaker demand from households with less income; more choice of workers, less wage pressure |
| Government | Higher benefit spending, lower tax receipts, more spending on social services |
| The economy | Output below potential (inside the production possibility curve), wasted resources, greater inequality, social problems |

**Evaluation.** The cost depends on type and duration. Frictional unemployment is brief and can match workers to better jobs. Long-term structural unemployment does the most lasting harm. Impact also depends on benefit levels and on whether the unemployed are concentrated in one region.

## 4.6.1: inflation, deflation and disinflation

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Inflation | A persistent increase in the average level of prices across the economy |
| Deflation | A persistent decrease in the average level of prices across the economy |
| Disinflation | Inflation that is still positive but lower than before, so the price level climbs more slowly |

One month of higher prices does not make inflation. Disinflation is not deflation, because the price level still rises.

## 4.6.2: the consumer price index

The CPI measures the change in the cost of a typical household's spending. A statistics office surveys households to find what they buy, groups it into categories and gives each a **weight** equal to its share of spending. It collects prices, builds an index for each category with the base year equal to 100, and combines them using the weights.

**Worked example 6.** In year 0 all indices are 100.

| Category | Weight | Index, year 1 | Index, year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 30 | 108 | 113 |
| Housing | 35 | 104 | 108 |
| Transport | 15 | 112 | 106 |
| Recreation | 20 | 100 | 103 |

```
CPI year 1 = (30×108 + 35×104 + 15×112 + 20×100) ÷ 100 = 105.6
CPI year 2 = (30×113 + 35×108 + 15×106 + 20×103) ÷ 100 = 108.2
Inflation, year 1 = 5.6%
Inflation, year 2 = (108.2 − 105.6) ÷ 105.6 × 100 = 2.5%
```

Inflation fell from 5.6% to 2.5% while the CPI kept rising: this is disinflation. If the CPI then fell to 107.1, the change would be (107.1 − 108.2) ÷ 108.2 × 100 = −1.0%, which is deflation.

**Difficulties.** The basket and weights go out of date as habits change. A fixed basket ignores switching to cheaper items. Quality improvements and new products are hard to price. An average household does not exist: pensioners, rural families and high earners buy different baskets. Informal-market prices may be missed.

## 4.6.3: nominal and real data

**Nominal** (money) values are measured at the prices of the time. **Real** values are adjusted for price changes, so they show purchasing power. Real value = nominal value ÷ price index × 100.

**Worked example 7.** A worker earns 2,400 cu a month in year 0, 2,520 in year 1 and 2,640 in year 2. Use the CPI from example 6.

```
Real wage year 1 = 2,520 ÷ 105.6 × 100 = 2,386.4 cu   (nominal +5.0%, real −0.6%)
Real wage year 2 = 2,640 ÷ 108.2 × 100 = 2,439.9 cu   (nominal +4.8%, real +2.2%)
```

Real growth is close to nominal growth minus inflation (5.0 − 5.6 = −0.6). The same method gives real GDP. If nominal GDP rises from 400 to 436 billion cu (+9.0%) while the price index rises to 105.6, real GDP is 436 ÷ 1.056 = 412.9 billion cu, which is real growth of 3.2%.

## 4.6.4: causes of inflation

| | Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Aggregate demand rises faster than the economy can supply | Costs of production rise |
| Curve that shifts | AD to the right | SRAS to the left |
| Effect on output and employment | Output and employment rise (until capacity is reached) | Output and employment fall |
| Typical causes | Lower taxes, higher consumer confidence, higher government spending, export boom | Higher wages without productivity gains, dearer raw materials or energy, a weaker currency raising import costs, higher indirect taxes |

**Worked example 8.** An invented economy starts with price level 100 and real output 500. In case 1 AD shifts right and the new equilibrium is price level 108 and output 520: demand-pull. In case 2 SRAS shifts left and the equilibrium is price level 108 and output 470: cost-push. Prices rise 8% in both, but output rises in case 1 and falls in case 2. Falling output with rising prices is stagflation.

A **wage-price spiral** can link the two: higher prices lead workers to demand higher pay, which raises costs and prices again, and so on.

## 4.6.5: consequences of inflation

**Worked example 9.** Using the 5.6% inflation of year 1:

```
Fixed pension of 1,200 cu: real value = 1,200 ÷ 1.056 = 1,136.4 cu (−5.3%)
Savings of 10,000 cu earning 3% (10,300 cu): real value = 10,300 ÷ 1.056 = 9,753.8 cu, a real loss
Debt of 20,000 cu fixed in money terms: real value = 20,000 ÷ 1.056 = 18,939.4 cu, a fall of 1,060.6 cu
```

- **Redistribution.** People on fixed incomes and savers whose interest lags prices lose. Borrowers with fixed repayments gain, and lenders lose.
- **Menu and shoe-leather costs.** Firms reprint prices and households spend time moving money.
- **Uncertainty.** Unpredictable inflation makes firms delay investment.
- **International competitiveness.** If Country A's export prices rise 6% while a rival's rise 2%, its goods become 106 ÷ 102, or 3.9%, dearer relative to the rival, so exports may fall and imports rise.

**Evaluation.** The damage depends on the rate and on whether inflation is anticipated. Low, steady inflation is easier to plan around, and wages that rise with prices protect workers. Unexpected, fast inflation does the most harm.

## Common errors

- Using the whole population, not the labour force, as the base for the unemployment rate.
- Treating a falling rate as proof that more jobs exist, without checking who left the labour force.
- Calling disinflation a fall in prices.
- Dividing by the new CPI to get the inflation rate instead of by the old one.
- Using the wrong formula for real values: real = nominal ÷ index × 100.
- Drawing cost-push as an AD shift.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are unemployment and price stability AS or A Level content in Cambridge 9708?**
They are sections 4.5 and 4.6 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus, which is AS Level content in topic 4. The syllabus treats it as assumed knowledge for A Level Papers 3 and 4.

**Who counts as unemployed?**
A person is unemployed if they are able and willing to work at the going wage, are actively looking for work and have no job. Full-time students, carers and the retired who are not looking are not counted.

**What is the difference between the labour force survey and the claimant count?**
A labour force survey asks a sample of households about their situation using an agreed definition of looking for work, which allows comparison across countries and years. The claimant count counts people registered for unemployment benefit, so it is quick but depends on benefit rules.

**What are the difficulties of measuring inflation with the CPI?**
The basket and weights go out of date, a fixed basket ignores switching to cheaper items, and quality changes and new products are hard to price. No household matches the average, so groups such as pensioners face different inflation rates.

**What is a wage-price spiral?**
A wage-price spiral happens when higher prices lead workers to demand higher pay, which raises firms' costs and pushes prices up again, and so on.
