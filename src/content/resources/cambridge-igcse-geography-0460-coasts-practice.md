These questions cover topic 2.3 Coasts in Theme 2 (The natural environment) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The coastline and measurements in Question 7 are fictional and the data are invented.

Revise first with the [Coasts study guide](/resources/igcse-geography-coasts/) and the [Coasts revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-coasts-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Name two processes of coastal erosion. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "wave-cut platform". **[1]**

**3.** State two conditions needed for coral reefs to develop. **[2]**

**4.** State two conditions needed for mangrove swamps to develop. **[2]**

**5.** State two differences between constructive and destructive waves. **[2]**

**6.** Give two ways in which coral reefs or mangrove swamps benefit people. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Students measured the distance from fixed posts to the cliff edge at five points along a fictional coastline in 2015 and again in 2025. The table shows how far the cliff edge retreated at each point.

| Point | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retreat 2015-2025 (m) | 3.5 | 5.0 | 12.0 | 4.5 | 6.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of cliff retreat per year for the five points. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two reasons why the cliff retreated much further at Point C than at the other points. **[2]**
**(c)** A house at Point C is 18 m from the cliff edge. Assuming the rate of retreat at Point C stays the same, calculate how many years it will be before the cliff edge reaches the house. **[1]**

**8.** Explain how a spit is formed. You may use a labelled diagram. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how a headland can be eroded to form a cave, an arch, a stack and a stump. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how coastal sand dunes form. **[4]**

**11.** Describe and evaluate one hard engineering method and one soft engineering method of managing coastal erosion. **[6]**

**12.** For a named area of coastline you have studied, to what extent do the opportunities it offers outweigh the hazards? **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Hydraulic action [1]. Abrasion (corrasion) [1]. Also credit attrition or solution (corrosion). **[2]**

**2.** A gently sloping area of flat rock at the foot of a cliff, exposed at low tide, left behind as the cliff retreats [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Warm sea water, generally above about 18°C [1]. Clear, shallow water so that sunlight reaches the coral [1]. Also credit salty water away from river mouths and freshwater or sediment. **[2]**

**4.** Sheltered tropical or subtropical coasts with warm temperatures [1]. Shallow, muddy, tidal water, such as estuaries and deltas, where mangroves tolerate salt water [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Constructive waves have a stronger swash than backwash, whereas destructive waves have a stronger backwash than swash [1]. Constructive waves are low and flat with a long wavelength, whereas destructive waves are high and steep and break frequently [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each difference needs both types of wave; describing constructive waves alone earns no marks in a "differences" question.

**6.** They protect the coast by absorbing wave energy, reducing erosion and storm damage [1]. They support fishing and tourism, providing food and income [1]. Also credit timber or fuel from mangroves. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Mean retreat = (3.5 + 5.0 + 12.0 + 4.5 + 6.0) ÷ 5 = 31.0 ÷ 5 = 6.2 m in 10 years [1]. Mean rate = 6.2 ÷ 10 = 0.62 m per year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The rock at Point C may be softer or more heavily jointed, so it erodes more easily [1]. Point C may be more exposed to the largest waves, or have no beach to absorb wave energy [1]. Also credit the absence of sea defences at C. **[2]**
**(c)** Rate at C = 12.0 ÷ 10 = 1.2 m per year; 18 ÷ 1.2 = 15 years [1]. **[1]**

**8.** Waves approach the coast at an angle, driven by the prevailing wind [1]. Swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle and backwash returns it straight down the slope, so sediment moves along the coast by longshore drift [1]. Where the coastline changes direction, such as at a river mouth or bay, sediment continues to be deposited out into the sea [1]. Over time the deposits build up into a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle attached to the land at one end [1]. Changes in wind direction can curve the end into a hook, and a salt marsh may form in the sheltered water behind the spit [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Waves attack a weakness such as a crack or joint in the headland by hydraulic action and abrasion [1]. The crack is enlarged into a cave [1]. If caves form on both sides of the headland, they can meet to form an arch [1]. Erosion at the base of the arch and weathering of the roof cause the roof to collapse, leaving a stack separated from the headland [1]. The stack is eroded at its base until it collapses, leaving a stump, which may be covered at high tide [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Onshore winds blow dry sand from a wide beach exposed at low tide [1]. The sand is trapped by obstacles such as driftwood or seaweed and begins to build up [1]. Plants such as marram grass colonise the sand; their roots bind it and their leaves trap more sand [1]. Over time the dunes grow higher and move inland, with older, more stable dunes further from the sea [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Hard engineering: a sea wall reflects wave energy, protecting the land and buildings behind it [1]. It is effective and long-lasting [1], but expensive to build and maintain, and reflected waves can erode the beach in front of it [1]. Soft engineering: beach nourishment adds sand or shingle to widen the beach so it absorbs wave energy [1]. It looks natural and supports tourism [1], but it must be repeated regularly as sediment is washed away, so costs continue [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Accept groynes, rock armour, gabions, dune stabilisation or managed retreat; each needs a description and an evaluation.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative creditworthy content:

Named coastline with location [1]. Opportunity: tourism based on beaches, reefs or scenery, providing jobs [1]. Opportunity: fishing, ports, trade or farmland on coastal lowlands [1]. Hazard: coastal erosion threatening homes, roads or farmland [1]. Hazard: tropical storms or storm surges causing flooding and damage, where relevant [1]. Management: a named method used on that coastline, with its success [1]. Place-specific detail throughout [1]. Judgement: for example, the opportunities outweigh the hazards for the local economy, but not for communities on the most rapidly eroding stretches [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the total retreat but not dividing by the number of years.
- Spit answers that do not explain longshore drift.
- Cave, arch, stack and stump answers that miss the role of a weakness in the rock.
- Coral reef conditions with no idea of temperature or light.
- Management answers that describe methods without evaluating them.
