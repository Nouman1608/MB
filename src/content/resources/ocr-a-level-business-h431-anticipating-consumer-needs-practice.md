## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between a planned purchase and an impulse purchase. **[3]**

**2.** State the four stages of the AIDA model. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by niche marketing. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between customer orientation and product orientation. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**5.** Quill Stationery spent £18,000 on an online campaign seen by 80,000 people. 5% showed interest, 25% of those developed desire, and 40% of those bought.

**(a)** Calculate how many people bought. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the overall conversion rate from viewers to buyers. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the campaign cost per purchase. **[1]**

**(d)** A redesigned advertisement raises the interest rate to 7.5%. The other rates stay the same. Calculate the new cost per purchase. **[3]**

**(e)** Evaluate the usefulness of the AIDA model to Quill Stationery. **[6]**

**6.** Quill is deciding which segment to target. Expected sales equal the segment's total spending multiplied by the share Quill expects to win.

| Segment | People | Yearly spend each | Expected share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 24,000 | £50 | 10% |
| Office workers | 60,000 | £35 | 4% |
| Artists | 15,000 | £120 | 8% |

**(a)** Calculate the expected sales for each segment and the total. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify a segmentation strategy for Quill Stationery. **[9]**

**7.** Heath & Hawk is a small bookshop in a market town. It stocks bestsellers near the till and offers a coffee with every book token. It has recently been losing customers to online sellers. Evaluate the importance of customer service to Heath & Hawk in influencing consumer buyer behaviour. **[9]**

**8.** Larder & Co makes jam from rare local fruit and sells 4,000 jars a year at £9 to specialist shops. A supermarket has offered to stock a cheaper recipe at £2 a jar in 1,200 stores. Evaluate whether Larder & Co should pursue niche or mass marketing. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A planned purchase is decided before the shopper reaches the point of sale, often after research [1]. An impulse purchase is unplanned and made on the spot [1], influenced by display, promotion or mood [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Attention, Interest, Desire, Action (1 mark for two correct stages, 2 marks for all four) [2]. **[2]**

**3.** Targeting a small, specific segment of a market [1] with a product and marketing mix designed for that group [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Customer orientation designs the product from customer needs identified through research [1]. Product orientation starts with what the business can make and then looks for buyers [1]. The first responds to demand; the second leads with the product or technology [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 80,000 × 0.05 = 4,000 [1]; 4,000 × 0.25 = 1,000; 1,000 × 0.40 = **400 buyers** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 400 ÷ 80,000 [1] = **0.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 18,000 ÷ 400 = **£45** [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** Interest: 80,000 × 0.075 = 6,000 [1]. Buyers: 6,000 × 0.25 × 0.40 = 600 [1]. Cost per purchase = 18,000 ÷ 600 = **£30** [1]. **[3]**

**(e)** Useful: it shows that 95% of viewers (76,000) do not engage, so the interest stage is where improvement is easiest [1]; the result of the redesign, a fall from £45 to £30, shows how the model guides spending [1]; it gives managers a simple framework and measures at each stage [1].
Limits: customers may buy on impulse or because of price and skip stages [1]; it ignores competitors and word of mouth [1].
Judgement: AIDA is useful for planning and testing online advertisements, where each stage can be counted, but it should be combined with market research on why customers buy [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** Students: 24,000 × 50 = £1,200,000; × 10% = **£120,000** [1]. Office workers: 60,000 × 35 = £2,100,000; × 4% = **£84,000** [1]. Artists: 15,000 × 120 = £1,800,000; × 8% = **£144,000** [1]. Total = **£348,000** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Evidence: the artists segment is the smallest by people, but it gives the highest expected sales (£144,000) because each person spends £120 [1]; the students are second (£120,000) [1]; office workers are the largest group but give the lowest sales as competition limits the share to 4% [1].
Recommendation: target artists as the main segment and students as the second, together £264,000, which is about 76% of the total (264,000 ÷ 348,000) [2].
Mix: artists value quality, so a premium range sold online and in art shops; students need low-priced packs and social media promotion [2].
Risks: the shares are estimates; artists are few, so a loss of share would hurt, and two segments need two mixes and more cost [1].
Judgement: this concentrates resources where return per person is highest, but research and a trial should check the estimated shares before full commitment [1]. **[9]**

**7.** Importance: customers now compare prices online, so the bookshop competes on advice, atmosphere and personal service [1]; good service encourages repeat visits and recommendations [1]; helpful staff turn planned purchases into extra impulse sales [1]; poor service would send customers to online sellers [1].
Costs and limits: good service needs trained staff, which costs money [1]; some customers care only about price, so service alone will not hold them [1]; the extras such as coffee must be paid for [1].
Judgement: service is very important to a small bookshop, because it is the main thing it can offer that online sellers cannot [1]. It matters most for customers who value advice and browsing, and so the shop should measure repeat visits to see if it pays [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Niche: the jam sells at £9 to specialist shops, so revenue is currently 4,000 × £9 = £36,000 [1]; the rare fruit and the brand allow a premium price and loyal customers [1]; a niche protects Larder from direct price competition [1].
Mass: a supermarket order in 1,200 stores would greatly increase volume [1], but at £2 a jar, Larder would need 18,000 jars to match the current £36,000 revenue [1], which is 4.5 times the current volume; the cheaper recipe might damage the premium brand [1].
Further: the supermarket could demand lower prices later, and Larder might lose its specialist shops [1].
Judgement: Larder should stay with niche marketing unless the supermarket guarantees large volumes and a separate brand name, since the current strategy protects margin and brand [1] and large orders could exceed its limited supply of rare fruit [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing the AIDA letters without using the case figures.
- Choosing the largest segment without checking spend and share.
- Saying niche is always better for small businesses with no evidence.
- Forgetting to multiply by the achievable share when finding expected sales.
