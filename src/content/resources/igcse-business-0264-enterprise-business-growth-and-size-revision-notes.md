## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Entrepreneur | A person who takes the risk of starting and running a business |
| Enterprise | The activity of starting and running a business, taking risks |
| Business plan | A document setting out what a business intends to do and how |
| Internal growth | Growth using the firm's own resources, for example new products or markets |
| External growth | Growth by joining with another business |
| Merger | Two firms agree to combine as a single business |
| Takeover | One firm buys enough of another to control it |
| Horizontal integration | Joining firms at the same stage of the same industry |
| Vertical integration | Joining firms at different stages of the supply chain |
| Capital employed | The long-term finance in the business (standard form: total assets less current liabilities) |

## 1.3.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship

**Characteristics of successful entrepreneurs:** risk-taking, innovation, determination, hard work, self-confidence, leadership, good decisions.

**Business plan: eight key elements.** Learn them in this order.

1. Overview/summary
2. Objectives
3. Resources
4. Market research
5. Marketing
6. Finance
7. People
8. Operations

**Why a plan matters:**
- makes the owner think through problems before spending money;
- helps to persuade lenders and investors;
- sets targets to measure progress against;
- but it relies on forecasts, so it cannot guarantee success.

**Why governments support start-ups:** create jobs, reduce unemployment, raise tax revenue, increase competition, encourage innovation, support economic growth.

**How governments support start-ups**

| Method | One-line effect |
|---|---|
| Grants | Money not repaid |
| Advice | Guidance on planning, finance and rules |
| Low-cost loans | Cheaper borrowing |
| Training schemes | Skills for owner and staff |

**Why versus how:** "why" is about benefits to the country; "how" is the method used.

## 1.3.2 Measuring business size

| Method | Measures | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Number of people employed | Workers | Ignores machines; part-time versus full-time |
| Value of output/sales | Money value | Affected by price changes |
| Volume of output/sales | Units | Hard to compare different products |
| Capital employed | Money invested | Asset values can be hard to judge |

**Profit is not a method of measuring business size.**

**Method in steps: choosing a size measure**
1. Identify the industry (labour-intensive or capital-intensive).
2. Pick the measure that suits the question.
3. Note its weakness.
4. Where possible, use two measures and compare.

**Useful calculation (standard form):** sales per employee = sales revenue ÷ number of employees.

*Worked reminder.* Firm A: 60 employees, sales revenue £1.5 million. Firm B: 12 employees, sales revenue £1.2 million. Sales per employee: A = £25,000; B = £100,000. A is five times larger by employees (60 ÷ 12) but only 1.25 times larger by sales (1.5 ÷ 1.2).

**Problems of measuring size**
- Different industries give different answers.
- Capital-intensive firms have few workers.
- Value and volume can tell different stories.
- Inflation and exchange rates change sales values.
- Part-time, seasonal and outsourced staff blur headcount.

## 1.3.3 Growth and remaining small

**Why owners want growth:** more profit, larger market share, lower unit costs, spread risk, status, security.

**Internal growth** (own resources): new products, new markets, new outlets.
- Plus: control kept, steady, less risky.
- Minus: slow, needs finance, new products can fail.

**External growth** (joining another firm)

| Method | Plus | Minus |
|---|---|---|
| Merger | Quick growth, shared costs and skills | Clashing cultures, job losses |
| Takeover | Quick growth, gains customers and brands | Expensive; may be resisted |
| Horizontal integration | Removes a rival; larger share | May reduce choice for customers |
| Vertical integration | Secures supply or outlets | Managers face unfamiliar stages of production |

Standard sub-types of vertical integration: backward (towards suppliers) and forward (towards retailers).

**Problems linked to growth:** cash flow strain, harder management and control, poorer communication, rising average costs if too big, falling quality, borrowing risk.

**Why some stay small:** small or niche market, owner wants control, lack of finance, wish to avoid risk, personal service, content with lifestyle.

**Method in steps: a growth recommendation**
1. State the options (internal or external).
2. Give one advantage and one drawback for each, in context.
3. Weigh them against the firm's finance, management and aims.
4. Reach a reasoned decision.

## 1.3.4 Success and failure

| Factor | Success | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Management skills | Planning, leadership | Poor decisions |
| Availability of finance | Funds for early losses | Cash runs out |
| Suitability of product | Meets needs | Not wanted |
| Demand for products | Strong | Falls |
| Changes in the economy | Rising incomes | Recession |
| Level of competition | Few close rivals | Strong rivals |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Merger and takeover:** agreed joining of two firms versus one firm gaining control of another.
- **Horizontal and vertical:** same stage versus different stages.
- **Internal and external growth:** own resources versus joining with another firm.
- **Value and volume:** money from sales versus number of units.
- **Why and how** of government support.

## Linking points to the case

Application (AO2) carries a large share of the marks. Before you write, underline three facts in the case: the size of the business, its aim, and any money figure. Then use at least one in each paragraph. For example, a firm that wants to keep control suits internal growth, while a firm short of cash may struggle with a takeover.

## Command words for this section

| Command word | Meaning in the syllabus | What it means here |
|---|---|---|
| State | Express in clear terms | Give the point briefly, for example one characteristic |
| Identify | Name, select, recognise | Pick the element, method or type from a list or case |
| Define | Give precise meaning | Say exactly what a term such as takeover means |
| Calculate | Work out from given facts | Show the formula, the figures and the answer |
| Explain | Set out reasons, say why or how, with evidence | Point, reason, then link to the business |
| Justify | Support a case with evidence or argument | Weigh both sides, then reach a decision with reasons |

**Worked reminder: growth percentage.** A firm grows from £5 million to £6.5 million. Increase = 1.5 million. Percentage growth = 1.5 ÷ 5 × 100 = 30%.

**Worked reminder: share after a takeover.** A takeover joins a firm with sales revenue of £3 million to one with £1 million. Combined = £4 million. The larger firm accounts for 3 ÷ 4 × 100 = 75% of the combined sales revenue.

**Internal against external growth at a glance.** Internal growth is slower, cheaper and keeps control. External growth is faster, costs more and brings the task of joining two businesses together.

## Quick self-test

1. State two characteristics of a successful entrepreneur.
2. Identify four key elements of a business plan.
3. State one reason why a government supports start-ups.
4. Name one way a government can support a start-up.
5. Identify two ways of measuring business size.
6. Why is profit not a method of measuring size?
7. A company has 90 employees and sales revenue of £4.5 million. Calculate sales per employee.
8. Sales rose from £800,000 to £1,000,000. Calculate the percentage increase.
9. Define vertical integration.
10. A bakery buys a rival bakery in the next town. What type of integration is this?
11. State two reasons why a business might stay small.
12. Identify two reasons why a business might fail.

### Answers

1. Any two of: risk-taking, innovation, determination, hard work, self-confidence, leadership.
2. Any four of: overview/summary, objectives, resources, market research, marketing, finance, people, operations.
3. For example: to create jobs, to raise tax revenue, or to increase competition.
4. Grants, advice, low-cost loans or training schemes.
5. Any two of: number of people employed, value of output/sales, volume of output/sales, capital employed.
6. A small firm can earn a high profit and a large firm can make a loss, so profit says little about size.
7. 4,500,000 ÷ 90 = **£50,000** per employee.
8. (1,000,000 − 800,000) ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **25%**.
9. Joining firms at different stages of the supply chain of the same industry.
10. Horizontal integration (same stage of the same industry).
11. For example: the market is small or niche, or the owner wants to keep control.
12. For example: poor management skills, shortage of finance, unsuitable product, falling demand, a recession, strong competition.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving profit as a measure of business size.
- Naming a characteristic or element without saying what it means or showing it in the case.
- Answering "why does the government help" from the firm's point of view.
- Mixing up merger, takeover, horizontal and vertical integration.
- Quoting one growth advantage without any link to the business named.
- Writing a list of problems of growth with no explanation of how each affects the firm.
- Giving a one-sided answer where the question says justify or consider.
- Forgetting units or to show the working in percentage and per-employee calculations.
