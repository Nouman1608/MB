Section 2.7 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has two outcomes. You must describe metallic bonding as "the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a 'sea' of delocalised electrons", and explain in terms of structure and bonding two properties of metals: good electrical conductivity, and malleability and ductility. Both outcomes are required for every 5070 candidate.

The [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding guide](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) gives the definition in a few lines. This guide unpacks every phrase of it, works through the two explanations step by step, and compares metallic bonding with the ionic and giant covalent structures from sections 2.4 and 2.6, which is where many questions go.

## The model, phrase by phrase

The syllabus definition has three parts. Each one is worth understanding.

**"Positive ions"**. In a solid metal, each atom releases its **outer-shell electrons**. What remains of each atom is a positive ion. Sodium (2,8,1) releases one electron and becomes Na⁺; magnesium (2,8,2) releases two and becomes Mg²⁺; aluminium (2,8,3) releases three and becomes Al³⁺.

**"Giant metallic lattice"**. The positive ions are packed closely together in a regular arrangement of **layers**, extending throughout the piece of metal. As in an ionic lattice, there are no separate molecules.

**"Sea of delocalised electrons"**. The released electrons do not belong to any one ion. They are **delocalised**: free to move throughout the whole lattice. They form a mobile "sea" (or cloud) of negative charge surrounding the ions.

**"Electrostatic attraction"**. The **metallic bond** is the attraction between the positive ions and the negative sea of electrons. Each ion is attracted to the electrons all around it, and each electron to many ions, so the bonding acts in all directions and holds the whole lattice together.

### The metal stays neutral

The electrons are not lost from the metal; they are shared across the whole structure. The total negative charge of the sea of electrons equals the total positive charge of the ions. For example, a piece of aluminium containing a certain number of Al³⁺ ions holds exactly three times that number of delocalised electrons.

### Describing a diagram

A typical diagram shows circles labelled with a positive charge (such as 2+ for magnesium) in neat rows, with small e⁻ symbols scattered in the spaces between them. When asked to label one, name the **positive ions** and the **delocalised electrons**. A diagram showing electrons in shells, or negative ions, is not a metallic lattice.

**Worked example 3.** Describe the bonding in solid calcium.

*Annotated answer.* Calcium has a **giant metallic lattice** of **Ca²⁺ ions** arranged in layers [1]. Each calcium atom (2,8,8,2) has released its **two outer-shell electrons**, which are **delocalised** and free to move throughout the lattice [1]. The bonding is the **strong electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and this sea of delocalised electrons [1]. *(Giving the ion's charge and linking it to the configuration shows the examiner you understand where the electrons come from.)*

## Explaining electrical conductivity

Electric current is a flow of charge. For a substance to conduct, it needs **charged particles that are free to move** through it.

In a metal:

1. The **delocalised electrons** are charged (negative) and **free to move** throughout the lattice.
2. When a potential difference is applied across the metal, the delocalised electrons **move through the lattice** towards the positive terminal.
3. This flow of electrons is the electric current.

Because the delocalised electrons are present whether the metal is solid or molten, metals conduct in **both states**. This is one difference from ionic compounds, which conduct only when molten or aqueous.

**Worked example 1.** Explain why copper is used for electrical wiring.

*Annotated answer.* Copper has a giant metallic lattice with a **sea of delocalised electrons** [1]. These electrons are **free to move** through the structure [1], so they can carry charge when a potential difference is applied: copper is a good electrical conductor [1]. It is also **ductile**, so it can be drawn into thin wires (an extra point if the question asks about wires, explained below).

## Explaining malleability and ductility

- **Malleable**: can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking.
- **Ductile**: can be drawn out (stretched) into wires.

Both properties have the same explanation.

1. The positive ions are arranged in **layers**.
2. When a force is applied, the **layers of ions can slide over one another** into new positions.
3. The **delocalised electrons move with them** and continue to attract the ions in their new positions.
4. So the **metallic bonding is not broken**: the metal changes shape instead of shattering.

The phrase that earns the final mark is the idea that the bonding remains, because the sea of electrons is not tied to particular ions. Simply writing "the layers slide" describes what happens; it does not explain why the metal holds together.

**Worked example 2.** Aluminium is rolled into thin sheets to make foil. Explain why this is possible.

*Answer.* Aluminium is malleable. Its lattice consists of layers of Al³⁺ ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. When rolled, the layers of ions slide over each other, and the delocalised electrons continue to hold the ions together in their new positions, so the foil forms without the metal breaking.

## Comparing metals with other giant structures

| | Metallic (e.g. magnesium) | Ionic (e.g. magnesium oxide) | Giant covalent (e.g. diamond) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particles in lattice | positive ions + delocalised electrons | positive and negative ions | atoms |
| Bonding | attraction of ions to electron sea | attraction between oppositely charged ions | shared pairs of electrons |
| Conducts when solid? | yes: delocalised electrons move | no: ions fixed | no (diamond); yes (graphite: delocalised electrons) |
| Conducts when molten? | yes | yes: ions move | not applicable at this level |
| Malleable? | yes: layers slide, bonding remains | no: shatters | no: very hard, rigid |

A useful contrast for "explain" questions: in an ionic solid, shifting a layer brings ions of the **same charge** next to each other, and they repel, so the crystal shatters. In a metal, every ion is positive and the electron sea surrounds them all, so a shifted layer is still held in place.

Note that graphite conducts for the same reason metals do: delocalised electrons. But graphite is not a metal and has covalent bonding within its layers.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "sea of ions" or "sea of protons" instead of a sea of **delocalised electrons**.
- Saying metals conduct because "ions move". In metals it is the **electrons**.
- Saying the electrons are "lost" from the metal, making it positive.
- Describing malleability as "atoms slide" without mentioning that the bonding is maintained.
- Calling metallic bonds weak because the electrons are mobile. The attraction is strong.
- Confusing malleable (hammered into sheets) with ductile (drawn into wires).

## Exam technique

- **Describe** metallic bonding: positive ions, lattice, delocalised electrons, electrostatic attraction. Four key terms; use all of them.
- **Explain** conductivity: delocalised electrons **free to move** through the structure and carry charge.
- **Explain** malleability or ductility: **layers** of ions **slide**; **bonding remains** because the delocalised electrons still attract the ions.
