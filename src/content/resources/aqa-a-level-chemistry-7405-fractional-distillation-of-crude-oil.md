## Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons

A **hydrocarbon** contains carbon and hydrogen only. A **saturated** hydrocarbon has only single bonds between carbon atoms, so no more atoms can be added without breaking a bond. Alkanes have the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.

| Alkane | Formula | Mᵣ | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | 16.0 | –162 |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | 30.0 | –89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | 44.0 | –42 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | 58.0 | –1 |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 72.0 | 36 |
| Hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 86.0 | 69 |
| Octane | C₈H₁₈ | 114.0 | 126 |
| Decane | C₁₀H₂₂ | 142.0 | 174 |

Alkanes contain only C–C and C–H bonds. These are σ bonds and are almost non-polar, because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativity, so alkane molecules are non-polar and the only forces between them are **London forces** (induced dipole-induced dipole).

## Crude oil is a mixture

Crude oil (petroleum) is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons, mainly alkanes, formed over millions of years from the remains of marine organisms. It also contains some cycloalkanes, aromatic hydrocarbons and small amounts of sulfur compounds. It is a **mixture**, not a compound, so each hydrocarbon keeps its own properties, and the components can be separated by a physical method that depends on a difference in boiling point.

## How fractional distillation works

The process separates the mixture into **fractions**: each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points, and with a similar number of carbon atoms.

### Steps in the refinery

1. Crude oil is **heated** in a furnace to about 350 °C (or higher), so most of it vaporises.
2. The hot vapour and liquid pass into the bottom of a tall **fractionating column**.
3. The column is **hot at the bottom** and **cooler at the top**, because there is a temperature gradient.
4. As the vapours rise, they cool. Each hydrocarbon **condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point**.
5. The liquid is collected on **trays** (with bubble caps that let vapour pass up through the liquid) at different heights, and drawn off.
6. The very long-chain molecules, with the highest boiling points, remain as a liquid at the bottom. The smallest molecules, with the lowest boiling points, stay as gases and leave at the top.

No chemical bonds are broken. It is a **physical separation** based on differences in boiling point.

### The fractions

| Fraction | Approximate number of carbons | Approximate boiling range / °C | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Refinery gases | 1 to 4 | Below 25 | Heating and cooking fuel (LPG) |
| Petrol (gasoline) | 5 to 10 | 40 to 205 | Fuel for cars |
| Naphtha | 7 to 14 | 90 to 200 | Feedstock for the chemical industry |
| Kerosene | 10 to 16 | 175 to 275 | Jet fuel |
| Diesel oil | 14 to 20 | 250 to 350 | Fuel for lorries and trains |
| Fuel oil | 20 to 70 | Above 350 | Ships and power stations |
| Bitumen | Over 70 | Residue | Road surfaces, roofing |

The ranges are approximate and differ between refineries, so use them to compare, not to memorise.

## Why boiling point rises with chain length

This is the key explanation, and it is asked in most exams on the topic.

- Alkane molecules are held together by **London forces**.
- A longer molecule has **more electrons** and a **larger surface area** for contact with its neighbours.
- So the temporary dipoles are bigger and there are **more points of contact**, which makes the London forces stronger.
- More energy is needed to overcome the stronger forces between molecules, so the boiling point is higher.

Do not write that "the bonds are stronger" or that covalent bonds break on boiling. They do not: only the intermolecular forces are overcome.

### Branching

For alkanes with the same number of carbons, the **branched** isomer has a lower boiling point than the straight-chain isomer. Branched molecules are more compact and have a smaller surface area for contact, so the London forces are weaker.

| Isomer (C₆H₁₄) | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Hexane (straight chain) | 69 |
| 2-methylpentane | 60 |
| 2,2-dimethylbutane | 50 |

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: which fraction leaves first?

A mixture contains propane, octane and hexadecane (C₁₆H₃₄). In which order do they come off a fractionating column, from top to bottom?

Propane has the shortest chain and lowest boiling point (–42 °C), so it stays as a gas to the top. Octane (126 °C) condenses in the middle. Hexadecane has the longest chain, strongest London forces and highest boiling point, so it condenses lowest. Order from top: propane, octane, hexadecane.

### Worked example 2: explaining a boiling point difference

Explain why pentane (36 °C) boils at a lower temperature than decane (174 °C).

Both are non-polar molecules held by London forces. Decane has more electrons and a larger surface area, so its London forces are stronger and more energy is needed to separate the molecules.

### Worked example 3: explaining a branched isomer

Explain why 2,2-dimethylbutane (50 °C) boils at a lower temperature than hexane (69 °C).

They have the same molecular formula. 2,2-dimethylbutane is more compact and has less surface contact between molecules, so the London forces are weaker and less energy is needed.

### Worked example 4: relative molecular mass

Calculate the Mᵣ of decane, C₁₀H₂₂ (Aᵣ: C = 12.0, H = 1.0).

10 × 12.0 + 22 × 1.0 = 120.0 + 22.0 = 142.0.

## Laboratory fractional distillation

In the laboratory, a crude oil substitute is heated in a flask connected to a fractionating column (a column packed with glass beads or rings that give a large surface for condensation) and a condenser. A thermometer at the top of the column records the temperature of the vapour. Fractions are collected over different temperature ranges: the first fraction comes off at the lowest temperature, and the temperature of the vapour rises as heavier fractions distil. Use a water bath or an electric heater, because the vapours are flammable.

## Common mistakes

- Saying alkanes are "unsaturated" or "have double bonds".
- Saying crude oil is a compound.
- Saying the process breaks bonds, or that the covalent bonds are overcome on boiling.
- Explaining the trend by "stronger bonds" or "heavier molecules" without London forces.
- Saying the column is hotter at the top.
- Forgetting that the fractions are mixtures, not pure compounds.
- Confusing fractional distillation (physical) with cracking (chemical).
- Writing "intermolecular bonds" instead of "intermolecular forces".

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe the process**: heat, vaporise, rising vapours, temperature gradient, condense at their boiling point, collect.
- **Explain**: link the boiling point to the number of electrons, surface area and London forces.
- **Explain a branched isomer's lower boiling point**: compact shape, less surface contact, weaker London forces.
- **State**: a fraction contains hydrocarbons with similar boiling points.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the column hot at the bottom and cool at the top?**
The hot crude oil enters at the bottom, and heat is lost as the vapours rise, so the temperature falls with height.

**Why can't heavy fractions be used for petrol?**
Their molecules are too long, so they are less volatile and burn poorly. Cracking turns them into shorter molecules.

**Is petroleum a renewable resource?**
No. It takes millions of years to form, so it is finite.
