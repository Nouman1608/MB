These notes condense section 5.3, *Influences on the curriculum*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. Syllabus strands: the social construction of knowledge; factors influencing curriculum content (power, status, culture, economic demands, gender); cultural reproduction through the ethnocentric, gendered and hidden curricula; and the curriculum and cultural capital.

Full explanations: [influences on the curriculum study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-influences-on-the-curriculum/).

## Core argument

School knowledge is selected, divided and ranked by people with power. The selection tends to match dominant-class, dominant-ethnic and male culture, so the curriculum helps reproduce inequality while appearing neutral. The counter-argument: a shared curriculum can build solidarity and give every pupil access to powerful knowledge.

## Three layers of curriculum

| Layer | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | Written subjects, syllabuses, exams | National curriculum subjects |
| Hidden | Lessons from how school is organised | Punctuality, hierarchy, competition |
| Null | What is left out | A minority group's history absent from textbooks |

## Social construction of knowledge

- **Young (1971):** high-status knowledge is **literate, individualistic, abstract, unrelated to everyday life**.
- **Bernstein (1971):**
  - *classification* = strength of boundaries between subjects;
  - *framing* = control over what and how things are taught;
  - *collection code* (separate subjects) versus *integrated code* (blended).
- **Keddie (1971):** on one humanities course, higher streams received abstract knowledge and lower streams everyday content.
- **Apple (1979):** whose knowledge becomes "official"?
- **Freire (1968; English 1970):** the "banking" concept treats pupils as passive containers; education either adapts people or helps them question.
- **Gramsci:** hegemony makes dominant content seem like common sense.

## Five factors

| Factor | Key point | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Power | States and elites set content and exams | Teachers and pupils can reinterpret content in class |
| Status | Academic subjects outrank vocational ones | Some vocational routes now lead to high pay |
| Culture | Dominant language, religion, history built in | Many curricula now include diverse content |
| Economic demands | Science, computing and vocational skills pushed | Who defines "economic need" is itself political |
| Gender | Stereotyped subjects; women's contributions omitted | Compulsory core subjects reduce choice-based gaps |

## Cultural reproduction

- **Ethnocentric curriculum:** one ethnic group's history and language as the norm. Coard (1971) on West Indian pupils in Britain; Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Decolonising the Mind* (1986), on colonial languages of instruction.
- **Gendered curriculum:** stereotyped subject routes, male-centred content, boys taking more classroom space (Spender, 1982). Liberal feminists stress reform; radical feminists stress continuing patriarchy.
- **Hidden curriculum:** Jackson (1968). Functionalist reading — preparation for adult roles; Marxist — obedient workers; feminist — gender roles through dress codes and classroom jobs.

## Cultural capital and the curriculum

- Bourdieu: curriculum rewards dominant-class culture, so familiar content makes some pupils appear naturally able; embodied capital becomes institutionalised capital (qualifications).
- **Young (2008), *Bringing Knowledge Back In*:** every pupil has a right to **powerful knowledge**; giving disadvantaged pupils only "relevant" content denies them what elites take for granted.
- **Critique:** deciding what counts as powerful is itself an exercise of power.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Social construction of knowledge | Selection and ranking of knowledge reflect human decisions and power |
| High-status knowledge | Knowledge given most prestige in schools |
| Classification | Strength of boundaries between subjects |
| Framing | Degree of control over content and pace |
| Collection / integrated code | Separate subjects / blended subjects |
| Hegemony | Dominance through consent as well as force |
| Banking concept | Pupils treated as passive receivers of approved knowledge |
| Ethnocentric curriculum | Centres one ethnic group's culture |
| Gendered curriculum | Content and routes shaped by gender stereotypes |
| Hidden curriculum | Lessons from school organisation, not formal content |
| Null curriculum | What the curriculum omits |
| Cultural capital | Knowledge, tastes and dispositions valued by dominant groups |
| Powerful knowledge | Specialised knowledge that lets people think beyond experience |

## Exam traps

- Calling the hidden curriculum "unplanned lessons in class content"; it comes from organisation and routine.
- Treating ethnocentrism as individual prejudice rather than a feature of content.
- Writing about only one factor in a "factors" question; the syllabus names five.
- Forgetting that Young changed position: the 1971 and 2008 arguments pull in different directions.
- Ignoring application; language of instruction and contested history content work for any country.

## Self-test

1. Name Young's four features of high-status knowledge.
2. Distinguish classification from framing.
3. What did Keddie find on the humanities course?
4. Define the null curriculum.
5. What is the banking concept of education, and who described it?
6. How does hegemony explain acceptance of an unequal curriculum?
7. Give one way the curriculum may be gendered other than subject choice.
8. What did Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argue about language of instruction?
9. How does cultural capital turn into qualifications?
10. What is "powerful knowledge"?
11. Give one criticism of the powerful knowledge argument.
12. Give one functionalist argument for a shared national curriculum.

### Answers

1. Literate, individualistic, abstract and unrelated to everyday life.
2. Classification is the strength of boundaries between subjects; framing is how much control teachers and pupils have over content and pace.
3. Teachers gave higher streams abstract, theoretical knowledge and lower streams everyday content, so one course became different knowledge for different pupils.
4. The knowledge a curriculum leaves out, which teaches that such knowledge does not count.
5. Teachers deposit approved knowledge in passive pupils; Paulo Freire.
6. Dominant groups' content comes to seem like common sense, so people consent to it rather than having it forced on them.
7. Content that omits women's contributions, or classroom practices in which boys take more space and attention.
8. That teaching through a colonial language shapes how children see their own culture.
9. Pupils who already know the dominant culture find school content familiar, appear able, and convert this into exam success.
10. Specialised, subject-based knowledge that lets people think beyond their own experience (Young, 2008).
11. Deciding which knowledge is powerful is itself an exercise of power by dominant groups.
12. A common curriculum passes on shared values and builds social solidarity across different backgrounds.
