## Questions

**1.** Give the two forms of the definition of the half-life of a radioactive isotope: one in terms of the nuclei in a sample and one in terms of the count rate. **[2]**

**2.** A sample contains 9.6 × 10⁹ nuclei of an isotope with a half-life of 15 hours. Calculate the number of undecayed nuclei of this isotope after 60 hours. **[3]**

**3.** A detector placed near a source reads 410 counts per minute. With the source removed it reads 26 counts per minute. The half-life of the source is 8 minutes. Calculate the count rate the detector will read 32 minutes later. **[4]**

**4.** A detector gives these readings near a source. The background count rate is 12 counts per minute.

| Time / days | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading / counts per minute | 252 | 132 | 72 | 42 | 27 |

**(a)** Calculate the count rate due to the source at each time. **[1]**
**(b)** Determine the half-life of the source. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the reading you would expect at 15 days. **[2]**

**5.** Radioactive decay is a random process, yet a half-life can be used to make predictions. Explain how both statements can be true. **[3]**

**6.** Food packages are passed through a beam of gamma rays from a sealed cobalt-60 source. In another laboratory, a radioactive solution is spilled on a bench.
**(a)** State whether the food packages become radioactive. **[1]**
**(b)** Give two ways in which the hazard from the spilt solution differs from the hazard to the packages. **[2]**
**(c)** State one precaution that protects a worker near the gamma source. **[1]**

**7.** A hospital needs an isotope to be injected into a patient and detected from outside the body. Isotope J emits alpha particles and has a half-life of 2 hours. Isotope K emits gamma rays and has a half-life of 5 hours. Isotope L emits gamma rays and has a half-life of 30 years. Evaluate which isotope is most suitable. **[4]**

**8.** Two waste samples contain the same number of radioactive nuclei. Sample P has a half-life of 5 minutes. Sample R has a half-life of 20 000 years.
**(a)** State which sample emits more radiation each second at the start. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the sample that remains a hazard for the longer time, giving a reason. **[2]**

**9.** A reactor uses uranium-235 as fuel. Describe the sequence of events in one fission of a nucleus of this fuel. **[4]**

**10.** In one process each fission releases 2 neutrons, and every neutron goes on to cause a new fission. One fission starts the process.
**(a)** Calculate the number of fissions in the eighth generation and the total number of fissions in the first eight generations. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how control rods make a reactor operate at a steady rate. **[2]**
**(c)** State what happens to the rate of fission if, on average, fewer than one neutron per fission causes another fission. **[1]**

**11.** Explain why the waste from nuclear reactors needs careful management, and why this may influence a country's decision to generate electricity from nuclear power. **[4]**

**12.** This question is about nuclear fusion.
**(a)** State what is meant by nuclear fusion. **[1]**
**(b)** State what happens to some of the mass of the smaller nuclei. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why fusion needs very high temperatures and pressures. **[3]**
**(d)** State where energy is released by fusion in nature. **[1]**
**(e)** State one difference between fission and fusion in what happens to the nuclei. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The average time for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve [1]; the time for the count rate from a sample containing the isotope to fall to half its initial level [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Half the time" does not say what is halving. Name the quantity, as in "number of nuclei" or "count rate".

**2.** 60 ÷ 15 = 4 half-lives [1]; 9.6 × 10⁹ ÷ 2⁴ = 9.6 × 10⁹ ÷ 16 [1]; **6.0 × 10⁸ nuclei** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the number of half-lives separately lets a slip in the later division be traced. A bare final value gives no working to assess.

**3.** Corrected start = 410 − 26 = 384 [1]; 32 ÷ 8 = 4 half-lives [1]; 384 ÷ 16 = 24 [1]; add background: 24 + 26 = **50 counts per minute** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for the detector reading, so the background goes back on at the end. Stopping at 24 answers a different question.

**4. (a)** 240, 120, 60, 30, 15 counts per minute [1].
**(b)** 240 falls to 120 in 3 days [1]; check: 120 falls to 60 between 3 and 6 days. Half-life = **3 days** [1].
**(c)** 15 ÷ 3 = 5 half-lives, so 240 ÷ 32 = 7.5 [1]; reading = 7.5 + 12 = **19.5 counts per minute** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Halving the raw readings gives 126 then 63, which is not the half-life. Subtract background from every reading first.

**5.** For a single nucleus, there is no way to predict whether or when it will decay [1]; in a sample containing very many nuclei, the proportion that decays in a set time can be predicted [1]; the half-life is the average time for the number of nuclei (or the count rate) to halve, so it describes the sample, not one nucleus [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the single nucleus and the large sample as two separate statements. Answers that run them together often leave neither point clear.

**6. (a)** **No**, irradiation does not make the object radioactive [1].
**(b)** The solution contains radioactive atoms that can stick to skin or be taken into the body [1]; the hazard continues until the atoms are removed or have decayed, whereas irradiation stops when the exposure stops [1].
**(c)** Any one of: keep the exposure time short, stay far from the source, use shielding, handle with remote tools [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Contamination is worse" is a judgement. Give the reason, such as atoms entering the body or the exposure continuing.

**7.** Gamma rays can pass out of the body to reach a detector, but alpha particles would be absorbed in the tissue, so J cannot be detected [1] and would damage nearby cells [1]; L stays active for decades, so the patient would be exposed to radiation for a very long time [1]; K's half-life is long enough to complete the scan, yet the activity soon falls, so K is the most suitable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* On an evaluate question, reach a clear choice and reject each alternative for a stated reason.

**8. (a)** **P** [1].
**(b)** **R** [1]; its half-life is very long, so it takes a very long time for the activity to fall, even though it emits little radiation each second [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A short half-life means more radiation in a short time. A long half-life means less each second but for far longer. Keep these linked to the right sample.

**9.** The nucleus first absorbs a neutron [1]; this makes it unstable, and it splits into two smaller nuclei [1]; two or three neutrons are released [1]; energy is released, much more than in a chemical reaction involving a similar mass [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The neutron absorbed first is easy to leave out. Use the order of events: absorb, split, release.

**10. (a)** Generations double: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 [1]; eighth generation = 2⁷ = **128 fissions** [1]; total = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 + 128 = **255 fissions** [1].
**(b)** Control rods absorb fission neutrons [1]; so that on average only one neutron per fission goes on to cause another fission, keeping the rate steady [1].
**(c)** The rate of fission falls and the reaction dies away [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The eighth generation is 2⁷, not 2⁸. Counting the starting fission as generation 1 is the usual convention here, and listing the terms shows it.

**11.** The waste is radioactive, so it emits ionizing radiation that is harmful [1]; it may remain radioactive for a long time, depending on its half-life and products [1]; so its disposal must be managed with care [1]; this is a factor that may influence whether nuclear power is used to generate electricity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links waste to half-life and then to the decision about power, rather than saying "nuclear is dangerous".

**12. (a)** Two light nuclei join to make one heavier nucleus [1].
**(b)** It is converted into energy [1].
**(c)** The nuclei are both positive, so they repel each other [1]; high temperature gives them enough speed (kinetic energy) to get close despite the repulsion [1]; high pressure holds them close together so they approach each other often [1].
**(d)** In stars [1].
**(e)** In fission one large nucleus splits into smaller nuclei; in fusion light nuclei join to form a heavier one [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention the repulsion in an explanation like (c). Temperature and pressure alone do not give a reason.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using half the elapsed time as the number of half-lives.
- Forgetting to subtract background before halving, or forgetting to add it back when the detector reading is asked for.
- Defining half-life as "half the time" without saying what is halving.
- Saying irradiated objects become radioactive.
- Describing a long half-life as a high activity.
- Judging alpha emitters without saying whether the source is inside or outside the body.
- Missing the neutron absorbed before fission starts.
- Saying control rods stop the reaction, rather than keeping it at one neutron per fission.
- Giving temperature and pressure for fusion without the repulsion between positive nuclei.
