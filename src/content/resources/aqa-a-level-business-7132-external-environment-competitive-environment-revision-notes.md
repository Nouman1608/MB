Condensed notes on section **3.7.7 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: the competitive environment** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is A-level only. The Fenwick Biscuits example is in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-competitive-environment/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-competitive-environment-practice/).

## Specification content

- Porter's five forces, how and why these might change, and the implications for strategic and functional decision making and profits.
- The five forces: entry threat (barriers to entry), buyer power, supplier power, rivalry, substitute threat.
- How the five forces shape competitive strategy.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Barrier to entry | Anything making it hard for a new firm to enter a market |
| Buyer power | Customers' ability to push down prices or demand better terms |
| Supplier power | Suppliers' ability to raise prices or lower quality |
| Rivalry | Intensity of competition between existing firms |
| Substitute | A different product meeting the same need |
| Switching cost | Cost to a buyer of moving to another supplier |
| Backward integration | Buying or setting up a supplier |
| Forward integration | Buying or setting up a customer |

## The five forces

| Force | Stronger when | Weaker when |
|---|---|---|
| Entry threat | Low start-up costs; no brand loyalty; open distribution; no patents or licences | Economies of scale; high capital cost; strong brands; patents; retaliation likely |
| Buyer power | Few large buyers; standard products; low switching costs; price information available | Many small buyers; differentiated product; high switching cost |
| Supplier power | Few suppliers; unique input; high switching costs; threat of forward integration | Many suppliers; standard input; easy switching |
| Rivalry | Many similar rivals; slow growth; high fixed costs; high exit barriers | Few rivals; fast growth; differentiated products |
| Substitutes | Close alternatives at lower price or better quality; low switching cost | No close alternatives; high loyalty |

## Barriers to entry

Economies of scale; start-up cost; brand loyalty; switching costs; distribution access; legal barriers (patents, licences); know-how; fear of retaliation.

## Effects on profit

Strong forces move profit away from the industry: to **customers** (lower prices), **suppliers** (higher costs), **rivals** (costs of competing) and **new entrants** (lost share). Substitutes cap prices.

## Responses

| Force | Possible response |
|---|---|
| Entry | Build brand and scale; patents; exclusive distribution |
| Buyers | Widen customer base; differentiate; loyalty schemes |
| Suppliers | Several suppliers; long-term contracts; vertical integration |
| Rivalry | Differentiate; avoid price wars; focus on a niche |
| Substitutes | Improve value; add features; move into the substitute |

## Why forces change

Technology; regulation (deregulation; patent expiry); economic conditions (recession raises rivalry); mergers (concentrated buyers or suppliers); customer behaviour; globalisation.

## Worked mini-example

A firm earns £500,000 profit on £5m revenue. A retailer buying 30% of output demands a 4% price cut.
Revenue lost = £5m × 30% × 4% = **£60,000**. Profit falls to £440,000, a fall of **12%**.

## Evaluating the model

- **Strengths:** structured; covers more than current rivals; links to strategy.
- **Limits:** snapshot; hard to define the industry; ignores government and cooperation; forces are not measured; industry-wide not firm-specific.

## Exam traps

- Distinguish **buyers** (customers) from **suppliers** (providers of inputs).
- A substitute meets the same need in a different way.
- Do not call every force strong; rank them for the case.
- Finish with a conclusion on profit or strategy.
- Cite evidence from the case.

## Self-test

1. Name Porter's five forces.
2. Give two barriers to entry.
3. State two conditions that give buyers high power.
4. State two conditions that give suppliers high power.
5. Why does slow market growth increase rivalry?
6. Give an example of a substitute for a train journey between two cities.
7. A firm has £3m revenue, 25% of it to one buyer. The buyer demands a 6% price cut. Calculate the lost revenue.
8. If the firm's profit was £300,000, what is the new profit and percentage fall?
9. Explain one way a firm could reduce supplier power.
10. Why might a recession increase rivalry?
11. Why might deregulation raise the entry threat?
12. Give one limitation of the five forces model.

### Answers

1. Entry threat, buyer power, supplier power, rivalry, substitute threat.
2. Any two: economies of scale; high start-up costs; brand loyalty; switching costs; distribution access; patents or licences.
3. Any two: few large buyers; standard products; low switching costs; easy price comparison.
4. Any two: few suppliers; unique input; high switching costs; threat of forward integration.
5. Firms can only grow by taking share from rivals, so they compete harder on price and promotion.
6. Any sensible example: coach, car, or video conferencing.
7. £3m × 25% × 6% = **£45,000**.
8. £300,000 − £45,000 = **£255,000**; fall = 45,000 ÷ 300,000 = **15%**.
9. Any one: use several suppliers; sign long-term contracts; buy a supplier (backward integration); develop substitute inputs.
10. Demand falls, so firms compete for fewer customers, often cutting prices.
11. Removing legal barriers makes it easier and cheaper for new firms to start.
12. Any one: it is a snapshot; industry boundaries are hard to define; it does not measure the forces; it ignores the firm's own strengths.
