## Section A

**1.** State the marks for Question 1(a), Question 1(b) and Section B on Paper 2, and the word limit for Question 1(a). **[4]**

**2.** State the **four** areas of knowledge and understanding that the syllabus lists for Paper 2. **[4]**

**3.** Match each form to the convention that is most characteristic of it. **[4]**

- **(i)** a news story
- **(ii)** a scripted speech
- **(iii)** a review
- **(iv)** a diary entry

- **(A)** a verdict at the end, supported by evaluative vocabulary
- **(B)** a first paragraph that answers who, what, where, when and why
- **(C)** first-person reflection in a private tone
- **(D)** direct address, repetition and a memorable ending

**4.** Name the linguistic element that each underlined choice illustrates. **[4]**

- **(a)** "Nobody _must_ go hungry tonight."
- **(b)** "The old house _groaned_ in the wind."
- **(c)** "_Gone_. Every last one."
- **(d)** "Mistakes _were made_."

**5.** Rewrite this informal sentence for an older, formal audience reading a council newsletter, keeping the meaning: *"Loads of folk popped along to the park clean-up and it was brilliant, so cheers to everyone!"* **[3]**

## Section B

Read the passage below (original, written for this page) and answer Questions 6 and 7.

> **Library to stay open late all summer**
>
> Hartfield Library will open until 9 p.m. on weekdays this summer after a campaign by local students, the council confirmed yesterday.
>
> The change, which begins on 1 June, follows a petition signed by 2,300 residents. "Many of us have no quiet place to study at home," said sixth-form student Amara Okoye, who led the campaign. A council spokesperson said the extra hours would be reviewed in September.

**6.** Identify **four** conventions of the form that the passage shows, giving a short example of each, and state the likely audience. **[5]**

**7.** Explain how the genre, purpose and context affect the language of the passage. Refer to **two** features. **[4]**

**8.** Write a descriptive text of **no more than 150 words** for a travel brochure about an evening market, aimed at holidaymakers. Then write a reflective commentary of **about 80 words** explaining how your choices of form, structure and language fit the brief. **[13]**

**9.** A student wrote this commentary on her text:

> *"My text is a brochure. I used a metaphor and an adjective. I used short sentences. I think it is good because it is interesting."*

Identify **three** weaknesses and rewrite the commentary in a stronger form. **[5]**

**10.** The facts are these: a new park opened on Monday; it has a playground and a lake; it cost £2 million. Write **one sentence** about the park for an advertisement and **one sentence** for a letter of complaint, so that the same facts are shaped differently. **[4]**

**11.** "Knowing technical terms matters more than knowing conventions." Discuss, referring to Paper 2. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Question 1(a) is worth 15 marks [1]; Question 1(b) is worth 10 marks [1]; Section B is worth 25 marks [1]; the limit for Question 1(a) is no more than 400 words [1]. **[4]**

**2.** The conventions of a wide range of written textual forms [1]; the linguistic elements and literary features of texts [1]; the significance of audience in the design and reception of texts [1]; the ways in which genre, purpose and context contribute to the meaning of texts [1]. **[4]**

**3.** (i) B [1]; (ii) D [1]; (iii) A [1]; (iv) C [1]. **[4]**

**4.** (a) modality (modal verb "must" shows obligation) [1]; (b) personification (or a precise verb choice: the house is given a voice) [1]; (c) minor sentence (or sentence structure used for emphasis) [1]; (d) voice, the passive, which hides who made the mistakes [1]. **[4]**

**5.** For example: *A large number of residents attended the park clean-up, which was a great success, and the council would like to thank everyone for their contribution.* Credit a more formal vocabulary (for example "residents", "attended") [1], a formal sentence structure with no slang or exclamation [1], and the meaning kept: many attended, it was a success, thanks [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any four conventions with an example, one mark each, plus one for audience [5]: a headline that states the main news ("Library to stay open late all summer"); a first paragraph with the key facts (who, what, when); quoted sources ("Many of us have no quiet place ... said Amara Okoye"); a named spokesperson or authority ("A council spokesperson"); precise facts and figures ("2,300 residents", "1 June"); a neutral, impersonal tone and past or present reporting tenses. Audience: local residents or general readers of a local news outlet. **[5]**

**7.** Genre: a news story reports events, so the language is factual and concise, with the key point first [1], for example "will open until 9 p.m." in a short opening sentence [1]. Purpose and context: it informs a local community, so it quotes local voices and places the event in time ("this summer", "yesterday") [1], which helps readers see how it affects them; the neutral tone suits an informative purpose [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Practice marking guide: 8 marks for the text and 5 for the commentary.

*Text, up to 8:* appropriate brochure form with a headline or short sections [1]; tone and register suited to holidaymakers [1]; sensory detail and imagery [2]; a controlled structure, such as arrival, centre of the market, closing [1]; precise vocabulary [1]; sentence variety [1]; accurate spelling and punctuation, and within 150 words [1].

*Example text:* **Evening at the Night Bazaar.** *As the sun drops behind the old town, the lanterns come on one by one. The air is warm and sweet with grilled corn and cinnamon. Stalls glow gold along the narrow lanes: silk scarves ripple, brass bells chime, and a vendor calls out a song. Follow the crowd to the square, where musicians play until midnight. Taste, bargain, wander. Whatever you buy, bring home the evening.*

*Commentary, up to 5:* identifies the form and audience [1]; names at least two language choices, for example imagery and sensory detail [1]; explains an effect on the reader, such as creating an inviting atmosphere [1]; explains structure, for example moving from arrival to the centre to an invitation [1]; links choices to purpose and audience, for example holidaymakers who want an experience [1]. Example: *I chose a brochure form with a bold headline to attract holidaymakers. I opened with the sunset to set a calm, inviting mood. Sensory images such as "warm and sweet with grilled corn and cinnamon" help readers imagine the experience, and the list of imperatives, "Taste, bargain, wander", invites them to take part. The text moves from arrival to the market's centre to a closing invitation so that it feels like a journey.* **[13]**

**9.** Weaknesses, any three [3]: "a metaphor and an adjective" names features but does not quote them [1]; there is no effect or meaning explained [1]; there is no reference to audience or purpose [1]; "interesting" is vague and unsupported [1]; the commentary is too short to analyse form, structure and language [1]. Rewrite, up to 2 [2]: for example, *My brochure uses the metaphor "a carpet of lanterns" to make the market feel magical for tourists, and short imperatives such as "Taste, bargain, wander" to invite them to take part.* Credit a quoted example, an explained effect and a link to the audience. **[5]**

**10.** Advertisement, for example: *Discover our brand-new lakeside park, where children play and families picnic beside the sparkling water.* [2: persuasive, positive vocabulary] Complaint, for example: *I am writing to ask why the council spent £2 million on a park whose playground is already crowded and whose lake is unfenced.* [2: formal, critical, focused on cost] Credit differences in tone and selection of facts. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content, up to 6: up to 2 for terms, up to 2 for conventions, up to 2 for a judgement.

Terms: technical terms are needed in the reflective commentary to explain choices precisely, for example "modal verb", "imperative", "second person" [1]; they help to analyse why a choice works [1].

Conventions: conventions are needed in every writing task, because a text that does not follow the form of the brief will not fulfil it [1]; the brief asks for a specific form, purpose and audience (AO2 is about 80% of the paper) [1].

Judgement, for example: conventions matter more for the larger writing marks, but terms matter for the commentary, so a strong candidate needs both and should use terms to explain conventions and choices [2]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1 and 2**: forgetting the marks for the commentary or writing "AO1" as an area of knowledge.
- **Question 6**: naming a convention without a quotation.
- **Question 8**: going over the word limit or writing a commentary that retells the text.
- **Question 9**: calling a text "interesting" without naming an effect.
- **Question 10**: changing the facts instead of shaping them.
