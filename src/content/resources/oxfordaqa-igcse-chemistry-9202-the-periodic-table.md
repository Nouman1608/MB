Section 3.1.3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, has three statements. Statement a: the periodic table is arranged in order of atomic (proton) number, elements with similar properties are in columns called groups, and it is called periodic "because similar properties occur at regular intervals"; you should know the current table is based on the work of **Mendeleev**. Statement b: elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their highest energy level (outer electrons), which gives them similar chemical properties; you should know that grouping elements by similar properties allowed the prediction of undiscovered elements. Statement c: Group 0 elements, the noble gases, are unreactive because their atoms have stable arrangements of electrons; they have eight outer electrons, except helium, which has two. The qualification is untiered.

The broader [Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-chemistry-atomic-structure-and-periodic-table/) summarises these points. This guide explains the logic of the table's layout, tells the Mendeleev story at the depth the specification needs, and practises reading electronic structure from position.

## How the table is laid out

- Elements are placed in order of **atomic number**, the number of protons.
- **Groups** are the vertical columns. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
- **Periods** are the horizontal rows. Moving along a period, each element has one more proton (and one more electron) than the one before.
- The name "periodic" comes from the pattern: similar properties return at regular intervals as atomic number increases. Lithium (3), sodium (11) and potassium (19) are reactive metals that appear at intervals of 8 for these early elements, so they sit in the same column, Group 1.

## Why elements in a group behave alike

Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons, those in the highest occupied energy level. Elements in the same group have the **same number of outer electrons**, so they react in similar ways.

| Group | Example elements | Electronic structures | Outer electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Li, Na, K | 2,1 / 2,8,1 / 2,8,8,1 | 1 |
| 2 | Be, Mg, Ca | 2,2 / 2,8,2 / 2,8,8,2 | 2 |
| 7 | F, Cl | 2,7 / 2,8,7 | 7 |
| 0 | He, Ne, Ar | 2 / 2,8 / 2,8,8 | 2 (He) or 8 |

For Groups 1 to 7, the group number equals the number of outer electrons. A useful reading aid: the number of occupied shells equals the period number. Chlorine (2,8,7) has three occupied shells and seven outer electrons, so it is in Period 3, Group 7.

## Worked example 1: from structure to position, and back

*(a) Element Q has the electronic structure 2,8,3. State its group and period and identify it. (b) Give the electronic structure of the element in Group 6, Period 2.*

**(a)** Three outer electrons: **Group 3**. Three occupied shells: **Period 3**. Atomic number = 2 + 8 + 3 = 13: **aluminium**.

**(b)** Period 2: two shells. Group 6: six outer electrons. Structure **2,6**: atomic number 8, oxygen.

## Mendeleev's table

Before atomic structure was known, chemists tried to organise elements by their atomic masses. **Dmitri Mendeleev** published his periodic table in 1869. Two features made it more successful than earlier attempts:

1. **He grouped elements by their properties.** Where placing elements strictly by atomic mass would have put an element in a group whose properties did not match, he swapped the order. For example, he placed tellurium before iodine, although tellurium has the higher atomic mass, because iodine's properties match those of the other elements in its group (chlorine and bromine).
2. **He left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered**, and used the properties of neighbouring elements to predict the properties of the missing ones. When elements such as gallium and germanium were later discovered, their properties closely matched his predictions. This success convinced chemists that the table was correct.

### Why the order now makes sense

The modern table is ordered by **atomic number**, not atomic mass. Ordering by atomic number puts tellurium (52) before iodine (53), exactly where Mendeleev placed them by properties. Tellurium's higher relative atomic mass (128, compared with 127 for iodine) comes from its mix of isotopes with more neutrons, which does not affect its chemistry.

## The noble gases

Group 0 contains the **noble gases**: helium, neon, argon and the elements below them. They are unreactive because their atoms already have **stable arrangements of electrons**: eight electrons in the outer energy level, except helium, which has two in its only shell. They have no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons.

The noble gases had not been discovered when Mendeleev published his table. When they were found, they fitted in as a complete new group, which further supported the idea of grouping by properties.

## Worked example 2: an extended answer

*Explain why sodium and potassium have similar chemical properties, and why argon is unreactive. [4]*

**Model answer.** Sodium (2,8,1) and potassium (2,8,8,1) both have one electron in their outer energy level [1]. Chemical properties depend on the outer electrons, so elements with the same number of outer electrons react in similar ways [1]. Argon (2,8,8) has eight electrons in its outer energy level [1], which is a stable arrangement, so it does not easily gain, lose or share electrons [1].

## Using the periodic table on the data sheet

In the exam you have a copy of the periodic table. Use it actively:

- The **atomic number** above or below each symbol gives the number of protons and, for an atom, the number of electrons. From it you can write the electronic structure of any of the first twenty elements.
- The **relative atomic mass** is the average mass of the element's atoms. Do not confuse it with the atomic number when deciding the order of elements.
- The **column** gives the group, so you can predict that an element you have not studied, such as rubidium in Group 1, will react like sodium and potassium, because it also has one outer electron.

This last skill is exactly the kind of prediction that made Mendeleev's table useful: knowing an element's position tells you a great deal about how it will behave.

## Common mistakes

- **"The periodic table is ordered by atomic mass."** The modern table uses atomic (proton) number.
- **"Elements in a group have the same number of shells."** That describes a period.
- **"Noble gases have full shells, so they have eight electrons."** Helium has two.
- **Saying Mendeleev knew about protons.** He worked from atomic masses and properties; atomic structure was discovered later.
- **Describing the gaps without the predictions.** The key point is that he predicted the properties of undiscovered elements, and later discoveries matched.

## Exam technique

- **State** the basis of the table: atomic (proton) number.
- **Explain** similar properties: same number of outer electrons.
- **Describe** Mendeleev's work: grouped by properties, swapped some pairs, left gaps, predicted properties.
- **Evaluate**-type questions on Mendeleev: the strongest evidence for his table was that his predictions were confirmed by elements discovered later.
