## 3.1.10.1 Proton donors and proton acceptors

In Brønsted–Lowry theory:

- **acid** = **proton donor**
- **base** = **proton acceptor**
- in an acid–base equilibrium, protons pass from one species to another.

A proton is an H⁺ ion. In water it attaches to a water molecule, so H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) mean the same thing.

```
HCOOH + H₂O ⇌ HCOO⁻ + H₃O⁺
CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻
```

Water is the base in the first equation and the acid in the second.

### Worked example 1: both directions

```
H₂PO₄⁻ + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ HPO₄²⁻ + HCO₃⁻
```

Forward: H₂PO₄⁻ loses a proton, so it is the **acid**; CO₃²⁻ gains it, so it is the **base**.
Reverse: HCO₃⁻ donates a proton to HPO₄²⁻, so HCO₃⁻ is the **acid** and HPO₄²⁻ the **base**.

## 3.1.10.2 Defining and finding pH

[H⁺] spans many powers of ten, so a log scale is used:

```
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]        and so        [H⁺] = 10^(−pH)
```

One pH unit lower means ten times more H⁺. Quote pH to 2 decimal places.

A **strong acid** dissociates completely. For a monoprotic strong acid such as hydrobromic acid, HBr → H⁺ + Br⁻, so [H⁺] equals the acid concentration.

### Worked example 2

(a) [H⁺] = 7.9 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³: pH = −log₁₀(7.9 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.10**

(b) pH = 5.38: [H⁺] = 10^(−5.38) = **4.17 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³**

(c) 0.0480 mol dm⁻³ HBr: [H⁺] = 0.0480 mol dm⁻³, so pH = **1.32**

## 3.1.10.3 The ionic product of water, Kw

Pure water ionises to a tiny extent:

```
H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)        Kc = [OH⁻][H⁺] / [H₂O]
```

Hardly any water ionises, so [H₂O] is effectively constant:

```
Kc × [H₂O] = Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]        (mol² dm⁻⁶)
```

At 25 °C its value is 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. **The value changes with temperature**: warming water increases Kw, so the dissociation must be endothermic. At 50 °C it is about 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. In pure water [H⁺] = √Kw = 2.34 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, so the pH is **6.63**, yet the water is still **neutral** because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

A **strong base** dissociates completely, so [OH⁻] equals its concentration. Use Kw to get [H⁺].

### Worked example 3: pH of a strong base

Find the pH of 0.0150 mol dm⁻³ LiOH at 25 °C.

```
[OH⁻] = 0.0150 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0150 = 6.67 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³
pH = −log₁₀(6.67 × 10⁻¹³) = 12.18
```

## 3.1.10.4 Ka and pKa for weak acids

In water, only a **small fraction** of a weak acid or weak base dissociates.

```
for HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻:     Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]     (mol dm⁻³)
pKa = −log₁₀ Ka,  so  Ka = 10^(−pKa)
```

A bigger Ka, or a smaller pKa, means a stronger acid. Two approximations link pH, concentration and Ka:

1. [H⁺] = [A⁻], since each HA gives one of each and H⁺ from water is negligible.
2. [HA] at equilibrium ≈ the concentration made up, since so little dissociates.

So Ka ≈ [H⁺]² / [HA], which gives **[H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA])**.

### Worked example 4: pH from Ka

Methanoic acid has Ka = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³. Find the pH of a 0.0600 mol dm⁻³ solution and its pKa.

```
[H⁺]² = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.0600 = 1.08 × 10⁻⁵
[H⁺] = 3.29 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³
pH = 2.48
pKa = −log₁₀(1.80 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.74
```

### Worked example 5: Ka from pH, and pKa to Ka

(a) A 0.120 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid HX has pH 3.21.

```
[H⁺] = 10^(−3.21) = 6.17 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³
Ka = (6.17 × 10⁻⁴)² / 0.120 = 3.17 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³
pKa = 5.50
```

(b) An acid with pKa 7.53 has Ka = 10^(−7.53) = **2.95 × 10⁻⁸ mol dm⁻³**, so it is weaker than HX.

## 3.1.10.5 pH curves, titrations and indicators

### Working from titration data

**Worked example 6.** A 25.0 cm³ sample of methanoic acid is titrated with 0.115 mol dm⁻³ KOH. Titres: 24.30 cm³ (rough), 23.85, 23.95 and 23.90 cm³.

```
mean of concordant titres = (23.85 + 23.95 + 23.90) / 3 = 23.90 cm³
n(KOH) = 0.115 × 23.90 / 1000 = 2.75 × 10⁻³ mol = n(HCOOH)   (1 : 1)
[HCOOH] = 2.75 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.110 mol dm⁻³
```

**Worked example 7: pH when base is in excess.** 16.0 cm³ of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ HBr is added to 20.0 cm³ of 0.300 mol dm⁻³ KOH at 25 °C.

```
n(H⁺) = 0.250 × 0.0160 = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol
n(OH⁻) = 0.300 × 0.0200 = 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol
excess OH⁻ = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol in 36.0 cm³
[OH⁻] = 2.00 × 10⁻³ / 0.0360 = 0.0556 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0556 = 1.80 × 10⁻¹³      pH = 12.74
```

### Shapes of the four pH curves

Acid in the flask, base from the burette, all monoprotic:

| Combination | Starting pH | Vertical section | pH at equivalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | About 1 | Long: roughly pH 3 to 11 | 7 |
| Weak acid + strong base | Higher, often about 3 | Shorter, roughly pH 7 to 11 | Above 7 |
| Strong acid + weak base | About 1 | Shorter, roughly pH 3 to 7 | Below 7 |
| Weak acid + weak base | Higher | None: a gradual bend | Hard to locate |

Why each feature appears:

- **Higher start for a weak acid**: it is only slightly dissociated.
- **Gentle rise early on (weak acid + strong base)**: HA and A⁻ together form a buffer.
- **Half-neutralisation point**: half the HA has become A⁻, so [HA] = [A⁻]. Ka = [H⁺] there, so **pH = pKa**.
- **Equivalence above 7 (weak acid + strong base)**: A⁻ accepts protons from water, releasing OH⁻. **Below 7 (strong acid + weak base)**: an ion such as NH₄⁺ donates protons to water.

### Worked example 8: Ka from a pH curve

A weak acid HZ is titrated with NaOH. The equivalence point is at 22.40 cm³. At 11.20 cm³ the pH is 4.31.

11.20 cm³ is half the equivalence volume, so pH = pKa = 4.31. Ka = 10^(−4.31) = **4.90 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³**.

### Choosing an indicator

An indicator works when its **whole colour-change range falls inside the vertical section** of the curve.

| Indicator | Colour change | pH range |
|---|---|---|
| Methyl orange | red → yellow | 3.1–4.4 |
| Methyl red | red → yellow | 4.4–6.2 |
| Bromothymol blue | yellow → blue | 6.0–7.6 |
| Phenolphthalein | colourless → pink | 8.3–10.0 |

- Strong acid + strong base: any of these.
- Phenolphthalein for a weak acid against a strong base.
- Methyl orange or methyl red for a strong acid against a weak base.
- Nothing suits a weak acid against a weak base: with no sharp change, follow it with a pH meter.

### Required practical 7

You follow the pH as a **strong base is added to a weak acid**, and again as a **strong acid is added to a weak base**.

1. Calibrate the meter first using buffers of known pH; rinse the probe with deionised water between solutions.
2. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the weak acid (such as methanoic acid) into a beaker.
3. Add the strong base from a burette in 1–2 cm³ portions, stirring, and record pH once the reading settles.
4. Near the end point, add 0.10–0.20 cm³ at a time so the steep section is well defined. Carry on past equivalence.
5. Plot pH against volume. Repeat with a weak base (such as aqueous ammonia) and a strong acid.

From each graph read the equivalence volume (middle of the vertical section) and, for the weak acid, pKa at half that volume.

## 3.1.10.6 Buffer action

A **buffer solution** keeps a roughly constant pH when it is diluted or when small amounts of acid or base are added.

- **Acidic buffers** contain a weak acid and a salt of that weak acid, often made by partly neutralising the acid with a strong base.
- **Basic buffers** contain a weak base and a salt of that weak base, such as methylamine with methylammonium chloride.

### How the buffers work

In an acidic buffer, HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ lies to the left and the salt supplies plenty of A⁻.

- **Acid added**: H⁺ combines with A⁻ to form HA; the equilibrium moves left.
- **Base added**: OH⁻ removes H⁺ as water; more HA dissociates, moving the equilibrium right and replacing most of the H⁺.

[HA] and [A⁻] are both large, so their ratio, and the pH, barely changes. Dilution changes both by the same factor.

In a basic buffer:

```
added acid:   CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺
added base:   CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → CH₃NH₂ + H₂O
```

### Calculating the pH of an acidic buffer

Rearrange the Ka expression:

```
[H⁺] = Ka × [HA] / [A⁻]
```

[A⁻] comes from the salt. Both share one volume, so moles can be used.

**Worked example 9.** 500 cm³ of buffer holds 0.0500 mol of HZ and 0.0800 mol of NaZ (Ka = 4.90 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³).

```
[H⁺] = 4.90 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0500 / 0.0800 = 3.06 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³      pH = 4.51
```

**Worked example 10: partial neutralisation.** A buffer is made by running 10.0 cm³ of 0.300 mol dm⁻³ NaOH into 30.0 cm³ of 0.180 mol dm⁻³ HZ.

```
n(HZ) at start = 5.40 × 10⁻³ mol;   n(OH⁻) = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol
HZ left = 2.40 × 10⁻³ mol;   Z⁻ formed = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol
[H⁺] = 4.90 × 10⁻⁵ × 2.40 / 3.00 = 3.92 × 10⁻⁵      pH = 4.41
```

### Applications

- Calibrating pH meters, as in required practical 7.
- Blood, where a carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate system holds pH in a narrow range.
- Foods and drinks, where sodium citrate is added as an acidity regulator.

## Common errors

- Applying the weak-acid square-root formula to a buffer, where [A⁻] is far bigger than [H⁺].
- Ignoring the total volume after mixing.
- Describing pure water above 25 °C as acidic just because its pH is under 7.
- Choosing an indicator when only one end of its range is on the steep part.
- Averaging the rough titre with the concordant ones.
