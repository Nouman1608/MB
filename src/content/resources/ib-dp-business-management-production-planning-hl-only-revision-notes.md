## Checklist of the content points

- Local and global supply chain process.
- Difference between JIT and just-in-case (JIC).
- Stock control charts: lead time, buffer stock, reorder level, reorder quantity.
- Capacity utilization rate.
- Defect rate.
- Labour productivity, capital productivity, productivity rate, operating leverage.
- Cost to buy (CTB) and cost to make (CTM).

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply chain | All the steps and organizations from raw materials to the customer |
| Local supply chain | Most stages within one region or country |
| Global supply chain | Stages spread across several countries |
| JIT | Ordering and producing only what is needed, when needed, to keep stock minimal |
| JIC | Holding larger stocks in case of delay or a demand surge |
| Lead time | Time from placing an order to receiving it |
| Buffer stock | Minimum stock kept as a safety margin |
| Reorder level | Stock level that triggers a new order |
| Reorder quantity | Amount ordered each time |
| Capacity | Maximum output with present resources |
| Defect rate | Share of output that fails quality checks |
| Operating leverage | How strongly a change in sales changes profit, because of fixed costs |

## Formulas

The guide's formulae section gives the first two rows. The rest are standard forms, so say "standard form" in an answer.

| Measure | Formula | In the guide's formulae section? |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity utilization rate | actual output ÷ productive capacity × 100 | Yes |
| Productivity rate | total output ÷ total input × 100 | Yes |
| Reorder level | buffer stock + (usage per day × lead time) | No, standard form |
| Maximum stock | buffer stock + reorder quantity | No, standard form |
| Days between orders | reorder quantity ÷ usage per day | No, standard form |
| Defect rate | defective units ÷ total units × 100 | No, standard form |
| Labour productivity | output ÷ number of employees | No, standard form |
| Capital productivity | output ÷ capital employed | No, standard form |
| Operating leverage | total contribution ÷ profit from operations | No, standard form |
| CTB | price per unit × quantity | No, standard form |
| CTM | variable cost per unit × quantity + extra fixed cost | No, standard form |

## Method in steps

Stock control chart
1. Work out usage during lead time: daily usage × lead time.
2. Add the buffer stock to get the reorder level.
3. Add the reorder quantity to the buffer to get maximum stock.
4. Divide reorder quantity by daily usage for the days between deliveries.
5. If use rises, recheck whether the delivery arrives before stock falls below zero.

Make or buy
1. Find CTB: unit price × quantity.
2. Find CTM: extra fixed cost + variable cost per unit × quantity.
3. Compare. The lower cost is cheaper for that quantity.
4. Find the crossover: extra fixed cost ÷ (buy price − variable cost).
5. Add non-numerical factors: quality, control, reliability, flexibility, capacity, confidentiality, stakeholders.
6. Reach a judgement linked to the forecast volume.

Operating leverage
1. Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit.
2. Total contribution = contribution per unit × output.
3. Profit = total contribution − fixed costs.
4. Operating leverage = total contribution ÷ profit.
5. Percentage change in profit is roughly operating leverage × percentage change in sales volume (with price, variable cost per unit and fixed costs unchanged).

## Small worked reminder

Sedgewick Tiles makes 18,000 tiles with 45 workers. Sales value is USD 90,000 and total input cost is USD 75,000, of which USD 45,000 is variable cost and USD 30,000 is fixed cost.

- Labour productivity = 18,000 ÷ 45 = 400 tiles per worker.
- Productivity rate = 90,000 ÷ 75,000 × 100 = 120%.

Total contribution = 90,000 − 45,000 = USD 45,000 and profit = 90,000 − 75,000 = USD 15,000, so operating leverage = 45,000 ÷ 15,000 = 3. A 5% rise in sales volume gives about a 15% rise in profit.

## Must-know distinctions

JIT versus JIC
- JIT: low holding cost, little cash tied up, high dependence on suppliers, risk of stoppage.
- JIC: high holding cost, more cash tied up, protection against delays and surges, risk of waste and obsolescence.

Local versus global supply chain
- Local: shorter lead time, easier control, often higher input price, less transport.
- Global: often lower unit cost, longer lead time, exchange-rate and disruption risk, harder oversight.

Buffer stock versus reorder level
- Buffer stock is the floor you try not to go below.
- Reorder level is higher: it equals the buffer plus the stock used while waiting for delivery.

Labour productivity versus productivity rate
- Labour productivity is output per worker or hour, in physical units.
- Productivity rate compares total output with total input, as a percentage, with both measured the same way.

CTB versus CTM
- CTB is mostly variable in quantity. CTM includes extra fixed cost, so making wins at high volume and buying at low volume.

## Evaluation prompts for AO3 points

Several points in 5.6 carry AO3, so practise weighing, not only calculating. For each, state a point, develop it with the case, then judge.

- JIT or JIC: What does a stoppage cost compared with the cost of holding stock? How reliable are the suppliers? How steady is demand? Is cash tight? A reasoned judgement often favours JIT with a small buffer, but only if the case supports it.
- Local or global chain: Does the lower unit price outweigh longer lead time, extra stock and exchange-rate risk? Which stakeholders gain or lose, for example workers, suppliers and communities? Would a mix of both reduce risk?
- CTB or CTM: Is the demand forecast reliable enough to commit to the fixed cost of making? What happens to the answer if volume falls below the crossover? Does the business have spare capacity, skills and space? Would making protect quality or confidential designs?

A short model structure for a 6-mark or 8-mark answer: one definition, two developed points for one side, two for the other, a link to the business named in the case, and a final sentence beginning "overall" that makes a choice and gives its condition.

## Linking the measures

The measures work together. A rise in capacity utilization usually raises labour productivity, because fixed labour is spread over more output. A rising defect rate lowers the value of useful output, so productivity rate falls even if units made are unchanged. High operating leverage makes profit sensitive to utilization, since low utilization leaves fixed costs uncovered. A short buffer lowers holding costs but raises the risk that a delayed delivery cuts output and utilization. When asked to analyse a business, use two or three of these links and quote the figures.

## Quick self-test

1. A business uses 30 units a day, lead time is 4 days and buffer stock is 60 units. Calculate the reorder level.
2. The reorder quantity in question 1 is 360 units. Calculate the maximum stock and the days between orders.
3. Capacity is 800 units a month and output is 640. Calculate the capacity utilization rate.
4. Output rises by 80 units in question 3. State the new rate.
5. A firm makes 1,200 units and 48 are defective. Calculate the defect rate.
6. Give one cost of a high defect rate and one possible cause.
7. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a global supply chain.
8. State one condition that makes JIT more suitable than JIC.
9. Operating leverage is 3. Sales volume falls 5%. Estimate the change in profit.
10. Buying costs USD 8 per unit. Making costs USD 5 per unit variable plus USD 4,500 fixed. For 2,000 units, compare CTB and CTM, then find the crossover quantity.

### Answers

1. 60 + (30 × 4) = 180 units.
2. Maximum stock = 60 + 360 = 420 units. Days between orders = 360 ÷ 30 = 12 days.
3. 640 ÷ 800 × 100 = 80%.
4. 720 ÷ 800 × 100 = 90%.
5. 48 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 4%.
6. Cost: scrap, rework, refunds or damaged reputation. Cause: worn machinery, poor materials or inadequate training.
7. Advantage: often lower unit cost. Disadvantage: longer lead time and more exposure to delay, exchange rates or disruption.
8. Reliable, nearby suppliers with fast delivery, and predictable demand.
9. Profit falls by about 15%.
10. CTB = 8 × 2,000 = 16,000. CTM = 4,500 + (5 × 2,000) = 14,500. Making is cheaper by 1,500. Crossover = 4,500 ÷ (8 − 5) = 1,500 units. At 1,200 units buying costs 9,600 and making costs 10,500, so buying wins below the crossover.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the buffer when calculating reorder level.
- Stating a formula but not showing the substitution.
- Dividing utilization by actual output instead of capacity.
- Reporting a percentage without the percent sign or a figure without units.
- Mixing units in the productivity rate.
- Describing JIT or JIC with no link to the business in the case.
- Comparing CTB and CTM with numbers only and no non-numerical factors, or the reverse.
- Writing a one-sided evaluation with no judgement.
- Treating high capacity utilization as always good, with no mention of the lack of slack.
