## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between an aim and a hypothesis, and between a directional and a non-directional hypothesis. **[4]**

**2.** Define operationalisation and explain why it matters. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **four** sampling methods, giving one strength and one weakness of each. **[8]**

**4.** Explain the difference between independent groups, repeated measures and matched pairs designs, giving one advantage and one disadvantage of each. **[9]**

**5.** Explain the following and how each is controlled: demand characteristics, investigator effects, order effects. **[6]**

**6.** A study reports a correlation of +0.78 between hours of revision and exam score.

**(a)** Describe what this shows. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why this does not prove revision causes higher scores. **[4]**
**(c)** State one appropriate way to display these data. **[1]**

**7.** Explain the difference between reliability and validity, and describe one way of assessing each. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An **aim** is a **general statement of what the researcher intends to investigate**; a **hypothesis** is a **precise, testable prediction** about the relationship between variables [1] [1]. A **directional (one-tailed)** hypothesis **states the direction of the expected difference**, used where previous research suggests it; a **non-directional (two-tailed)** hypothesis states only that **there will be a difference**, used where the evidence is mixed or absent [1] [1].

**2.** Operationalisation is **defining a variable in terms of how it will be precisely measured or manipulated** [1]. It matters because a vague variable such as "aggression" **cannot be measured consistently** [1]; operationalising it as "number of physical contacts in a ten-minute observation" makes the study **replicable and the results comparable** [1].

**3.** Any four, 2 marks each: **Random** — everyone in the population has an equal chance of selection; free from researcher bias, but **may still produce an unrepresentative sample by chance** and requires a full list of the population [1] [1]. **Opportunity** — whoever is available; quick and cheap, but **highly unrepresentative and open to researcher bias** in whom they approach [1] [1]. **Volunteer** — participants respond to an advertisement; convenient and gives willing participants, but produces **volunteer bias**, as volunteers tend to be more motivated and helpful than average [1] [1]. **Stratified** — the sample reflects the proportions of subgroups in the population; the most **representative method**, but **time-consuming** and the strata must be known in advance [1] [1]. **Systematic** — every nth person is selected; objective and simple, but **not truly random** and a periodic pattern in the list could bias it [1] [1].

**4. Independent groups** — different participants in each condition; **no order effects**, but **participant variables may confound the results** and more participants are needed [1] [1] [1]. **Repeated measures** — the same participants in every condition; **participant variables are controlled** and fewer participants are needed, but there are **order effects and greater risk of demand characteristics** [1] [1] [1]. **Matched pairs** — different participants matched on key variables; **controls participant variables without order effects**, but **matching is time-consuming and can never be perfect** [1] [1] [1].

**5. Demand characteristics** — cues that lead participants to guess the aim and change their behaviour [1]; controlled by a **single-blind procedure or a cover story** [1]. **Investigator effects** — the researcher unintentionally influences the outcome through expectations or manner [1]; controlled by a **double-blind procedure or standardised instructions** [1]. **Order effects** — practice or fatigue from doing one condition before another [1]; controlled by **counterbalancing**, so half the participants do the conditions in each order [1].

**6. (a)** A **strong positive correlation** [1] — as revision hours increase, exam scores tend to increase [1].
**(b)** Correlation **does not establish the direction of causation** — students who find the subject easy may enjoy it and therefore revise more, so high ability could cause both [1] [1]. A **third variable** such as motivation, family support or teaching quality may cause both the revision and the score [1] [1].
**(c)** A **scattergram** [1].

**7. Reliability** is the **consistency of a measure** — whether it gives the same result on repeated use [1]. **Validity** is whether the measure **actually measures what it claims to measure** [1]. Reliability can be assessed by **test–retest**, giving the same test to the same people after an interval and correlating the scores, or by **inter-observer reliability**, correlating two independent observers' records [1] [1]. Validity can be assessed by **concurrent validity**, correlating the new measure with an established one, or by **face validity**, judging whether the items appear to measure the intended construct [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing an aim in place of a hypothesis.
- Not operationalising variables in a stated hypothesis.
- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Accepting a correlation as evidence of cause.
