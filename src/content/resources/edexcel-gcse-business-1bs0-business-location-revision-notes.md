These notes condense content item 1.4.2, Business location, in Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. For worked examples read the [business location study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-location/), then test yourself with the [business location practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-location-practice/). The wider [limited liability and location revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-limited-liability-and-business-location-revision-notes/) put 1.4.1 and 1.4.2 side by side.

## What the specification lists

Factors influencing business location:

- proximity to: market, labour, materials and competitors
- nature of the business activity
- the impact of the internet on location decisions: e-commerce and/or fixed premises.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Location | Where a business operates from |
| Proximity | Nearness |
| Market | The customers who buy, or might buy, the product |
| Labour | The workers, and the skills they bring |
| Materials | The inputs used to make a product |
| Footfall | The number of people passing or entering a site |
| Fixed premises | A physical site such as a shop, office, workshop or warehouse |
| E-commerce | Buying and selling goods and services online |
| Nature of the business activity | What the business actually does, which decides which factor matters most |

## Each factor: pull and cost

| Factor | Why it can pull a business close | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Market | More customers, more sales; easier delivery of perishable or costly-to-move goods | Busy sites charge high rent |
| Labour | Easier to recruit people with the right skills | Competition for staff can push wages up |
| Materials | Lower transport costs for heavy, bulky or perishable inputs; less waste | Sites near materials may be far from customers |
| Competitors | Customers who compare products visit the area; demand already proven | Trade is shared; price competition |

## Matching factor to activity

- **Customers come to the business** (café, gym, salon): market and footfall first.
- **The business goes to customers** (plumber, mobile caterer): a cheap base near the area served.
- **Heavy inputs, lighter outputs** (sawmill, quarry products): materials first.
- **Specialist skills** (design studio, engineering firm): labour first.
- **Online sellers**: low-cost space, delivery links and storage.

## The internet and location

**E-commerce reduces the pull of the market when:** goods can be posted; customers do not need to visit; a cheaper site cuts fixed costs.

**Fixed premises are still needed when:** the service is delivered in person; stock must be stored and dispatched; customers want to see or try products; a shop builds trust.

**Both together:** a shop plus a website reaches local and distant customers; the shop can act as a collection or returns point.

## Useful calculations

The specification's quantitative skills include percentages and revenue, costs and profit, so location data often appears as figures.

- Revenue = price × quantity
- Difference in cost = cost at site A − cost at site B
- Percentage of revenue spent on rent = rent ÷ revenue × 100
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Example: rent rises from £9,600 to £11,040 a year. Change = £1,440. Percentage change = £1,440 ÷ £9,600 × 100 = 15%.

## Exam traps

- "Near the market" is not always the answer; check the nature of the activity first.
- Being close to competitors can help, for example where shoppers like to compare.
- An online business still needs somewhere to store and send stock.
- Comparing sites on cost alone ignores the revenue each site could bring.
- A forecast of customers at a new site is only a forecast; say so in Evaluate answers.
- Use the figures in the source; a generic list of factors earns little in Analyse questions.
- "Proximity to labour" is about the right skills as well as the number of workers; a site near many people may still lack the specialists a business needs.
- A cheaper site is not automatically better: lower rent can come with lower footfall, so compare revenue as well as cost.

## Self-test

1. List the four "proximity to" factors in the specification.
2. Define footfall.
3. Give one reason a bakery selling fresh bread might locate near its customers.
4. Give one reason a business making garden paving slabs might locate near its materials.
5. Why might a software company locate where many skilled developers live?
6. Give one advantage of locating near competitors.
7. Give one disadvantage of locating near competitors.
8. Explain why e-commerce may reduce the importance of proximity to the market.
9. Give two reasons an online retailer still needs fixed premises.
10. Site X rent is £15,000 and site Y rent is £11,400. Calculate the difference as a percentage of site Y's rent.
11. A café's revenue is £96,000 and its rent is £14,400. Calculate rent as a percentage of revenue.
12. Why should a location answer refer to the nature of the business activity?

### Answers

1. Market, labour, materials, competitors.
2. The number of people passing or visiting a site.
3. Fresh bread is perishable and customers buy it often, so being close brings regular passing trade and keeps it fresh.
4. Stone and sand are heavy and bulky, so transporting them far is expensive.
5. It can recruit the specialist staff it depends on more easily.
6. Customers who come to the area to compare products may also visit the business; demand is already proven.
7. Customers and sales are shared, and rivals may cut prices.
8. Customers can buy from anywhere and goods are delivered, so the business does not need to be near them to make sales.
9. Any two: to store stock; to pack and dispatch orders; to handle returns; to offer collection.
10. Difference = £3,600. £3,600 ÷ £11,400 × 100 = 31.6%.
11. £14,400 ÷ £96,000 × 100 = 15%.
12. What the business does decides which factor matters most, so the right location differs between, for example, a salon and a sawmill.
