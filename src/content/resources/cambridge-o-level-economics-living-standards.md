## What the syllabus asks for

| Reference | Skill |
|---|---|
| 5.1.1 | Use two indicators: income per person at constant prices and the Human Development Index (HDI). Know what goes into each, and weigh the strengths and weaknesses of each. |
| 5.1.2 | Compare living standards and the sharing of income. Explain why they differ inside one country and from one country to another. |

The syllabus prints no formula for the HDI and does not ask you to work out an index. You do need to read figures, compare countries and judge what a measure shows or hides.

## Living standards: the idea

A person's **standard of living** is how well off they are in terms of the goods, services and conditions they can enjoy: food, housing, health care, schooling, safety and leisure. Economists cannot observe this directly, so they use **indicators**, figures that stand in for it. No single indicator is perfect. Most exam questions ask you to choose one, use it, and say what it leaves out.

## 5.1.1 Real GDP per head

### Components of the measure

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the worth of every final good and service that a country's economy produces over one year. Its spending components are consumption, investment, government spending, and exports minus imports.

- **Nominal GDP** is valued at current prices, so inflation alone can push it up.
- **Real GDP** is valued at fixed base-year prices, so only a change in output moves it. Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100.
- **Real GDP per head** (per capita) = real GDP ÷ population. It is an average, so it shows what each person would receive if output were shared equally.

### Worked example: Country J (illustrative)

In 2024 (the base year, price index 100) Country J has nominal GDP of 540 billion and a population of 12.0 million. In 2026 nominal GDP is 741 billion, the price index is 130 and the population is 12.8 million.

1. Real GDP in 2026 = 741 ÷ 130 × 100 = 570 billion.
2. Real GDP growth = (570 − 540) ÷ 540 × 100 = 5.56%.
3. Real GDP per head in 2024 = 540 billion ÷ 12.0 million = 45,000.
4. Real GDP per head in 2026 = 570 billion ÷ 12.8 million = 44,531.25.
5. Change in real GDP per head = (44,531.25 − 45,000) ÷ 45,000 × 100 = −1.04%.

Nominal GDP rose by 37.2%, but real output rose by only 5.56%. Population rose by 6.67%, faster than output. So the average person is slightly worse off, even though the economy has grown. The fall in real GDP per head is the reason to say living standards, on this measure, have fallen.

### Advantages of real GDP per head

- Governments measure it regularly, using common methods, so figures are easy to find and compare over time.
- It is one number, so it is quick to rank countries.
- Using real values stops inflation passing for progress, and dividing by population allows for countries of different size.
- Higher income per head usually allows more spending on goods, health and education, so it tends to go with better living standards.

### Disadvantages of real GDP per head

- It is an **average**. It says nothing about how income is shared (see 5.1.2).
- It leaves out output that is not recorded: subsistence farming, unpaid household work and the informal or hidden economy. Official GDP can therefore understate living standards. If hidden output is 60 and recorded GDP is 400, recorded GDP understates total output by 15%.
- It counts all output as a gain, including goods that harm people, and it ignores pollution, long working hours and loss of leisure.
- It does not directly measure health, education, life expectancy or freedom.
- Prices differ between countries, so a given income can buy more in one country than another.

## 5.1.1 The HDI as an indicator

The HDI is a combined index published by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) to rank countries. It has three components:

| Component | What it captures | Typical measure |
|---|---|---|
| Health | Length and quality of life | Life expectancy at birth |
| Education | Knowledge | Years of schooling |
| Income | Command over goods and services | Income per head |

Each component is turned into a score between 0 and 1 and the three are combined into a single HDI between 0 and 1. A higher figure means higher human development. The HDI combines the three scores with a geometric mean (the cube root of their product), so a very low score in one component cannot be fully hidden by high scores in the others.

### Worked example: reading HDI scores (illustrative)

| | Health | Education | Income | HDI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Country R | 0.60 | 0.55 | 0.85 | 0.655 |
| Country S | 0.85 | 0.80 | 0.70 | 0.781 |

Country R has the higher income score (0.85 against 0.70), so it would probably rank higher on real GDP per head. S has the higher HDI because its health and education scores are far higher. The lesson: **a country can be richer yet have lower human development**. The HDI shows that income is only one route to a better life.

(HDI figures above are rounded to three decimal places. The method of combining scores is for understanding only; you are not asked to calculate it.)

### Advantages of the HDI

- It covers health and education as well as income, so it is broader than GDP per head.
- It is one figure, so countries are easy to rank and compare.
- It shows when a rich country neglects health or schooling, and when a poorer country does well on both.
- The income part is based on the logarithm of income, so extra income counts for less as income rises, which fits the idea that an extra 1,000 matters more to a poor household.

### Disadvantages of the HDI

- Only three components. It leaves out inequality, poverty, safety, freedom, pollution, housing and leisure.
- It is still an average, so it hides gaps between rich and poor, men and women, regions.
- The components are given equal importance, which is a judgement. A country could argue that one matters more.
- Data on schooling, life expectancy and income are collected with varying accuracy.
- Because the scores tend to move together, the HDI often ranks countries much like income does, so it adds less than first appears.

### Choosing between them

| Question | Better indicator |
|---|---|
| Has output per person grown since last year? | Real GDP per head |
| Is a country using its income to improve health and schooling? | HDI |
| How is income shared? | Neither (use income distribution data) |

## 5.1.2 Comparing living standards

### Between countries

Two checks come before any comparison. First, use **real** figures when comparing over time. Second, remember that prices differ across countries, so equal money incomes do not mean equal buying power.

**Reasons for differences between countries** include:

- **Productivity**: output per worker. Better machinery, skills and organisation raise it.
- **Saving and investment**: poor households save little, so there is little money for machinery and tools, so output per worker stays low and incomes stay low. This is a vicious circle.
- **Education and health care**: healthier, better-taught workers produce more and live longer.
- **Population growth**: if population grows faster than output, real GDP per head falls.
- **Structure of the economy**: a country that relies on the primary sector (farming, mining) usually has lower incomes than one with a large secondary and tertiary sector (manufacturing and services).
- **Resources, trade, stability and government**: natural resources, access to world markets, peace and sound institutions.

A chain of reasoning, written in steps, scores better than a list:

```
better education -> more skilled workers -> higher output per worker
-> higher incomes and tax revenue -> more spending on schools -> better education
```

### Within a country

Income and living standards also differ **inside** one country.

- **Wages**: skilled jobs earn more than unskilled ones.
- **Ownership of assets**: owners of land, shares and businesses receive profit, rent and interest as well as wages.
- **Unemployment**, age, illness and part-time work lower household income.
- **Region**: cities often offer more jobs and services than rural areas.
- **Government**: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps. Taxes that fall most heavily on the poor widen them.

### Worked example: income distribution (illustrative)

Countries M and N both have average income of 30,000 per person. The table gives each fifth (quintile) of the population's share of total income.

| Fifth of population | Share in M | Share in N |
|---|---|---|
| Poorest | 5% | 9% |
| Second | 9% | 13% |
| Middle | 13% | 17% |
| Fourth | 21% | 23% |
| Richest | 52% | 38% |
| Total | 100% | 100% |

1. Average income of the poorest fifth in M = 5% × 30,000 ÷ 20% = 7,500. In N it is 9% × 30,000 ÷ 20% = 13,500.
2. Average income of the richest fifth in M = 52% × 30,000 ÷ 20% = 78,000. In N it is 57,000.
3. The richest fifth earns 10.4 times the poorest in M (52 ÷ 5) and 4.2 times in N (38 ÷ 9).

M and N have the same average, yet income is far more equal in N. Most people in N are better off than their counterparts in M. This is why GDP per head alone is a poor guide to how most people live.

### Evaluating a comparison

When asked to judge which country has the higher living standard:

1. State what each indicator says.
2. Say what it leaves out (distribution, hidden output, environment, freedom).
3. Check whether the figures are real and per head.
4. Reach a conclusion that depends on the evidence. It may be that Country B ranks higher on income but Country A ranks higher on health.

## Common errors

- Comparing nominal figures across years and calling the change growth.
- Calling GDP per head a measure of how income is shared; it is only an average.
- Listing HDI components as "GDP, health, education". The income component is income per head.
- Saying the HDI measures happiness. It measures three things only.
- Giving a list of reasons for differences without linking each to living standards.
