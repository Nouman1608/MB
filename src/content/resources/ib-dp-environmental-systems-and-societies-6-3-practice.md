This set covers ESS subtopic 6.3, Climate change -- mitigation and adaptation. Revise first with the [6.3 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-6-3/) and the [6.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-6-3-revision-notes/). ## Section A

**1.** Define *net zero*. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a carbon tax and a cap-and-trade system. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by blue carbon and why it matters for climate mitigation. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why carbon capture and storage fitted to a gas power station is not a method of carbon removal. **[2]**

**5.** State two risks of solar radiation modification. **[2]**

**6.** Define *maladaptation* and give one example. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) A fictional country must cut 2.0 million t CO₂e a year. Its options are:

| Option | Potential / million t CO₂e per year | Cost / $ per t CO₂e |
|---|---|---|
| Methane leak repairs | 0.4 | −10 |
| Onshore wind | 0.9 | 20 |
| Electric buses | 0.3 | 60 |
| Direct air capture | 1.0 | 300 |

**(a)** Using the cheapest options first, identify the options needed to meet the target and the amount taken from each. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total annual cost of meeting the target. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the average cost per tonne. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest one reason why the country might choose electric buses before onshore wind, despite the higher cost. **[1]**

**8.** (Data response) A fictional coastal region emits 3.6 million t CO₂ a year. It can restore 12 000 ha of mangroves, which are assumed to store 6 t CO₂ per hectare per year.

**(a)** Calculate the annual CO₂ uptake of the restored mangroves. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate this as a percentage of the region's emissions. **[1]**
**(c)** Outline two benefits of mangrove restoration other than carbon storage. **[2]**
**(d)** Using your answers, evaluate mangrove restoration as a climate strategy for the region. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why warming will not stop until net carbon dioxide emissions reach about zero. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate a sea wall as a way of adapting a coastal town to sea-level rise. **[4]**

**11.** Outline how the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities is reflected in international climate finance. **[3]**

**12.** "Adaptation is now more urgent than mitigation." To what extent do you agree? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A state in which any remaining greenhouse gas emissions are balanced by an equal amount removed from the atmosphere [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A carbon tax fixes the price per tonne emitted, leaving the amount of emissions cut uncertain [1]; cap-and-trade fixes the total quantity of emissions with tradable permits, leaving the price to the market [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Blue carbon is carbon stored by coastal and marine ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes [1]; it is stored in waterlogged sediments where decomposition is slow, so protecting and restoring these ecosystems keeps carbon out of the atmosphere for long periods [1]. **[2]**

**4.** CCS captures CO₂ from the power station before it is released, so it prevents new emissions [1], but it does not take any CO₂ already in the atmosphere out of it, so it is emission reduction, not removal [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two: it does not reduce ocean acidification [1]; it could change rainfall patterns in some regions [1]; sudden termination would cause rapid warming; it raises disputes over who controls it; it may reduce pressure to cut emissions. **[2]**

**6.** An action taken to adapt that increases vulnerability or emissions in the long run [1]; example, any one: a sea wall that increases erosion along the coast; air conditioning powered by fossil fuels; building in flood zones behind defences [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Methane leak repairs 0.4, onshore wind 0.9 and electric buses 0.3 million t (total 1.6) [1], then 0.4 million t from direct air capture to reach 2.0 [1].
**(b)** (0.4 × −10) + (0.9 × 20) + (0.3 × 60) + (0.4 × 300) = −4 + 18 + 18 + 120 = $152 million [1]; total = **$152 million per year** [1].
**(c)** 152 ÷ 2.0 = **$76 per t CO₂e** [1].
**(d)** Any one: buses also reduce urban air pollution and improve health; they can be introduced faster; they are more publicly visible and popular [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Costs in $ per tonne multiplied by millions of tonnes give millions of dollars; keep track of the units.

**8. (a)** 12 000 × 6 = **72 000 t CO₂ per year** [1].
**(b)** 72 000 ÷ 3 600 000 × 100 = **2%** [1].
**(c)** Any two: protection of the coast from storm surges and erosion [1]; nursery habitat for fish, supporting fisheries [1]; biodiversity; filtering of sediment and pollutants.
**(d)** Mangrove restoration offsets only 2% of the region's emissions, so it cannot replace emission cuts [1]; but its adaptation and biodiversity benefits mean it is worthwhile as part of a combined strategy [1]. **[6]**

**9.** CO₂ persists in the atmosphere for centuries to thousands of years, so emissions accumulate [1]. Warming depends on the cumulative total of CO₂ emitted [1]. As long as net emissions are positive, the total and therefore the warming continue to rise; only at net zero does the total stop increasing [1]. **[3]**

**10.** A sea wall protects property and infrastructure behind it from flooding and erosion [1] and gives a predictable level of protection that can be designed for a given sea level [1]. However, it is expensive to build and maintain, and may increase erosion further along the coast or remove beaches (maladaptation) [1]. Judgement: worthwhile for dense, high-value towns in the short to medium term, but combined with planning controls or managed retreat as sea level keeps rising [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The principle holds that all countries must act but richer countries, with greater historical emissions and resources, should lead [1]. Climate finance moves money from richer to poorer countries for mitigation and adaptation, for example through the Green Climate Fund [1]. The loss-and-damage fund agreed at COP27 in 2022 extends this to harm that adaptation cannot prevent [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- Some warming is already locked in by past emissions, so impacts will grow whatever is done now [1].
- Vulnerable communities face present harm from heatwaves, floods and drought, so adaptation saves lives now [1].
- Adaptation benefits are local and immediate, so communities and governments may support it more readily [1].
- However, without mitigation, warming continues and the costs and limits of adaptation rise [1].
- Some impacts, such as ice-sheet loss and coral reef death, cannot be adapted to [1].
- Mitigation reduces the risk of crossing tipping points [1].
- Many strategies do both: mangrove restoration, efficient buildings, agroforestry [1].
- Who should pay differs: mitigation by high emitters; adaptation finance for vulnerable countries [1].
- Judgement: both are urgent; adaptation is urgent for vulnerable people now, but mitigation determines how much adaptation will be needed, so the statement is only partly true [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking options out of cost order, or using more of an option than its potential.
- Losing track of millions in cost calculations.
- Calling CCS on a power station "removal".
- Evaluating a sea wall with no mention of maladaptation or long-term limits.
- Answering "to what extent" questions without showing that some strategies do both.
