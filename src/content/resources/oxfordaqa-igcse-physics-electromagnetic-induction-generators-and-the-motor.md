## The generator effect (3.6.1 a)

A wire sitting still in a steady magnetic field has no pd across its ends. A pd appears only when the wire and the field are changing relative to each other. There are two ways this can happen.

- The conductor moves relative to the field, so it cuts through magnetic field lines.
- The conductor sits in a field that is itself changing, for example the field of a magnet that is being moved nearby.

This is the **generator effect**, and the pd is called an **induced pd**. When the conductor forms part of a complete circuit, the pd drives an induced current. An open circuit has the pd but no current.

Moving the wire along the field lines does not cut them, so no pd is induced. Only movement that cuts across the lines counts.

## A magnet and a coil (3.6.1 b)

A coil gives a bigger effect than a single wire because every turn adds to the pd. The set-up is a coil joined to a sensitive centre-zero meter.

| Action | Meter reading |
|---|---|
| Magnet pushed into the coil | Deflects one way |
| Magnet held still, inside or outside | Zero |
| Magnet pulled out | Deflects the other way |
| Coil moved towards a fixed magnet | Deflects, as if the magnet had moved towards the coil |

Only relative movement matters, so it makes no difference whether you move the magnet or the coil.

## The opposing field (3.6.1 c)

When the induced current flows, it produces a magnetic field of its own. This field opposes the field of the permanent magnet, so the coil resists the movement that created the current.

Push the north pole of a magnet towards a closed coil. The end of the coil facing the magnet becomes a north pole, and the two like poles repel. You feel a push back. Pull the magnet away and the induced current reverses. The nearer end of the coil becomes a south pole, which pulls on the departing north pole and resists you again.

This is also an energy argument. The work you do against the repulsion is the energy that appears in the circuit. If the induced field helped the movement, energy would be created from nothing.

### Worked example 1: induced polarity

The north pole of a bar magnet is lowered towards the top of a vertical closed coil, then lifted away again. State the pole formed at the top of the coil in each movement.

1. Lowering: the induced field opposes the magnet, so the top becomes a north pole and pushes back on the incoming north pole.
2. Lifting: the current reverses, the top becomes a south pole, and it pulls on the north pole as it leaves.

**North while lowering, south while lifting.**

## Reversing the pd and current (3.6.1 d)

Reverse the direction of motion and the polarity of the induced pd reverses, so any current flows the opposite way. Reverse the magnet instead, so the other pole leads, and the same thing happens. If you reverse both, the pd goes back to its original direction.

## Factors that increase the induced pd (3.6.1 e)

| Factor | Why it increases the pd |
|---|---|
| Faster movement | The wire cuts field lines at a greater rate |
| Stronger magnetic field | There are more field lines to cut |
| More turns on the coil | Each turn contributes, so the contributions add |
| Larger area of coil | The coil cuts through more of the field |

### Worked example 2: reading experimental data

A magnet is dropped through coils of different lengths of the same wire, all wound with the same area. A data logger records the peak pd each time.

| Number of turns | Peak pd (mV) |
|---|---|
| 100 | 0.9 |
| 200 | 1.8 |
| 300 | 2.7 |
| 400 | 3.6 |

1. Peak pd rises as the number of turns rises.
2. Each 100 turns adds 0.9 mV, so doubling the turns doubles the pd. The two quantities are in direct proportion.
3. Prediction for 500 turns: 5 × 0.9 = **4.5 mV**.
4. Variables to control: the same magnet, the same release height (so the same speed through the coil), and the same coil area.

## Alternators and dynamos (3.6.1 e)

Both machines spin a coil between the poles of a magnet. As the coil turns, its sides cut field lines and a pd is induced.

### Alternator (produces ac)

The coil's connection to the outside circuit does not swap over. In one half of each turn a given side of the coil travels upwards and in the other half it travels downwards. The induced pd therefore changes polarity every half turn. That is alternating current.

The pd also changes in size.

| Coil position | Sides of the coil | Induced pd |
|---|---|---|
| Plane of coil parallel to the field lines | Cutting across the lines at the fastest rate | Maximum |
| Plane of coil at right angles to the field lines | Moving along the lines, not cutting them | Zero |
| Past the zero position | Cutting the lines the other way | Maximum, reversed |

The graph of pd against time is a smooth wave. It rises from zero to a positive peak, falls through zero to a negative peak of the same size, and returns to zero. One full wave is one rotation.

### Dynamo (produces dc)

A dynamo has the same coil and field, but the connections to the outside circuit swap every half turn (the job of a split ring, which you do not need to describe in detail). Each time the induced pd would reverse, the connections reverse too, so the output keeps the same direction. The pd-time graph is a row of humps that never cross the axis, dropping to zero between them. Its size still varies, but the direction does not change.

### Worked example 3: reading an alternator graph

The trace from an alternator shows a peak pd of 8.0 V. One complete cycle takes 0.050 s. The coil makes one rotation per cycle.

1. Frequency = 1 ÷ period = 1 ÷ 0.050 = **20 Hz**.
2. Rotations per second = 20, so per minute: 20 × 60 = **1200 rotations per minute**.
3. The coil is now turned twice as fast. The period halves to 0.025 s and the frequency doubles to 40 Hz. The sides cut the lines faster, so the peak pd increases above 8.0 V.

## Power station generators (3.6.1 f)

A power station turns a wire coil between magnets using a **turbine**. A fuel, or a flow of water or wind, supplies the energy that spins the turbine. In a fuel-burning station, the fuel heats water to make steam, and the steam pushes the turbine blades. The turbine is on the same shaft as the generator, so the coil turns, its sides cut the magnetic field lines, and a pd is induced.

## The motor effect (3.6.4 a)

A wire carrying a current has a magnetic field around it. Put it in the field of a magnet so that it cuts across the field lines, and the two fields interact. Each of the magnet and the wire pushes on the other with a force. This is the **motor effect**.

If the wire is parallel to the magnetic field, it does not cut the lines and there is no force. The force is largest when the wire lies across the field at 90 degrees.

## Increasing the force (3.6.4 b)

- Increase the strength of the magnetic field.
- Increase the size of the current.
- Increase the length of the conductor that lies in the field.

## Direction and Fleming's left-hand rule (3.6.4 c)

The force reverses if the current reverses, and it reverses if the magnetic field reverses. Reverse both and the force is back where it started.

**Fleming's left-hand rule:** with your left hand, spread the thumb, first finger and second finger so that each is at 90 degrees to the other two.

| Digit | Represents |
|---|---|
| First finger | Direction of the magnetic **F**ield, north to south |
| Second finger | Direction of the **C**urrent, positive to negative |
| Thumb | Direction of the force, or **M**otion |

### Worked example 4: direction of the force

A straight wire carries current into the page. It lies in a magnetic field directed towards the right-hand edge of the page. Find the direction of the force.

1. First finger points right (field).
2. Second finger points into the page (current).
3. The thumb points **down the page**, which is the direction of the force.

Reverse the current, or the field, and the force points up the page.

## The electric motor (3.6.4 d)

Take a rectangular coil in a field pointing from left to right, seen end-on. The left side carries current into the page and the right side carries it out of the page, because the current goes round the loop.

- The left side has current into the page, so the force is down (worked example 4).
- The right side has current out of the page, so the force is up.

The two forces are opposite, so they turn the coil. When the coil lies square-on to the field, the two forces act along the coil's own plane and give no turning effect. In a practical motor, the connections to the coil swap at this point (a split ring does this), so the forces keep turning it the same way. As with the generator, more current, a stronger field or more turns increase the turning force.

## Common errors

- Writing that a stationary magnet inside a coil induces a pd. It does not, because nothing is changing.
- Saying the induced field "stops" the motion. It opposes it, so the movement continues but is harder.
- Mixing up the two fingers in Fleming's rule. Second finger is current, first finger is field.
- Treating a dynamo as a generator that produces a steady pd. The output is dc but still varies in size.

## Frequently asked questions

**How can you increase the induced pd in a coil?**
Move the magnet or coil faster, use a stronger magnetic field, add more turns to the coil or use a coil of larger area. Each change makes the wire cut field lines at a greater rate.

**When is the induced pd in an alternator at its maximum?**
The pd is at its maximum when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field lines, because the sides are cutting the lines at the fastest rate. It is zero when the plane of the coil is at right angles to the field lines.

**What happens when a magnet is held still inside a coil?**
No pd is induced, because the field through the coil is not changing. A meter only deflects while the magnet or coil is moving, and the deflection reverses when the motion reverses.
