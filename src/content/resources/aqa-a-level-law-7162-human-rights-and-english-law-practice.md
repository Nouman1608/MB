## Section A

**1.** Which section of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 governs the use of reasonable force in self-defence? A: section 3. B: section 24. C: section 76. D: section 127. **[1]**

**2.** Which body investigates deaths following contact with the police in England and Wales? A: the Crown Prosecution Service. B: the Independent Office for Police Conduct. C: the Committee of Ministers. D: the Judicial Appointments Commission. **[1]**

**3.** State the rule for householder cases under section 76 of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** features of an effective investigation into a death under Article 2, as set out in Jordan v United Kingdom (2001). **[2]**

**5.** State why the tort of false imprisonment is described as "strict", referring to one case. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the Osman test for the operational duty to protect life under Article 2. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why a claim in negligence against the police may fail where a claim under Article 2 succeeds, referring to two cases. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how the inquest system satisfies the investigative duty in Article 2, referring to R (Middleton) v West Somerset Coroner (2004). **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** criminal law provisions that protect privacy and **two** legal provisions that permit the state to restrict it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain the tension between the test for self-defence in English law and the wording of Article 2.2, referring to Da Silva v United Kingdom (2016). **[4]**

**11.** *Officers carrying out a planned armed operation shoot dead Ade, whom they wrongly believe to be about to detonate a bomb. The briefing given to the officers contained inaccurate information about his identity.* Advise Ade's family on the criminal, civil and investigatory routes available under English law. **[7]**

**12.** *A climate group plans a slow march along a main road and some members intend to sit in the road for an hour. A newspaper prints a columnist's article saying the group's leader "should be locked up", and the leader, Femi, wants to stop further articles.* Consider the English law provisions that protect or restrict the group's rights of assembly and Femi's position on expression. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: section 76. **[1]**

**2.** B: the Independent Office for Police Conduct. **[1]**

**3.** In a householder case, force is not reasonable if it was **grossly disproportionate** in the circumstances as the defendant believed them to be [1]; merely disproportionate force may still be reasonable, though it must be reasonable in all the circumstances (Collins, 2016) [1].

**4.** Any two: **independence** from those implicated; **effectiveness**, capable of identifying and punishing those responsible; **promptness**; **public scrutiny**; **involvement of the family** [1] [1].

**5.** Liability does not depend on **fault**: unlawful detention is enough [1]. In **R v Governor of Brockhill Prison, ex parte Evans (No 2) (2001)** a prisoner held beyond her correct release date recovered damages although the governor had followed the law as then understood [1].

**6.** The authorities must have **known or ought to have known** at the time [1] of a **real and immediate risk** [1] to the **life of an identified individual** from the criminal acts of a third party [1], and failed to take **measures within their powers** which, judged reasonably, might have been expected to avoid the risk [1].

**7.** In negligence, **Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire (1989)** established that the police owe **no general duty of care** to protect the public from criminals [1]. **Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales (2015)** confirmed there was no duty for a **delayed response to a 999 call** [1], but allowed the **Article 2 claim** to proceed to trial [1]. Article 2 imposes a **free-standing operational duty** (Osman) [1] which does not depend on a duty of care in tort, so a human rights claim under **section 7 HRA** can succeed where negligence fails [1].

**8.** An inquest is an **independent judicial inquiry** led by a coroner [1], with a **jury** where a death in state detention was violent, unnatural or of unknown cause [1]. **Middleton** held that, to satisfy Article 2, the inquest must examine not only by what means but **in what circumstances** the person died [1], now reflected in the **Coroners and Justice Act 2009**, allowing findings on systemic failures [1].

**9.** Protecting: any two of the **unlawful interception offence under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016**; **Computer Misuse Act 1990 s1**; **harassment and stalking offences under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997**; **unlawfully obtaining personal data under the Data Protection Act 2018** [1] [1]. Restricting: any two of **PACE search warrants and powers of entry (s8, s17, s18)**; **stop and search (PACE s1)**; **interception warrants under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016**; **surveillance under RIPA 2000** [1] [1].

**10.** Article 2.2 permits lethal force only where **absolutely necessary** [1], a strict, partly objective standard. English law judges the defendant on the facts **as genuinely believed**, even if the belief was unreasonable (s76 CJIA 2008) [1]. In **Da Silva v UK (2016)**, after the shooting of Jean Charles de Menezes [1], the Grand Chamber held the English test was **not significantly different** from the Convention standard and the decision not to prosecute individual officers did not breach Article 2 [1].

**11.** **Criminal**: officers may claim self-defence or prevention of crime; under **s76 CJIA 2008** they are judged on the facts as genuinely believed [1]. **Planning**: under **McCann v UK (1995)** the state is responsible for planning and control; an inaccurate briefing points to a failure in planning [1], and the organisation could face prosecution, as the Metropolitan Police did under **health and safety law** after the de Menezes shooting [1]. **Civil**: a claim under **section 7 HRA** for breach of Article 2 [1], and claims for dependants under the **Fatal Accidents Act 1976** [1]. **Investigation**: an **IOPC investigation** of the shooting [1] and an **Article 2 inquest** with a jury examining in what circumstances Ade died (Middleton) [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- A **slow march** is a public procession: **notice** under Public Order Act 1986 s11, and the police may impose **conditions** under s12 [1].
- Sitting in the road may be **obstruction of the highway** under Highways Act 1980 s137 [1].
- Under **DPP v Ziegler (2021)**, whether there is a **lawful excuse** requires a proportionality assessment; an hour's targeted sit-down may be protected [1].
- Wider powers under the **Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022** and **Public Order Act 2023** may also apply to serious disruption [1].
- For Femi, an injunction against further articles engages **HRA s12**: the court must have particular regard to freedom of expression [1].
- Under **s12(3)** and **Cream Holdings**, Femi must show he is more likely than not to succeed at trial [1].
- In **defamation**, he must show **serious harm** (Defamation Act 2013 s1), and the paper may rely on **honest opinion** (s3) for a comment piece [1].
- **Conclusion**: the group's assembly is protected subject to proportionate conditions, and Femi is unlikely to obtain an injunction against an opinion column [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a statute without saying whether it protects or restricts the right.
- Assuming a death proves a breach of the Osman duty.
- Ignoring the Article 2 claim where negligence fails.
- Forgetting the investigatory route (inquest, IOPC) in right-to-life scenarios.
- Omitting section 12 HRA 1998 in a question about stopping publication.
