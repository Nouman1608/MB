This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.2 Understanding different business forms** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **reasons for choosing different forms of business and for changing business form**, covering sole traders, private limited companies and public limited companies, private sector and public sector organisations, non-profit organisations and social enterprises, and the issues of unlimited and limited liability, ordinary share capital, market capitalisation and dividends
- **the role of shareholders and why they invest**
- **influences on share price and the significance of share price changes**
- **the effects of ownership on mission and objectives**.

The combined [nature, purpose and business forms guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-understanding-the-nature-and-purpose-of-business-and-understanding/) describes each form. This page concentrates on the decisions: why a business chooses or changes its form, what a share price movement really means for a company, and how the owners shape what a business tries to do. Companion pages: [business forms revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-different-business-forms-revision-notes/) and [business forms practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-different-business-forms-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## The forms side by side

| Form | Owned by | Liability | Main sources of capital | Who receives any surplus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited | Owner's savings, loans, retained profit | The owner |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | Shareholders, often family or founders; shares not sold to the general public | Limited | Share capital from chosen investors, loans, retained profit | Shareholders, as dividends |
| Public limited company (plc) | Shareholders; shares can be offered to the public and traded | Limited | Share issues to the public, loans, retained profit | Shareholders, as dividends |
| Public sector organisation | The state | Not owned by private individuals | Government funding, charges for services | Used for public services |
| Non-profit organisation (for example a charity) | Run for a purpose, not for owners | Depends on its legal form | Donations, grants, trading income | Reinvested in its purpose |
| Social enterprise | Varies; can be a company | Usually limited if incorporated | Trading income, social investors, grants | Mainly reinvested in its social aim |

**Limited liability** means shareholders can lose only the money they have invested if the company fails. **Unlimited liability** means a sole trader's personal possessions can be taken to pay business debts. The difference exists because a company is a separate legal entity from its owners, while a sole trader is not.

## Reasons for choosing a form

The choice is a trade-off between five issues:

1. **Risk.** If the business may run up large debts, limited liability protects the owners' personal wealth.
2. **Finance needed.** Selling shares raises more capital than one person's savings; a plc can raise the most because it can offer shares to the public.
3. **Control.** A sole trader decides alone. Every share sold gives away part of the ownership and, with ordinary shares, votes.
4. **Privacy and administration.** Companies must register and publish accounts, and a plc faces far more scrutiny. A sole trader's affairs stay private and setting up is simple.
5. **Purpose.** If the founders' main aim is social, a social enterprise or non-profit form signals that surplus will be reinvested, which can attract grants and customers who share the aim.

## Reasons for changing form

| Change | Usual reasons | Usual costs |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader → Ltd | Growing debts make unlimited liability risky; new investors want shares | Loss of some control; registration and published accounts |
| Ltd → plc | Large capital needed for expansion; shareholders want to be able to sell their shares easily | High cost of flotation; risk of takeover; pressure for short-term results |
| plc → Ltd (going private) | Escape short-term pressure from the stock market; regain control | Must buy out existing shareholders, often with borrowed money |
| Public sector → private sector | Government wants private finance or greater efficiency | Profit motive may conflict with universal service |

A business does not need to change form simply because it grows. Many large firms stay private because the owners value control more than access to public share capital.

## Ordinary share capital and dividends

**Ordinary share capital** is the money raised by selling ordinary shares. Ordinary shareholders usually have a vote at the annual general meeting, a right to any dividends the directors declare, and a claim on what is left if the company is wound up after debts are paid. The company does not have to repay share capital or pay a fixed return, which makes it lower risk for the company than a loan.

**Dividends** are payments of profit to shareholders, usually expressed per share. They are decided by the directors and can be cut or skipped when profit falls or the company wants to reinvest.

## The role of shareholders and why they invest

**Role:** shareholders provide long-term capital, bear the risk of loss, and hold directors to account by voting on their appointment and on major decisions.

**Why they invest:**

- **Dividend income.**
- **Capital growth**, if the share price rises.
- **Influence**, for large shareholders who can shape strategy.
- **Personal or social reasons**, such as supporting a family business or a social enterprise whose aims they share.

## Market capitalisation and share prices

**Market capitalisation** = number of shares issued × current share price. It is the stock market's valuation of the whole company.

### Influences on share price

A share price rises when more people want to buy than to sell. Influences include:

- the company's **profits and forecasts**, including announcements that results will be better or worse than expected
- **dividend** announcements
- **the economy**, including interest rates (higher rates make saving more attractive than shares)
- **news about the industry**, such as new regulation or a competitor's success
- **takeover rumours**
- general **market confidence**.

### Why changes matter

A change in share price does **not** directly change the company's cash, because shares traded on the stock market pass between investors. It matters because:

- shareholders' wealth rises or falls, which affects confidence in the directors
- a falling price makes the company **cheaper to take over**
- a higher price makes it easier to **raise new capital** by issuing shares
- directors' bonuses are often linked to the share price
- a sharp fall can damage the company's **reputation** with suppliers and lenders.

## Worked example: a profit warning

Brackwater Foods plc has 40 million shares in issue at £2.50. It pays a total dividend of £6 million.

- Market capitalisation = 40m × £2.50 = **£100m**.
- Dividend per share = £6m ÷ 40m = **15p**.

Brackwater warns that profits will be lower than expected. The share price falls 18% to £2.05.

- New market capitalisation = 40m × £2.05 = **£82m**, a fall of £18m.
- An investor with 10,000 shares sees their holding fall from £25,000 to £20,500.
- Brackwater's bank balance is unchanged by the fall.

**Analysis.** A rival could now offer £2.60 a share, a premium of about 27% on the new price, valuing Brackwater at £104m, only 4% above its value before the warning. The fall has made a takeover much more tempting. Directors are likely to respond by explaining their plans to investors, and may avoid cutting the dividend to keep shareholders' support.

## The effects of ownership on mission and objectives

- **Sole trader**: the owner's personal values and lifestyle shape the mission; objectives may be satisfactory profit and independence.
- **Family-owned Ltd**: long-term survival and keeping control in the family often matter more than fast growth.
- **plc**: investors expect returns, so objectives often focus on profit, dividends and share price. Because shareholders own the company but directors run it, directors may pursue growth or their own rewards unless pay is linked to shareholder returns.
- **Public sector**: the mission is a public service; objectives focus on access, quality and value for money rather than profit.
- **Social enterprise and non-profit**: the social aim leads, and profit is a means of achieving it.

When ownership changes, objectives often change with it. A family Ltd that floats as a plc may find new investors pressing for faster growth and higher dividends than the family wanted.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: for example, market capitalisation is the number of shares issued multiplied by the current share price.
- **Calculate**: show the formula, and say whether you have given a total or a per-share figure.
- **Analyse**: trace the consequence of a form or a share price change for this business.
- **Assess / Evaluate**: weigh benefits against costs for the owners in the case, and say what the decision depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a plc is in the public sector.
- Writing that a falling share price takes cash from the company.
- Treating limited liability as protection for the business rather than for its owners.
- Recommending a plc for every growing business, ignoring control and cost.
- Describing ownership forms without linking them to objectives.
