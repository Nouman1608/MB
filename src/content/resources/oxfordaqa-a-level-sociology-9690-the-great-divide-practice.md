## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by **imperialism**. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what Polanyi meant by **fictitious commodities**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **two** differences between colonialism and neocolonialism. **[4]**

**4.** Outline **two** ways in which colonial economies were organised to benefit European powers.
**[4]**

**5.** Outline **two** criticisms of Weber's Protestant work ethic as an explanation of why Europe
industrialised. **[4]**

**6.** Outline **two** development factors, other than income, named in the specification, and
explain why each matters. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Study Item A.

> **Item A (fictional data).** Share of the fictional country of Ostrel's export earnings by type of
> product.
>
> | Product type | 1950 (%) | 2020 (%) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Primary commodities (crops, minerals) | 85 | 62 |
> | Manufactured goods | 15 | 38 |

**(a)** Calculate the change, in percentage points, in the share of manufactured goods between 1950
and 2020. **[2]**
**(b)** Using Item A, explain how a dependency theorist might interpret Ostrel's export structure.
**[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which Rodney argued Europe underdeveloped Africa. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how Braudel's distinction between the market economy and capitalism helps explain
the Great Divergence. **[6]**

**10.** Explain **two** legacies of colonialism or enslavement that may still affect development
today. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate modernisation theory as an explanation of why Europe industrialised before other
regions. **[12]**

**12.** Using examples from your own society or a society you know about, evaluate the view that
the gap between rich and poor countries is mainly a legacy of colonialism. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The extension of a state's power and control over other territories and peoples [1], through
conquest, colonial rule or control of trade, as with the European empires [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Land, labour and money [1], which Polanyi argued are treated as commodities in a market
society even though they are not produced for sale, causing social dislocation [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Colonialism is direct political rule by a foreign power [1], with colonial officials governing
the territory [1]. Neocolonialism is indirect control after formal independence [1], exercised through
debt, trade terms, aid conditions or foreign ownership [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Export of raw materials [1]: colonies supplied crops and minerals to European industry, while
importing European manufactured goods [1]. Extractive infrastructure [1]: railways and ports linked
mines and plantations to the coast rather than connecting local markets [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Capitalism elsewhere [1]: commercial capitalism developed in Catholic regions such as northern
Italy, and later in East Asia, so Protestantism was not necessary [1]. Missing exploitation [1]: the
explanation ignores the wealth Europe gained from colonies and enslavement, stressed by dependency
theorists such as Frank and Rodney [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Any two, 2 marks each (factor [1], why it matters [1]): life expectancy [1], because it reflects
health care, nutrition and living conditions across the whole population [1]; education [1], because
literacy and skills expand people's choices and productivity [1]; rights of women and other groups
[1], because development that excludes half the population is incomplete (Sen's capabilities) [1];
democracy and human rights [1], because people need political freedom to shape their society [1].
**[4]**

**7. (a)** 38 − 15 = 23 [1]; an increase of 23 percentage points [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Ostrel still earns most of its export income (62%) from primary commodities [1], a pattern
dependency theorists trace to colonial economies built to supply raw materials [1]. Prices of
primary goods are often volatile and low relative to manufactured imports, so surplus flows to
core economies [1]. The growth of manufacturing to 38% might be read as limited or "dependent"
development, possibly controlled by foreign firms, rather than an escape from dependency [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Two ways, 3 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1]):
- **The slave trade** [1]: removed millions of young, productive people from Africa [1], weakening
  agriculture and encouraging warfare and raiding [1].
- **Colonial economic structure** [1]: colonies were made to produce cash crops and minerals for
  Europe [1], blocking local industry and leaving economies dependent on exports [1].
- Credit: colonial education designed to produce clerks rather than broad skills; profits sent to
  Europe.
**[6]**

**9.** 6 marks, point-marked:
- Braudel separated material life, the market economy and capitalism [1].
- The market economy is ordinary, transparent local exchange [1].
- Capitalism is the top layer of large merchants and financiers who dominate long-distance trade [1].
- European capitalism grew over centuries through control of trade networks centred on dominant
  cities [1].
- This suggests Europe's lead had deep roots in commercial power before factories appeared [1].
- It also links the divergence to control over world trade rather than to values alone, which
  connects with dependency and world-systems approaches [1].
**[6]**

**10.** Two legacies, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example [1]):
- **Primary-export dependence** [1]: colonial economies were built around exporting crops and
  minerals [1], so many countries still depend on a few commodities with volatile prices [1]; for
  example, an economy relying on one crop suffers when world prices fall [1].
- **Colonial borders and state structures** [1]: borders drawn by colonial powers cut across ethnic
  and linguistic groups [1], contributing to conflict that damages development [1]; credit
  examples from the partition of Africa after the Berlin Conference [1].
- Credit: racism and colonial languages in education; debt, as with Haiti's indemnity of 1825.
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content, 1 mark per relevant, developed point up to 12:
- Modernisation theory locates causes of development inside societies: values and institutions [1].
- Values of achievement, science and enterprise are said to have spread first in Europe [1].
- McClelland linked growth to a cultural "need for achievement" [1].
- Weber's Protestant ethic supports a value-based account [1].
- Secure property rights and scientific institutions encouraged investment and invention [1].
- Application: Britain's early industrialisation with scientific societies and patents [1].
- Criticism: ethnocentric, treating the Western path as the model for all [1].
- Criticism: ignores enslavement and colonial exploitation (Rodney, Frank, Williams) [1].
- Criticism: Polanyi shows market society was created through enclosure and state action, not
  values alone [1].
- Criticism: material factors such as coal and colonial land (Pomeranz) or geography (Sachs) [1].
- Strength: explains why institutions matter, supported by later institutional research [1].
- Judgement: useful for internal factors but incomplete without external and material ones [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content, 1 mark per relevant, developed point up to 12:
- Define colonialism and the gap between rich and poor countries [1].
- Rodney: enslavement and colonialism drained Africa [1].
- Frank: underdevelopment created by the same process as development [1].
- Legacy of primary-export dependence and extractive infrastructure [1].
- Neocolonialism: debt and structural adjustment, criticised by Bello [1].
- Application to a named society's colonial history [1].
- Against: some former colonies, such as South Korea and Singapore, grew rapidly [1].
- Against: some countries that were never fully colonised remain poor, so other factors matter [1].
- Sachs: geography, disease burden and poverty traps [1].
- Modernisation theorists: internal values, governance and corruption [1].
- Wars and conflicts, and global trade rules today, also shape the gap [1].
- Judgement: colonialism is a major historical cause, but present-day factors keep the gap open [1].
**[12]**
