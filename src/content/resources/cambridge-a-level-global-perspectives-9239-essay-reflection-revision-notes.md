These notes cover the two AO2 assessment points in Component 2, the Essay, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028: "reflect on the impact of alternative perspectives on their viewpoint" and "suggest further relevant research". For the essay as a whole, see the [Component 2 Essay study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-essay/); for full explanations and a worked example, read the [essay reflection study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-reflection/).

## What the syllabus says

- AO2 points in the essay: "reflect on the impact of alternative perspectives on their viewpoint" and "suggest further relevant research".
- Candidates "should reach a personal, supported view on the question".
- AO2 objectives: "acknowledge different perspectives and evaluate their impact on the learner's own standpoint"; "consider the extent and limits of the learning and research that has been undertaken".
- AO2 weighting: 22% of Component 2 (0% of Component 1).
- Critical Path stage: **Reflection**, "explore the impact of research on personal perspectives".

## The five-step reflection sequence

1. **Starting point**: your view before research.
2. **Encounter**: the perspective that challenged or confirmed it.
3. **Effect**: strengthened, qualified or reversed.
4. **Reason**: the specific argument or evidence that caused the effect (the step that earns most).
5. **Next step**: research that would test your new view.

Each step answers one of the Critical Path's reflection questions, from "What was your personal viewpoint before carrying out research?" to "What additional research might be useful?"

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Standpoint | Your own position on the essay question |
| Alternative perspective | A coherent view on the issue different from yours |
| Impact | The effect a perspective has on your standpoint |
| Strengthened view | Survived the strongest opposing case, with reasons |
| Qualified view | Still held, but only under stated conditions |
| Reversed view | Changed by the research, with the cause named |
| Limits of research | Gaps in coverage or evidence that reduce confidence in your conclusion |
| Relevant further research | Research that would help answer your question more securely |

## Where reflection goes

- **Introduction**: a sentence stating your starting view.
- **Evaluation paragraphs**: brief reflective sentences where a perspective changes your thinking.
- **Conclusion**: personal, supported view; why it moved or held; limits; further research.

## Useful limits to reflect on

| Limit | Example consequence |
|---|---|
| Coverage | "... so my conclusion applies more confidently to cities than to rural areas." |
| Type of evidence | "... so the scale of the effect is uncertain." |
| Own standpoint | "... I may have found the economic sources more persuasive because I started there." |
| Unresolved disagreement | "... so the judgement depends on which of two credible studies is right." |

## The what-why-change test for further research

- **What**: a specific kind of evidence or perspective.
- **Why**: the limit it would fill.
- **Change**: how a result could alter your judgement.

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-reflection--06-further-research.webp" width="900" height="584" alt="A strong suggestion has three parts, each with an example: what (household surveys from rural areas of countries that have introduced the policy), why (my sources are mostly from cities), and how it could change the judgement (the conclusion might need to be limited to cities). “More research is needed” fails all three." loading="lazy">

</figure>

## Model sentence frames

- "I began this essay believing ... because ..."
- "The perspective that most challenged this was ..., which showed me that ..."
- "My view has therefore been qualified: I still think ..., but only where ..."
- "Because my sources ..., I cannot be confident that ..."
- "Research into ... would test whether ..., and if it showed ..., my conclusion would need to ..."

## Condensed example: weak to strong

Question: "Should rich countries fund adaptation to rising sea levels in small island states?"

- **Weak:** "My research showed me many different views and I now understand the issue better."
- **Strong:** "I began believing funding was a matter of charity. The perspective based on historical emissions persuaded me it is a matter of responsibility, because it showed that the states most at risk have contributed least to the cause. My view moved from voluntary to obligatory funding. My sources were mainly from donor governments, so reports from island governments on how adaptation money has been spent would test whether funding reaches the communities at risk."

The strong version names the starting view, the perspective, the reason, the effect, a limit and a specific next step.

## Exam traps

- Reflection confined to the final sentence.
- Feelings ("I found it interesting") instead of reasoning.
- No starting view, so no visible change.
- "My view changed" with no cause named.
- Limits about time or effort rather than about evidence.
- Further research unrelated to the question.
- A summary of both sides presented as a personal view.
- An invented change of mind with no explanation.

## Self-test

1. Quote the two AO2 assessment points in the essay.
2. What percentage of Component 2 is AO2?
3. Which step of the reflection sequence earns most credit, and why?
4. Name the three ways a view can move.
5. Is a strengthened view valid reflection? Explain.
6. Why include your starting view in the introduction?
7. Give one example of a limit that affects a conclusion.
8. What three things should a suggestion for further research include?
9. Why is "I did not have enough time" a weak limit?
10. Is it acceptable to use "I" in reflection? Why?
11. Turn this into reflection: "After my research I see both sides."

**Answers**

1. "Reflect on the impact of alternative perspectives on their viewpoint" and "suggest further relevant research".
2. 22%.
3. Step 4, the reason, because it shows the reasoning that moved you rather than just claiming a change.
4. Strengthened, qualified, reversed.
5. Yes, if you explain why the strongest opposing argument did not persuade you.
6. It gives a baseline so the reader can see how your view moved or held.
7. For example: "My sources were mostly from high-income countries, so I cannot be sure the policy would work where public services are weaker."
8. What evidence or perspective, why (the limit it fills), and how a result could change the judgement.
9. It does not affect how confident the reader can be in the conclusion; limits should be about evidence and coverage.
10. Yes. AO2 concerns the impact on "the learner's own standpoint", which is naturally expressed in the first person.
11. For example: "I began by supporting the policy; the evidence on costs to low-income households qualified my view, so I now support it only with targeted exemptions."
