## 4.1 Geometrical terms

### Points, lines and angles

- A **point** marks a position. A **vertex** is a corner where lines or edges meet.
- A **line** is straight. A **plane** is a flat surface (**Extended only**).
- **Parallel** lines never meet. **Perpendicular** lines meet at a **right angle** (90°).
- The **perpendicular bisector** of a segment AB cuts AB in half at right angles (**Extended only**, as a term).

| Angle type | Size |
|---|---|
| Acute | less than 90° |
| Right angle | exactly 90° |
| Obtuse | between 90° and 180° |
| Reflex | between 180° and 360° |

An **interior angle** of a polygon is inside the shape at a vertex. An **exterior angle** is between one side and the next side extended outwards. At each vertex they add up to 180°, because they lie on a straight line. Calculating them is subtopic 4.6, in the [Geometry study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-geometry/).

### Similar, congruent and scale factor

**Congruent** shapes have the same shape and size. **Similar** shapes have the same shape, but one is an enlargement of the other: angles are equal and corresponding lengths are in the same ratio. That ratio is the **scale factor** (new length ÷ original length). A 3 cm by 5 cm rectangle and a 6 cm by 10 cm rectangle are similar with scale factor 2, but not congruent.

### Triangles, quadrilaterals and polygons

| Triangle | Property |
|---|---|
| Equilateral | three equal sides, three 60° angles |
| Isosceles | two equal sides, two equal angles |
| Scalene | no equal sides |
| Right-angled | one 90° angle |

| Quadrilateral | Key properties |
|---|---|
| Square | four equal sides, four right angles |
| Rectangle | opposite sides equal, four right angles |
| Rhombus | four equal sides, opposite sides parallel |
| Parallelogram | opposite sides equal and parallel |
| Trapezium | exactly one pair of parallel sides |
| Kite | two pairs of equal adjacent sides |

A square is also a rectangle and a rhombus, so when asked to name a shape give the most specific name that fits.

A **polygon** is a closed shape with straight sides. It is **regular** if all sides and all angles are equal, otherwise **irregular**. Learn **pentagon** (5 sides), **hexagon** (6), **octagon** (8) and **decagon** (10).

### Solids

A **face** is a flat surface of a solid, an **edge** is where two faces meet, and the **surface** is the whole outside. A **net** is a flat shape that folds up into a solid.

- **Cube**, **cuboid**: six square or rectangular faces.
- **Prism**: the same cross-section all the way through. **Cylinder**: a circular cross-section.
- **Pyramid**: a polygon base with triangular faces meeting at a vertex. **Cone**: a circular base and a curved surface meeting at a vertex.
- **Sphere**: a ball. **Hemisphere** (half a sphere) and **frustum** (a cone or pyramid with the top cut off parallel to the base) are **Extended only**.

**Worked example 1.** Count the faces, edges and vertices of a pentagonal prism and of a square-based pyramid.

```
Pentagonal prism: 2 pentagons + 5 rectangles = 7 faces
                  5 + 5 + 5 joining edges = 15 edges;  10 vertices
Square-based pyramid: 1 square + 4 triangles = 5 faces
                  4 base + 4 sloping = 8 edges;  4 + 1 apex = 5 vertices
```

### Circle vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Centre, radius (plural radii) | the middle point; the distance from it to the circle |
| Diameter | a chord through the centre; twice the radius |
| Circumference | the distance all the way round |
| Semicircle | half a circle, cut off by a diameter |
| Chord | a straight line joining two points on the circle |
| Tangent | a straight line touching the circle at one point |
| Arc | part of the circumference; **minor arc** shorter, **major arc** longer (**Extended only**) |
| Sector | region between two radii and an arc |
| Segment | region between a chord and an arc |

A sector is bounded by two radii, a segment by a chord. They are the pair most often confused.

## 4.2 Geometrical constructions

### Measuring and drawing

Use a ruler for every straight edge and measure to the nearest millimetre. With a protractor, put the centre on the vertex, line up the zero line with one arm, and count from the zero on that arm. If an angle looks acute, your reading must be under 90°.

### Constructing a triangle from three sides

Use only a ruler and compasses, and show the construction arcs.

**Worked example 2.** Construct triangle ABC with AB = 9 cm, AC = 7.5 cm and BC = 6 cm.

```
1. Draw AB = 9 cm (start with the longest side).
2. Compasses at 7.5 cm, point on A: draw an arc above AB.
3. Compasses at 6 cm, point on B: draw an arc crossing the first.
4. The crossing point is C. Join AC and BC with a ruler.
5. Leave both arcs showing.
```

Check: angle BAC should measure about 41°, angle ABC about 56° and angle ACB about 83°. If one side is as long as the other two together, the arcs never cross and no triangle exists.

### Constructing a rhombus from two triangles

The syllabus gives this as an example. A rhombus is two congruent isosceles triangles sharing a diagonal.

**Worked example 3.** Construct rhombus PQRS with sides 5 cm and diagonal PR = 8 cm.

```
1. Draw PR = 8 cm.
2. Compasses at 5 cm: arcs from P and R above PR meet at Q.
3. Same radius: arcs from P and R below PR meet at S.
4. Join PQ, QR, RS and SP. Keep all four arcs.
```

Check: diagonal QS should measure 6 cm, angles P and R about 74°, angles Q and S about 106°.

### Nets: drawing, using and interpreting

Sketch the solid first, then lay out every face so that edges which meet in the solid are equal in the net. Count faces at the end: a cuboid net has six rectangles; a triangular prism net has two triangles and three rectangles.

Surface area is the total area of the net. The volume formulas for a prism (V = Al) and a pyramid (V = (1/3)Ah) are on the formula list printed in every paper.

**Worked example 4.** A net has two right-angled triangles with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm, and three rectangles 10 cm long with widths 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm. Find the surface area and volume of the solid.

```
Solid: triangular prism, length 10 cm
Triangles: 2 x (1/2) x 3 x 4 = 12 cm^2
Rectangles: (3 + 4 + 5) x 10 = 120 cm^2
Surface area = 132 cm^2
Volume = 6 x 10 = 60 cm^3
```

**Worked example 5.** A net is a 6 cm square with an isosceles triangle on each side; each triangle is 5 cm high from the middle of its base. Find the surface area and volume of the pyramid.

```
Surface area = 6 x 6 + 4 x (1/2) x 6 x 5 = 36 + 60 = 96 cm^2
5 cm is the slant height. Vertical height h (Pythagoras,
hypotenuse 5, base 3 = half of 6): h = sqrt(25 - 9) = 4 cm
Volume = (1/3) x 36 x 4 = 48 cm^3
```

More on this in the [Mensuration study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-mensuration/).

## 4.3 Scale drawings

### Using a scale

A scale can be a ratio, such as 1 : 50 000 (both parts in the same unit), or a statement, such as "1 cm represents 2 km". To turn a statement into a ratio, use one unit: 2 km = 200 000 cm, so 1 : 200 000.

**Worked example 6.** A map has scale 1 : 50 000.

```
(a) Two farms are 7.3 cm apart on the map.
    7.3 x 50 000 = 365 000 cm = 3650 m = 3.65 km
(b) A lake is 2.4 km long. Map length?
    2.4 km = 240 000 cm;  240 000 / 50 000 = 4.8 cm
```

Drawing to real: multiply. Real to drawing: divide. On Papers 1 and 2 there is no calculator, so practise multiplying and dividing by powers of 10. A scale drawing of a room works the same way: at 1 : 50, a 4.6 m wall is 460 ÷ 50 = 9.2 cm long.

### Three-figure bearings

A bearing is an angle measured **clockwise from north**, written with three figures from 000° to 360°: 45° is 045°. North is 000°, east 090°, south 180°, west 270°. "D is due east of C" means the bearing of D from C is 090°.

The point after **from** is where you stand. "The bearing of B from A" means: stand at A, face north, turn clockwise until you face B. Draw a north line at A before measuring.

**Reverse bearings.** North lines are parallel, so the two bearings between a pair of points differ by 180°.

```
Less than 180°: add 180°.   180° or more: subtract 180°.
Bearing of Q from P = 063°  ->  P from Q = 243°
Bearing of T from S = 290°  ->  S from T = 110°
```

### Scale drawing with bearings

**Worked example 7.** A boat sails 12 km from harbour H on a bearing of 070° to buoy A, then 8 km on a bearing of 160° to buoy B. Using 1 cm to 2 km, draw the route and find the distance and bearing of B from H.

```
Drawing lengths: 12 / 2 = 6 cm;  8 / 2 = 4 cm
1. Draw a north line at H. Measure 70° clockwise; draw HA = 6 cm.
2. Draw a north line at A. Measure 160° clockwise; draw AB = 4 cm.
3. Measure HB: about 7.2 cm, so about 7.2 x 2 = 14.4 km.
4. Measure clockwise from north at H to HB: about 104°.
```

Checks: the bearing of H from A is 250°, so angle HAB = 250° − 160° = 90°. Pythagoras' theorem and trigonometry give 14.4 km and 103.7°, matching the drawing. The bearing of H from B is about 104° + 180° = 284°. The trigonometry subtopics say knowledge of bearings may be required; see the [Trigonometry study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-trigonometry/).

## Common errors

- Rubbing out construction arcs, or drawing edges freehand.
- Reading the wrong protractor scale, so 70° becomes 110°.
- Measuring a bearing anticlockwise or from the wrong point.
- Writing 70° instead of 070°, or adding 180° to a bearing already over 180°.
- Converting units twice in a scale question.
- Using the slant height as the vertical height in a pyramid.
- Calling a segment a sector, a trapezium a parallelogram, or a 200° angle obtuse.
