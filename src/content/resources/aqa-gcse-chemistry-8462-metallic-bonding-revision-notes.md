These notes condense **4.2.1.5 Metallic bonding** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for
teaching from September 2016, version 1.1). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. The full explanations and worked examples are in the
[metallic bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metallic-bonding/); test yourself with the
[metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metallic-bonding-practice/).

## Core facts

- Metals are **giant structures of atoms** in a **regular pattern**.
- The **outer-shell electrons** are **delocalised**: free to move through the **whole structure**.
- The **sharing of delocalised electrons** gives **strong metallic bonds**.
- The bond is the **electrostatic attraction** between **positive metal ions** and **delocalised electrons**.
- Metallic bonding occurs in **metallic elements and alloys**.

## Electrons and ion charges

| Metal | Outer electrons | Delocalised per atom | Ion in structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li, Na, K | 1 | 1 | 1+ |
| Mg, Ca | 2 | 2 | 2+ |
| Al | 3 | 3 | 3+ |

Overall charge of the structure = **zero**: total electron charge balances total ion charge.
Example: 20 Al³⁺ ions are balanced by 60 delocalised electrons.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metallic bond | Strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Delocalised electron | An outer electron not tied to one atom, free to move through the structure |
| Giant structure | A structure with a vast number of particles in a repeating pattern, no molecules |
| Regular pattern | Particles arranged in ordered rows or layers |
| Positive metal ion | A metal atom that has released its outer electrons into the structure |
| Alloy | A mixture of a metal with other elements; it still has metallic bonding |

## Drawing the model

- Circles of the same size in **regular rows**, close together.
- Each circle labelled with its **positive charge** (+, 2+ or 3+).
- Electrons (e⁻) drawn **between** the ions, not in shells.
- A **key** for "positive metal ion" and "delocalised electron".
- If asked for a neutral diagram, **count** electrons to balance the charges.

## Recognising a metallic diagram

| Feature | Metallic? |
|---|---|
| One kind of positive particle with free electrons | Yes |
| Two sizes of positive particle with free electrons | Yes (an alloy) |
| Positive **and negative** ions alternating | No: ionic |
| Atoms joined by lines in a network | No: giant covalent |
| Separate small groups of atoms | No: small molecules |

## Comparing metallic and ionic bonding

| | Metallic | Ionic |
|---|---|---|
| Positive particles | Metal ions | Metal ions |
| Negative particles | Delocalised electrons | Non-metal ions |
| Electrons | Shared through the whole structure | Transferred from metal to non-metal |
| Occurs in | Metals and alloys | Compounds of metals with non-metals |
| Structure | Giant | Giant |

## Model answers to learn

**Describe the bonding in a metal such as zinc.**
Zinc is a giant structure of atoms in a regular pattern. The outer-shell electrons are delocalised and free
to move through the whole structure, leaving positive zinc ions. Strong electrostatic attraction between the
positive ions and the delocalised electrons holds the structure together.

**Explain why brass (copper and zinc) has metallic bonding, not ionic bonding.**
Brass contains only metals. Ionic bonding needs a metal combined with a non-metal, with electrons
transferred to form negative ions. In brass the outer electrons of both metals are delocalised and shared
through the structure.

**Explain how a diagram shows that a substance is a metal.**
It shows one kind (or, for an alloy, more than one size) of positive particle in a regular arrangement,
with electrons between the particles and no negative ions.

## Exam traps

- **"Sea of ions".** It is a sea of **electrons**; the ions are in fixed positions in the pattern.
- **Negative ions in a metal.** There are none.
- **"Electrons are lost".** They leave their atoms but stay in the structure.
- **Weak bonds.** Free-moving electrons do not make the bond weak; the attraction is strong.
- **Missing charges.** "Attraction between electrons and atoms" is weaker than "between delocalised
  electrons and **positive** ions".
- **Alloys as compounds.** Alloys are mixtures with metallic bonding, not ionic compounds.

## Quick self-test

1. Describe the arrangement of atoms in a metal.
2. Which electrons become delocalised?
3. What does "delocalised" mean?
4. What is the metallic bond an attraction between?
5. How many delocalised electrons does each magnesium atom provide?
6. What is the charge on the ions in solid potassium?
7. A diagram shows 8 Ca²⁺ ions. How many electrons make it neutral?
8. Give one feature of a diagram that tells you it shows a metal, not an ionic compound.
9. What type of bonding is present in an alloy?
10. Give one limitation of a 2D diagram of metallic bonding.
11. Give one similarity between metallic and ionic bonding.
12. Why is it wrong to say metal atoms "lose" their electrons in metallic bonding?

### Answers

1. A giant structure in a regular pattern (layers).
2. The outer-shell electrons.
3. Not held by one atom; free to move through the whole structure.
4. Positive metal ions and delocalised electrons (electrostatic attraction).
5. Two.
6. 1+ (K⁺).
7. 16.
8. It has positive ions with free electrons and no negative ions.
9. Metallic bonding.
10. It shows one layer only (or shows moving electrons in fixed positions, or shows a tiny part of the
    structure).
11. Both are strong electrostatic attractions involving positive metal ions (or both form giant structures).
12. The electrons stay within the metal structure; they are shared, not removed or transferred.
