## Questions

**1.** Deduce the Lewis formula of hypochlorous acid, HOCl, in which O is the central atom. **[2]**

**2.** Boron trifluoride reacts with a fluoride ion to form the tetrafluoroborate ion, BF₄⁻.

**(a)** Deduce the Lewis formula of BF₄⁻. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a coordination bond forms in this reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Predict the shape of BF₄⁻ and its F–B–F bond angle. **[2]**
**(d)** (HL only) The complex ion [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ forms in aqueous solution. State the number of coordination bonds in the ion and which atom donates the electron pair in each. **[2]**

**3.** Hydrazine (N₂H₄), diazene (N₂H₂) and nitrogen (N₂) contain nitrogen–nitrogen single, double and triple bonds respectively. Arrange the three nitrogen–nitrogen bonds in order of increasing bond length and explain the order. **[3]**

**4.** For each species, state the electron domain geometry, the molecular geometry and the predicted bond angle.

**(a)** SiCl₄ **[2]**
**(b)** H₃O⁺ **[2]**
**(c)** SCl₂ **[2]**

**5.** In carbonyl chloride, COCl₂, carbon is the central atom. Explain why the Cl–C–Cl bond angle is less than 120°. **[2]**

**6.** Use these electronegativity values: H 2.2, B 2.0, C 2.6, N 3.0, F 4.0.

**(a)** Show the bond polarity of a C–F bond using partial charges. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce whether each of CF₄, CHF₃, BF₃ and NF₃ has a net dipole moment. Give a reason for each. **[4]**

**7.** This question is about network structures and allotropes.

**(a)** Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why C₆₀ fullerene melts at a much lower temperature than diamond. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the structure of silicon dioxide. **[2]**
**(d)** Use these bond enthalpies: C–C 346 kJ mol⁻¹, Si–Si 226 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain the difference in strength. **[2]**

**8.** State the strongest type of intermolecular force acting between:

**(a)** molecules of fluoromethane, CH₃F **[1]**
**(b)** molecules of methanol, CH₃OH **[1]**
**(c)** a chloromethane molecule and an iodine molecule **[1]**
**(d)** molecules of hexane, C₆H₁₄ **[1]**

**9.** Use these data.

| Substance | Molar mass / g mol⁻¹ | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| ethane, C₂H₆ | 30.08 | −89 |
| methanol, CH₃OH | 32.05 | 65 |
| fluoromethane, CH₃F | 34.04 | −78 |

Explain the order of boiling points in terms of intermolecular forces. **[5]**

**10.** Covalent substances and their physical properties.

**(a)** Explain why methanol is completely miscible with water but hexan-1-ol is only slightly soluble. **[2]**
**(b)** Hydrogen chloride dissolved in water conducts electricity, but hydrogen chloride dissolved in methylbenzene does not. Explain this difference. **[2]**

**11.** A mixture of three dyes, P, Q and R, is separated by thin layer chromatography on a silica plate using a non-polar solvent. The solvent front moves 8.0 cm from the base line. Spot P moves 1.6 cm, Q moves 4.4 cm and R moves 6.8 cm.

**(a)** Calculate the RF value of each dye. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce which dye is the most polar. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** A pure reference dye run on the same plate has RF 0.55. Identify which dye it may be. **[1]**
**(d)** State why the reference must be run on the same plate with the same solvent. **[1]**
**(e)** On a second plate, with a different solvent, dye P has RF 0.36 and the solvent front moves 7.5 cm. Calculate the distance moved by P. **[2]**

**12.** This question is about ethanenitrile, CH₃CN.

**(a)** Deduce its Lewis formula. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the bond angle around each carbon atom. **[2]**
**(c)** Deduce whether the molecule is polar. **[1]**
**(d)** State the strongest intermolecular force between ethanenitrile molecules. **[1]**
**(e)** Suggest why ethanenitrile mixes with water even though it cannot form hydrogen bonds with itself. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** H–O–Cl with single bonds [1]; two lone pairs on O and three lone pairs on Cl (14 valence electrons in total) [1].
*Examiner insight:* a Lewis formula with missing lone pairs on the outer atoms is incomplete; show every valence electron, not just the bonds.

**2. (a)** Four B–F single bonds with three lone pairs on each F (32 electrons) [1]; square brackets with a 1− charge outside [1].
**(b)** The fluoride ion donates a lone pair into the vacant orbital of the electron-deficient boron atom, so both shared electrons come from F⁻ [1].
**(c)** Tetrahedral [1]; 109.5° [1].
**(d)** Six coordination bonds [1]; in each, the O atom of a water molecule donates the lone pair to Fe³⁺ [1].
*Examiner insight:* in part (b), "boron shares electrons with fluorine" is unlikely to earn credit; state that both electrons of the pair come from one atom.

**3.** N≡N < N=N < N–N (shortest to longest) [1]. More shared pairs mean more electron density between the nuclei [1], so the nuclei are attracted more strongly and pulled closer together [1].
*Examiner insight:* an order alone is unlikely to earn more than the first point; link shared pairs to the pull of the nuclei.

**4. (a)** Tetrahedral electron domain geometry and tetrahedral molecular geometry [1]; 109.5° [1].
**(b)** Tetrahedral electron domain geometry; trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry [1]; about 107° [1].
**(c)** Tetrahedral electron domain geometry; bent molecular geometry [1]; about 104.5° [1].
*Examiner insight:* give both geometries when both are asked for; writing only "tetrahedral" for H₃O⁺ does not answer the molecular geometry.

**5.** The C=O double bond holds more electron density than a C–Cl single bond, so it repels the other domains more strongly [1]; this pushes the two C–Cl bonds closer together, so the Cl–C–Cl angle closes below 120° [1].
*Examiner insight:* the first step needs a comparison of repulsion between domains, not just "double bonds are bigger".

**6. (a)** δ+C–Fδ− [1].
**(b)** CF₄: tetrahedral, four identical polar bonds cancel, so no net dipole [1]. CHF₃: the C–H bond (0.4) differs from the C–F bonds (1.4), so the dipoles do not cancel; it is polar [1]. BF₃: trigonal planar, three identical dipoles at 120° cancel; no net dipole [1]. NF₃: trigonal pyramidal with a lone pair on N, so the N–F dipoles cannot cancel; it is polar [1].
*Examiner insight:* each verdict needs its reason (shape or unequal bonds); a bare list of verdicts is unlikely to earn full credit.

**7. (a)** Graphite: each carbon bonds with three electrons; the fourth is delocalised and carries charge within a layer [1]. Diamond: all outer electrons sit in localised bonds [1].
**(b)** C₆₀ consists of separate molecules held together only by London forces [1]; melting diamond requires breaking many strong covalent bonds throughout the network [1].
**(c)** A covalent network in which each Si atom is bonded tetrahedrally to four O atoms [1] and each O atom is bonded to two Si atoms [1].
**(d)** C–C is stronger [1] because carbon atoms are smaller, so the bond is shorter and the shared pair is closer to both nuclei [1].
*Examiner insight:* in part (b), "fullerene has weak bonds" is unlikely to earn credit; name the intermolecular force and contrast it with covalent bonds in diamond.

**8. (a)** Dipole–dipole forces [1].
**(b)** Hydrogen bonding [1].
**(c)** Dipole-induced dipole forces [1].
**(d)** London (dispersion) forces [1].
*Examiner insight:* fluoromethane is a common trap: its H atoms are bonded to C, not F, so it cannot hydrogen bond.

**9.** Molar masses are similar, so London forces are comparable [1]. Ethane is non-polar and has London forces only, so it has the lowest boiling point [1]. Fluoromethane is polar, so it also has dipole–dipole forces and boils higher than ethane [1]. Methanol has an H atom bonded to O, so it forms hydrogen bonds between molecules [1]; these are the strongest forces, so methanol boils highest [1].
*Examiner insight:* begin by noting that the molar masses are similar; without that step, the comparison of force types is not justified.

**10. (a)** Methanol's O–H group forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules [1]; hexan-1-ol's long non-polar chain cannot hydrogen bond with water, so it dissolves only slightly [1].
**(b)** In water, HCl reacts to form ions (H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻), which are mobile charged particles [1]; in methylbenzene it stays as covalent molecules, so there are no mobile ions [1].
*Examiner insight:* conductivity answers must name the mobile charged particles; "it dissolves" is not enough.

**11. (a)** RF = distance moved by dye ÷ distance moved by solvent front [1]; P = 1.6 ÷ 8.0 = **0.20**, Q = 4.4 ÷ 8.0 = **0.55**, R = 6.8 ÷ 8.0 = **0.85** [1].
**(b)** **P** is the most polar [1]: silica is a polar stationary phase, so the most polar dye is attracted most strongly to it and travels least far [1].
**(c)** **Q** (same RF) [1].
**(d)** RF depends on the solvent and stationary phase, so values compare only under identical conditions [1].
**(e)** Distance = RF × solvent front distance [1] = 0.36 × 7.5 = **2.7 cm** [1].
*Examiner insight:* an RF above 1, or one quoted with a unit, signals a wrong method; measure both distances from the base line.

**12. (a)** H₃C–C≡N with three C–H bonds, one C–C bond and one C≡N triple bond [1]; one lone pair on N (16 valence electrons) [1].
**(b)** Around the CH₃ carbon: 109.5° (four domains) [1]; around the nitrile carbon: 180° (two domains, the triple bond counting as one) [1].
**(c)** Polar: the C≡N bond is polar and the linear C–C≡N end has no opposing dipole to cancel it [1].
**(d)** Dipole–dipole forces [1].
**(e)** The lone pair on N can accept a hydrogen bond from an O–H of water [1].
*Examiner insight:* for part (b), counting the triple bond as three domains gives a wrong shape; state the domain count for each carbon separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing Lewis formulas with bonds only, missing the lone pairs on the outer atoms.
- Forgetting the brackets and charge on an ion such as BF₄⁻.
- Describing a coordination bond without saying both electrons come from one atom.
- Treating a multiple bond as more than one electron domain.
- Giving electron domain geometry instead of molecular geometry.
- Stating that a molecule is polar without referring to its shape.
- Claiming hydrogen bonding in CH₃F or CH₃Cl, where H is bonded to carbon.
- Saying "bonds break" when a simple molecular substance boils.
- Missing the step that molar masses are comparable before ranking forces.
- Measuring RF to the end of the plate or paper instead of the solvent front.
