## Nucleotides and nucleic acids

Both DNA and RNA are **polymers of nucleotides**. Each nucleotide has three parts:

- a **pentose** (a five-carbon sugar);
- a **phosphate group**;
- a **nitrogen-containing organic base**.

Nucleotides join by condensation, with the phosphate group of one nucleotide bonding to the pentose of the next. This forms a **sugar-phosphate backbone**, with the bases pointing out from the chain.

### DNA

- Pentose: **deoxyribose**.
- Bases: **adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) and thymine (T)**.
- A molecule of DNA is a **double helix** with two polynucleotide chains held together by **hydrogen bonds** between **complementary base pairs**.
- A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and C pairs with G (three hydrogen bonds). The two chains run in opposite directions (they are antiparallel).

Because the pairing is strict, the amount of A equals the amount of T and the amount of C equals the amount of G in any double-stranded DNA.

**Worked example 1.** A sample of DNA is 28 % adenine. Calculate the percentage of guanine.

1. Thymine = adenine = 28 %.
2. A + T = 56 %, so C + G = 100 - 56 = 44 %.
3. Cytosine = guanine, so guanine = 44 / 2 = **22 %**.

The structure suits the functions of DNA. The double helix is stable because of the many hydrogen bonds, and the sugar-phosphate backbone protects the bases. The base sequence carries the information. The weak individual hydrogen bonds allow the strands to separate for replication and for protein synthesis. Complementary pairing allows accurate copying.

### RNA

- Pentose: **ribose**.
- Bases: **adenine, cytosine, guanine and uracil (U)**. Uracil replaces thymine.
- A molecule of RNA is a relatively **short polynucleotide chain**, usually single-stranded.

### The structure of mRNA and tRNA

**Messenger RNA (mRNA)** is a single, linear strand copied from a gene in the nucleus. It carries the code for a polypeptide to a ribosome. Groups of three bases on mRNA are **codons**. It is not folded into a fixed shape and is relatively short-lived.

**Transfer RNA (tRNA)** is a single strand folded into a clover-leaf shape, held in shape by hydrogen bonds between some of its base pairs. One loop carries a triplet of bases called the **anticodon**, which is complementary to a codon on mRNA. At the other end is a site that attaches one specific amino acid. There are different tRNA molecules for different amino acids.

| Feature | DNA | mRNA | tRNA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strands | Two, in a double helix | One, linear | One, folded |
| Pentose | Deoxyribose | Ribose | Ribose |
| Bases | A, C, G, T | A, C, G, U | A, C, G, U |
| Hydrogen bonds | Between the two chains | None between strands | Within the folded strand |
| Length | Very long | Medium | Short |

## DNA, genes and chromosomes

### Eukaryotic DNA

In the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell, DNA is in the form of **linear molecules** associated with proteins. Some of these proteins are **histones**. The DNA is wound around the histones, which package and order it so that a very long molecule fits inside the nucleus and can be organised into a **chromosome**.

### Other DNA

DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts, and in prokaryotic cells, is **shorter, circular and not associated with proteins**. This is one of the pieces of evidence that mitochondria and chloroplasts have features in common with prokaryotes.

### Genes and loci

A **gene** is a section of DNA that codes for a polypeptide (or functional RNA) that determines the nature and development of an organism. Most genes occupy fixed positions, called **loci** (singular: locus), on particular DNA molecules. Alleles of the same gene occupy the same locus on homologous chromosomes.

### Non-coding DNA, exons and introns

In eukaryotes, much of the nuclear DNA does not code for polypeptides.

- There are **non-coding multiple repeats** of base sequences between genes.
- Within a gene, only some sequences, called **exons**, code for amino acid sequences.
- Exons are separated by non-coding sequences called **introns**.

Introns are removed from the pre-mRNA before translation, as the next section of the specification explains (3.1.8). Prokaryotic genes generally do not contain introns.

The **genome** is the complete set of genes in a cell.

**Worked example 2.** A eukaryotic gene has 4,200 base pairs. Its introns total 1,800 base pairs. Calculate how many base pairs are in the exons.

1. Exons = total - introns = 4,200 - 1,800 = **2,400 base pairs**.
2. Three bases code for one amino acid, so up to 2,400 / 3 = 800 codons can be present. This includes the stop codon, so the polypeptide has up to 799 amino acids.

## DNA replication

DNA is copied before cell division so that each daughter cell receives the same genetic information. The mechanism is **semi-conservative**, which means that each new DNA molecule contains one original (parent) strand and one newly made strand.

The steps, in the order the specification gives them:

1. **Unwinding.** The double helix unwinds.
2. **Strand separation.** **DNA helicase** breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs, so the two strands separate and each acts as a **template**.
3. **Base pairing.** Free DNA nucleotides in the nucleus are attracted to the exposed bases on each template strand and pair by complementary base pairing (A with T, C with G).
4. **Joining.** **DNA polymerase** joins the adjacent nucleotides by condensation reactions, forming the sugar-phosphate backbone of the new strand.
5. **Result.** Two identical DNA molecules, each made of one old and one new strand.

**Worked example 3: tracing the strands.** Start with one DNA molecule in which both strands are labelled "old". After one round of replication there are two molecules, each with one old strand and one new strand. After three rounds there are 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 molecules. The two original strands are still present, in two of the eight molecules, so 2 / 8 x 100 = 25 % of the molecules contain an original strand. The other six molecules are made entirely of new strands.

The semi-conservative model, with its accurate base pairing, explains why daughter cells inherit the same sequence as the parent. Mistakes that are not corrected become mutations.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that DNA contains "uracil" or that RNA contains "thymine".
- Saying that hydrogen bonds join the nucleotides along one strand. They join the two strands. Along a strand the bonds are between the sugar and the phosphate.
- Writing that helicase "joins" nucleotides or that DNA polymerase "unzips" DNA. Each does the opposite.
- Saying that "a gene codes for a protein" without noting that polypeptides are the product.
- Describing mitochondrial DNA as linear and attached to histones.
- Mixing up exons and introns. Exons are expressed.
- Saying replication is "conservative", or that the new molecule is "half-new" without explaining that one strand is old.

## Exam technique

- **Give the enzyme with its job** in replication questions: helicase breaks hydrogen bonds; DNA polymerase joins nucleotides.
- **Base pairing questions need the rule** (A-T, C-G, and U in RNA).
- **Compare molecules in a table-like way**, giving DNA and RNA properties pair by pair.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know about the direction of strands, 5 prime and 3 prime?**
The specification does not name them. Be able to say that the strands run in opposite directions if asked, but you will not need the numbering.

**Is a codon the same as a triplet?**
A triplet is three bases on DNA that code for an amino acid. A codon is the corresponding three bases on mRNA.
