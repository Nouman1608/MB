Condensed notes on section **3.7.8 Analysing strategic options: investment appraisal** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is A-level only. The full Cadogan Bakery calculations are in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-options-investment-appraisal/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-options-investment-appraisal-practice/).

## Specification content

- Financial methods of assessing an investment: calculation and interpretation of payback, average rate of return and net present value.
- Factors influencing investment decisions: investment criteria, non-financial factors, risk and uncertainty.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Payback | Time taken for cash inflows to recover the initial cost |
| Average rate of return (ARR) | Average annual profit as a percentage of the initial investment |
| Net present value (NPV) | Present value of future cash inflows minus the initial cost |
| Discount factor | 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ, converting a future sum to today's value |
| Time value of money | A sum today is worth more than the same sum later |
| Capital rationing | Funding the best projects until the money runs out |
| Sensitivity analysis | Testing how results change if assumptions change |

## Formulas

| Method | Formula |
|---|---|
| Payback | Years before recovery + (amount still to recover ÷ cash flow in the recovery year) |
| ARR | (Total inflows − cost) ÷ number of years ÷ cost × 100 |
| Present value | Cash flow × discount factor |
| NPV | Sum of present values − initial cost |

**Converting a fraction of a year into months:** multiply by 12.

## Worked mini-example

A project costs £100,000. Inflows: Year 1 £40,000; Year 2 £50,000; Year 3 £60,000. Discount rate 10% (factors 0.909, 0.826, 0.751).

- **Payback:** cumulative £40,000, £90,000, £150,000. Still need £10,000 in year 3: 10,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.167 years = 2 months. **2 years 2 months.**
- **ARR:** total inflows £150,000; profit £50,000; average £16,667; ARR = 16,667 ÷ 100,000 = **16.7%**.
- **NPV:** 40,000 × 0.909 = 36,360; 50,000 × 0.826 = 41,300; 60,000 × 0.751 = 45,060; total 122,720; NPV = 122,720 − 100,000 = **+£22,720**.

## Decision rules

- Payback: accept if within the target period; shorter is better.
- ARR: accept if above the target or the cost of finance; higher is better.
- NPV: accept if positive; choose the higher NPV between projects.

## Strengths and weaknesses

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | Simple; focus on cash and risk | Ignores later flows and time value; target arbitrary |
| ARR | Uses all years; a percentage | Ignores timing and time value |
| NPV | Time value; value created | Complex; depends on discount rate; far forecasts unreliable |

## Non-financial factors

Strategic fit; employees (morale, redundancies, new skills); environment and ethics; brand and customers; competitor reaction; flexibility of the asset; legal rules.

## Risk and uncertainty

- **Risk:** probabilities can be estimated. **Uncertainty:** they cannot.
- Sources: demand, costs, technology, competitors, regulation; longer projects are less reliable.
- Responses: sensitivity analysis; scenarios; higher discount rate; shorter payback target; staged investment or pilots; better market research.

## Comparing two projects

| Result | Project X | Project Y | Which is better on this measure? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payback | 2 years 6 months | 3 years 2 months | X (quicker recovery) |
| ARR | 9% | 14% | Y (higher average return) |
| NPV | +£6,000 | +£21,000 | Y (more value created) |

When methods disagree, state which matters most for this business. A firm short of cash puts weight on payback. A firm with secure finance and a long-term view puts weight on NPV.

## Writing the interpretation

1. State the result with units.
2. Compare it with the criterion or target (for example "payback target of three years").
3. Say accept or reject on this measure.
4. Qualify with a risk: forecasts, discount rate, or an assumption that could be wrong.
5. Add a non-financial point if the case gives one.

## Effect of changing the discount rate

A higher discount rate lowers every present value, so NPV falls. A project with a positive NPV at 8% may be negative at 15%. The rate at which NPV is exactly zero marks the highest return the project can earn. Projects with most of their cash flows in the later years are most sensitive to the rate.

## Exam traps

- Subtract the initial cost to get NPV.
- ARR uses **average** annual profit (divide by years).
- Convert fractions of a year to months if asked.
- Match each discount factor to the right year.
- Interpret: accept or reject, compared with the criterion, and add a caution about forecasts.

## Self-test

1. Define payback.
2. A project costs £60,000 and returns £20,000 a year for five years. Calculate payback.
3. Calculate ARR for the project in question 2.
4. State the NPV decision rule.
5. Calculate the discount factor for year 2 at 10%, to three decimal places.
6. Present values of inflows total £78,000 and the cost is £70,000. State the NPV and whether to accept.
7. Give two weaknesses of payback.
8. Why does NPV discount future cash flows?
9. Give two non-financial factors that might lead a firm to reject a project with a positive NPV.
10. Explain what sensitivity analysis shows.
11. How can a firm allow for a risky project when using NPV?
12. Why might a firm choose a project with a shorter payback but lower NPV?

### Answers

1. The time taken for the cash inflows to recover the initial investment.
2. £60,000 ÷ £20,000 = **3 years**.
3. Total inflows £100,000; profit £40,000; average £8,000; ARR = 8,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **13.3%**.
4. Accept if NPV is positive (and choose the higher NPV between projects).
5. 1 ÷ 1.1² = 1 ÷ 1.21 = **0.826**.
6. £78,000 − £70,000 = **+£8,000**; accept (on this measure).
7. Any two: ignores cash flows after payback; ignores the time value of money; the target is arbitrary; does not measure profitability.
8. Because money received later is worth less than money today (it could be invested, and inflation and risk reduce its value).
9. Any two: damage to the brand or the environment; redundancies and low morale; poor strategic fit; legal risk.
10. How much the result changes if key assumptions, such as sales or costs, turn out different from forecast.
11. Use a higher discount rate, or test pessimistic cash flows.
12. It recovers its cash sooner, which reduces risk and helps if cash is tight or the future is uncertain.
