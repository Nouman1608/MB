Sub-topic 5.4 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029) is about collecting reliable data on living things. It has five statements: applying the scientific method (5.4.1), describing random and systematic sampling (5.4.2) and discussing their benefits and limitations (5.4.3), then describing seven sampling techniques (5.4.4) and discussing those (5.4.5). The syllabus places practical, method-based questions on Paper 2, but the skills also support data questions anywhere. For the topic overview and a quadrat population estimate, see the [Ecosystems, biodiversity and fieldwork study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study-guide/).

## The scientific method, step by step (5.4.1)

The syllabus lists nine parts. Think of them as the stages of one investigation.

1. **Aims and hypotheses.** An aim states what you want to find out: "to investigate whether ground beetle numbers differ between woodland and grassland." A hypothesis is a testable prediction: "more ground beetles will be caught in woodland than in grassland." A good hypothesis names both variables and the direction of the expected difference.
2. **Plan methods.** Choose a sampling strategy (random or systematic), a sampling technique (such as pitfall traps), and, where people are involved, questionnaires or surveys. A **pilot study** is a small trial run that tests whether the method works, for example whether a questionnaire is understood or whether traps catch anything, so problems are fixed before the main investigation.
3. **Measured and control variables.** The measured variable is what you record. Control variables are kept the same so they do not affect the result, such as trap size, time left out and time of day.
4. **Independent and dependent variables.** The independent variable is the one you change or choose (habitat type). The dependent variable is the one that responds and is measured (number of beetles).
5. **Repeats and replicates.** Repeats are measurements taken again in the same way; replicates are extra samples at different places in the same conditions, such as five traps in each habitat rather than one. Both reduce the effect of chance and let you calculate a mean.
6. **Record data.** Use a results table drawn before you start, with units in the column headings and a column for each repeat.
7. **Analyse data.** Calculate means, totals or percentages and display them in a suitable graph, such as a bar chart for categories or a line graph or scatter graph for a continuous change.
8. **Identify and process anomalies.** An anomaly is a result that does not fit the pattern. Check for an error, then exclude it from the mean or repeat the measurement, and say that you have done so.
9. **Form conclusions.** State whether the data support the hypothesis, quote figures, and note how reliable the conclusion is.

## Sampling strategies (5.4.2 and 5.4.3)

You sample because counting every organism is impossible. The strategy decides **where** samples are taken.

**Random sampling.** Every point in the area has an equal chance of being sampled. Lay out a grid using two tape measures at right angles and use random numbers, for example from a calculator, as coordinates.
- Benefits: avoids bias from choosing convenient or interesting spots; suits a fairly uniform area; allows a fair comparison of two areas.
- Limitations: may miss rare species or small patches by chance; needs many samples to be representative; unsuitable for showing how a population changes along a gradient.

**Systematic sampling.** Samples are taken at regular intervals, such as every 5 m along a line.
- Benefits: shows change along a gradient, such as from a path, a river bank or a shore; quick and simple to set out; covers the whole area evenly.
- Limitations: can miss organisms between sample points; can give biased results if the interval matches a regular pattern on the ground, such as plough lines.

Choose systematic sampling when the question describes a gradient and random sampling when it asks you to compare two similar areas.

## Sampling techniques (5.4.4 and 5.4.5)

The technique decides **how** organisms are caught or counted.

| Technique | How it works | Best for | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pitfall trap | Container sunk level with the soil, often with a cover against rain | Ground-living invertebrates such as beetles | Cheap; works unattended | Catches only active ground animals; predators may eat others in the trap; must be checked often |
| Pooter | Suck through one tube so small animals are drawn up another into a chamber | Very small invertebrates on plants or soil | Collects tiny animals without crushing them | Slow; only very small animals; tiring for large areas |
| Sweep net | Strong net swept through long grass or low plants | Insects and spiders in vegetation | Samples many animals quickly | Hard to standardise sweep force; poor in wet or short grass |
| Quadrat | Square frame for counting individuals or estimating percentage cover | Plants and slow-moving animals | Simple; gives density per unit area | Judging percentage cover is subjective; unsuitable for mobile animals |
| Transect | A line across an area; samples taken along it with quadrats or at points | Change along a gradient | Shows zonation clearly | Time-consuming; one line may not be typical |
| Aerial photography and drones | Images taken from above | Large animals, herds, nesting colonies, vegetation cover | Covers large or dangerous areas fast | Costly; needs permits; cannot see under the canopy; may disturb animals |
| Automated sampling | Camera traps, sound recorders or data loggers recording without an observer | Shy or night-active animals; long periods | Works day and night with no disturbance by people | Expensive; theft, battery and weather problems; large amounts of data to sort |

When a question asks you to choose a technique, match it to the organism: pitfall traps for ground beetles, sweep nets for grassland insects, quadrats for plants, drones for herds.

## Worked example 1: an anomaly and a mean

The data are invented. A student set five pitfall traps in woodland and counted beetles after 24 hours: 4, 6, 5, 21, 5.

1. The value 21 does not fit the pattern of the others (4 to 6), so it is an anomaly. A possible cause is a trap placed near an ant nest or by a log where beetles shelter.
2. Mean including the anomaly = (4 + 6 + 5 + 21 + 5) ÷ 5 = 41 ÷ 5 = 8.2 beetles per trap.
3. Mean excluding it = (4 + 6 + 5 + 5) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4 = 5.0 beetles per trap.

The second mean better represents the woodland. Say in the method that the anomaly was excluded, and suggest repeating that trap.

## Worked example 2: annotated planning answer

**Question:** Plan an investigation to test the hypothesis that plant species richness decreases closer to a footpath. [5]

**Model answer:** Lay a transect at right angles to the path, from the path edge into the field [1]. Place a 0.5 m by 0.5 m quadrat every 2 m along the transect, which is systematic sampling, as the hypothesis is about a gradient [1]. In each quadrat count the number of different plant species; this is the dependent variable, and distance from the path is the independent variable [1]. Use the same quadrat size and survey on the same day to control variables [1]. Repeat with three transects at different points along the path and calculate the mean number of species at each distance [1].

## Common mistakes

- Writing "repeat it" without saying what is repeated and why.
- Swapping independent and dependent variables.
- Calling quadrats placed by eye "random".
- Suggesting a pitfall trap for flying insects or a sweep net for soil animals.
- Ignoring ethics: release animals where they were caught and avoid trampling habitats.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a technique: say how it is set up, used and what is recorded.
- **Discuss** a strategy or technique: give a benefit and a limitation with reasons, ideally linked to the organism or site in the question.
- **Suggest** an improvement: name it and say what problem it fixes.
- Always give units, sample sizes and distances in a plan.
