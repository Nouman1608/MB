These questions cover section 2.6 (price changes) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All markets and figures are invented. Revise first with the [price changes study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-changes-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [price changes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-price-changes-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

Questions 4 and 7 use this monthly schedule for bicycles in the town of Seld (invented).

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 500 | 200 |
| 120 | 450 | 300 |
| 140 | 400 | 400 |
| 160 | 350 | 500 |
| 180 | 300 | 600 |

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which change would cause the equilibrium price of wheat to fall and equilibrium sales to rise? **[1]**

A: A rise in the cost of fertiliser

B: An exceptionally good harvest

C: A rise in demand for bread

D: A fall in the price of rice, a substitute

**2.** The demand for a product decreases while its supply increases. What is certain to happen? **[1]**

A: The equilibrium price falls

B: The equilibrium price rises

C: Equilibrium sales fall

D: Equilibrium sales rise

**3.** State **two** changes in market conditions that would raise the equilibrium price of cinema tickets. **[2]**

**4.** Using Seld's schedule:

**(a)** Identify the equilibrium price and sales. **[1]**

**(b)** Demand rises by 150 bicycles at every price. Calculate the new equilibrium price and sales. **[2]**

**5.** Equilibrium in a market moves from a price of 20 and sales of 3,000 to a price of 24 and sales of 2,600. Calculate the percentage change in price and in sales. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a rise in the price of a product does not, by itself, shift its demand curve. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Using Seld's original schedule, a new tax raises producers' costs so that supply falls by 150 bicycles at every price.

**(a)** Calculate the shortage at the old equilibrium price. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and sales. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the change in total spending on bicycles. **[2]**

**8.** Explain, using a demand and supply diagram, the effect on the market for chicken of a disease that kills many chickens. **[6]**

**9.** The price of petrol rises sharply. Analyse the likely effects on the markets for **(a)** large cars and **(b)** bus travel. **[4]**

**10.** In the market for houses, incomes rise and, at the same time, builders face higher costs of materials. Explain what will happen to the equilibrium price and quantity of houses. **[4]**

**11.** A drought reduces the coffee harvest. Explain how this affects the market for tea and the market for coffee shop workers. **[4]**

**12.** A government plans to subsidise solar panels while a popular advertising campaign is also raising demand for them. Discuss whether the price of solar panels will rise or fall. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A good harvest shifts supply right, lowering price and raising sales. A shifts supply left, C shifts demand right (price up), D shifts demand left (sales down). **[1]**

**2.** **A** [1]. Both shifts lower the price; their effects on sales pull in opposite directions. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: a rise in incomes [1]; a rise in the price of streaming services (a substitute), a popular new film, higher costs for cinemas, a new tax on tickets [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Price **140**, sales **400 bicycles a month** [1].

**(b)** New demand: 600 at 120, 550 at 140, 500 at 160 [1]. At 160, quantity demanded (500) equals quantity supplied (500), so the new equilibrium is price **160** and sales **500 bicycles** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Price: 4 ÷ 20 × 100 = **+20%** [1]. Sales: 400 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = **−13.3%** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A change in the product's own price causes a movement along the existing demand curve (a contraction or extension) [1]. The curve shifts only when a condition of demand, such as income or the price of a substitute, changes [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** New supply at 140 = 400 − 150 = 250; shortage = 400 − 250 = **150** [1].

**(b)** New supply: 350 at 160, 450 at 180. At 160 demand 350 equals supply 350 [1], so the new equilibrium is price **160**, sales **350** [1].

**(c)** Before: 140 × 400 = 56,000. After: 160 × 350 = 56,000 [1]. Total spending is **unchanged** (a change of 0) [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Diagram with labelled axes, original D and S1, and S2 to the left [1], with the original and new equilibrium prices and quantities marked [1]. The disease reduces the number of chickens farmers can sell at every price, so supply decreases [1]. At the original price there is now a shortage [1]. The price rises and quantity demanded contracts along the unchanged demand curve [1]. The new equilibrium has a higher price and lower sales [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Petrol is a complement to large cars, which use a lot of it [1], so demand for large cars decreases, lowering their price and sales [1].

**(b)** Bus travel is a substitute for driving [1], so demand for bus travel increases, raising fares and the number of journeys [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Higher incomes shift demand for houses to the right [1]; higher material costs shift supply to the left [1]. Both push the price up, so the equilibrium price will rise [1]. Their effects on quantity are opposite, so the change in the number of houses sold depends on which shift is larger [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The fall in the coffee harvest decreases supply, so the price of coffee rises [1]. Tea is a substitute, so demand for tea increases, raising its price and sales [1]. Coffee shops face higher costs and may sell fewer drinks [1], so their demand for workers falls, which may lower wages or the number employed [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The subsidy lowers producers' costs, so supply shifts right, which tends to lower the price [1]. The advertising campaign shifts demand right, which tends to raise the price [1]. Sales are certain to rise, because both shifts increase quantity [1]. The price will fall if the supply shift is larger and rise if the demand shift is larger [1]; a large subsidy that firms can respond to quickly is likely to dominate, but if producers cannot expand quickly, the demand rise will dominate [1]. Conclusion: the direction of the price change cannot be known without the sizes of the two shifts; the likeliest outcome depends on how fast producers can expand output [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* For double shifts, state the certain result first, then explain why the other result depends on the relative sizes of the shifts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting demand when an event changes costs, or supply when it changes tastes.
- Circular reasoning that has the price rise and then fall again.
- Forgetting to give both price and sales.
- In double-shift questions, stating a definite outcome for the uncertain variable.
- Not using the new schedule when calculating a new equilibrium.
