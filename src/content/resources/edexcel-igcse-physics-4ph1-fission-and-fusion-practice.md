## Section A

**1.** Name the particle that must be absorbed by a uranium-235 nucleus to cause it to split. **[1]**

**2.** Complete the fission equation by finding the mass number of the strontium nucleus:
¹₀n + ²³⁵₉₂U → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + ?₃₈Sr + 2 ¹₀n **[2]**

**3.** In another fission of uranium-235, the products are caesium-137 (¹³⁷₅₅Cs), rubidium-96 (⁹⁶₃₇Rb) and some neutrons. Calculate the number of neutrons released, and show that charge is conserved. **[2]**

**4.** State the form in which most of the energy released by fission first appears. **[1]**

**5.** Complete the fusion equation: ²₁H + ²₁H → ³₂He + ? **[1]**

**6.** State the energy source for stars. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe how a chain reaction can be set up in a lump of uranium-235. **[3]**

**8.** A nuclear reactor contains a moderator and control rods.

**(a)** State the role of the moderator. **[2]**

**(b)** State the role of the control rods. **[1]**

**(c)** A student writes: "The moderator and control rods both slow the reaction down." Evaluate this statement. **[2]**

**9.** The operator of a reactor notices that the power output is rising above the required value.

**(a)** State what the operator should do with the control rods. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how this action brings the power output back to a steady value. **[3]**

**10.** Explain the role of the thick concrete shielding around a nuclear reactor. **[2]**

**11.** Explain why the daughter nuclei produced by fission are a problem after the fuel has been used. **[2]**

**12.** Nuclear fusion takes place in the core of the Sun but not in a container of hydrogen gas at room temperature and pressure. Explain why. **[4]**

**13.** Compare nuclear fission with nuclear fusion. Your answer should include what happens to the nuclei, how each process is started, and where each process is used or occurs. **[4]**

**14.** In a model of a chain reaction, each fission releases neutrons, and on average two of them go on to cause another fission.

**(a)** Starting from one fission, state the number of fissions in the fifth generation. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why a reactor must be designed so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes another fission. **[2]**

**(c)** State the part of the reactor that is adjusted to achieve this. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A neutron [1].

**2.** 1 + 235 = 140 + A + 2 [1]; A = **94**, so the nucleus is ⁹⁴₃₈Sr [1].

**3.** 236 = 137 + 96 + n, so n = **3** neutrons [1]; atomic numbers: 0 + 92 = 55 + 37 + 0 = 92, so charge is conserved [1].

**4.** Kinetic energy of the fission products (daughter nuclei and neutrons) [1].

**5.** ¹₀n (a neutron) [1].

**6.** Nuclear fusion [1].

**7.** A neutron is absorbed by a U-235 nucleus, which splits (fission) [1]. The fission releases two or three neutrons [1]. These neutrons are absorbed by other U-235 nuclei, causing further fissions that release more neutrons, so the process continues [1].

**8. (a)** It slows down the fast neutrons released by fission [1], so that they are more likely to be absorbed by U-235 nuclei and cause further fission [1].
**(b)** They absorb neutrons, controlling the rate of fission [1].
**(c)** The statement is wrong for the moderator: slowing neutrons makes fission **more** likely, so it keeps the reaction going [1]; only the control rods reduce the rate, by absorbing neutrons [1].

**9. (a)** Lower the control rods further into the core [1].
**(b)** The rods absorb more neutrons [1], so fewer neutrons are available to cause fission and the rate of fission falls [1]. The rods are then adjusted so that, on average, one neutron from each fission causes another fission, keeping the rate steady [1].

**10.** The reactor core emits dangerous radiation, such as neutrons and gamma rays [1]; the thick shielding absorbs this radiation, protecting the workers and the environment outside [1].

**11.** The daughter nuclei are radioactive [1], so the used fuel continues to emit ionising radiation and must be stored safely as radioactive waste [1].

**12.** Hydrogen nuclei (protons) are positively charged, so they repel each other electrostatically [1]. At room temperature they move too slowly to get close enough to fuse [1]; in the Sun's core the temperature is extremely high, so the nuclei move fast enough to overcome the repulsion [1]. The very high pressure in the core also packs the nuclei closely, so collisions are frequent [1].

**13.** Any four, with at least one point on each of the two processes: in fission a large nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei [1]; in fusion small nuclei join to form a larger nucleus [1]; fission is started by absorbing a neutron [1]; fusion needs very high temperature and pressure [1]; fission is used in nuclear power stations [1]; fusion occurs in stars [1]; both release energy [1]; maximum 4.

**14. (a)** 1, 2, 4, 8, 16: **16** fissions in the fifth generation [1].
**(b)** If more than one neutron per fission causes another fission, the number of fissions grows rapidly and the energy release becomes uncontrollable (explosive) [1]; with exactly one, the rate of fission, and so the energy output, stays constant [1].
**(c)** The control rods (raised or lowered to absorb more or fewer neutrons) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the atom splits" instead of "the nucleus splits".
- Forgetting the extra neutrons when balancing mass numbers (Questions 2 and 3).
- Counting the first fission as generation zero in Question 14: the sequence starts at 1.
- Giving the moderator the control rods' job.
- In Question 12, missing "positively charged" or "electrostatic repulsion".
- In Question 13, writing two separate descriptions with no comparison.
