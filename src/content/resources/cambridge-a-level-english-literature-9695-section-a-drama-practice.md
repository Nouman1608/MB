**3.** Define **dramatic irony** and explain its usual effect on an audience. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between a **soliloquy** and an **aside**. **[2]**

## Section B — Analysing original extracts

**7.** Read this **original** extract.

> *(A kitchen at night. RUTH is ironing. DANIEL enters with a suitcase, and stops when he sees her.)*
>
> RUTH: *(without looking up)* Shirts are on the chair.
>
> DANIEL: I wasn't going to take—
>
> RUTH: The blue one needs a button. *(She keeps ironing.)*

**(a)** Identify **two** stage directions and explain what each shows about the situation. **(b)** Explain
how the interruption in Daniel's line creates tension. **[6]**

**8.** Using the extract in Question 7, explain how the dramatist uses an **ordinary domestic activity**
to present conflict between the two characters. **[4]**

**9.** Read this **original** extract, written in verse.

> DUKE: You swore you would be silent. Swear again.
>
> LORD: I swore to keep a secret, not a crime.
>
> DUKE: There is no crime that I have not forgiven.
>
> LORD: Then you forgive yourself too easily.

Name the technique used in the exchange of single lines, and explain **two** ways it presents the
relationship between the speakers. **[5]**

**10.** In the extract in Question 9, the LORD repeats and alters the DUKE's words. Explain the dramatic
effect of this repetition. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate begins a (b) answer: "In this passage Ruth irons, then Daniel comes in with a case,
then Ruth tells him about the shirts." Explain why this opening is weak, and rewrite it as a stronger
opening sentence. **[4]**

**12.** A student is planning an (a) essay on the question "Discuss the dramatic presentation of power in
the play you have studied." Explain **three** features of a well-planned answer to this question. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A (b) question prints a **passage from the set play** on the paper, to be analysed in relation to
the whole text [1]. The (a) question is an **essay on the play as a whole**, with no passage printed [1].

**2.** **AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4** are tested [1]. **AO5** (evaluation of opinion) is not tested on Paper 1
[1].

**3.** Dramatic irony occurs when the **audience knows something a character does not** [1]. It gives
the character's words a second meaning, which can build **tension, suspense or comedy** as the audience
waits for the truth to emerge [1].

**4.** A **soliloquy** is spoken by a character **alone on stage**, voicing private thoughts [1]. An
**aside** is a brief remark heard by the audience but **not by other characters who are present** [1].

**5.** An embedded stage direction is an instruction to the actor **contained in the dialogue** itself,
such as a line telling someone to kneel or look [1]. Early modern plays print few stage directions, so
these lines are often the main evidence of **what the audience would see** [1].

**6.** Plays are written to be **performed**, so meaning is created through what is seen and heard on
stage: movement, silence, props, timing [1]. Referring to the audience keeps the analysis focused on the
**dramatist's theatrical choices**, which AO2 rewards [1].

**7. (a)** "Without looking up" shows Ruth **refusing eye contact**, so her control of the scene is
established physically [1]; this suggests she already knows why he is there [1]. "She keeps ironing"
shows her **refusing to stop her routine** [1], which makes his departure seem unremarkable to her, a
deliberate coldness [1]. **(b)** The dash shows Daniel **being cut off mid-sentence** [1], so he is denied
the chance to explain himself and the audience senses that Ruth will not allow a conversation to begin
[1].

**8.** The ironing gives Ruth a **task to hide behind**, so her emotion is displaced into action [1]. Her
practical remarks about shirts and a button **avoid naming the departure** [1], which creates subtext:
the audience understands what neither says [1]. The continuing domestic routine also suggests the
**ordinariness of the marriage being ended**, which makes the moment more painful [1].

**9.** The technique is **stichomythia** [1]. It presents the relationship as a **contest**: each line
answers and turns the previous one, like moves in a duel [1] [1]. It also shows the **LORD gaining
ground**, since he has the final, most pointed line, which shifts the balance of power [1] [1].

**10.** By taking up the DUKE's words ("swore", "forgive") and **twisting their meaning** [1], the LORD
turns the DUKE's own language against him [1], so the audience sees a subordinate **challenging
authority through wit** rather than open defiance [1].

**11.** The opening **retells the action** in sequence and contains no analysis, interpretation or
argument [1] [1]. A stronger opening states the dramatic function, for example: "The dramatist stages the
end of a marriage without allowing it to be spoken, as Ruth's refusal to stop ironing turns Daniel's
departure into an interruption of her routine rather than an event." [1] [1] (One mark for a clear
thesis, one for reference to a dramatic method.)

**12.** A clear **thesis** that answers the question directly, for example arguing who holds power and
whether it shifts [1] [1]. A selection of **three or four moments from across the play, including the
ending**, to show range and development [1] [1]. Analysis of power **as theatre**: who speaks, who is
silent, who controls entrances and exits, and how staging presents status [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Summarising what happens in an extract instead of analysing how the dramatist makes it happen.
- Treating stage directions as background rather than quoting them as evidence.
- Naming stichomythia, a pause or an aside without explaining its effect at that point.
- Writing an (a) essay that never reaches the end of the play.
