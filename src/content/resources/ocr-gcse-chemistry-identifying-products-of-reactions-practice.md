## Questions

**1.** For each gas, describe the test and give the positive result.

**(a)** Oxygen **[2]**
**(b)** Chlorine **[2]**

**2.** A student is given three solids to flame test.

**(a)** Describe how the student should carry out a flame test on one solid. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the wire loop must be cleaned between samples. **[1]**

**3.** Four solids give these flame colours: W crimson, X green, Y lilac, Z yellow.

**(a)** Identify the metal ion in each solid. **[4]**
**(b)** A fifth solid gives an orange-red flame. Identify its metal ion. **[1]**

**4.** *(Higher Tier only)* A few drops of sodium hydroxide solution, and then an excess, are added to four solutions.

| Solution | Few drops | Excess |
|---|---|---|
| A | Blue precipitate | No change |
| B | White precipitate | Precipitate dissolves |
| C | Orange-brown precipitate | No change |
| D | White precipitate | No change |

**(a)** Identify the cation in each solution. **[4]**
**(b)** Give the ionic equation for the precipitate forming in solution C. Include state symbols. **[2]**

**5.** *(Higher Tier only)* A student thinks a solution contains bromide ions.

**(a)** Name the two reagents the student should add, in order. **[2]**
**(b)** State the result if bromide ions are present. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the student must not use hydrochloric acid in this test. **[1]**

**6.** *(Higher Tier only)* Two white solids are sodium carbonate and sodium sulfate. Both give a yellow flame. Describe tests, with results, that would show which solid is which. **[4]**

**7.** A colour-change test kit can detect a metal ion in water down to 0.5 mg/dm³. A laboratory instrument can detect the same ion down to 0.002 mg/dm³.

**(a)** Calculate how many times more sensitive the instrument is than the kit. **[2]**
**(b)** A water sample contains 0.010 mg/dm³ of the ion. State which method(s) would detect it. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one advantage of the instrument other than sensitivity, and say what it means. **[1]**

**8.** A forensic chemist has a small sample of a colourless liquid. Its mass spectrum is shown below as bar heights. Simplified reference data are supplied (use these values).

| m/z | Unknown (relative abundance) | Bar |
|---|---|---|
| 27 | 15 | █▌ |
| 43 | 18 | █▊ |
| 45 | 100 | ██████████ |
| 60 | 5 | ▌ |

| Reference | Main peaks, m/z (relative abundance) |
|---|---|
| Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH | 31 (100), 29 (15), 42 (15), 59 (10), 60 (6) |
| Propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ | 45 (100), 43 (18), 27 (15), 60 (5) |
| Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH | 43 (100), 45 (85), 60 (75) |

**(a)** State the relative molecular mass of the unknown and explain how you got it from the chart. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the unknown. Use the reference data to justify your answer. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why the chemist uses an instrument rather than test-tube reactions for this sample. **[2]**

**9.** *(Higher Tier only)* Two unknown solids, X and Y, are each a single ionic compound.

- X: crimson flame. Its solution gives no precipitate with sodium hydroxide. With dilute acid it fizzes, and the gas turns limewater milky.
- Y: orange-red flame. Its solution gives a white precipitate with sodium hydroxide that stays in excess, and a cream precipitate with dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate solution.

**(a)** Name compound X and write its formula. **[3]**
**(b)** Name compound Y and write its formula. **[3]**
**(c)** Write the ionic equation for the cream precipitate forming. **[1]**

**10.** *(Higher Tier only)* Four bottles of solution have lost their labels. They hold iron(II) chloride, iron(III) sulfate, zinc sulfate and calcium chloride. Describe a plan of tests, with the expected result for each solution, that would identify all four. **[8]**

**11.** A student adds a catalyst to hydrogen peroxide solution in an open flask on a balance. A gas bubbles off and relights a glowing splint. The balance reads 85.20 g at the start and 84.54 g at the end.

**(a)** Calculate the change in mass. **[1]**
**(b)** The student writes: "Mass was destroyed in the reaction." Explain why this is wrong. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the gas. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Put a glowing splint into the gas [1]; **it relights** [1].
**(b)** Damp blue litmus paper is placed in the gas [1]; **it goes red, then the colour is bleached to white** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give both the test and the result for each gas; "relights" with a lighted splint, or bleaching with dry paper, is unlikely to gain the marks.

**2. (a)** Clean a nichrome loop by dipping it in hydrochloric acid and heating it until it gives no colour [1]. Dip the loop in acid and then in the solid [1]. Hold it at the edge of a blue (roaring) Bunsen flame and record the colour [1].
**(b)** Traces of the previous solid would give their own colour, giving a **false result** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say "blue flame"; an answer that just says "put it in the flame" leaves out the condition that makes the colour visible.

**3. (a)** W: **lithium, Li⁺** [1]. X: **copper, Cu²⁺** [1]. Y: **potassium, K⁺** [1]. Z: **sodium, Na⁺** [1].
**(b)** **Calcium, Ca²⁺** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Crimson and orange-red are easily swapped; tie crimson to lithium and orange-red to calcium, and do not write a vague "red".

**4. (a)** A: **Cu²⁺** [1]. B: **Zn²⁺** [1]. C: **Fe³⁺** [1]. D: **Ca²⁺** [1].
**(b)** Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃ with the correct balancing [1]; **Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)** with all state symbols correct [1].
*Examiner insight:* Write "iron(III)" or Fe³⁺, not just "iron"; the oxidation state is what separates C from an iron(II) solution.

**5. (a)** Dilute nitric acid [1], then silver nitrate solution [1].
**(b)** **A cream precipitate** (silver bromide) [1].
**(c)** The chloride ions in hydrochloric acid react with silver nitrate too, so a **white precipitate** would appear even with no halide in the sample [1].
*Examiner insight:* The order matters: acid first, then silver nitrate. Naming the reagents in the wrong order, or "acid" with no name, weakens the answer.

**6.** Add dilute hydrochloric acid to each solid [1]; sodium carbonate fizzes and the gas turns limewater milky [1]. Dissolve the other solid and add barium chloride solution that has been acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid [1]; **a white precipitate shows sodium sulfate** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The flame test cannot separate these two, so marks come from the anion tests; give a reagent and an observation for each.

**7. (a)** 0.5 ÷ 0.002 [1] = **250 times more sensitive** [1].
**(b)** **Only the instrument**: 0.010 mg/dm³ is above the instrument's limit but below the kit's limit [1].
**(c)** Speed: results come quickly and many samples can be run; or accuracy: the result is reliable and close to the true value [1].
*Examiner insight:* For "how many times", divide the larger limit by the smaller and give a plain number; a lower detection limit means a more sensitive method.

**8. (a)** The peak with the highest m/z is at 60 [1], so **Mr = 60** [1].
**(b)** All three references have Mr 60, so the fragment pattern decides [1]. The base peak of the unknown is at 45, which rules out propan-1-ol (base peak 31) [1]. Ethanoic acid has a large peak at 60 (75) and its base peak at 43, but the unknown's peaks at 27, 43, 45 and 60 match propan-2-ol in position and height, so it is **propan-2-ol** [1].
**(c)** First reason, for example sensitivity: a very small forensic sample is enough [1]. Second reason, for example accuracy (the identification is reliable) or speed (results come quickly) [1].
*Examiner insight:* A justification that only says "it matches" gains less than one that names the matching peaks and the peak that rules out each other reference.

**9. (a)** Crimson flame: lithium ions [1]; fizzing with limewater milky: carbonate ions [1]; **lithium carbonate, Li₂CO₃** [1].
**(b)** Orange-red flame and white hydroxide that stays in excess: calcium ions [1]; cream precipitate with acidified silver nitrate: bromide ions [1]; **calcium bromide, CaBr₂** [1].
**(c)** **Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s)** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show which result points to which ion; a bare name with no reasoning gives the marker nothing to credit if the final formula slips.

**10.** Put a fresh sample of each solution in a separate test tube and add a few drops of sodium hydroxide solution [1].
- Green precipitate: **iron(II) chloride** [1].
- Orange-brown precipitate: **iron(III) sulfate** [1].
- The other two give white precipitates. Add excess sodium hydroxide [1].
- The white precipitate that dissolves in excess is **zinc sulfate** [1]; the one that stays is **calcium chloride** [1].

Confirm the anions on fresh samples. Dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride gives a white precipitate with iron(III) sulfate and zinc sulfate [1]. Dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate gives a white precipitate with iron(II) chloride and calcium chloride [1].
*Examiner insight:* In a long plan, set the tests out in a clear order; state the reagent, the observation and the conclusion for every bottle, and use fresh samples for each test.

**11. (a)** 85.20 − 84.54 = **0.66 g decrease** [1].
**(b)** Mass is conserved: atoms are not created or destroyed [1]. The flask is open, so the gas produced escapes into the air, and its mass is no longer on the balance [1].
**(c)** **Oxygen** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link the mass change to the gas leaving an open container; repeating "mass is conserved" without saying where the mass went is unlikely to earn both marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a test without its result, or a result without the test.
- Mixing up the splints: glowing is the oxygen test, lighted is the hydrogen test.
- Writing "red" for both lithium and calcium flames.
- Stopping after "white precipitate" without adding excess sodium hydroxide to separate zinc from calcium.
- Writing "iron" instead of iron(II) or iron(III).
- Adding hydrochloric acid before silver nitrate, or sulfuric acid before barium chloride.
- Reading Mr from the tallest peak instead of the highest m/z peak.
- Justifying a spectrum match with "it looks the same" instead of naming peaks.
- Giving "more reliable" or "better" as an instrumental advantage without using sensitivity, accuracy or speed.
