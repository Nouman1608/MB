This guide teaches section 3.2.3.3 Profit and cash of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1. The section has three parts. **Profit**: the meaning and significance of profit, gross, operating and net profit and their margins, "the difference between profit and cash", "the difference between profit and profitability", and ways of improving profits and profitability. **Budgeting**: "amend and interpret budget statements", adverse and favourable variances, and the value of variance analysis and of budgeting. **Cash flow forecasts**: the importance of cash, the purpose and value of forecasts, amending and completing them, the timing of inflows and outflows (payables, receivables, payables days and receivables days), and ways of improving cash flow. ## Profit and the three levels

Profit is revenue minus costs over a period. It matters because it rewards owners, provides retained profit for investment, and shows lenders and investors that the business is viable.

```
Gross profit     = Revenue − Cost of sales
Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses
Net profit       = Operating profit − Interest − Taxes
Each margin (%)  = That profit ÷ Revenue × 100
```

The **gross margin** reflects control of direct costs (materials, stock for resale); the **operating margin** adds overheads such as rent and marketing; the **net margin** shows what is left for owners after interest and tax.

### Worked example 1: reading margins over two years

Ashcombe Garden Centre:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (£) | 400,000 | 480,000 |
| Gross profit (£) | 160,000 | 177,600 |
| Gross margin | 40% | 37% |
| Operating expenses (£) | 112,000 | 134,400 |
| Operating profit (£) | 48,000 | 43,200 |
| Operating margin | 12% | 9% |

Revenue grew by 20% and gross profit grew by £17,600, so a quick reading says Ashcombe did well. The margins say otherwise. Operating expenses were 28% of revenue in **both** years (112,000 ÷ 400,000 and 134,400 ÷ 480,000), so overheads did not cause the fall. The whole three-point drop in operating margin comes from the gross margin. Ashcombe is earning less on each pound of sales, perhaps because it cut prices to win the extra sales or because its plant suppliers raised their prices. A strong answer names the level where the problem starts and suggests a cause that fits the case.

## Profit versus profitability

**Profit** is an amount of money. **Profitability** is profit measured against revenue, as a margin. Ashcombe's revenue rose, but its profitability fell from 12% to 9%. Comparing businesses of different sizes by profit alone misleads: a large supermarket can make far more profit than a small deli on a much lower margin.

## Profit versus cash

A business can be profitable and still run out of cash, because profit and cash flow measure different things. Profit records sales and costs when they are **earned and incurred**; cash flow records money when it actually **enters or leaves the bank**. The gap comes from five sources:

1. **Credit sales**: revenue counts towards profit straight away, but the cash arrives weeks later.
2. **Credit purchases**: supplies count as a cost, but are paid for later.
3. **Buying long-life assets**: a van is paid for in cash now, but its cost is spread over its useful life in the profit figures.
4. **Loans and new share capital**: cash comes in, but this is finance, not revenue, so profit is unchanged; loan repayments are cash out but not a cost (only interest is).
5. **Inventory**: buying stock that has not yet been sold uses cash before any profit is made on it.

### Worked example 2: profitable but overdrawn

In one quarter Brindlecombe Furniture sold £90,000 of furniture, but customers on 60-day terms paid only £62,000 in the quarter. Its costs were £72,000, of which it paid £54,000; the rest was owed to suppliers. It also paid £25,000 cash for a delivery van and received a £10,000 bank loan. Its opening bank balance was £4,000.

```
Profit       = 90,000 − 72,000 = £18,000
Cash in      = 62,000 + 10,000 = £72,000
Cash out     = 54,000 + 25,000 = £79,000
Net cash flow   = 72,000 − 79,000 = −£7,000
Closing balance = 4,000 − 7,000 = −£3,000 (overdrawn)
```

Brindlecombe made £18,000 profit yet ended the quarter £3,000 overdrawn. Cash pays wages and suppliers on time; profit does not. That is why the specification asks you to explain "the importance of cash".

## Improving profits and profitability

Each method has an advantage and a challenge, and the best answers say which fits the business.

- **Raise prices**: higher margin per sale, but volume may fall if customers are price sensitive.
- **Reduce direct costs** by finding cheaper suppliers or cutting waste: gross margin rises, but quality may suffer.
- **Reduce overheads** such as cheaper premises: operating margin rises, but cuts to marketing or staff can reduce sales.
- **Increase sales volume**: fixed costs are spread over more units, but promotion costs money and capacity may run out.

For Ashcombe, the evidence points at direct costs or pricing, not overheads, so cutting overheads would treat the wrong problem.

## Budgeting: amending and interpreting

**Variance = actual − budget.** A variance is **favourable (F)** if it increases profit and **adverse (A)** if it reduces it. "Amend" means updating a budget when an assumption changes, so that actual results are compared with a fair target.

### Worked example 3: why the amended budget matters

Drewsteignton Tiles budgeted for 2,000 boxes at £25, with materials £8 a box, wages £12,000 and overheads £7,000. Before the month began, managers cut the price to £24 and raised planned sales to 2,300 boxes, so they amended the budget.

| £ | Original budget | Amended budget | Actual | Variance against amended |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 50,000 | 55,200 | 54,000 | 1,200 A |
| Materials | 16,000 | 18,400 | 19,100 | 700 A |
| Wages | 12,000 | 12,000 | 12,600 | 600 A |
| Overheads | 7,000 | 7,000 | 7,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 15,000 | 17,800 | 15,300 | 2,500 A |

Against the original budget, profit looks £300 favourable. Against the amended budget, it is £2,500 adverse, with every line on or worse than target. The amended comparison is the fair one: the business planned for more because it changed its price, and it fell short. **Interpret** by asking why each variance arose: was revenue down because fewer boxes sold or because of extra discounts? Was the materials overspend a price rise or waste?

**Value:** variance analysis shows where and by how much results differ from plan, so managers act early; budgets set targets, control spending and co-ordinate departments, but mislead if built on poor forecasts.

## Cash flow forecasts: amending and timing

Every month, closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow, and the next month opens with that closing balance. So an amendment in one month changes every later balance until the timing difference unwinds.

### Worked example 4: negotiating longer supplier credit

Kittisford Cycles, opening balance £1,800:

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 15,000 | 14,000 | 18,500 |
| Total outflows | 16,500 | 17,200 | 15,000 |
| Net cash flow | (1,500) | (3,200) | 3,500 |
| Opening balance | 1,800 | 300 | (2,900) |
| Closing balance | 300 | (2,900) | 600 |

Kittisford's supplier agrees to let a £2,400 payment due in month 2 be paid in month 3. Amend: month 2 outflows fall to £14,800 and month 3 outflows rise to £17,400.

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash flow | (1,500) | (800) | 1,100 |
| Opening balance | 1,800 | 300 | (500) |
| Closing balance | 300 | (500) | 600 |

The month 2 low point improves from −£2,900 to −£500, but month 3 still closes at £600: moving a payment changes **timing**, not the total. Kittisford still needs a small overdraft for month 2.

### Payables and receivables days

```
Receivables days = Trade receivables ÷ Revenue × 365
Payables days    = Trade payables ÷ Cost of sales × 365
```

Morwick Engineering has revenue of £1,095,000, receivables of £150,000, cost of sales of £657,000 and payables of £54,000. Receivables days = 50; payables days = 30. Morwick pays its suppliers about 20 days before its customers pay it, so it must finance that gap. If it cut receivables days to 35, receivables would fall to 1,095,000 × 35 ÷ 365 = £105,000, bringing in £45,000 of cash.

## Choosing a way to improve cash flow

The specification names debt factoring, shortening payment time from customers, early payment incentives, credit checks, increasing payment time to suppliers, reducing outgoings and better invoice management. Several of them improve cash **at the expense of profit**: factoring costs a fee and early payment discounts reduce revenue. Match the method to the cause. Morwick's problem is slow-paying customers, so better invoice management or early payment incentives tackle the cause; Kittisford's is a short-term timing gap, which longer supplier credit or an overdraft can cover.

## Common mistakes

- Calling revenue "profit", or using operating profit in the net margin.
- Saying a bank loan increases profit.
- Treating every positive number in a variance column as favourable: a cost above budget is adverse.
- Comparing actual results with an out-of-date budget.
- Forgetting to carry each closing balance forward as the next opening balance after an amendment.
- Recommending factoring without mentioning the fee.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, the substitution and the answer with a unit (£, % or days).
- **Explain**: link cause and effect in context: "Credit sales count as revenue now, but the cash arrives in 60 days, so..."
- **Analyse**: follow the consequence through, for example from a cash shortfall to unpaid suppliers to lost trade credit.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the benefit to cash against the cost to profit or to relationships, and reach a judgement for this business.
