## Ownership types — the progression to memorise

Sole trader → partnership → private limited company. Each step trades away personal control for
access to more capital and less personal liability. Also know: cooperative, public limited company,
public corporation (public sector).

## Location factors — two lists, don't mix them

| Physical location factors | Ecommerce location factors |
|---|---|
| Labour, markets, transport, power | Wi-fi, warehousing |
| Raw materials, competition | Technology specialists |
| Suppliers, communication, government influence | Logistics |

## Why small-scale retailers survive — always give a reason

Don't assume small shops are simply being squeezed out. Personal service, flexible opening hours,
convenience and local knowledge of customers are the named reasons independent shops and market stalls
continue to survive alongside supermarkets and online sellers. Examiners reward this survival
explanation, not just growth-of-large-scale-retail description.

## Retail vs wholesale — keep the direction straight

- **Retailer**: sells to the final consumer.
- **Wholesaler**: sells to the retailer (breaks bulk — buys large quantities, sells smaller quantities).

Independent wholesalers survive mainly through **cash-and-carry** outlets and **voluntary chains**.

## Trading documents — sequence, not just a list

Enquiry → Quotation → Order → Invoice → Delivery note → Credit note (if goods returned) → Statement of
account → Receipt. Paper 2 often gives a scenario and asks you to identify or sequence the correct
document — build the sequence into memory, not just the individual names.

International trade adds: certificate of origin, bill of lading, air waybill, documentary credit
(letter of credit).

## Communication — match method to the scenario's factors

Choice of method depends on: **urgency, cost, security, distance, network availability, importance of
the message**. A "recommend a communication method" question is answered by weighing these factors
against the specific scenario, not by listing every method you know.

## Multinationals and franchises — weigh both sides

A multinational company (MNC) headquarters in one country and operates in one or more others. Hosting
one can bring jobs, investment, technology transfer and export earnings, but can also mean profits
leaving the country, competition local firms cannot match, and reduced control over strategic
decisions — a strong answer always weighs both, never states only the benefit or only the cost. A
franchise lets a franchisee trade under an established brand using the franchisor's systems, in
exchange for fees and a share of revenue: it lowers risk through a proven business model, but reduces
independence and requires ongoing payments back to the franchisor.

## Why this is the broadest topic in the syllabus

Topic 2 spans six named sub-topics — commercial enterprises, outsourcing, retailing, wholesaling,
trading documents and communication — more than any other single topic in 0715. It sits directly after
the introductory Topic 1 (Commerce and production) and before Topic 3 (Globalisation of trade),
building the vocabulary — sole trader, partnership, franchise, multinational, wholesaler, retailer —
that later topics on logistics, aids to trade and sustainability all assume candidates already know.
Because it covers so much ground, resist the temptation to revise it as six disconnected mini-topics;
every enterprise, whichever sector owns it, faces the same underlying decisions this topic works
through in sequence: what legal form to take, where to locate, whether to make or buy, and how to reach
customers.

## Exam traps

- Confusing a retailer with a wholesaler on who the final customer is.
- Naming a trading document without knowing which stage of the transaction it belongs to.
- Listing franchise features without weighing the loss of independence against lower risk.
- Answering "why does the small retailer survive" questions with growth-of-large-retail facts instead
  of the specific survival reasons.

## Outsourcing — the shorter sub-topic, don't skip it

2.2 is easy to under-revise because it is short relative to the other five sub-topics, but it is still
examinable on its own: outsourcing has grown through **contract manufacturing** (paying another
company to produce goods) and **contract logistics** (paying another company to handle storage and
distribution). Be ready to explain why an enterprise might choose to outsource rather than produce
in-house — typically lower cost, access to specialist expertise, and freeing up capital and management
time for the enterprise's core activities.

## Digital retail and trading document trends

The retailing and trading-documents sub-topics both carry a digital dimension worth revising alongside
the traditional content: digital retail trends include omnichannel retailing, cashless stores, EPoS,
click and collect, self-service checkouts, subscription commerce, QR codes, VR showrooms, dropshipping
and mobile apps, while trading documents increasingly move online too, with digital signatures and
electronic receipts replacing paper equivalents at each stage of the same underlying sequence. Treat
these as the digital version of concepts you already know, rather than an entirely separate list to
memorise from scratch.

## Self-test

1. What is the ownership progression from sole trader to public limited company?
2. Name two physical location factors and two ecommerce location factors.
3. Why do small-scale retailers continue to survive?
4. What is the correct order of the first four home-trade documents in a transaction?
5. Name the five factors that determine choice of communication method.

**Answers:** 1. Sole trader → partnership → private limited company → public limited company (more
capital, less personal control at each step). 2. Physical: labour, transport (or markets, power, raw
materials). Ecommerce: wi-fi, warehousing (or technology specialists, logistics). 3. Personal service,
flexible opening hours, convenience, local knowledge of customers. 4. Enquiry, quotation, order,
invoice. 5. Urgency, cost, security, distance, network availability (plus importance of the message).
