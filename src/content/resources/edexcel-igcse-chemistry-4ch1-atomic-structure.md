## Atoms and molecules (1.14)

An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that has the chemical properties of that element. A **molecule** is a particle made of two or more atoms chemically bonded together.

A formula tells you how many atoms are in one molecule. Count each subscript, and remember that a symbol with no subscript means one atom.

**Worked example 1.** For one molecule of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, and one of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, state the number of atoms and the number of elements.

```
H2SO4:   H 2 + S 1 + O 4 = 7 atoms;  3 elements (H, S, O)
C2H5OH:  C 2 + H 5 + O 1 + H 1 = 9 atoms;  3 elements (C, H, O)
```

The trap in ethanol is the second H at the end of the formula: count it.

Molecules can be elements (N₂, P₄, S₈) or compounds (H₂O, CH₄). Noble gases exist as separate atoms, not molecules.

## The structure of the atom (1.15)

| Particle | Position | Relative mass | Relative charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | 1 | +1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 1 | 0 |
| Electron | Shells around the nucleus | 1/1840 (negligible) | −1 |

Three consequences are tested often:

- The **nucleus contains almost all the mass**, because protons and neutrons are about 1840 times heavier than electrons.
- The **nucleus is positive**, because it contains protons and neutral neutrons.
- An **atom is neutral**, because the number of electrons equals the number of protons, and their charges are equal and opposite.

The nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom; most of an atom's volume is the space occupied by the electrons.

## Atomic number, mass number and isotopes (1.16)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Atomic number | The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom |
| Mass number | The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom |
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers, because they have different numbers of neutrons |
| Relative atomic mass (Ar) | The weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12 |

In the notation ᴬ_Z X, the mass number A is at the top left and the atomic number Z at the bottom left. A charge at the top right shows an ion.

### Counting particles in unfamiliar species

```
protons   = atomic number
neutrons  = mass number - atomic number
electrons = protons - charge   (a 3+ ion: subtract 3; a 1- ion: add 1)
```

**Worked example 2.** Complete the table.

| Species | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺ | 26 | 56 − 26 = 30 | 26 − 3 = 23 |
| ⁷⁹₃₅Br⁻ | 35 | 79 − 35 = 44 | 35 + 1 = 36 |
| ¹³₆C | 6 | 13 − 6 = 7 | 6 |

**Worked example 3 — working backwards.** A particle has 20 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons. Identify it and write its symbol.

```
20 protons            -> atomic number 20 -> calcium
mass number = 20 + 20 = 40
charge = protons - electrons = 20 - 18 = +2
symbol: 40Ca2+ (mass number 40 top left, atomic number 20 bottom left)
```

### Why isotopes react the same way

Isotopes have the same number of protons, so their atoms have the same number of electrons arranged in the same way. Chemical reactions depend on electrons, so isotopes have the same chemical properties. They differ only in mass, so, for example, a compound made with a heavier isotope has a slightly greater relative formula mass.

### Why Ar uses carbon-12

Atoms are far too light to weigh in grams conveniently, so their masses are compared with a standard. One-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom is given the value exactly 1. Ar is therefore a ratio and has **no units**.

## Calculating relative atomic mass (1.17)

```
Ar = sum of (mass number x abundance) / total abundance
```

Divide by 100 for percentages, or by the total of the numbers when abundances are relative values that do not add to 100.

**Worked example 4 — percentages.** Copper is 69.2% copper-63 and 30.8% copper-65. Calculate Ar to 1 decimal place.

```
Ar = (63 x 69.2 + 65 x 30.8) / 100
   = (4359.6 + 2002.0) / 100
   = 6361.6 / 100
   = 63.616
   = 63.6 (1 d.p.)
```

Sense check: 63.6 lies between 63 and 65, nearer 63, the more abundant isotope.

**Worked example 5 — relative abundances.** A sample of an element contains three isotopes with mass numbers 28, 29 and 30 in the relative amounts 46 : 2.4 : 1.6. Calculate Ar to 1 decimal place.

```
total abundance = 46 + 2.4 + 1.6 = 50
sum of products = 28 x 46 + 29 x 2.4 + 30 x 1.6
                = 1288 + 69.6 + 48
                = 1405.6
Ar = 1405.6 / 50 = 28.112 = 28.1 (1 d.p.)
```

Dividing by 100 here would give 14.1, which is impossible because it is lighter than every isotope present.

**Worked example 6 — working backwards from Ar.** Boron has Ar 10.8 and two isotopes, boron-10 and boron-11. Calculate the percentage abundance of boron-10.

```
let boron-10 = x %, so boron-11 = (100 - x) %
10x + 11(100 - x) = 10.8 x 100
10x + 1100 - 11x = 1080
-x = -20
x = 20
boron-10 = 20 %, boron-11 = 80 %
```

Check: (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) ÷ 100 = (200 + 880) ÷ 100 = 10.8. Always substitute your answer back.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the electron a relative mass of 0. It is about 1/1840: negligible, not zero.
- Defining mass number as "the mass of the atom" or "protons plus electrons".
- Leaving "of the same element" (or "same number of protons") out of the isotope definition.
- Adding the charge to find electrons in a positive ion. A positive ion has **lost** electrons.
- Giving Ar with units such as g, or as a whole number when 1 decimal place is asked for.
- Dividing by 100 when the abundances do not total 100.

## Exam technique

- **State what is meant by**: give the full definition; one-word answers such as "protons" for atomic number need "the number of protons in the nucleus".
- **Calculate**: write the expression with numbers before evaluating, so a slip still earns method marks; give the requested decimal places.
- **Explain** why isotopes react the same way: the chain is same protons → same number and arrangement of electrons → same reactions.
- **Deduce** a particle's identity: find the element from the protons first, then the charge from the electrons.
