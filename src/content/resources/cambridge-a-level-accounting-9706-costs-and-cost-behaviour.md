This guide covers **2.1 Costs and cost behaviour** (sub-section 2.1.1 Materials and labour) of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting 9706 syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content, and A Level Papers 3 and 4 assume it. All businesses and figures are invented and all amounts are in dollars.

The [costs and traditional costing methods overview](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-costs-cost-behaviour-and-traditional-costing-methods/) introduces every cost term in sections 2.1 and 2.2. This page goes further into 2.1 alone, with new worked examples. Condensed notes are in the [costs and cost behaviour revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-costs-and-cost-behaviour-revision-notes/) and you can test yourself with the [costs and cost behaviour practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-costs-and-cost-behaviour-practice/).

## What 2.1 asks you to do

The syllabus asks you to classify costs by **traceability** and **behaviour**, and to understand why costs matter for pricing and decisions. The five points of 2.1.1 are:

| Syllabus point | In this guide |
|---|---|
| Accounting for material and labour costs | Sections 3 and 4 |
| Identify and calculate fixed, variable, semi-variable and stepped costs | Section 2 |
| Identify and calculate the elements of direct and indirect costs | Section 1 |
| Closing inventory by FIFO and AVCO, perpetual and periodic | Section 4 |
| Principles of just in time (JIT) management of inventory | Section 5 |

You must also use this knowledge to evaluate information and make decisions, so most questions end with a judgement.

## 1. Building a cost from its elements

Traceability sorts each cost into **direct** (traced to a cost unit) or **indirect** (an overhead shared by many units). The elements build up in layers:

- **Prime cost** = direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses.
- **Production cost** = prime cost + production overheads.
- **Total cost** = production cost + non-production overheads (administration, selling and distribution).

**Worked example: a cost card.** Fenwick Lamps makes a brass desk lamp. Per lamp: brass parts 6.40, a fabric shade 3.10, 0.75 direct labour hours at 16.00 an hour, and a design royalty of 1.50. Production overheads are charged at 8.00 per direct labour hour, and non-production overheads are 4.50 per lamp. Fenwick prices at cost plus 20%.

| Element | $ |
|---|---|
| Direct materials (6.40 + 3.10) | 9.50 |
| Direct labour (0.75 × 16.00) | 12.00 |
| Direct expense (royalty) | 1.50 |
| **Prime cost** | **23.00** |
| Production overhead (0.75 × 8.00) | 6.00 |
| **Production cost** | **29.00** |
| Non-production overheads | 4.50 |
| **Total cost** | **33.50** |
| Mark-up 20% | 6.70 |
| **Selling price** | **40.20** |

The royalty is a **direct expense**: traceable to each lamp, though neither material nor labour. Closing inventory of lamps is valued at production cost (29.00), not total cost, because selling and administration costs do not belong to unsold goods.

## 2. Cost behaviour and the high-low method

Behaviour sorts costs by how they respond to a change in activity, within a **relevant range** of output:

| Behaviour | Total cost as output rises | Cost per unit as output rises |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Unchanged | Falls |
| Variable | Rises in proportion | Unchanged |
| Semi-variable | Rises, but not in proportion | Falls |
| Stepped | Unchanged, then jumps at a threshold | Falls between steps, rises at each step |

The per-unit column is where answers go wrong: rent of 18,000 a month is 6.00 a unit at 3,000 units but 4.00 at 4,500 units.

**Worked example: the high-low method with a misleading month.** Ardley Plastics records machine maintenance cost for four months:

| Month | Machine hours | Cost ($) |
|---|---|---|
| January | 1,800 | 9,940 |
| February | 2,600 | 12,180 |
| March | 1,200 | 8,260 |
| April | 2,200 | 12,400 |

April has the highest **cost**, because of a one-off repair, but February has the highest **activity**. The high-low method uses the highest and lowest **activity** levels, so use February and March.

- Variable cost per hour = (12,180 − 8,260) ÷ (2,600 − 1,200) = 3,920 ÷ 1,400 = **2.80**
- Fixed cost = 12,180 − (2,600 × 2.80) = 12,180 − 7,280 = **4,900** a month
- Forecast at 2,000 hours: 4,900 + 5,600 = **10,500**

January fits the line (9,940), but April does not (the line predicts 11,060), confirming the repair as unusual. Because the method uses only two points, one unusual month at either extreme would distort it.

**Stepped costs in a decision.** Ardley needs one inspector, paid 2,600 a month, per 1,500 units or part of 1,500. At 4,000 units three cost 7,800. An order taking output to 4,600 needs a fourth, so the extra 600 units carry 2,600 more inspection cost, about 4.33 a unit, which a price must cover.

## 3. Accounting for labour

Gross pay is split between **direct labour** (productive time on cost units) and **indirect labour** (overhead). Two items are usually treated as overheads:

- **Overtime premium**, the extra above the basic rate, unless a customer's request caused the overtime, when it is charged to that job.
- **Idle time**, paid hours when no production happens (machine breakdown, waiting for materials).

**Worked example.** A Fenwick assembler is paid 15.00 an hour with overtime at time-and-a-half. In one week she is paid for 44 hours, 6 of them overtime, and 3 hours were idle while a press was repaired.

- Basic pay 44 × 15.00 = 660; overtime premium 6 × 7.50 = 45; gross pay **705**
- Direct labour = productive hours at basic rate = 41 × 15.00 = **615**
- Indirect labour = idle time 3 × 15.00 + overtime premium 45 = **90**

**Piece rate** pays per unit produced. At 1.20 a unit with a guaranteed 480 a week, a worker making 380 units earns 456 but is paid 480; one making 450 units is paid 540. The guarantee makes labour behave as a semi-variable cost at low output.

## 4. Accounting for materials and valuing inventory

Materials issued to production are charged as direct materials if used in a cost unit and as indirect materials (overheads) if consumed generally, such as cleaning materials. The value placed on each issue depends on the inventory method, and the syllabus requires FIFO and AVCO, each **periodic** (valued once, at the period end) and **perpetual** (updated after every receipt and issue).

**Worked example with falling prices.** Ardley's records for one raw material in May:

| Date | Transaction | Units | Price ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 May | Opening inventory | 200 | 9.00 |
| 4 May | Purchase | 300 | 8.50 |
| 10 May | Issue | 350 | |
| 19 May | Purchase | 250 | 8.00 |
| 27 May | Issue | 280 | |

Goods available cost 1,800 + 2,550 + 2,000 = 6,350 for 750 units; closing inventory is 120 units.

- **FIFO.** Closing inventory is the newest 120 units: 120 × 8.00 = **960**. Perpetual and periodic FIFO always agree. Issues cost 6,350 − 960 = 5,390.
- **Periodic AVCO.** 6,350 ÷ 750 = 8.4667 a unit; 120 units = **1,016.00**.
- **Perpetual AVCO.** After 4 May: 500 units cost 4,350, average 8.70. The 10 May issue costs 350 × 8.70 = 3,045, leaving 150 units at 1,305. After 19 May: 400 units cost 3,305, average 8.2625. The 27 May issue costs 280 × 8.2625 = 2,313.50, leaving 120 units at **991.50**.

If the 630 units issued were sold for 8,820, gross profit is 3,430 under FIFO, 3,461.50 under perpetual AVCO and 3,486 under periodic AVCO. With **falling** prices FIFO gives the lowest closing inventory and the lowest profit, the reverse of the rising-price case. Over the life of the business total profit is the same; only its timing changes, so consistency matters once a method is chosen.

**Evaluating the choice.** FIFO puts closing inventory at recent prices but charges issues at older prices, which can mislead when quoting. AVCO smooths price changes and suits identical items mixed in storage, such as liquids, but perpetual AVCO needs a new calculation after every receipt.

## 5. Just in time (JIT)

JIT aims to receive materials only as production needs them, so inventory is close to zero. Its principles include small, frequent deliveries from a few reliable suppliers, production pulled by actual demand, and a focus on quality, because there is no buffer to cover faulty goods.

**Worked example: should Ardley adopt JIT?** Ardley buys 120,000 units a year at 5.00 and holds an average of 8,000 units. It estimates holding costs at 18% a year of the value held. Under JIT the average would fall to 1,000 units, but Ardley would lose a 2% bulk discount and pay 3,000 a year more in delivery charges.

- Holding cost now: 8,000 × 5.00 × 18% = 7,200; under JIT: 1,000 × 5.00 × 18% = 900; saving **6,300**
- Lost discount: 120,000 × 5.00 × 2% = **12,000**; extra delivery **3,000**
- Net effect: 6,300 − 12,000 − 3,000 = **8,700 worse** each year

On these figures Ardley should not adopt JIT. A good answer then weighs factors the numbers miss: less space and less risk of obsolete stock favour JIT; a single late delivery stopping the factory, and dependence on supplier reliability, count against it. The recommendation should follow from both.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the high and low **costs** instead of the high and low **activity** levels in the high-low method.
- Saying fixed cost per unit is constant. It is total fixed cost that stays the same.
- Valuing closing inventory of finished goods at total cost, including selling and administration costs.
- Charging all overtime pay as an overhead, rather than only the premium (and only when no job caused it).
- Forgetting to recalculate the average after each receipt in perpetual AVCO.
- Claiming FIFO always gives the higher profit: only when prices are rising.

## Exam technique for the 9706 command words

- **Calculate**: work out from the figures given. Show each step so method marks survive a slip.
- **Prepare**: present information in a suitable format, such as an inventory record with date, units, price and balance columns.
- **Explain**: say why or how, linking cost behaviour to its effect on price or profit.
- **Evaluate** and **Advise**: judge, then recommend. State the financial result, give developed points each way including non-financial factors, and end with the decision and the deciding reason.
