## 1. Meaning and significance of the five forces

An **industry** is a group of businesses supplying products customers see as close alternatives. **Porter's five forces** is a model for analysing an industry's competitive structure. The profit firms can earn depends on five pressures:

1. **Rivalry** between existing firms.
2. **Buyer power**: the ability of customers to push prices down or demand more for the same price.
3. **Supplier power**: the ability of suppliers of inputs to push their prices up or cut quality.
4. **Entry threat**: the likelihood that new firms join the industry.
5. **Substitute threat**: the likelihood that customers switch to a different product meeting the same need.

**Significance.** When the forces are strong, prices are held down or costs pushed up, so industry profit margins tend to be low. When they are weak, firms have more control over price. The model helps managers judge whether an industry is attractive to enter, stay in or leave, spot threats for a SWOT, and decide where action is needed.

The model analyses an **industry**. You then apply the result to the business in the case: strong buyer power may hurt a small firm more than a large rival with a famous brand.

## 2. Influences on the strength of each force

| Force | Stronger when... | Weaker when... |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | many similar-sized firms; slow market growth; little product differentiation; high fixed costs or spare capacity; high exit costs; low switching costs for customers | few firms or one clear leader; fast market growth; strong brands; customers face costs to switch |
| Buyer power | buyers are few and large, or buy a big share of the firm's output; products are standardised; buyers can compare prices easily; switching is cheap; buyers could make the product themselves | many small buyers; differentiated product; buyers lack price information; switching is costly |
| Supplier power | few suppliers; input is unique or patented; no substitute inputs; switching suppliers is costly; the industry is a minor customer for the supplier; suppliers could enter the industry themselves | many suppliers of a standard input; the industry is a major customer; inputs can be swapped easily |
| Entry threat | low barriers to entry: low entry costs, few legal requirements, little specialist knowledge, weak customer loyalty, easy access to resources and distribution | high barriers: large set-up costs or economies of scale, licences, specialist know-how, loyal customers, control of supplies or distribution channels |
| Substitute threat | close substitutes exist from other industries; they offer better value for money; switching is easy | no close substitutes; substitutes are dearer or worse; switching is costly or inconvenient |

**Rivals versus substitutes.** A rival sells the same type of product (another cement maker); a substitute meets the same need differently (steel or timber frames instead of concrete).

### Worked example 1: rating the forces in an industry

Granthorn Cement is one of the cement producers in a coastal province. The case gives this data:

- Market shares by volume: Granthorn 31%, three other producers 24%, 17% and 12%; the rest is supplied by several small importers.
- Granthorn sold 1.2 million tonnes last year. Its three largest customers, all national construction groups, bought 0.54 million tonnes.
- A new kiln costs hundreds of millions and needs an environmental permit.
- Energy is 35% of production cost and comes from a single gas supplier.
- Steel and timber frames are used in some buildings, but most foundations still need concrete.

**Step 1: calculate what you can.**

```
Four firm concentration ratio = 31 + 24 + 17 + 12 = 84%
Share of Granthorn's sales to top three buyers
  = 0.54 ÷ 1.2 × 100 = 45%
```

**Step 2: rate each force, with the evidence.**

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Medium | 84% concentration means few large rivals, but cement is a standard product, so rivals compete on price |
| Buyer power | High | Three buyers take 45% of Granthorn's sales; losing one would leave a large gap in output |
| Supplier power | High | One gas supplier; energy is over a third of costs |
| Entry threat | Low | Huge set-up cost and permits; importers are the main entry route |
| Substitute threat | Low to medium | Steel and timber replace concrete in some uses, not in foundations |

**Step 3: judge.** The two power forces squeeze Granthorn's margin from both sides, although weak entry and substitute threats keep the industry fairly attractive for existing producers.

## 3. Impact of the five forces on a business

Strong forces work through price, cost and volume. Rivalry, buyer power and substitutes limit **price**; supplier power raises **costs** and rivalry raises marketing spend; entrants and substitutes take **volume**. Lower margins then mean less retained profit for investment and lower shareholder returns.

Effects reach every functional area: **marketing** may differentiate to escape price competition, **operations** may cut unit costs or find new suppliers, **finance** may face tighter cash flow if powerful buyers demand longer credit, and **human resources** may face pressure on pay.

### Worked example 2: the impact of a stronger supplier

Saffron Crate sells meal kits for £9.40 a box. Variable cost is £6.10 a box, of which £0.80 is packaging. It sells 15,000 boxes a week and has fixed costs of £38,000 a week. Its packaging supplier, the only local maker of insulated boxes, raises its price by 45%.

```
New packaging cost   = 0.80 × 1.45 = £1.16 (rise of £0.36)
Old contribution     = 9.40 − 6.10 = £3.30 per box
New contribution     = 3.30 − 0.36 = £2.94 per box
Old weekly profit    = 15,000 × 3.30 − 38,000 = £11,500
New weekly profit    = 15,000 × 2.94 − 38,000 = £6,100
Fall in profit       = (11,500 − 6,100) ÷ 11,500 × 100 = 47.0%
```

Can Saffron Crate pass the cost on? Raising the price by £0.36 to £9.76 (a 3.8% rise) restores the £3.30 contribution, but customers can switch to rival kits, ready meals or takeaways. If volume falls 8% to 13,800 boxes:

```
Profit = 13,800 × 3.30 − 38,000 = £7,540
```

Better than £6,100, still well below £11,500: the supplier force cut profit, and the buyer and substitute forces limit the recovery.

## 4. How a business might influence the five forces

A business can act to weaken the forces it faces. Each action has a cost, so assess it, don't just list it.

| Force | Possible actions | Drawbacks to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Differentiate through brand, design or service; take over or merge with a rival; target a niche | Branding is costly and slow; takeovers need finance and may face competition law |
| Buyer power | Widen the customer base; add features or services that raise switching costs; loyalty schemes; sell direct to end users | New customers take time to win; loyalty schemes cut margins |
| Supplier power | Use several suppliers; sign long-term contracts; find substitute inputs; buy or set up a supplier (backward integration) | Smaller orders lose volume discounts; integration needs capital and new skills |
| Entry threat | Build economies of scale; protect ideas with patents; tie up distribution with exclusive deals; build strong loyalty | Large capacity adds fixed costs; exclusive deals may be challenged legally |
| Substitute threat | Show better value than the substitute; develop the product to meet the need better; bundle related products | Development spending is risky; substitutes may keep improving |

**Example of influence.** Granthorn could cut supplier power by adapting some kilns to a second fuel, and cut buyer power by winning smaller regional builders so its top three buyers take less of its sales. Both take time.

## 5. Responding to changes in the forces

Forces change: a new technology creates a substitute, suppliers merge, or a trade agreement lets importers in. A useful approach:

1. **Identify** which force has changed and in which direction.
2. **Measure** the likely effect on price, cost, volume and profit.
3. **List options:** defend (cut costs, lower prices, add value), adapt (move to a niche, change the product), combine (merge, form an alliance), or exit.
4. **Judge** using the firm's resources, objectives and how long the change will last.

### Worked example 3: responding to a stronger entry threat

A trade agreement removes import tariffs on cement, so overseas producers can sell in Granthorn's province more cheaply. The entry threat rises.

- **Cut price to match imports:** keeps volume but cuts margin; works only if unit costs are low enough.
- **Compete on reliability:** local plants can promise next-day delivery, which importers struggle to copy.
- **Long-term contracts with major buyers:** locks in volume, but powerful buyers will demand lower prices in return.
- **Drop basic grades:** leave the cheapest segment to imports and focus on specialist mixes.

**Judgement.** Price cutting alone risks a price war that weakens all local producers. Reliable supply plus specialist grades is stronger because it avoids head-on price competition. The choice depends on Granthorn's costs compared with importers and how long the tariff change lasts.

## Evaluating the five forces model

**Strengths:** simple, structured, covers suppliers and substitutes that a quick look at rivals would miss, and links clearly to profit.

**Limitations:** it is a snapshot of a changing industry; boundaries are hard to draw (is a meal-kit firm in food retail or delivery?); the forces are judged, not measured; firms within one industry differ in size, brand and cost; and it should be used with [PESTLE analysis (3.3.4)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-analysing-the-external-environment-to-assess/), because laws, the economy and technology shift the forces.

## Common errors

- Treating the model as an analysis of one firm's strengths. It analyses the industry; you then apply it to the firm.
- Confusing substitutes with rival products of the same type.
- Saying "buyer power is high" with no evidence from the case, such as buyer concentration or switching costs.
- Listing ways to influence a force without the cost, time or risk of each.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement on which force matters most for this business.
