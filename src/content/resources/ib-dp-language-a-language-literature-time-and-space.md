This guide covers **Time and Space**, one of three syllabus components
for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and Literature, first
assessment 2021. The [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/)
confirms all three components carry equal teaching hours (50 SL / 80
HL each).

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Time and Space is not examined as a separate, named section on any
paper — like the other two syllabus components, its concepts are woven
into Paper 1's guided textual analysis and Paper 2's comparative essay
rather than tested in isolation. This means revision should focus on
being able to apply its production/reception framework fluently to
whatever text appears in the exam, rather than memorising Time and
Space as a standalone content block disconnected from actual exam
tasks.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP LANGUAGE A: LANGUAGE AND LITERATURE — TIME AND SPACE**

The component's central question is not "what happened in this text"
but how the **context of production and reception** shapes meaning —
the historical period, culture and place a text was written in, and
the historical period, culture and place it is now being read in, are
not the same thing, and both matter to how meaning is made. Because
the course studies both literary and non-literary texts under every
component, the same Time and Space questions apply equally to both
text types: for a literary work, this means asking not just what
historical period it depicts, but how its original readers would have
understood references, values or assumptions that a modern reader
might miss or misread; for a non-literary text, this means asking how
the same event or issue might be represented differently by different
media, institutions, or audiences at a different moment.

## How to approach it

The skill this component specifically rewards is identifying a shift
between original and current reception — not simply describing a
text's context once and moving on, and not discussing only the
original context while ignoring how a reader today receives the text
differently. For every text studied under this component, keep
separate short notes on its production context and a distinct set of
notes on how reception today might differ from that original context;
conflating the two into a single "background" note is a common source
of vague answers. Because the individual oral connects a literary work
and a non-literary body of work through a global issue, this
component's vocabulary of production and reception context is a
natural source of material for that assessment too — a strong oral
commentary can explicitly discuss how the chosen global issue is
understood differently now than when either text was produced.

## Worked example: applying Time and Space to a non-literary text

A 19th-century newspaper editorial on a public health issue, read
alongside a modern public-health campaign leaflet on a comparable
topic.

```
Production context (editorial):  written for a literate, middle-
                                  class 19th-century readership;
                                  assumes formal register and the
                                  authority of the writer's own
                                  opinion is unquestioned

Production context (leaflet):    written for a broad, general modern
                                  audience; assumes visual literacy,
                                  short attention span, direct
                                  address ("you")

Reception today:                  a modern reader may find the
                                   editorial's tone paternalistic
                                   where its original readers would
                                   have found it authoritative -- this
                                   SHIFT in reception is itself the
                                   analytical point, not just a
                                   description of two texts' differing
                                   styles
```

Identifying the shift between original and current reception, rather
than only describing two texts side by side, is what distinguishes a
strong Time and Space analysis.

## Applying the same lens to a literary text

Because it can feel more natural to discuss "context" for a
non-literary text (a newspaper, an advertisement, a speech) than for a
literary one, deliberately practise the same move on a novel, play or
poem: identify one assumption its original readers would have held
without question -- about social class, gender roles, religion, or
everyday life -- that a contemporary reader is likely to notice, question,
or misread. A 19th-century novel's assumptions about social class, for
instance, may read very differently to a contemporary audience than
they did to its first readers, and identifying that specific gap, with
a concrete textual example, is exactly the analytical move this
component rewards -- a generalised statement that "attitudes have
changed since this was written" does not, on its own, demonstrate the
skill.

## Common mistakes

Treating "context" as background information mentioned once, rather
than as something that actively shapes how meaning is made and
received. Discussing only a text's original context and ignoring how
a reader today receives it differently. Applying this component's
ideas only to literary texts, when the course's dual
literary/non-literary structure expects the same analytical questions
to apply to both text types equally. Treating Time and Space as a
memorisable content block, when it is instead a framework applied
fluently to whatever text a Paper 1 or Paper 2 question presents.

## Quick revision checklist

- For every text studied under this component, identify both its
  production context and a distinct reception context today.
- Practise applying the "how might this be represented differently at
  a different time or place" question to a non-literary text type not
  yet discussed in class.
- Keep production-context and reception-context notes genuinely
  separate for each text.
- Practise identifying a specific shift in reception, not just
  describing a text's original context.
- Use this component's vocabulary directly when preparing the
  individual oral's discussion of a global issue.
