## Why the tropics weather rock so deeply

Chemical weathering needs water, and reaction rates increase with temperature. A common rule of thumb is that the rate of a chemical reaction roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.

**Worked example.** *Compare a temperate site with a mean temperature of 10 °C with a humid tropical site at 27 °C.*

- Difference = 27 − 10 = 17 °C, which is 1.7 lots of 10 °C.
- Rate factor = 2^1.7 ≈ **3.2**. On temperature alone, reactions run about three times faster.

Add heavy rain that keeps rock permanently moist, abundant organic acids from rapid decay, and long periods of tectonic stability without glaciation, and weathering can reach tens of metres below the surface.

## Granite and its weathering profile

Granite is made of quartz, feldspar and mica. **Hydrolysis** attacks the feldspar: hydrogen ions in water replace potassium in the mineral, which breaks down to kaolinite clay, releasing silica and potassium in solution. In simplified form:

> orthoclase feldspar + water + hydrogen ions → kaolinite + dissolved silica + potassium ions

Mica (biotite) is also altered, partly by **oxidation** of its iron. Quartz is very resistant and survives as sand grains. The rotted rock is called **saprolite** (or grus where it is sandy).

Water enters the rock along **joints**, so weathering follows them. A typical deep weathering profile, from top to bottom:

| Zone | Description |
|---|---|
| Soil | Leached, iron-rich, often red |
| Saprolite | Rotted rock that keeps the original rock structure but can be dug with a spade |
| Corestone zone | Rounded blocks of fresh granite surrounded by saprolite, largest where joints are widely spaced |
| Weathering front | Sharp boundary between rotted and fresh rock |
| Bedrock | Unweathered granite |

The weathering front is uneven. It dips deeply where joints are close together and stays high where the rock is massive. This **irregular weathering front** is the key to all three granite landforms.

## Tors

A tor is a pile of rounded granite blocks standing above the surrounding surface.

**Two-stage (deep weathering) model.** D. L. Linton (1955) proposed that:

1. Under warm, humid conditions, deep chemical weathering rotted closely jointed granite to saprolite, while widely jointed areas survived as corestones.
2. A later change, such as uplift, a fall in base level or a drier or colder climate, allowed erosion to strip away the loose saprolite. The corestones were left exposed as tors.

**Alternative: one-stage periglacial model.** For the tors of Dartmoor in south-west England, Palmer and Nielson (1962) argued that frost shattering and solifluction under cold conditions could produce tors directly, without earlier deep weathering.

**Evaluation.** Both models may be right in different places, an example of **equifinality**: similar landforms produced by different processes. In the tropics, the deep weathering model fits evidence such as corestones exposed in road cuttings and the presence of tors on stripped weathering fronts, as in the kopjes of southern Africa.

## Inselbergs and bornhardts

An **inselberg** is any isolated hill rising abruptly from a plain. Several types occur on granite:

- **Bornhardts:** large, dome-shaped, steep-sided hills of bare rock.
- **Castle koppies (nubbins):** smaller hills covered with blocks and boulders, often thought to form when a bornhardt breaks down along its joints.
- **Tors:** the smallest scale.

Not all inselbergs are granite: Uluru in central Australia is made of arkose, a feldspar-rich sandstone.

**Why bornhardts are domes.** Massive granite with very few joints resists weathering. When the overlying rock is removed, the release of pressure produces **sheeting joints** parallel to the surface. Slabs peel away along these joints (exfoliation), keeping the rounded, dome shape.

**Two explanations for their isolation:**

| Model | Process | Evidence for | Evidence against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Etching (deep weathering and stripping) | Massive granite survives while surrounding jointed rock is rotted, then saprolite is stripped | Domes emerging from saprolite; fresh rock at the base of many inselbergs | Does not explain inselbergs on rocks with no deep weathering |
| Scarp retreat (pediplanation) | Slopes retreat parallel to themselves across a plain, leaving remnants | Gently sloping pediments at the foot of some inselbergs | Does not explain why remnants are often on the most massive rock |

Many inselbergs may have formed over tens of millions of years, through several cycles of weathering and stripping under changing climates. Answers that recognise this long timescale reach higher levels.

## Tropical karst

Limestone is mainly calcium carbonate. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and, much more importantly in the tropics, from the soil, where plant roots and microbes release it. The weak carbonic acid dissolves limestone by **carbonation**:

> calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide → calcium hydrogen carbonate (in solution)

Carbon dioxide dissolves less well in warm water than in cold, which would slow solution. In the humid tropics this is outweighed by very high soil carbon dioxide and very large volumes of rainfall, so solution is rapid.

### Cockpit karst

Water drains into the rock at points where joints intersect, enlarging them into enclosed hollows (dolines). Hollows grow and merge into star-shaped **cockpits** separated by rounded residual hills and ridges. Drainage is underground. **Example:** Cockpit Country, Jamaica.

### Cone karst (fengcong)

Conical or hemispherical hills sharing a common rocky base, separated by closed depressions. Cockpit karst is a form of cone karst in which the hollows are the most striking feature. **Examples:** much of southern China; the Chocolate Hills of Bohol, Philippines.

### Tower karst (fenglin)

Isolated, steep-sided towers rising from a flat alluvial plain. **Examples:** Guilin, southern China; Ha Long Bay, Vietnam, where tower and cone karst have been drowned by the sea; the mogotes of the Viñales valley, Cuba.

How towers form:

1. Solution lowers the depressions of cone karst until they reach the **water table**.
2. Rivers and swamps then spread alluvium across the floor, forming a plain.
3. Water at the base of the hills, rich in carbon dioxide from decaying vegetation, dissolves the foot of each hill, cutting **foot caves** and notches.
4. The sides are undercut and collapse, so the hills become steep-walled towers.

**Evaluation.** The sequence cone karst to tower karst is widely taught, but it is not universal. Tower karst may also develop where uplift is slow and the water table is close to the surface, while cone karst forms where uplift is faster and the depressions cannot reach the water table. Rock purity, joint pattern and thickness also matter. Some tower karst, as in parts of China, is partly inherited from warmer, wetter climates of the past.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain the formation of tower karst. [6]*

> In humid tropical areas, rain charged with soil carbon dioxide dissolves limestone along joints (**process**). Depressions are lowered until they reach the water table (**stage 1**). Rivers then deposit alluvium, forming a flat plain between the remaining hills (**stage 2**). Water at the foot of each hill is acidic from rotting vegetation, so it dissolves the base, cutting foot caves and notches (**stage 3: undercutting**). The undercut walls collapse, giving steep-sided, isolated towers (**result**), as at Guilin in southern China (**example**).

## Common mistakes

- Saying granite weathers by "freeze-thaw" in the tropics. The dominant process is hydrolysis.
- Forgetting the role of joint spacing, which explains why some rock survives.
- Calling all inselbergs bornhardts.
- Describing karst types without explaining how they form.
- Ignoring the alternative explanations; essays need evaluation, and equifinality is a strong point.
- Assuming high temperature alone speeds limestone solution; carbon dioxide from the soil matters more.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the formation** questions need a sequence: conditions, process, stages, result, and an example.
- **Draw** a labelled diagram where it saves words: a weathering profile, or a cross-section of cone and tower karst. Integrate it into the answer.
- **Evaluate** or **To what extent** essays on whether landforms reflect present climate: use deep weathering profiles, tors and tower karst that may be relict.
