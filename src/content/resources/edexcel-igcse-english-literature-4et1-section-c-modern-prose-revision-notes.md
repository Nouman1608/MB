## The task in one box

| Feature | Section C |
|---|---|
| Text | One novel from the modern prose list, **not** available in the exam |
| Choice | One question from two on your novel |
| Marks | 40 (the largest tariff on Paper 1) |
| Assessment objectives | AO1 (knowledge, argument, personal engagement) and AO4 (context) |
| Recommended time | 45 minutes |

**Set novels:** *To Kill a Mockingbird*, *Of Mice and Men*, *The Whale Rider*, *The Joy Luck Club*,
*Things Fall Apart*, *Klara and the Sun*, *Western Lane*. Set texts change with the specification
issue, so confirm yours.

## What the objectives mean here

AO2 is not one of the objectives for this section. Comments on narrative voice, structure or imagery
still help, but only when they push your argument forward (AO1) or show why a writer, in a particular
time and place, made a choice (AO4). A list of techniques with no argument earns little.

## The set novels: where to look and what context explains

| Novel | Structure and key moments | Context that explains choices | Themes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *To Kill a Mockingbird* (Harper Lee) | Part One: childhood games and the mystery of Boo Radley; Part Two: Tom Robinson's trial (Chapters 17 to 21), Bob Ewell's attack (Chapter 28), Scout on the Radley porch (Chapter 31) | Set in 1930s Alabama under segregation and the Depression; published in 1960 during the civil rights movement | Prejudice, justice, courage, growing up, empathy |
| *Of Mice and Men* (John Steinbeck) | Six sections; opens and closes at the pool by the river; Candy's dog is shot (section 3); Crooks's room (section 4); the death of Curley's wife (section 5) | Migrant ranch workers in 1930s California with no security; racial segregation; limited roles for women | Loneliness, dreams, friendship, power, the powerless |
| *The Whale Rider* (Witi Ihimaera) | Kahu's birth disappoints her great-grandfather Koro Apirana; she retrieves the stone from the sea; the whales are stranded; she rides the ancient whale | Māori tradition at Whangara, the legend of the ancestor Paikea, and male succession to leadership | Tradition and change, gender, belonging, humans and nature |
| *The Joy Luck Club* (Amy Tan) | Sixteen stories in four sections, with the mothers' sections framing the daughters'; Jing-mei takes her late mother's place at the mah jong table and travels to China at the end | Chinese mothers who emigrated to the USA and their American-born daughters; women's lack of choice in the mothers' early lives in China | Mothers and daughters, identity, cultural inheritance, storytelling |
| *Things Fall Apart* (Chinua Achebe) | Part One: Okonkwo in Umuofia and the killing of Ikemefuna; Part Two: exile in Mbanta as missionaries arrive; Part Three: return, conflict with church and colonial government, Okonkwo's death | Igbo society before and during British colonial rule; published in 1958 as a response to European portrayals of Africa | Masculinity and fear of weakness, tradition, colonialism, change |
| *Klara and the Sun* (Kazuo Ishiguro) | Klara waiting in the shop window; her life with the sick child Josie; her bargain with the Sun; the plan for her to take Josie's place; the final scene in the yard | Published in 2021 amid debates about artificial intelligence and genetic enhancement ("lifted" children) | Love, loneliness, what makes a person, hope and faith |
| *Western Lane* (Chetna Maroo) | Eleven-year-old Gopi narrates the months after her mother's death, as her father trains her at squash | A British Gujarati family, with grief carried through routine, sport and silence rather than speech | Grief, family, discipline, sisterhood, what goes unsaid |

## The four-moment plan

| Moment | What to show | Context question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | How the focus is first established | What was normal in this society? |
| Turning point | The event that tests or changes it | What pressures make this happen? |
| Development | Consequences and reactions | How do other characters reflect their society? |
| Ending | Where the writer leaves it | What does the writer want readers of the time, or now, to think? |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning in Section C |
|---|---|
| AO1 | A clear, sustained argument answering the question, with precise reference across the novel |
| AO4 | How the contexts of writing and setting shape the text's meaning |
| Overview | Your answer to the question in one or two opening sentences |
| Textual reference | A short quotation or a precise paraphrase of a specific moment |
| Embedded quotation | A quotation woven into your own sentence |
| Narrative voice | Who tells the story (first person, retrospective, an unusual narrator) and how that shapes what we see |
| Structure | The order of events: openings, turning points, endings, circular patterns |

## The quotation bank

- Fifteen to twenty short quotations for your novel.
- For each: where it appears, who says it, which themes and characters it can serve.
- Practise writing them from memory; an accurate paraphrase beats a misquotation.

## Context that works

- Name the specific condition (segregation, economic depression, colonial rule, migration, a debate
  about technology), not "the time".
- Link it to one moment in the text in the same sentence.
- Use it to explain **why** the writer shows something.

## Annotated model paragraph

Question: *How does Achebe present the effects of colonial rule in* Things Fall Apart*?*

> Achebe ends the novel by showing that colonial rule changes not only who governs Umuofia but who
> tells its story. **[AO1: argument, not plot]** In the final paragraph the narration moves into the
> mind of the District Commissioner, who plans to give Okonkwo's life no more than a paragraph in
> his book on "pacification". **[AO1: precise reference through paraphrase and one remembered word]**
> The shift feels abrupt because the reader has spent almost the whole novel inside Igbo society and knows the complexity he is about to reduce. **[structure used to support the argument]**
> Writing in 1958, when European accounts of Africa often presented its peoples as having no history
> of their own, Achebe shows the colonial version as partial and dismissive, so the novel itself
> becomes the fuller account the Commissioner will never write. **[AO4: context explains the
> writer's choice in the same sentence]**

Notice that the context sentence would be pointless without the moment it explains, and the moment
would be plot summary without the context.

## Building a quotation bank that travels

- Choose words that serve **two or more** themes: a single word linked to fear, a phrase about home.
- Tag each with a section, part or chapter so you can place it in your argument's structure.
- Pair each with a context note, so the AO4 link is ready before the exam.

## Traps

- Plot summary instead of argument.
- A history paragraph at the end.
- Discussing one scene instead of the whole novel.
- Long, inaccurate quotations.
- Arriving at Section C with too little time.
- Mixing in details from a film adaptation that the novel does not contain.
- Treating a first-person narrator, such as Scout, Klara or Gopi, as if they understood everything
  they describe.
- Using context that belongs to the wrong time frame: the setting and the time of writing can differ,
  as in *To Kill a Mockingbird*.

## Self-test

1. Why does Section C demand a quotation bank when Sections A and B do not?
2. Which two assessment objectives are tested, and what does each require?
3. Rewrite as a stronger AO4 sentence: "People back then were unfair to workers."
4. What four moments make a reliable essay plan?
5. Which is better in the exam: a misremembered long quotation or a precise paraphrase?
6. Is AO2 assessed in Section C? How should you treat the writer's methods?
7. Name the two time frames that matter for *To Kill a Mockingbird*.
8. Which three moments from *Of Mice and Men* would support an essay on the powerless?
9. How is *Things Fall Apart* divided, and what happens to Okonkwo in Part Two?
10. Who narrates *Klara and the Sun*, and why does that matter for an argument about love?
11. Why should each quotation in your bank carry a context note?

**Answers:** 1. It is closed book: the novel is not available, whereas the unseen and anthology poems
are on or with the paper. 2. AO1, a sustained, informed argument with close reference to the text;
AO4, understanding of how the text relates to its contexts. 3. For example: "Because migrant
labourers in the Depression had no job security, Steinbeck shows Candy's fear of being sent away once
he can no longer work as a realistic, not exaggerated, anxiety." 4. Opening, turning point,
development, ending. 5. A precise paraphrase: it is accurate textual reference, while a misquotation
undermines your knowledge. 6. No; methods earn credit only when they support the argument or are
explained through context. 7. The 1930s Alabama setting and the time of writing, published in 1960.
8. For example: Candy's dog being shot (section 3), Crooks's room (section 4) and the death of Curley's
wife (section 5). 9. Three parts; in Part Two Okonkwo is in exile in Mbanta, where the missionaries
arrive. 10. Klara, an artificial "friend"; because she observes human love from outside, the reader
must judge how far her devotion is itself a kind of love. 11. So that AO4 can be linked to a specific
moment in the same sentence, rather than added as a separate paragraph.
