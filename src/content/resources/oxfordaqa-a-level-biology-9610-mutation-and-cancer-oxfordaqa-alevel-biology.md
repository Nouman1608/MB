## Mutations and mutagens

A gene mutation is any change in the base sequence of DNA. Mutations arise spontaneously during DNA replication, when an incorrect base is occasionally inserted or a base is missed. **Mutagens** increase the frequency of mutations. Physical mutagens include ultraviolet light and ionising radiation such as X-rays; chemical mutagens include some substances in tobacco smoke.

Mutations in body cells are not inherited by offspring, but they are copied to every daughter cell produced by mitosis from the mutated cell. That is how one mutation can affect a whole tumour.

## Predicting the outcome: a supplied genetic code

Questions give you the codons you need. The method:

1. Write the original sequence in triplets and translate it.
2. Make the change exactly as described.
3. Re-divide the new sequence into triplets **from the original start point**.
4. Translate again and compare.

**Worked example 1.** Part of an mRNA molecule reads, from the start codon:

AUG UUC GGA CAU UGG AAA UAA

Supplied codons: AUG Met; UUC and UUU Phe; GGA and GGU Gly; CAU His; CGU Arg; UGG Trp; AAA Lys; UCG Ser; GAC Asp; AUU Ile; AAU Asn; UAA and UGA stop.

Original polypeptide: Met-Phe-Gly-His-Trp-Lys.

| Mutation (in the DNA, shown as the change to the mRNA) | New codons | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution: third base of codon 3, GGA → GGU | Gly is still coded | **No change** to the polypeptide, because the code is degenerate |
| Substitution: second base of codon 4, CAU → CGU | Arg instead of His | **One amino acid changes**; the rest are the same |
| Substitution: third base of codon 5, UGG → UGA | Stop | **Chain ends early**: Met-Phe-Gly-His |
| Deletion of the fourth base (the first U of UUC) | AUG UCG GAC AUU GGA AAU AA... | **Every amino acid after Met changes**: Met-Ser-Asp-Ile-Gly-Asn, and the original stop codon is no longer read |

A substitution affects at most one codon. A deletion shifts the reading frame, changing every triplet after it, so it usually has a much larger effect.

**From sequence to function.** A change in amino acid sequence changes the primary structure of the protein. This may change the way the chain folds, and so its tertiary structure. If the protein is an enzyme, the shape of its active site may change so that the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme stops working. Whether one amino acid change matters depends on where it is and how different the new R group is.

## Benign and malignant tumours

A tumour is a mass of cells produced by uncontrolled cell division.

| Characteristic | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
|---|---|---|
| Rate of growth | Usually slow | Usually rapid |
| Boundary | Often enclosed in a capsule; stays compact | No capsule; grows into surrounding tissues |
| Spread | Does not spread to other parts of the body | Cells can break away and spread in blood or lymph, forming secondary tumours |
| Cell appearance | Cells resemble the normal cells of the tissue | Cells look less like normal cells (less specialised), often with larger nuclei |
| Effect on health | Usually less serious, but can press on organs or block vessels | Often life-threatening because it invades and spreads |

"Benign" does not mean harmless: a benign tumour in the brain, for example, can cause damage by pressure.

## Two groups of genes that control division

Think of cell division as a car: proto-oncogenes are the accelerator, and tumour suppressor genes are the brake.

- **Proto-oncogenes** code for proteins that stimulate cell division when the cell receives the right signal.
- **Tumour suppressor genes** code for proteins that slow cell division.

Two kinds of mutation lead towards cancer:

1. A mutation turns a proto-oncogene into an **oncogene**. The oncogene stimulates cells to divide too quickly: the accelerator is stuck down.
2. A mutation **inactivates** a tumour suppressor gene. Its protein no longer slows division, so the rate of division increases: the brake has failed.

In both cases, the mutated cell divides more than it should, and all its daughter cells carry the same mutation. Usually several mutations build up in one cell line before a malignant tumour develops, which is one reason why cancer risk rises with age and with exposure to mutagens.

## Analysing risk-factor data for cancers

**Worked example 2 (illustrative data).** A study recorded new cases of a skin cancer per 100 000 adults per year, by average hours of strong sun exposure per day.

| Daily sun exposure | Under 1 hour | 1 to 3 hours | Over 3 hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cases per 100 000 per year | 6.0 | 9.6 | 15.0 |

1. Over 3 hours against under 1 hour: 15.0 ÷ 6.0 = 2.5 times the incidence.
2. Percentage increase from the lowest to the middle group: (9.6 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = 60%.
3. Pattern: incidence rises as exposure rises, a positive correlation.
4. Evaluation: there is a plausible mechanism, because ultraviolet light is a mutagen. But other variables, such as skin type, age and use of sunscreen, may differ between the groups; exposure may be self-reported and inaccurate. The data support a link but do not on their own prove that sun exposure causes the cancer.

## Anticancer drugs and the cell cycle

Many anticancer drugs act on dividing cells. Relate each to a stage of the cycle:

| Drug action (as a question might describe it) | Stage affected | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Stops DNA replication | S phase | Cells cannot copy their DNA, so they cannot complete a cycle and divide |
| Prevents spindle fibres forming | Mitosis (metaphase to anaphase) | Chromatids cannot be separated, so cells cannot divide |

Cancer cells divide frequently, so a larger proportion of them are in these stages at any moment and they are affected more. Normal cells that also divide often, such as those in hair follicles and the lining of the gut, are affected too, which explains side effects such as hair loss. Doses are given in cycles so that normal tissues have time to recover between treatments.

**Worked example 3 (illustrative data).** A drug that prevents spindle formation is added to dividing cancer cells. Before treatment, 8% of cells are in mitosis. After 12 hours, 27% are in mitosis, almost all with condensed chromosomes and no anaphase figures. Explanation: cells enter mitosis but cannot pass beyond metaphase without a spindle, so they accumulate there, and no new daughter cells are produced.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how a mutation in a tumour suppressor gene can lead to the formation of a tumour. [4 marks]

**Answer:** Tumour suppressor genes code for proteins that slow cell division (1). A mutation changes the base sequence, so the protein has a different amino acid sequence and shape and no longer works: the gene is inactivated (1). Cell division is no longer slowed, so the rate of division increases (1). The mutated cell and its daughter cells divide uncontrollably, forming a mass of cells: a tumour (1).

*Why it scores:* it states the normal role, the effect of the mutation on the protein, the change in division rate and the outcome. Answers that say "the gene stops cancer" without naming its role in slowing division lose the first mark.

## Common mistakes

- Saying all mutations change the protein. The degenerate code means some substitutions do not.
- Re-dividing into triplets from the point of the mutation rather than from the start codon.
- Saying a deletion changes "one amino acid". It shifts the reading frame.
- Confusing oncogenes with tumour suppressor genes, or saying a mutated tumour suppressor gene is "overactive".
- Describing benign tumours as harmless.
- Saying anticancer drugs target only cancer cells.

## Exam technique

- **Predict** mutation outcomes: show the new triplets and the new amino acid sequence.
- **Explain** cancer formation: normal gene role, mutation, change in division rate, tumour.
- **Compare** tumours: paired statements on growth, spread and cell appearance.
- **Evaluate** risk data: trend, size of effect, confounding variables, mechanism.
- **Relate** drugs to the cycle: name the stage and the consequence for division.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a base deletion usually have a larger effect than a substitution?**
A deletion shifts the reading frame, so every triplet after it changes. A substitution changes only one codon, and because the code is degenerate it may not change the amino acid at all.

**What is the difference between a proto-oncogene and an oncogene?**
A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that stimulates cell division. An oncogene is a mutated proto-oncogene that stimulates cells to divide too quickly.
