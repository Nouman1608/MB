## Short-answer questions

Cambridge IGCSE Accounting 0452 question papers are not divided into lettered sections, so the questions below are grouped only by length.

**1.** The owner of a shop pays the electricity bill for her home from the business bank account. It is recorded as drawings. Which concept is applied?

A: Business entity

B: Duality

C: Matching

D: Money measurement

**[1]**

**2.** Which concept requires inventory to be valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value?

A: Consistency

B: Historic cost

C: Prudence

D: Realisation

**[1]**

**3.** Name the accounting concept applied in each situation.

**(a)** A business with a year end of 30 June records the wages owing for the last week of June. **[1]**

**(b)** A building is shown at the $90,000 paid for it, although it is now worth $140,000. **[1]**

**(c)** A calculator costing $12 that will last several years is charged as an expense. **[1]**

**(d)** The business's good reputation is not recorded as an asset. **[1]**

**4.** Rent of $7,200 was paid on 1 April for the following 12 months. The year ends on 31 December. Calculate the rent expense for the year and the amount prepaid at the year end. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the going concern concept and give **one** situation in which it would not apply. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a business should not change its depreciation method every year. **[2]**

## Longer questions

**7.** Wexcombe Hardware has a draft profit for the year ended 31 December of $38,500. The following have not been dealt with correctly.

1. The owner's son's school fees of $1,400 were paid from the business bank account and debited to general expenses.
2. Commission of $2,700 was received on 1 October for the nine months to 30 June. All of it was credited to commission received.
3. Insurance of $2,400 was paid on 1 July for 12 months and charged in full as an expense.
4. Closing inventory includes items that cost $1,950 and can be sold for only $1,200.
5. Goods worth $2,250 ordered by a customer on 30 December, for delivery in January, were recorded as a credit sale.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. Show the effect of each item. **[6]**

**(b)** Name the accounting concept that applies to item 2 and to item 4. **[2]**

**8.** A machine cost $30,000 two years ago and is depreciated at 20% per year using the reducing balance method. The owner has now decided to close the business next month. A dealer will pay $8,000 for the machine.

**(a)** Calculate the net book value of the machine. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain which value is now more relevant for the machine, naming the concept involved. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the loss that would be recognised if the machine is shown at the dealer's price. **[1]**

**9.** State the effect of each transaction on the business's total assets, liabilities and capital.

**(a)** Inventory of $2,000 is bought on credit. **[1]**

**(b)** The owner brings her own van, worth $9,000, into the business. **[1]**

**(c)** A supplier is paid $1,500 by bank transfer. **[1]**

**(d)** The owner takes $400 cash for personal use. **[1]**

**10.** Imran's business has machinery that cost $60,000 two years ago. It has been depreciated at 20% a year on the reducing balance, so its net book value is $38,400 at the start of this year. Imran is applying for a bank loan and proposes to switch this year to straight line depreciation at 10% of cost, because it gives a lower charge. Evaluate Imran's proposal. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how the prudence concept affects each of the following.

**(a)** Inventory that cost $500 and is expected to sell for $800. **[2]**

**(b)** A customer who owes $800 and is unlikely to pay. **[2]**

**12.** Pellow Bakery's financial statements do not show the value of its highly skilled bakers, and it charges all items costing under $100 to expenses. Discuss whether the money measurement and materiality concepts make Pellow's statements less useful. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A [1]. The owner's private spending is not a business expense. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Prudence: do not overstate assets or profit. **[1]**

**3. (a)** Matching / accruals [1].

**(b)** Historic cost [1].

**(c)** Materiality [1].

**(d)** Money measurement [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Expense 7,200 × 9/12 = **$5,400** [1]. Prepaid 7,200 × 3/12 = **$1,800** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The business is assumed to continue trading for the foreseeable future, so assets are shown at cost less depreciation rather than what they would sell for [1]. It would not apply if the business is about to close or cannot continue, for example if the owner has decided to stop trading [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The consistency concept requires the same methods to be used each period [1]. Changing the method changes profit even when trading is unchanged, so users could not compare results from year to year [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Draft profit | 38,500 |
| 1. School fees are drawings | + 1,400 [1] |
| 2. Commission: only 2,700 × 3/9 = 900 earned; in advance 1,800 | − 1,800 [1] |
| 3. Insurance prepaid 2,400 × 6/12 | + 1,200 [1] |
| 4. Inventory written down 1,950 − 1,200 | − 750 [1] |
| 5. Sale not yet made | − 2,250 [1] |
| Corrected profit | **36,300** [1] |

**(b)** Item 2: matching / accruals (realisation also accepted) [1]. Item 4: prudence [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** Year 1: 30,000 × 20% = 6,000, leaving 24,000; year 2: 24,000 × 20% = 4,800 [1]; net book value **$19,200** [1].

**(b)** The going concern assumption no longer holds because the business will close [1], so the expected sale value of $8,000 is more relevant than the book value based on historic cost [1].

**(c)** 19,200 − 8,000 = **$11,200** [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Assets + $2,000; liabilities + $2,000; capital unchanged [1].

**(b)** Assets + $9,000; capital + $9,000; liabilities unchanged [1].

**(c)** Assets − $1,500; liabilities − $1,500; capital unchanged [1].

**(d)** Assets − $400; capital − $400 (drawings); liabilities unchanged [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Reducing balance charge this year: 38,400 × 20% = $7,680 [1]. Straight line charge: 60,000 × 10% = $6,000, so profit would be $1,680 higher [1]. The change breaks the consistency concept, because trading has not changed, yet profit would rise [1]. Changing method to make profit look better for the bank would mislead the lender [1]. A change of method is acceptable only if it gives a fairer view of how the machinery is used, not to raise profit [1]. Conclusion: Imran should keep the reducing balance method; the proposal should be rejected [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** The inventory stays at its cost of $500 [1]; the expected gain of $300 is not recorded until the goods are sold, so profit is not overstated [1].

**(b)** The expected loss is recognised now [1], by writing off the debt or making an allowance for it, so trade receivables and profit are not overstated [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Money measurement: the bakers' skill affects quality and future sales, but it cannot be reliably valued, so it is left out [1]. Users may therefore underestimate Pellow's strengths [1]. However, recording a value that cannot be measured reliably would make the statements less reliable [1].

Materiality: charging items under $100 as expenses saves time and has little effect on profit for a business of Pellow's size [1]. It makes the statements less useful only if the limit is too high compared with the size of the business, or if it is not applied consistently [1].

Conclusion: both concepts slightly limit what the statements show, but they make the figures more reliable and practical, so overall they do not make the statements much less useful; users should seek non-financial information alongside them [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a concept without linking it to the figures in the case.
- Counting the months used instead of the months still to come for income received in advance.
- Treating a private payment as a business expense.
- Saying prudence means understating profit.
- In evaluation answers, giving no conclusion.
