This guide covers **3.3.2 Gas exchange** from AQA AS and A-level Biology (7401/7402), in Topic 3, *Organisms exchange substances with their environment*. It is AS content, examined on both AS papers and on A-level Papers 1 and 3, and it is one of the sub-topics where required practical 5 (dissection of a gas exchange or mass transport system, or of an organ within one) can be set. The topic overview is in the [exchange with the environment guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-exchange-with-environment/). For quick recall use the [gas exchange revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-gas-exchange-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [gas exchange practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-gas-exchange-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

| Area | Named content |

| Compromises | Efficient gas exchange against limiting water loss, in terrestrial insects and xerophytic plants |
| Human gas exchange system | Gross structure: alveoli, bronchioles, bronchi, trachea, lungs; essential features of the alveolar epithelium; ventilation and exchange of gases |
| Mechanism of breathing | Diaphragm; antagonistic external and internal intercostal muscles; pressure changes in the thoracic cavity |
| Data skills | Effects of lung disease on gas exchange or ventilation; effects of pollution and smoking on lung disease; risk factors; correlation and causation |

Every adaptation you write about should be tied to one of three things: a **large surface area**, a **short diffusion distance**, or a **maintained concentration gradient**.

## Single-celled organisms

Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse directly across the cell-surface membrane. The large surface area to volume ratio and short distance to every part of the cell make this fast enough. There is no ventilation and no transport system. This is the baseline the other systems are compared against.

## Insects: the tracheal system

Air enters through **spiracles**, pores along the sides of the body that can open and close. Each spiracle leads into a **trachea**, a tube held open by rings of chitin. Tracheae branch into **tracheoles**, very narrow tubes with thin walls that reach into the tissues and run between individual cells.

How each part helps:

- Tracheoles are very numerous and branched, giving a large surface area.
- Tracheoles end next to, or inside, respiring cells, so oxygen diffuses only a short distance and the blood is not needed for gas transport.
- Oxygen is used by respiring cells, keeping a gradient from the spiracles to the tissues; carbon dioxide diffuses the other way.
- In some insects, rhythmic abdominal movements squeeze the tracheae and ventilate the system, moving air by mass flow.
- During activity, muscle cells produce lactate, lowering their water potential. Water moves out of the ends of the tracheoles into the cells by osmosis, so more of the tracheole fills with air and gases diffuse faster to the muscles.

## Fish: gills and the counter-current principle

Water enters the mouth and is forced over the gills and out under the operculum. Each gill is made of **gill filaments**; each filament is covered in many thin plates called **gill lamellae**, which carry capillaries close to the surface.

- Many filaments, each with many lamellae: large surface area.
- Thin lamella epithelium and capillaries close to the surface: short diffusion distance.
- Constant flow of water and blood: gradients are maintained.

**The counter-current principle.** Blood in the lamellae flows in the opposite direction to the water passing over them. At every point along the lamella, the blood meets water that has a higher oxygen concentration than itself, because the water it meets has given up less oxygen so far. A diffusion gradient therefore exists across the whole length of the lamella, and the blood leaving it has nearly the oxygen concentration of the water entering.

If the two flowed in the same direction (parallel flow), the gradient would be steep at the start and then disappear once the concentrations became equal, about halfway along. No more oxygen would diffuse after that point, so the blood could never reach more than that equilibrium value.

## Dicotyledonous leaves

Leaves have no ventilation and no gas transport system, because the whole leaf is thin. Gases diffuse through **stomata**, mostly in the lower epidermis, into air spaces in the spongy **mesophyll**. Mesophyll cells have a large total surface area in contact with the air spaces, and no cell is far from an air space. Guard cells open stomata to allow exchange and close them to reduce water loss. In the light, photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and releases oxygen, so the gradients run in the opposite direction to those caused by respiration.

## The compromise with water loss

The features that make gas exchange efficient — a large, thin, moist surface open to the air — also let water evaporate. Land organisms show trade-offs.

| Organism | Feature that limits water loss | How |
|---|---|---|
| Terrestrial insect | Waxy, waterproof cuticle over the exoskeleton | Reduces evaporation from the body surface |
| | Spiracles closed when gas exchange need is low | Water vapour cannot diffuse out |
| | Hairs around spiracles | Trap moist air, reducing the water potential gradient |
| | Small surface area to volume ratio | Less surface for evaporation |
| Xerophytic plant | Thick waxy cuticle | Longer, waterproof barrier to evaporation |
| | Rolled leaves and hairs | Trap moist air near stomata |
| | Stomata in pits (sunken) | Humid air trapped, lower water potential gradient |
| | Fewer stomata; needle-like leaves | Less surface for water loss |

In answers, explain how the feature reduces water loss, for example "traps moist air, so the water potential gradient between the leaf and the air is reduced, so less water diffuses out".

## The human gas exchange system

Air passes down the **trachea**, which divides into two **bronchi**, one to each **lung**. The bronchi branch repeatedly into narrower **bronchioles**, which end in clusters of **alveoli**. Gas exchange happens across the alveolar epithelium.

**Essential features of the alveolar epithelium:**

- It is a single layer of thin, flattened cells, and the capillary wall next to it is also one cell thick: short diffusion distance.
- There are very many alveoli: large total surface area.
- Each alveolus is surrounded by a dense network of capillaries; blood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide.
- Ventilation replaces alveolar air, keeping oxygen concentration high and carbon dioxide low in the alveoli.

Together, blood flow and ventilation maintain the concentration gradients for oxygen to diffuse into the blood and carbon dioxide to diffuse out.

## The mechanism of breathing

| | Inspiration | Expiration at rest | Forced expiration |
|---|---|---|---|
| External intercostals | Contract | Relax | Relax |
| Internal intercostals | Relax | Relax | Contract |
| Ribcage | Up and out | Down and in | Pulled down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts, flattens | Relaxes, domes upward | Relaxes |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases | Decreases further |
| Pressure in lungs | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric | Rises further |
| Air movement | In | Out (largely passive, helped by elastic recoil) | Out |

The two sets of intercostal muscles are **antagonistic**: when one contracts the other relaxes, and they move the ribs in opposite directions. Air always moves from higher to lower pressure, so state the pressure change and the direction of air movement in every breathing answer.

## Working with lung data

Data questions often use spirometer traces. Two terms appear repeatedly: **tidal volume**, the volume of air breathed in or out in one normal breath, and **breathing rate**, breaths per minute. Pulmonary ventilation (volume of air moved into the lungs per minute) is calculated as tidal volume × breathing rate.

**Worked example (invented data).** A resting trace shows peaks every 5 seconds and a tidal volume of 0.48 dm³.

```
Breathing rate        = 60 ÷ 5        = 12 breaths per minute
Pulmonary ventilation = 0.48 × 12     = 5.76 dm³ per minute
```

For questions on lung disease, reason from structure: thickened alveolar walls lengthen the diffusion distance; breakdown of alveolar walls reduces surface area; narrowed airways reduce the rate of airflow; loss of elasticity makes breathing out harder. Then link to less oxygen entering the blood.

For smoking, pollution and risk factor data, separate **correlation** (two variables change together) from **causation** (one causes the other). A correlation alone does not show causation, because other factors may be involved; a strong answer names a possible confounding variable and says what further evidence, such as a controlled experiment or a known mechanism, would support a causal link.

## Common mistakes

- Saying gases "diffuse through the spiracles into the blood" in insects: the tracheal system delivers gases to the tissues directly.
- Describing counter-current flow as "water and blood flow in opposite directions" without explaining why the gradient is kept along the whole lamella.
- Writing "alveoli have thin walls" without "one cell thick" or "short diffusion distance".
- Saying air is "sucked in" by the lungs: describe volume and pressure changes.
- Saying the internal intercostals contract during inspiration.
- Treating a correlation in smoking data as proof of cause.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

| Command word | What earns the marks |
|---|---|
| Describe | Sequence of events, for example in breathing: muscle → ribs and diaphragm → volume → pressure → air movement |
| Explain | Structure plus consequence for diffusion: area, distance or gradient |
| Compare | Paired statements, for example parallel flow against counter-current flow |
| Evaluate | Use the data, then weigh the strength of evidence and correlation against causation |
