In the exam, Section B is one 30-mark comparative essay on two prose texts from one theme, assessed on
AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4. Section A of this page practises comparison of narrative methods on two short
original openings; Section B gives one essay per theme with indicative content.

---

## The openings

> **Opening A** *(original)*
>
> I was eleven the summer my uncle came back from the war, and I believed then, as children do, that the
> world had been arranged for my instruction. I have since learned otherwise. But it is the child's
> version I want to set down first, because it was the child who opened the door.

> **Opening B** *(original)*
>
> The door, Lena thought, would need painting again; the blue had gone the colour of old milk. And there
> was the letter still on the mat, his handwriting, ridiculous, after twenty years. She would not pick it
> up. She would make the coffee first. Then she would pick it up.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify the narrative method of each opening. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how Opening A creates a gap between the older narrator and the younger self. **[3]**

**3.** Identify **one** example of free indirect discourse in Opening B and explain its effect. **[2]**

**4.** Compare how the two openings use the door. **[3]**

**5.** Compare the way each opening handles time. **[2]**

**6.** Write a comparative topic sentence on how the two openings create suspense. **[2]**

**7.** Explain the difference between context of production and context of reception, with an example
from **either** text you have studied for Unit 3. **[2]**

**8.** A student writes: *"Paragraph 1: Text A. Paragraph 2: Text B. Paragraph 3: Text A..."* Explain why
this structure is likely to limit AO4 credit, and suggest a better approach. **[2]**

## Section B: comparative essays

Answer **one** question on your chosen theme. In your answer you must compare your two texts and
consider relevant contextual factors. Each essay is marked out of 30.

**9. Theme 1: Growing Up.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts present the
search for a sense of self. **[30]**

**10. Theme 2: Colonisation and After.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
present journeys and their consequences. **[30]**

**11. Theme 3: Science and Society.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
explore what it means to be human. **[30]**

**12. Theme 4: Women and Society.** Compare the ways in which the writers of your two chosen texts
present the home. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Opening A: first-person retrospective narration (1). Opening B: close third person using free
indirect discourse (1).

**2.** The past tense and "I was eleven" place the narrator long after events (1); the comment "I have
since learned otherwise" contrasts adult knowledge with childish belief (1); choosing to tell "the
child's version" first signals that the narrative will show both perspectives, creating dramatic irony
(1).

**3.** Example: "his handwriting, ridiculous, after twenty years" or "She would not pick it up" (1). The
narration takes on Lena's own idiom and judgement while staying in the third person, so the reader
hears her defensive irritation and the feeling it hides (1).

**4.** In Opening A the door is something the child "opened", a metaphor for the start of the story and
of new knowledge (1); in Opening B the door is literal and described in decaying detail (1), and the
letter on the mat beside it suggests the past arriving uninvited; in both the door marks a threshold
between the self and something from outside (1).

**5.** Opening A spans decades in a sentence, moving between the adult present and childhood (1);
Opening B stays in a few minutes of present action, with the past only implied ("after twenty years")
(1).

**6.** One mark for naming both openings (1); one for a comparative claim about method (1). Example:
*"Both openings withhold the event that matters, but where Opening A delays it through the adult
narrator's reflection, Opening B delays it through Lena's small, stubborn routines."*

**7.** Context of production is the situation in which a text was written: time, place, society,
literary movement (1). Context of reception is how it has been read, reviewed, adapted or interpreted
since; for example, the debate about Achebe's critique of *Heart of Darkness*, or feminist readings of
*Wuthering Heights* (1).

**8.** It keeps the texts separate, so connections appear only occasionally or at the end (1). A better
approach integrates both texts in every paragraph, organised by point of comparison (1).

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 12

Marked holistically on AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 25–30 | Sophisticated comparative argument; perceptive analysis of narrative methods in both texts; context of production and reception integrated to explain differences |
| 19–24 | Clear, sustained comparison; detailed analysis; relevant context linked to the texts |
| 13–18 | Some sustained comparison; explanation of methods; context sometimes general |
| 7–12 | Mainly separate treatment; some comment on methods; context bolted on |
| 1–6 | Little comparison; largely narrative |

**9. Growing Up: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | Pip's older narrating self judging his younger snobbery and shame of Joe; Celie's voice growing in confidence across her letters, from letters to God to letters to Nettie; Maisie's restricted viewpoint and her final choice of Mrs Wix |
| AO4 | Selfhood found through relationships (Shug, Joe, Mrs Wix) or through disillusion; the role of money and class; endings as achieved or uncertain identities |
| AO3 | Victorian ideas of the gentleman and self-improvement; race and gender in the American South; late-Victorian divorce and the treatment of children; modern readers' responses |

**10. Colonisation and After: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | Marlow's journey upriver told through a frame narrative; the arrival of migrants in London in Selvon's episodic, creolised narration; the expedition to the Marabar Caves at the centre of Forster's three-part structure |
| AO4 | Journeys into or out of the imperial centre; disillusion and loss of certainty; the destination as mystery or disappointment |
| AO3 | Late-nineteenth-century imperialism in the Congo; British rule and the independence movement in 1920s India; post-war Caribbean migration; postcolonial criticism as reception |

**11. Science and Society: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | The creature's eloquent narrative at the centre of *Frankenstein*; Offred's memories and private language resisting Gilead; Kathy's calm narration and the students' art at Hailsham, valued as evidence of an inner life |
| AO4 | Humanity defined by feeling, memory, art or love; the creators or systems that deny it; resistance and acceptance |
| AO3 | Romantic anxieties about science and creation; 1980s fears about religious and political control; debates on cloning and organ donation around the turn of the century |

**12. Women and Society: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | 124 Bluestone Road as a haunted house in *Beloved*; Clarissa's house and party as her creation and her limit; the opposition of Wuthering Heights and Thrushcross Grange; narrative structure moving between houses or minds |
| AO4 | Home as refuge, prison or battleground; houses that hold the past; women's roles defined by domestic space |
| AO3 | Slavery and its aftermath; post-war London society and women's changing roles; inheritance law and property in the early nineteenth century |

**What separates stronger answers.** They compare in every paragraph, analyse narrative method rather
than events, and use context to explain why two writers approach the same theme differently.
