## What section 3.2.7 covers

| Content (3.2.7, International A-level only) | Your task |
|---|---|
| Prepare and comment on the financial statements of manufacturers | Prepare a manufacturing account, an income statement and a statement of financial position for a business that makes its own goods, then comment on what they show |
| Calculation and recording of prime costs | Find direct materials consumed and add the other direct costs |
| Calculation and recording of overhead cost | Total the indirect factory costs, sharing mixed costs between factory and office |
| Calculation and recording of factory cost of finished goods | Adjust total factory cost for opening and closing work in progress |
| Calculation and recording of profit on manufacture | Add a mark-up (or use a market value) to transfer goods out of the factory |
| Calculation and recording of provisions for unrealised profit | Remove profit not yet earned from finished goods inventory, recording the change |

## Why manufacturers need an extra statement

A trader buys goods ready to sell, so cost of sales comes straight from purchases. A manufacturer buys
raw materials, pays people to work on them and runs a factory. The **manufacturing account** gathers
those factory costs and works out what the goods made in the year cost. That figure then feeds into the
income statement in place of (or as well as) purchases of finished goods.

A manufacturer usually holds three kinds of inventory:

- **raw materials** -- bought in, not yet used;
- **work in progress** -- started but not finished at the year end;
- **finished goods** -- complete and ready to sell.

All three appear under current assets in the statement of financial position.

## Prime cost

**Prime cost** is the total of the **direct costs**: costs that can be traced to each unit made.

- **Direct materials consumed** = opening inventory of raw materials + purchases of raw materials +
  carriage inwards − returns outwards − closing inventory of raw materials.
- **Direct labour**: wages of the people who make the product (machine operators, assemblers).
- **Direct expenses**: other costs tied to each unit, such as a royalty paid per item made.

Carriage inwards on raw materials belongs here. Carriage outwards is a selling cost and goes in the
income statement.

## Overhead cost

Factory **overheads** are indirect production costs. They are needed to make the goods but cannot be
traced to a single unit: supervisors' and maintenance wages, factory power, factory rent, insurance of
machinery and depreciation of factory machinery.

Some costs cover the whole site. Share them between factory and office on a fair basis -- floor area
for rent, meter readings for electricity, values for insurance. Only the factory share enters the
manufacturing account; the office share is an expense in the income statement.

## Factory cost of finished goods

Prime cost plus overhead cost gives the total cost of production in the year. Some of that production
is unfinished. Add opening work in progress (finished this year) and deduct closing work in progress
(carried forward). The result is the **factory cost of finished goods**: the cost of goods completed in
the year.

## Profit on manufacture

Many manufacturers transfer goods out of the factory at more than cost: either cost plus a set
percentage, or the price an outside supplier would charge for the same goods. The difference is the
**profit on manufacture**. It lets the owner judge the factory as if it were selling to the rest of the
business, and it can be used to reward factory managers.

The double entry: the transfer price is debited to the trading section of the income statement and
credited to the manufacturing account. The profit on manufacture is then added back below gross
profit, so it does not change the business's total profit for goods that have been sold.

## Provisions for unrealised profit

Closing finished goods that came from the factory are valued at **transfer price**, which includes
profit on manufacture. Those goods have not been sold to a customer outside the business, so that
profit is not yet earned. The **realisation** concept says profit is recognised only when goods pass
to the customer, and **prudence** says assets and profit must not be overstated. So you create a
**provision for unrealised profit** (PUP).

With a mark-up of m% on cost:

PUP = finished goods at transfer price × m / (100 + m)

With a margin of p% of the transfer price, PUP = finished goods at transfer price × p / 100.

Each year the income statement takes just the **movement** in the provision:

- **increase** -- deduct it from profit on manufacture;
- **decrease** -- add it to profit on manufacture.

In the statement of financial position, finished goods are shown at transfer price less the full
closing provision, which is cost.

## Worked example 2: income statement and inventories

Tolverne's finished goods at transfer price were 24,150 at 1 May 2025 and 29,900 at 30 April 2026.
Revenue was 640,000. Office salaries were 64,000, selling and distribution costs 31,500 and
depreciation of office equipment 4,000; the office pays 9,000 of the rent and insurance.

PUP at 1 May 2025 = 24,150 × 15/115 = **3,150**.
PUP at 30 April 2026 = 29,900 × 15/115 = **3,900**. The provision rises by **750**.


Check: if every transfer were at cost, opening and closing finished goods would be 21,000 and 26,000.
Cost of sales would be 21,000 + 442,500 − 26,000 = 437,500 and gross profit 202,500 -- the same
202,500 as above. The mark-up and the provision only split profit between factory and selling.

```

             work in progress                               11,800
             finished goods                     29,900
             less provision for unrealised profit 3,900     26,000
                                                            59,100
```

The ledger account for the provision:

```

                                | Income statement           750
                        3,900   |                          3,900
```

If finished goods at transfer price fall to 25,300 a year later, the provision becomes
25,300 × 15/115 = 3,300. The 600 decrease is debited to the provision account and **added** in the
income statement.

For a limited company the layout is the same down to gross profit plus the adjusted profit on
manufacture. Expenses then lead to profit from operations, then profit for the year before and after
tax, as in 3.1.7.

## Commenting on manufacturers' statements

The specification asks you to **comment**, not only prepare. Points that earn credit:

- **Factory cost per unit.** Tolverne made 25,000 pans, so the factory cost was 442,500 ÷ 25,000 =
  17.70 each. If an outside supplier quotes 19.80, making saves 2.10 a pan, or 52,500 in total.
- **Profit on manufacture is internal.** A 15% mark-up produced 66,375 of "profit", more than the
  52,500 real saving against the market. A mark-up chosen by the owner can flatter the factory; a
  transfer at market value shows its true advantage.
- **Make or buy.** Closing the factory saves only the costs that would stop. Leases, redundancy costs
  and lost control of quality and supply all count. Non-financial points (jobs, reliability,
  know-how) matter too.
- **Cost behaviour.** Prime costs rise with output; many overheads do not. A drop in output raises
  the cost per unit.
- **Performance measures.** A separate factory profit helps reward managers, but a manager who
  ignores quality or overproduces may still show a profit on manufacture.
- **Inventory levels.** Rising work in progress or finished goods ties up cash and may signal
  production bottlenecks or weak sales.

Always link the comment to the figures and finish with a judgement.

## Common errors

- Putting carriage outwards, office rent or sales commission in the manufacturing account.
- Treating the factory supervisor's wages as direct labour.
- Adjusting for work in progress before prime cost instead of after overheads.
- Calculating PUP as m% of transfer price (15% of 29,900) instead of m/(100 + m).
- Deducting the whole closing provision from profit instead of the change.
- Showing finished goods at transfer price in the statement of financial position.
- Commenting in general terms without using the factory cost per unit or the market price.
