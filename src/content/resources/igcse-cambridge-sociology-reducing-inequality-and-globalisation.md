How have societies tried to reduce inequality, and how has globalisation changed inequality
between countries? These are key questions 3.2 and 3.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), the last
part of the Social stratification and inequality topic in Paper 1. You need to know the main
attempts — legislation, welfare states and the global work of non-governmental organisations
(NGOs) — and sociological criticisms of them, then explain how migration, ecological issues and
theories of exploitation help explain global inequality.

Key question 3.1 (what stratification is) is covered in the
[Paper 1 overview guide](/resources/igcse-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495 (2025–2027):

- **3.2 What attempts have been made to reduce social inequalities?** — 3.2.1 legislation
  (progressive taxation, redistribution of wealth through benefits, a minimum wage, equal
  opportunities laws, other government measures); 3.2.2 welfare states (free and/or universal
  education, unemployment benefits, pensions, low-cost housing, free healthcare, universal basic
  income); 3.2.3 the global work of NGOs (reducing poverty, medical assistance, promoting business,
  improving education); 3.2.4 sociological views of success (New Right criticisms of the welfare
  state and the underclass, the culture of poverty and dependency culture, Marxist criticisms of the
  welfare state, criticisms of NGOs, feminist criticisms of equality legislation).
- **3.3 How has globalisation affected inequalities between societies?** — 3.3.1 migration
  (immigration/emigration, push and pull factors, increasing cultural diversity, economic
  consequences, negative perceptions of migrants); 3.3.2 global ecological issues (influence of
  transnational companies, climate change, pollution, urbanisation); 3.3.3 explanations (Marxist
  views of capitalist exploitation, colonialism, feminist ideas of patriarchy).

## How can laws reduce inequality? (3.2.1)

| Measure | How it reduces inequality |
|---|---|
| **Progressive taxation** | Higher earners pay a higher percentage of income in tax |
| **Redistribution through benefits** | Tax revenue is paid to low-income groups |
| **Minimum wage** | Sets a legal floor for pay, protecting the lowest paid |
| **Equal opportunities laws** | Make discrimination by sex, ethnicity, disability or age illegal in work and services |
| **Other measures** | Conditional cash transfers, job guarantees, quotas |

Examples from different societies: Brazil's *Bolsa Família* (from 2003) paid poor families on
condition that children attended school and had health checks; India's National Rural Employment
Guarantee Act (2005) guaranteed rural households a number of days of paid work each year.

## What is a welfare state? (3.2.2)

A welfare state is a system in which the government provides services and benefits to protect
citizens' life chances "from cradle to grave". In Britain the Beveridge Report (1942) led to the
National Health Service, founded in 1948, alongside national insurance benefits.

| Provision | Effect on life chances |
|---|---|
| Free and/or universal education | Opportunity for social mobility |
| Unemployment benefits | Protection from poverty when out of work |
| Pensions | Income in old age |
| Low-cost housing | Secure homes for low-income families |
| Free healthcare | Health care regardless of ability to pay |
| Universal basic income | A regular payment to everyone, without conditions; tested in trials such as Finland's 2017–2018 experiment with unemployed people |

## How do NGOs work globally? (3.2.3)

NGOs are non-profit organisations independent of governments. Examples of their work: **reducing
poverty** (cash transfers and food aid); **providing medical assistance** — Médecins Sans
Frontières delivers emergency health care in conflict and disaster zones; **promoting business** —
microcredit lending, associated with Muhammad Yunus and the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, which shared
the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize; **improving education** — building schools, training teachers and
supporting girls' schooling. BRAC, founded in Bangladesh in 1972, combines all four.

## Have these attempts succeeded? (3.2.4)

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **New Right** | Generous welfare discourages work and family responsibility and creates an **underclass**; Charles Murray (*Losing Ground*, 1984) |
| **Culture of poverty / dependency culture** | Oscar Lewis's studies of poor families in Mexico and Puerto Rico (1959, 1966) described values of fatalism passed between generations; dependency culture is the idea that people come to rely on benefits |
| **Marxist** | The welfare state only softens capitalism's worst effects, preventing revolt and keeping workers healthy enough to be exploited; wealth inequality remains |
| **Criticisms of NGOs** | Can create dependency, are accountable to donors rather than local people, may impose Western priorities, and are too small to change structural causes |
| **Feminist** | Equality laws have not closed the gender pay gap; women still do most unpaid care; laws treat symptoms not patriarchy |

**Evaluation:** supporters point to reduced absolute poverty and longer life expectancy where
welfare and NGO programmes operate; critics show inequality between rich and poor remains large.

## How does migration affect inequality? (3.3.1)

- **Immigration** is moving into a country; **emigration** is leaving one.
- **Push factors:** poverty, unemployment, conflict, persecution, natural disasters.
- **Pull factors:** jobs, higher wages, safety, education, family.
- **Cultural diversity** increases in receiving societies.
- **Economic consequences:** receiving countries gain workers and taxes; sending countries receive
  remittances but may lose skilled workers (brain drain).
- **Negative perceptions** of migrants include stereotyping, scapegoating and racism, often
  amplified by media.

## How do global ecological issues affect inequality? (3.3.2)

- **Transnational companies (TNCs)** bring jobs and investment but may exploit cheap labour, weak
  regulation and natural resources.
- **Climate change** harms poorer countries most (drought, flooding, crop failure) even though
  richer countries have produced more emissions.
- **Pollution** from mining, factories and waste often affects poor communities.
- **Urbanisation** — rapid growth of cities can create informal settlements without safe water or
  sanitation.

## What explains global inequality? (3.3.3)

- **Marxist views of capitalist exploitation:** global capitalism moves wealth from poorer to richer
  countries through cheap labour, raw materials and profit-taking.
- **Colonialism:** colonial powers extracted resources and shaped economies around exports,
  leaving lasting disadvantage.
- **Feminist ideas of patriarchy:** women do much of the world's work but own a small share of its
  property and are more likely to be poor; globalisation may increase exploitation of women's labour.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the welfare state the same in every country?** No. Provision varies widely; some countries have
universal services, others only targeted support.

**What is the difference between New Right and Marxist criticisms?** The New Right says welfare does
too much and creates dependency; Marxists say it does too little to challenge capitalist inequality.

**How should I link migration to inequality?** Explain who gains and who loses — migrants, receiving
countries and sending countries — and show that outcomes are unequal.
