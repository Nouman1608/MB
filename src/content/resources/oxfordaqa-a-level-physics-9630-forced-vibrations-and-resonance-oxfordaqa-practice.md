These questions cover section 3.5.2, Forced vibrations and resonance, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), shared International AS and A-level content. Some questions use period equations from section 3.5.1 and the first-harmonic equation from section 3.5.5. Learn the content first with the [forced vibrations and resonance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-forced-vibrations-and-resonance-oxfordaqa/) and the [forced vibrations and resonance revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-forced-vibrations-and-resonance-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/). Take g = 9.81 m s⁻².

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Distinguish between a free vibration and a forced vibration. **[2]**

**2.** Define resonance. **[2]**

**3.** A 0.30 kg mass hangs from a spring of constant 120 N m⁻¹. Calculate its natural frequency. **[2]**

**4.** A simple pendulum is 0.25 m long. State the driving frequency that would make it resonate. **[1]**

**5.** The drum of a washing machine spins at 3000 revolutions per minute. Calculate the frequency of the vibration it applies to the machine's casing, assuming one vibration per revolution. **[1]**

**6.** State two ways in which the resonance curve of a system changes when the damping is increased. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A 0.50 kg mass on a spring of constant 200 N m⁻¹ hangs from a vibration generator. The driving frequency is increased slowly from 1.0 Hz to 6.0 Hz while the driver's amplitude is kept constant.

**(a)** Calculate the natural frequency of the system. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe how the amplitude of the mass changes over the range of driving frequencies. **[3]**

**(c)** The mass is replaced by one of 2.0 kg. Calculate the new resonant frequency. **[1]**

**8.**

**(a)** Describe the resonance curves (amplitude against driving frequency) for the same system with light damping and with heavier damping. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the amplitude at resonance does not keep increasing. **[2]**

**9.** In Barton's pendulums, a heavy driver pendulum and several light pendulums of different lengths hang from one horizontal string. One light pendulum has the same length as the driver.

**(a)** Describe and explain what is observed after the driver is set swinging. **[4]**

**(b)** State the effect of attaching larger card discs to the light pendulums to increase their damping. **[1]**

**10.** A string of length 0.80 m and mass per unit length 6.0 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ is kept at a tension of 60 N. It is driven by a vibration generator.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the first harmonic. **[2]**

**(b)** State the next two driving frequencies at which the string resonates. **[1]**

**(c)** The tension is increased to 90 N. Calculate the new first-harmonic frequency. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the string has many resonant frequencies but a simple pendulum has only one. **[1]**

**11.** A light footbridge sways sideways with a large amplitude when many people walk across it together.

**(a)** Explain how the walkers can cause this large amplitude. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two changes an engineer could make to reduce the sway. **[2]**

**12.** A car's door mirror vibrates strongly only when the engine turns at 1500 revolutions per minute. The engine vibrates once per revolution.

**(a)** Calculate the driving frequency. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the mirror vibrates strongly only at this engine speed. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one modification to the mirror mounting that would reduce the effect. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A free vibration occurs when a system is displaced and released with no periodic force, and it vibrates at its natural frequency [1]; a forced vibration is caused by a periodic driving force, and the system vibrates at the driving frequency [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The frequency of each is the most important distinction; include it.

**2.** A large increase in the amplitude of a forced vibration [1] when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Vibrating at its natural frequency" alone is not a definition of resonance.

**3.** f₀ = (1/2π)√(k/m) = (1/2π)√(120/0.30) [1] = 3.2 Hz [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* f = 1/T, so invert the period equation rather than using T itself.

**4.** f₀ = (1/2π)√(9.81/0.25) = 1.0 Hz [1] **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* A 0.25 m pendulum has a period of almost exactly 1 s, a useful check.

**5.** 3000 / 60 = 50 Hz [1] **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* Revolutions per minute must be divided by 60.

**6.** Any two of: the peak amplitude is lower; the peak is broader (less sharp); the peak occurs at a slightly lower frequency [2] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "The peak is smaller" is accepted for one mark only if it refers to height.

**7. (a)** f₀ = (1/2π)√(200/0.50) = 3.2 Hz [1]

**(b)** At low frequencies the amplitude is small [1]; it rises to a sharp maximum at about 3.2 Hz, where resonance occurs [1]; above this it falls to a very small value by 6.0 Hz [1]

**(c)** f₀ = (1/2π)√(200/2.0) = 1.6 Hz [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Quadrupling the mass halves the natural frequency, a quick check for (c).

**8. (a)** Both curves are small at low frequency, rise to a peak near f₀ and fall at high frequency [1]; the lightly damped curve has a tall, narrow peak at f₀ [1]; the heavily damped curve has a lower, broader peak at a slightly lower frequency [1]

**(b)** Energy is transferred to the surroundings by damping in every cycle [1]; the amplitude increases until the energy lost per cycle equals the energy supplied by the driver per cycle [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* An energy-balance answer is expected; "damping stops it" is incomplete.

**9. (a)** All the light pendulums begin to swing at the driver's frequency [1]; the pendulum of the same length swings with the largest amplitude [1]; its natural frequency, set by its length, equals the driving frequency, so it resonates [1]; pendulums with lengths very different from the driver's have natural frequencies far from the driving frequency, so their amplitudes are small [1]

**(b)** The amplitude of the resonating pendulum is reduced, and the amplitudes of all the pendulums become more similar [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Link each observation to natural and driving frequency; describing what moves without explaining earns at most two marks.

**10. (a)** f₁ = (1/2l)√(T/μ) = (1/1.60) × √(60 / 6.0 × 10⁻³) [1] = 100 / 1.60 = 62.5 Hz [1]

**(b)** 125 Hz and 188 Hz [1]

**(c)** f₁ = (1/1.60) × √(90 / 6.0 × 10⁻³) = 77 Hz [1]

**(d)** The string can support stationary waves with any whole number of half-wavelengths along it, each with its own natural frequency, whereas a pendulum has a single natural frequency [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* f₁ ∝ √T, so increasing the tension by 1.5 times raises the frequency by √1.5 = 1.22.

**11. (a)** The walkers' footsteps apply a periodic sideways force [1]; if the frequency of this force matches a natural frequency of the bridge, resonance gives a large amplitude [1]

**(b)** Any two of: add dampers to increase damping; stiffen the structure or change its mass so its natural frequency is far from walking frequencies; limit the number of people crossing at once [2] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Each suggestion should say whether it changes the natural frequency or increases damping.

**12. (a)** 1500 / 60 = 25 Hz [1]

**(b)** The mirror has a natural frequency of about 25 Hz [1]; only at this engine speed does the driving frequency match it, so resonance occurs; at other speeds the amplitude stays small [1]

**(c)** Fit a rubber (damping) mounting, or make the mounting stiffer or change its mass to move the natural frequency away from engine frequencies [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong vibration at just one speed is the signature of resonance; use that word.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a forced system vibrates at its natural frequency.
- Defining resonance without the frequency match.
- Drawing heavier damping with a taller peak.
- No energy balance when explaining the finite amplitude.
- Forgetting to divide revolutions per minute by 60.
- Treating period and frequency as the same.
