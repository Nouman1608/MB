## Section A

**1.** Define **status** and **caste**. **[4]**

**2.** Identify **two** areas of life in which inequalities in life chances can be seen. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between **income** and **wealth**. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by **intersectionality**. **[3]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain **two** differences between the Marxist and Weberian views of social class. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the functionalist view that class inequality is a functional necessity, and give **one** criticism of it. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between intergenerational and intragenerational social mobility, and identify **two** factors that affect social mobility. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which feminists argue society is patriarchal, referring to the work of one named sociologist. **[4]**

**9.** Distinguish between **race** and **ethnicity**, and explain what is meant by **institutional racism**. **[6]**

## Section C

**10.** Discuss how far social democratic welfare states and development aid have reduced inequalities. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **Status** is the **social honour or prestige** attached to a person or position, for example the respect given to doctors [1] [1]. **Caste** is a **closed system of stratification** in which position is **ascribed at birth** and cannot be changed, traditionally with rules about marriage and occupation, as in the traditional Indian caste system [1] [1].

**2.** Any two: **employment**; **education**; **health**; **life expectancy**; **social mobility** [1] [1].

**3.** **Income** is a **flow of money** received over a period, such as wages or benefits [1]; **wealth** is a **stock of assets** owned, such as property, savings or shares [1].

**4.** Intersectionality is the idea, introduced by **Kimberlé Crenshaw**, that differences such as class, gender and ethnicity **combine and interact** [1], so a person's experience of inequality cannot be understood by looking at one difference alone [1] — for example, Black women may face forms of discrimination that are not recognised as either race or sex discrimination on its own [1].

**5.** Any two, 2 marks each: Marx sees class purely in terms of **ownership or non-ownership of the means of production**, whereas Weber sees class as **market situation** — skills and qualifications also matter [1] [1]. Marx sees inequality as essentially **economic**, while Weber adds **status** and **party** as separate dimensions [1] [1]. Marx predicts **class conflict** leading to revolution; Weber sees a more complex structure with many groups, making revolution less likely [1] [1].

**6.** Davis and Moore argue that some jobs are **more important** to society and need rare talent and long training [1], so they must carry **higher rewards** to motivate the most able people to fill them, making inequality functional and the system a **meritocracy** [1]. Criticism, any one: inherited wealth and unequal opportunities mean positions are not allocated purely by merit [1] [1]; rewards do not always match importance — some vital jobs, such as care work, are poorly paid [1] [1].

**7.** **Intergenerational** mobility compares a person's class position with that of their **parents** [1] [1]; **intragenerational** mobility is movement during **one person's own working life** [1] [1]. Any two factors: education and qualifications; family background; gender; ethnicity; changes in the job structure, such as the growth of professional work [1] [1].

**8.** Any two, 2 marks each, with a named sociologist: **Walby** argued patriarchy works through six structures, such as **paid work**, where women earn less and face the glass ceiling, and **household production**, where women do more unpaid domestic work [1] [1]; Walby also identified **male violence** and the **state** as structures that maintain men's power [1] [1]; **Connell** argued **hegemonic masculinity** sets a dominant ideal that places men above women [1] [1].

**9.** **Race** classifies people by supposed physical differences such as skin colour and is seen by sociologists as **socially constructed** [1] [1]; **ethnicity** refers to **shared culture** — language, religion, traditions and a sense of common origin [1] [1]. **Institutional racism** is discrimination **built into the policies and routines of an organisation**, producing unequal outcomes even without individual intent [1] [1].

**10.** Mark by levels:
- **10–12:** detailed knowledge of both approaches, balanced evaluation with evidence, and a reasoned conclusion on "how far".
- **7–9:** clear knowledge of both, some evaluation, a conclusion.
- **4–6:** some relevant points, mostly descriptive or one-sided.
- **1–3:** limited, basic points.

*Indicative content:* **Welfare states** use progressive taxation and universal services such as healthcare, education and benefits, which narrow gaps in health, education and income and support social mobility; but they are costly, do not remove inequalities of **wealth**, and Marxists argue they only soften capitalism rather than end exploitation. **Development aid** has funded improvements in health and education in poorer countries; but critics argue it can create **dependency**, may serve donor countries' political and economic interests, and leaves the legacy of colonialism untouched. A strong conclusion might argue both reduce the effects of inequality without removing its causes, which is why Marxists call for abolishing capitalism instead.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which paper assesses differences and inequalities?**
Paper 2, Exploring Sociology: 1 hour 30 minutes, 60 marks. Differences and inequalities is Section A (22 marks), socialisation and social control Section B (22 marks) and research methods Section C (16 marks).

**Do I need examples from my own country?**
The specification asks students to apply themes to different societies, so prepare at least one example of class, gender and ethnic inequality from a society you know.

**Which theory of class should I use?**
Use the one the question names; otherwise compare Marxism (class conflict, exploitation), functionalism (Davis and Moore, meritocracy) and Weberianism (class, status and party).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing income with wealth.
- Describing Weber's view as if it were Marx's.
- Naming Walby or Connell without stating what they argued.
- Writing a one-sided answer to a "discuss how far" question.
