This guide covers **section 3.1.1 Memory**, the first of the four sections of Paper 1 (Cognition and Behaviour) in AQA GCSE Psychology (8182). The [Paper 1 Cognition and Behaviour guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-cognition-and-behaviour/) gives the whole paper in outline; this page goes further into memory alone, with the evaluation points, applied examples and exam technique you need to turn knowledge into marks.

## What section 3.1.1 covers

- **Processes of memory:** encoding (input), storage and retrieval (output); episodic, semantic and procedural memory; how memories are encoded and stored.
- **Structures of memory:** the multi-store model (sensory, short-term and long-term stores) and the coding, capacity and duration of each store; primacy and recency effects (serial position); **Murdock's serial position curve study**.
- **Memory as an active process:** the Theory of Reconstructive Memory, including **effort after meaning**; **Bartlett's War of the Ghosts study**.
- **Factors affecting the accuracy of memory:** interference, context and false memories.

## Processes and types of memory

Information can be **encoded** in different forms: **acoustically** (by sound), **visually** (by image) or **semantically** (by meaning). Remembering a phone number by repeating it aloud is acoustic encoding; remembering the gist of a conversation, but not the exact words, is semantic encoding.

The three types of long-term memory differ in what they store and how easy they are to put into words:

| Type | What it stores | Example | Conscious recall? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Episodic | Personal experiences tied to a time and place | Your last birthday party | Yes, and it feels personal |
| Semantic | Facts and meanings, without a personal context | Knowing that a triangle has three sides | Yes |
| Procedural | Skills and actions | Tying a shoelace | No, it is hard to explain in words |

Case studies of people with brain damage support the idea that these are separate. **Clive Wearing**, a musician whose brain was damaged by a viral infection, lost most of his episodic memory but could still play the piano, a procedural skill. A useful exam point is that such case studies are rich in detail but concern unique individuals, so the findings are hard to generalise.

## The multi-store model in detail

Atkinson and Shiffrin's model describes memory as a flow of information through three stores:

```
stimulus -> SENSORY STORE --attention--> SHORT-TERM STORE --rehearsal--> LONG-TERM STORE
               |                              |                             |
        not attended: lost           not rehearsed: lost        retrieval back to STM
```

- The **sensory store** holds information in the form it arrived (sight, sound and so on). It has a very large capacity but lasts only a moment, so most of it is lost unless it is paid **attention** to.
- The **short-term store** codes mainly **acoustically**, holds roughly **5 to 9 items** and lasts about **18 to 30 seconds** without rehearsal.
- The **long-term store** codes mainly **semantically**, has a potentially **unlimited** capacity and can last a lifetime. Retrieval brings information back into the short-term store when it is used.

**Strengths.** The model gives clear, testable predictions, and the serial position effect fits it well. Case studies such as **HM** (Henry Molaison), who could hold information briefly but could not form new long-term memories after brain surgery, suggest short-term and long-term memory are separate.

**Weaknesses.** It treats each store as a single unit, when Clive Wearing's case suggests long-term memory has separate parts. It overstates rehearsal: people remember some vivid events without rehearsing them. Much of its evidence comes from word lists in laboratories, which are unlike everyday remembering.

## Serial position: Murdock's study

**Aim.** To see whether the position of a word in a list affects how likely it is to be recalled.

**Method.** Participants heard lists of unrelated words read aloud, one word at a time, and then recalled as many as they could in any order (**free recall**). The position of each word in the list was the independent variable; whether it was recalled was the dependent variable.

**Results.** Plotting recall against position gives a U-shaped **serial position curve**: words at the start (**primacy effect**) and at the end (**recency effect**) were recalled best, and words in the middle worst.

**Conclusion.** Early words had time to be rehearsed into the long-term store; the last words were still in the short-term store at recall. Middle words were pushed out of the short-term store before they were rehearsed enough. This supports the existence of separate stores.

**Evaluation.** It was a controlled laboratory experiment, so it is easy to replicate. However, learning lists of unrelated words is an artificial task, so the study lacks **ecological validity**.

## Memory as an active process: Bartlett

Bartlett's **Theory of Reconstructive Memory** says that we do not store an exact copy of events. We store fragments and rebuild the memory each time we recall it, filling gaps using **schemas** (mental frameworks built from experience). **Effort after meaning** is our attempt to make sense of unfamiliar information by changing it to fit what we already know.

In the **War of the Ghosts** study, English participants read a Native American folk tale that seemed strange to them and later reproduced it from memory several times. Over repeated reproductions the story became **shorter**, unfamiliar details were **left out**, and others were **changed** into more familiar forms so that the story made sense in terms of their own culture.

**Evaluation.** The study supports the idea that memory is shaped by culture and expectation, which has implications for eyewitness testimony. However, it was not tightly controlled: participants were not given standardised instructions or told to be accurate, so some changes may have been deliberate guesses rather than true distortions of memory. The data were also **qualitative**, so judging what counted as a change was open to interpretation.

## Factors affecting the accuracy of memory

- **Interference.** Similar memories compete. **Proactive** interference is when older learning disrupts newer learning (an old PIN keeps coming to mind); **retroactive** interference is when newer learning disrupts older learning (after learning a new timetable you cannot recall last term's).
- **Context.** Recall is better when the setting at retrieval matches the setting at encoding, because the context acts as a cue. Godden and Baddeley found that divers recalled words better when they were tested in the same environment, underwater or on land, as the one where they learned them.
- **False memories.** A person can sincerely recall something that never happened, or remember it differently from how it happened. Research by Elizabeth Loftus showed that suggestion from others can lead people to "remember" invented childhood events. False memories fit reconstructive theory: recall is rebuilt, so new information can be built in.

## Worked example 1: applying memory to a scenario

> *Jamal moved from a school where his locker code was 4729 to one where it is 8153. When he tries to open his new locker, he keeps typing 4729. Explain why, using your knowledge of memory.* [3 marks]

A strong answer names the concept, explains it and links it to Jamal:

"This is **proactive interference** [1], where information learned earlier disrupts the recall of newer, similar information [1]. Jamal's old code is interfering with retrieval of his new code because both are four-digit numbers used for the same purpose [1]."

The final sentence earns the application mark. Without "Jamal" or the codes, the answer is only a definition.

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

> *Describe and evaluate Bartlett's Theory of Reconstructive Memory.*

- **Describe (about half):** memory is rebuilt at recall; schemas fill gaps; effort after meaning; War of the Ghosts procedure and the three kinds of change (shortening, omission, transformation to familiar).
- **Evaluate (about half):** a strength (explains why eyewitness accounts differ, and false memory research supports it); a weakness (lack of control and standardised instructions); a further weakness (qualitative data and subjective scoring).
- **Conclusion:** one sentence weighing up, for example that the theory is influential but the original evidence is not tightly controlled.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the coding of the stores: short-term memory is mainly acoustic, long-term mainly semantic.
- Explaining the recency effect with long-term memory instead of short-term memory.
- Describing Bartlett's findings without the idea of **schemas** or **effort after meaning**.
- Giving the direction of interference the wrong way round.
- Describing a study without its aim, method, results and conclusion when asked to describe it.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Identify / Name / State:** a short answer is enough; do not explain.
- **Outline:** a brief description of the main points.
- **Describe:** give detail; for a study, cover what was done and what was found.
- **Explain:** give reasons, using "because" or "this means that".
- **Evaluate:** give strengths and weaknesses, each developed with evidence or a consequence, and reach a judgement.
- **Distinguish between:** make the difference explicit, for example "whereas".

In scenario questions, always quote or refer to the person or situation in the stem.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does section 3.1.1 Memory cover in AQA GCSE Psychology 8182?**
It covers the processes and types of memory, the multi-store model with coding, capacity and duration, Murdock's serial position study, Bartlett's reconstructive memory and factors affecting accuracy.

**What is effort after meaning?**
It is Bartlett's term for our attempt to make unfamiliar information make sense by changing it to fit our existing schemas, which is why recalled stories become more familiar.

**Why does Murdock's study support the multi-store model?**
The primacy effect suggests early words reached long-term memory through rehearsal, and the recency effect suggests the last words were still in short-term memory, which fits separate stores.
