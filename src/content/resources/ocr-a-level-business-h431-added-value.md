## What added value is

**Added value** is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials, components and services used to make it. It measures how much value the business itself has created through its own processes, skills and brand. Another name is **value added**.

Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs

The inputs are things the business buys from other businesses, such as flour, steel, fabric, packaging or electricity used in production. Wages and rent are not deducted: they are paid **out of** the added value.

A business adds value in many ways: turning raw materials into a finished product, improving quality, building a brand, offering convenience, providing good service or creating a design that customers want. A bag of coffee beans sold at a farm may be worth £4. Roasted, packed and branded, it may sell in a shop for £9. The extra £5, less the cost of packaging and transport, is added value.

## Calculating added value

There are three common measures.

1. **Added value per unit** = selling price − bought-in cost per unit.
2. **Added value as a percentage of price** = (added value ÷ selling price) × 100. This allows comparison between products with different prices.
3. **Total added value** = added value per unit × units sold. Dividing by the number of employees gives **added value per employee**.

### Worked example 1: a bakery's loaf

Hearth Bakery sells a loaf for **£2.80**. The bought-in costs are flour, yeast and packaging at **£0.90** a loaf. It sells 100,000 loaves a year and has 8 employees.

1. Added value per loaf = £2.80 − £0.90 = **£1.90**.
2. As a percentage of price = 1.90 ÷ 2.80 × 100 = **67.9%**.
3. Total added value = 100,000 × £1.90 = **£190,000**.
4. Added value per employee = £190,000 ÷ 8 = **£23,750**.

**Where does the added value go?** It pays for everything else the business does:

| Use | £ per year |
|---|---|
| Wages | 110,000 |
| Rent, heating and other overheads | 50,000 |
| Profit | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **190,000** |

The three amounts add to £190,000. This explains why added value is not profit: profit is only what is left after wages and overheads. A business with high added value but also high wages and overheads may make little profit.

### Worked example 2: comparing two businesses

Two furniture makers sell a chair.

| | Value Chairs | Atelier Chairs |
|---|---|---|
| Selling price | £60 | £420 |
| Bought-in cost | £35 | £150 |
| Added value | £25 | £270 |
| Added value as % of price | 25 ÷ 60 = **41.7%** | 270 ÷ 420 = **64.3%** |

Atelier adds far more value per chair, both in pounds and as a share of its price. Its design, hand-finishing and brand allow a high price compared with its inputs. Value Chairs makes a mass-market product with more competition on price. This does not mean Atelier is more profitable: it may sell fewer chairs and have higher wages and overheads. It shows that Atelier depends more on skill and brand than on cheap inputs.

## Ways to increase added value

Added value rises if the price rises more than input costs, or if input costs fall. Typical methods are:

- **improve quality or design** and charge more;
- **build the brand** so that customers pay a premium;
- **add services**, such as delivery, installation or guarantees;
- **do more of the work in-house**, rather than buying in a finished component;
- **cut wasteful or expensive inputs** by negotiating with suppliers or finding alternatives;
- **target a niche market** where customers pay more.

### Worked example 3: testing an improvement

Hearth considers switching to organic flour. Bought-in cost rises from £0.90 to **£1.10** and the price rises from £2.80 to **£3.30**. Sales are expected to fall by **8%** to 92,000 loaves.

1. New added value per loaf = £3.30 − £1.10 = **£2.20** (up from £1.90, an increase of £0.30).
2. As a percentage of price = 2.20 ÷ 3.30 × 100 = **66.7%** (down slightly from 67.9%).
3. New total added value = 92,000 × £2.20 = **£202,400**.
4. Change = £202,400 − £190,000 = **+£12,400**.

**Interpretation.** Total added value rises by £12,400 (6.5%) even though volume falls, because each loaf earns £0.30 more. Per employee, if the number of staff stays at 8, added value rises from £23,750 to £25,300. The percentage of price is almost unchanged, which shows that the gain comes from a higher price and more total money, not from a better proportion. The decision depends on whether the 8% fall in sales is realistic. If sales fell by 15% to 85,000 loaves, total added value would be 85,000 × £2.20 = £187,000, below the starting £190,000. So the change is worthwhile only if customers accept the higher price without too large a fall in demand. Hearth should test the product in a few shops first.

## Added value in services

Services add value too. A hair salon charges **£36** for a cut and colour. Products, towels and laundry bought in cost **£6**, so the added value is **£30**, or 83.3% of the price. The percentage is high because most of the cost is the stylist's time, which is paid for out of the added value, not deducted from it. This is why service businesses often show high added value and yet modest profit: wages and rent take most of it. With 5,000 treatments a year, total added value is £150,000. If the salon has six staff, that is £25,000 per employee, a figure the owner could compare with another salon of the same size. It would be wrong to say the salon with the higher figure is better without also looking at its prices, quality and costs.

Added value also helps a manufacturer decide between making a component and buying it. If bought in, the component cost is deducted before added value is calculated. If made in-house, the materials, not the finished component, are bought in, so the business's own added value is greater. The decision depends on whether the extra wages and equipment cost less than the extra added value.

## Usefulness and limits

| Useful | Limits |
|---|---|
| Shows how much value the business itself creates, not just the revenue | It is not profit; it ignores wages, overheads and other costs |
| Allows comparison between products, businesses and over time | Depends on what is counted as a "bought-in" input |
| Helps decide where to add value (design, service, branding) | A high price may reflect market power, not effort |
| Shows the capacity to pay wages and make profit | Does not measure quality or customer satisfaction |
| Useful to stakeholders: lenders, investors and staff can see the scope for wages and profit | Can be misleading if inputs are bought at unusually low prices |

**Stakeholders.** Owners and shareholders use it to see how much value is available for profit. Employees may see it as the pool from which wages are paid. Suppliers may be pressed to cut prices, since lower input costs raise added value. Customers pay for the added value in the price, so they get quality or convenience in return.

A balanced judgement: added value is a useful measure of what a business contributes, and it points to ways of raising margins, but it must be used with profit, costs and cash flow to judge overall performance.

## Common mistakes

- Subtracting wages or rent when calculating added value.
- Confusing added value with profit.
- Using total cost instead of bought-in cost.
- Forgetting to multiply by units when asked for total added value.
- Assuming that higher added value always means higher profit.

## Exam technique

Write the formula, substitute, and state the unit (£ per unit or £ in total). For "evaluate", give a benefit and a limit and a judgement that uses the figures in the case.
