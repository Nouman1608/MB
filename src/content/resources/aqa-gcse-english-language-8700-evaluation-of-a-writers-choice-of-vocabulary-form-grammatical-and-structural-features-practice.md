## Section A — Short questions

**1.** In the sentence *"The rain hammered the tin roof all night"*, identify the word class of "hammered" and explain **one** effect of the choice. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference in connotation between *"the old man shuffled to the gate"* and *"the old man strolled to the gate"*. **[2]**

**3.** Read this original sentence: *"She ran past the church, past the shuttered bakery, past the bus stop where her father used to wait, and did not stop until the road ran out."* Name **one** grammatical feature and explain its effect. **[3]**

**4.** Rewrite this active sentence in the passive voice, then explain how the change alters the reader's view: *"The manager closed the factory without warning."* **[3]**

**5.** A writer opens a newspaper article with the question *"When did you last speak to your neighbour?"* Name the sentence function and give **two** effects on the reader. **[3]**

**6.** For each feature below, say whether it would be analysed as **language** or **structure**: (a) a simile describing a sunset; (b) a one-sentence paragraph placed after three long paragraphs; (c) a shift from present tense to past tense in the final paragraph, analysed as a move into memory. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the form of a private diary entry can shape the reader's response, giving **two** conventions. **[4]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** Read this original passage.

> *The market woke before the town did. Shutters rattled up, one after another, like a row of startled eyelids. By six,
> the air was thick with oranges and diesel and the shouts of men who had known each other for forty years. Nobody
> noticed the boy at the edge of the square. He had been there since four.*

How does the writer use language to describe the market? Analyse **two** language choices in developed points. **[6]**

**9.** Using the passage in Question 8, explain how the final **two** sentences change the focus of the passage, and evaluate the effect of this structural choice. **[4]**

**10.** Read this original opening of a short story, in three paragraphs.

> *Paragraph 1: A wide description of a seaside town in winter: closed cafés, empty benches, a grey sea.*
>
> *Paragraph 2: The focus narrows to a single lit window above a fish shop, where a woman is packing a suitcase.*
>
> *Paragraph 3: One sentence: "She had told no one she was leaving."*

How has the writer structured this opening to interest the reader? Refer to the beginning, the middle and the end. **[6]**

**11.** Read this original extract from a campaign article.

> *"We are told the library is a luxury. A luxury. Tell that to the pensioner who comes in each morning for the only
> warm room she can afford. Tell that to the child whose home has no books. Tell that to anyone who has ever needed a
> quiet place to think. We must not let it close."*

How does the writer use language and grammar to persuade the reader? Analyse **three** choices. **[9]**

**12.** A student writes about the passage in Question 11: *"The writer uses repetition and a modal verb. This is very effective and persuades the reader."* Rewrite this into **one** analytical and evaluative sentence that would earn more credit. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** "Hammered" is a verb [1]. It suggests the rain is violent and relentless, making the noise sound like a physical attack on the roof [1].

**2.** "Shuffled" connotes weakness, age or reluctance, with feet barely leaving the ground [1]; "strolled" connotes ease, relaxation and leisure, so the same man seems contented rather than frail [1].

**3.** A long sentence built from a list of "past" phrases (repetition, or a long multi-clause sentence) [1]. The repeated "past" mimics the rhythm of running and the speed of the places flashing by [1], and the delayed end, "until the road ran out", suggests she is fleeing as far as she possibly can [1].

**4.** Passive: "The factory was closed without warning" (with or without "by the manager") [1]. Without the agent, the manager disappears from the sentence [1], so responsibility is hidden and the closure seems to happen to the workers rather than being someone's decision [1].

**5.** Interrogative, a rhetorical question [1]. It addresses the reader directly and makes them reflect on their own behaviour [1], and it implies the honest answer is "a long time ago", introducing the article's concern about isolation [1].

**6.** (a) language [1]; (b) structure [1]; (c) structure [1].

**7.** Two conventions, each explained, up to 4 marks: first-person, private voice [1], which creates intimacy and the sense that the reader is overhearing honest thoughts [1]; dated entries written close to events [1], which give immediacy and a feeling that the writer does not yet know what will happen next [1]. Also creditable: informal or fragmented sentences suggesting haste or emotion.

**8.** Three marks for each of two developed points, up to 6. For each: a precise quotation [1], an accurate term or method [1] and a developed effect linked to the description of the market [1]. For example: the simile "like a row of startled eyelids" [1] personifies the shutters [1], making the market seem a living creature jolted awake, full of sudden energy [1]; the list "oranges and diesel and the shouts" combines a sensory mix of smell and sound [1] using repeated "and" (polysyndeton) [1], so the reader feels the senses being crowded and overwhelmed [1].

**9.** The final two sentences shift the focus from the busy, crowded market to a single, isolated boy [1]. The short simple sentences contrast with the dense description before them [1]. Effect: the reader is made to notice the person nobody else notices, which creates curiosity or unease about why he has been there "since four" [1]. Evaluation: this is effective because it turns a setting description into the start of a story, giving the passage its real subject at the last moment [1].

**10.** Award up to 6 marks:
- beginning: a wide, establishing view of an empty winter town [1], which sets a bleak, lonely atmosphere [1];
- middle: the focus narrows to one lit window, a zoom from setting to a single character [1], which directs the reader's attention and raises the question of what the woman is doing [1];
- end: a single-sentence paragraph withholds the reason and reveals the secret [1], creating a hook that makes the reader want to find out why she is leaving and whom she is leaving [1].

**11.** Three marks for each of three choices, up to 9. For each: identification with evidence [1], accurate term [1], developed effect on the reader [1]. For example:
- "A luxury." as a minor sentence repeating the opponents' word [1] (repetition, minor sentence) [1], making the claim sound absurd and scornful [1];
- "Tell that to..." repeated three times [1] (anaphora, imperative, rule of three) [1], challenging opponents directly and building a list of real people who would suffer, so the argument feels morally urgent [1];
- "We must not let it close" [1] (modal verb "must", inclusive pronoun "we") [1], turning the reader into part of a community with a duty to act, and ending on certainty [1].

**12.** A strong rewrite names the specific repetition with a quotation [1], explains its effect on the reader [1] and gives a reason for the judgement of effectiveness [1]. For example: "The triple imperative 'Tell that to...' is particularly effective because each repetition adds a vulnerable person who needs the library, so the reader is made to feel that calling it 'a luxury' is heartless."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technique without explaining what it does.
- Writing a generic effect that could apply to any passage.
- Discussing a simile in an answer about structure.
- Quoting too much to analyse any word precisely.
- Explaining without ever judging how effective a choice is.
