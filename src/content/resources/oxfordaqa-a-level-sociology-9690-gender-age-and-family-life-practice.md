These questions cover specification section **3.1.1.4 Gender, age and the experience of family
life** in OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690), Unit 1 Section A (Families). The
specification lists: "traditional" male and female roles, including the female expressive role
(emotional support and the triple shift) and the male instrumental or breadwinner role; changes to
these roles and reasons; the division of domestic labour and gender differences in power and
decision-making; violence against women and children; the status and roles of children in different
societies and over time, and child-centredness; adult children, including boomerang families; and
older people, including grandparent roles. Tariffs (2, 2, 4, 4, 8 and 20) mirror the 40-mark Section
A pattern used on the course's other Marlbridge practice pages. The specification asks students to
use examples from their own society or one they know in some extended answers.

## Questions

**1.** Define what is meant by the **expressive role**. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by a **boomerang family**. **[2]**

**3.** Identify and briefly explain **two** reasons why conjugal roles may have changed. **[4]**

**4.** Identify and briefly explain **two** ways in which the status of children differs between
societies. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **two** ways in which power and decision-making may be unequal within families.
**[8]**

**6.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that men and women now share
domestic labour equally. **[20]**

### Extra practice

**7.** Outline **two** roles that grandparents may play in families. **[4]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by **child-centredness**, and give **one** criticism of the view that
families have become child-centred. **[4]**

## Mark schemes

**1. [2]** The role, associated by Parsons with women, of providing emotional warmth, care and
support to family members and socialising children (2). Partial: "caring role" (1).

**2. [2]** A family in which adult children return to live in the parental home after a period of
independence, for example after university or because of housing costs or unemployment (2).

**3. [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each (1 identify, 1 explain):
- **Women's employment** — more women in paid work bargain for more sharing at home.
- **Feminist ideas and laws** on equality change expectations.
- **Decline of manual industry** — the male breadwinner role weakens.
- **Smaller families and technology** reduce domestic workload and allow more flexibility.
- **Changing attitudes** among younger couples towards fatherhood.

**4. [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:
- **Work** — in some societies children contribute paid or unpaid labour; in others they are
  excluded from work by law and seen as dependent.
- **Education** — length of compulsory schooling varies, affecting how long childhood lasts.
- **Rights and protection** — legal rights and child-protection laws differ.
- **Age of marriage and adulthood** — transition to adult roles comes earlier in some societies.
- **Change over time** — Philippe Ariès argued "childhood" as a separate stage developed in Europe
  from the 17th century onwards.

**5. [8]** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify, explain, develop with evidence, apply):
- **Decision-making** — Stephen Edgell's study of middle-class couples (1980) found husbands made
  the major, important decisions (moving house, finance) while wives made minor everyday ones.
- **Money management** — Jan Pahl's research (*Money and Marriage*, 1989) found that control of
  money was often unequal, with men more likely to control or have access to more of the family's
  money, even where incomes were pooled.
- **Violence** — violence against women and children is a form of power; R. Emerson Dobash and
  Russell Dobash's *Violence Against Wives* (1979) linked it to patriarchal authority.
- **Economic dependence** — a non-earning partner has less bargaining power.

**6. [20]** Levels: 17–20 a detailed, analytical answer, using a range of evidence and examples from
a named society, with sustained evaluation and a justified conclusion; 13–16 good knowledge with
some evaluation; 9–12 reasonable knowledge, limited evaluation; 5–8 basic, mainly descriptive;
1–4 fragments.

*For the view:*
- More women in paid work and dual-earner households.
- "New man" and involved fatherhood; younger couples express egalitarian attitudes.
- Technology and smaller families reduce the domestic workload.
- Postmodern and individualisation arguments: relationships are negotiated rather than fixed
  (Carol Smart's work on personal life).
- Judith Stacey (*Brave New Families*, 1990) found women creating more equal "divorce-extended"
  families.

*Against the view:*
- Ann Oakley (*The Sociology of Housework*, 1974) found little evidence of shared housework.
- Women's **triple shift** — paid work, domestic labour and emotional work — and men "helping"
  rather than sharing responsibility.
- Parsons' instrumental–expressive division still shapes expectations in many societies.
- Engels (*The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*, 1884) linked women's
  subordination in the family to private property — a reminder that inequality is structural.
- Göran Therborn's comparative work shows patriarchal family systems remain strong in many
  regions of the world.
- Cultural and class differences: equality varies greatly between societies and groups.

*Application:* reward specific examples from a named society — for example time-use evidence, laws on
parental leave, or cultural expectations of women's roles.

*Conclusion:* reward a supported judgement, for example that attitudes have moved further than
practice.

**7. [4]** Two roles, 2 marks each: childcare while parents work; financial support; passing on
culture, language and religion; advice and emotional support; in some societies, authority as head
of the household; or being cared for when dependent.

**8. [4]** Child-centredness: family life organised around children's needs, with more time, money
and emotional investment in each child (2). Criticism (2): conflict views argue adults still closely
control children's time, space and bodies; or child-centredness varies by class and society, and
many children worldwide still work or face poverty.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sociologists does 3.1.1 suggest?** The specification says students would benefit from
studying Engels, Oakley, Parsons, Smart, Stacey and Therborn; other relevant sociologists are also
credited.

**Do I need examples from my own society?** The specification requires examples from students' own
society or one they know in selected extended answers, so practise naming them in question 6.

**What is the triple shift?** The combination of paid work, domestic labour and emotional work that
many women carry; the specification names it as part of the female expressive role.
