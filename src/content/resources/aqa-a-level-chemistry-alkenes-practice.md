These questions cover section **3.3.4 Alkenes** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification
(7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards: 3.3.4.1 Structure,
bonding and reactivity, 3.3.4.2 Addition reactions of alkenes and 3.3.4.3 Addition polymers. All of it is
AS content, so nothing here is A-level only. The topic is examined on AS Paper 2 and A-level Paper 2, and
A-level Paper 3 can test any content.

## Questions

**1.** Which carbocation is the most stable?

- A CH₃CH₂⁺
- B CH₃CH₂CH₂⁺
- C (CH₃)₂CH⁺
- D (CH₃)₂C⁺CH(CH₃)₂

**[1]**

**2.** Explain why ethene reacts with electrophiles but ethane does not. **[3]**

**3.** Propene reacts with bromine at room temperature.

**(a)** Name the type of mechanism. **[1]**
**(b)** Outline the mechanism for this reaction. **[4]**
**(c)** Name the organic product. **[1]**

**4.** Describe a chemical test that distinguishes hexane from hex-1-ene. Give the result for each
compound. **[3]**

**5.** Pent-1-ene reacts with hydrogen bromide. Give the structural formulas of the major product and the
minor product. **[2]**

**6.** Propene reacts with cold, concentrated sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Identify the electrophile. **[1]**
**(b)** Outline the mechanism for the formation of the major product. **[4]**

**7.** (calculator) A sample of 1.12 g of a non-cyclic alkene reacts with exactly 3.20 g of bromine,
Br₂. One Br₂ molecule adds to each alkene molecule.

**(a)** Calculate the Mr of the alkene and deduce its molecular formula. **[3]**
**(b)** Name two alkenes, which are structural isomers, that have this molecular formula. **[2]**

**8.** This question is about addition polymers.

**(a)** 1,1-dichloroethene is CH₂=CCl₂. Draw the repeating unit of the polymer it forms and name the
polymer. **[2]**
**(b)** A section of another polymer is –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–. Draw its repeating unit and
name its monomer. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the polymer in part (b) is unreactive. **[2]**

**9.** Poly(ethene) and poly(chloroethene) are both solids at room temperature.

**(a)** Explain why poly(ethene) is a solid even though only van der Waals forces act between its
chains. **[2]**
**(b)** Name one other type of intermolecular force between poly(chloroethene) chains and explain why
it is present. **[2]**
**(c)** Poly(chloroethene) is used both for rigid window frames and for flexible electrical cable
insulation. Explain how the same polymer can suit both uses. **[2]**

**10.** (calculator) A sample of poly(propene) has an average Mr of 2.10 × 10⁵.

**(a)** Calculate the average number of propene units in one chain. **[2]**
**(b)** State the atom economy of addition polymerisation and give a reason. **[1]**

**11.** Hex-3-ene, CH₃CH₂CH=CHCH₂CH₃, and 2-methylpent-1-ene, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃, are
isomers.

**(a)** Hex-3-ene gives only one product with hydrogen bromide. Name the product and explain why only
one product forms. **[2]**
**(b)** 2-methylpent-1-ene gives two products with hydrogen bromide. Name the major product and the
minor product. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain, with reference to the intermediates, why more of the major product forms. **[3]**
**(d)** State why each alkene gives only one product with bromine. **[1]**

**12.** (calculator) A student bubbles 0.700 g of ethene into excess bromine and obtains 3.99 g of pure
1,2-dibromoethane.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(c)** State the atom economy of this reaction. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest two reasons why the percentage yield is less than 100%. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. It is tertiary: three alkyl groups release electron density towards the positive
carbon. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* C is the usual wrong choice; check how many carbon groups are bonded to the C⁺, not
how many carbons the ion has in total.

**2.** The C=C double bond in ethene is a centre of high electron density [1]. Electrophiles are electron
pair acceptors, so they are attracted to it and accept a pair of electrons [1]. Ethane is saturated: it
has only single C–C and C–H bonds and no region of high electron density [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Ethene has a double bond" alone does not explain anything; the phrase "high electron
density" is what links the structure to the attack.

**3. (a)** **Electrophilic addition** [1]
**(b)** Br₂ shown with an induced dipole, Brδ+–Brδ− (as it approaches the high electron density) [1];
arrow from the C=C bond to the δ+ Br and arrow from the Br–Br bond to the δ− Br [1]; carbocation
CH₃C⁺HCH₂Br with Br⁻ [1]; arrow from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺ [1].
**(c)** **1,2-dibromopropane** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Arrows must be placed precisely: an arrow that starts from a carbon atom, rather than
from the C=C bond, does not show electrons leaving the double bond and is unlikely to be credited.

**4.** Add bromine water and shake [1]. Hex-1-ene: orange to **colourless** [1]. Hexane: **stays orange**
(no change) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Goes clear" is not the same as colourless, and a result is needed for both
compounds, not just the alkene.

**5.** Major: **CH₃CH₂CH₂CHBrCH₃** (2-bromopentane) [1]. Minor: **CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂Br** (1-bromopentane)
[1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for structural formulas; a correct name with no formula may not be
accepted when the command asks for the formula.

**6. (a)** A hydrogen of H₂SO₄ (Hδ+) [1]
**(b)** Arrow from the C=C bond to an H of H₂SO₄ and arrow from that O–H bond to the O [1]; secondary
carbocation CH₃C⁺HCH₃ [1]; hydrogensulfate ion with a lone pair on the negative O and an arrow from it to
the C⁺ [1]; product **CH₃CH(OSO₂OH)CH₃** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The bond to carbon must come from the negative oxygen; showing the S, or the OH
oxygen, bonding to carbon gives the wrong structure and loses the final marks.

**7. (a)** Mr(Br₂) = 159.8; n(Br₂) = 3.20 / 159.8 = 0.0200 mol [1]. n(alkene) = 0.0200 mol (1 : 1), so
Mr = 1.12 / 0.0200 = 55.9, which is **56** to the nearest whole number [1]. CₙH₂ₙ: 14n = 56, n = 4, so
**C₄H₈** [1].
**(b)** Any two of: **but-1-ene**, **but-2-ene**, **2-methylpropene** [1] [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An error in n(Br₂) can usually be carried forward to the Mr, but the formula mark
needs a whole-number n consistent with CₙH₂ₙ.

**8. (a)** Repeating unit with C–C, two H on one carbon, two Cl on the other, extension bonds and n:
**–[CH₂–CCl₂]ₙ–** [1]; **poly(1,1-dichloroethene)** [1].
**(b)** Repeating unit **–[CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)]ₙ–** [1]; monomer **but-2-ene** (CH₃CH=CHCH₃) [1].
**(c)** The chain is saturated, with no C=C bond for electrophiles to attack [1]. It has only strong,
non-polar C–C and C–H bonds [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) a three- or four-carbon repeating unit is wrong even if it repeats; the unit
must be the two carbons that came from one monomer.

**9. (a)** The chains are very long, with very many electrons [1], so there are many van der Waals
interactions along each chain and the total attraction between chains is large [1].
**(b)** **Permanent dipole–dipole forces** [1]; the C–Cl bond is polar because Cl is more
electronegative than C [1].
**(c)** Window frames use unplasticised PVC, which is rigid [1]. For cable insulation a plasticiser is
added; its molecules push the chains apart and weaken the forces between them, so the chains slide and
the PVC is flexible [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) talking about breaking covalent bonds when the polymer melts contradicts the
answer; the forces are between chains, not within them.

**10. (a)** Mr(C₃H₆) = 3(12.0) + 6(1.0) = 42.0 [1]; 2.10 × 10⁵ / 42.0 = **5000** [1].
**(b)** **100%**, because the polymer is the only product [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The monomer and the repeating unit have the same formula, so dividing by the
monomer's Mr is valid; using Mr(CH₂) = 14.0 gives an answer three times too large.

**11. (a)** **3-bromohexane** [1]. Hex-3-ene is symmetrical, so both possible carbocations are identical
and give the same product [1].
**(b)** Major: **2-bromo-2-methylpentane** [1]. Minor: **1-bromo-2-methylpentane** [1].
**(c)** The major product forms via a tertiary carbocation and the minor via a primary carbocation [1].
Alkyl groups release electron density and stabilise the positive charge; the tertiary carbocation has
three, so it is more stable [1]. The more stable carbocation forms more readily, so more product forms
through it [1].
**(d)** Both atoms that add are the same (Br), so either route gives the same product [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) needs the intermediates named as tertiary and primary; "the tertiary
product is more stable" talks about the product, not the carbocation, and misses the point.

**12. (a)** **C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂** (CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br) [1]
**(b)** n(C₂H₄) = 0.700 / 28.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]. Mr(C₂H₄Br₂) = 187.8; theoretical mass = 0.0250 × 187.8
= 4.695 g [1]. Yield = 3.99 / 4.695 × 100 = **85.0%** [1].
**(c)** **100%** [1]
**(d)** Any two: some ethene passes through without reacting; product lost during transfer or
purification; impurities in the ethene [1] [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Allow error carried forward from a wrong Mr in (b); give the yield to 3 significant
figures to match the data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Curly arrows that start from an atom rather than from the C=C bond or a lone pair.
- Missing the induced dipole on Br₂ or the δ+/δ− on HBr.
- Carbocation + on a carbon that does not match the product given.
- Hydrogensulfate bonded through S or the OH oxygen.
- Explaining major products using the product's stability instead of the carbocation's.
- Saying Br₂ gives major and minor products.
- Repeating units longer than two main-chain carbons, or drawn with C=C.
- Describing van der Waals forces as covalent bonds that break on melting.
- Forgetting that plasticiser works by weakening forces between chains.
- Rounding Mr too early in a moles calculation.
