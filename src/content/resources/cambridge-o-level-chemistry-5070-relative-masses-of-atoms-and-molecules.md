Section 3.2 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has two outcomes. You must describe **relative atomic mass**, Ar, "as the average mass of the isotopes of an element compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of ¹²C", and define **relative molecular mass**, Mr, "as the sum of the relative atomic masses", with **relative formula mass** (also given the symbol Mr) used for ionic compounds. Both are required for every 5070 candidate.

These two ideas underpin every calculation in section 3.3. The [Formulae, Equations and the Mole guide](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/) gives the definitions and one example. This guide explains why the carbon-12 scale is used, then trains the formula-mass skill on the formulae where errors are most common: brackets, hydrated salts and working backwards.

## Why relative masses?

A single atom has an extremely small mass. Quoting atomic masses in grams would mean numbers with many zeros after the decimal point. Chemists instead compare the masses of atoms with a **standard atom**, so that the numbers are convenient.

The standard is the **carbon-12** atom, which is given a mass of exactly 12 units. One-twelfth of its mass is therefore exactly 1 unit, and every other atom's mass is compared with this.

Because they are comparisons (ratios), relative masses have **no units**. Write "Ar of Mg = 24", not "24 g".

## Relative atomic mass, Ar

**Relative atomic mass** is the average mass of the isotopes of an element compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.

The word **average** matters. Most elements are a mixture of isotopes, so Ar is a weighted average that takes account of how common each isotope is. That is why many values are not whole numbers: chlorine is 35.5 because it is a mixture of chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Section 2.3 shows the calculation; see the [isotopes study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-isotopes/).

**Worked example 1.** Explain what is meant by "the relative atomic mass of magnesium is 24".

*Answer.* The average mass of a magnesium atom, taking account of its isotopes, is 24 times one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Equivalently, an average magnesium atom is twice as heavy as a carbon-12 atom.

The second sentence shows that you can **compare** atoms directly from their Ar values: a sulfur atom (Ar 32) is twice as heavy as an oxygen atom (Ar 16), and a calcium atom (Ar 40) is ten times as heavy as a helium atom (Ar 4) on average.

## Relative molecular mass and relative formula mass

**Relative molecular mass**, Mr, is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in one molecule. For ionic compounds, which have no molecules, the same sum over the formula is called **relative formula mass** (symbol also Mr).

| Substance | Structure | Name of Mr |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂, C₂H₅OH, Cl₂ | simple molecular | relative molecular mass |
| NaCl, Fe₂O₃, Ca(NO₃)₂ | ionic | relative formula mass |

### Method

1. Write the formula correctly.
2. Count the atoms of each element, multiplying out any brackets.
3. Multiply each count by the Ar of that element.
4. Add the results.

The examples below use: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Pb = 207.

**Worked example 2 (simple molecule).** Ethanol, C₂H₅OH.

Atoms: C 2, H 6 (five plus one), O 1.
Mr = (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 24 + 6 + 16 = **46**.

Count every hydrogen, including the one in the OH group.

**Worked example 3 (brackets).** Calcium nitrate, Ca(NO₃)₂.

The subscript 2 multiplies everything inside the bracket: N 2, O 6.
Mr = 40 + (2 × 14) + (6 × 16) = 40 + 28 + 96 = **164**.

A common wrong answer is 102, from ignoring the bracket and working out CaNO₃.

**Worked example 4 (two sets of brackets).** Aluminium sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃.

Atoms: Al 2, S 3, O 12.
Mr = (2 × 27) + (3 × 32) + (12 × 16) = 54 + 96 + 192 = **342**.

**Worked example 5 (hydrated salt).** Magnesium sulfate crystals, MgSO₄·7H₂O.

The dot means "combined with": add the mass of seven water molecules to the mass of MgSO₄. Each H₂O has Mr = 18.
Mr = 24 + 32 + (4 × 16) + (7 × 18) = 24 + 32 + 64 + 126 = **246**.

Do not multiply the 7 into the MgSO₄ part. It applies only to the water.

## Elements: Ar or Mr?

For an element, which value you need depends on the particles it is made of.

| Element | Particles | Value to use |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen gas | O₂ molecules | Mr = 2 × 16 = 32 |
| Chlorine gas | Cl₂ molecules | Mr = 2 × 35.5 = 71 |
| Bromine | Br₂ molecules | Mr = 2 × 80 = 160 |
| Magnesium | atoms in a metallic lattice | Ar = 24 |
| Carbon (diamond or graphite) | atoms in a giant structure | Ar = 12 |

A question about "the relative molecular mass of oxygen" means O₂, so the answer is 32, not 16. Using 16 is one of the most common slips in the whole of Topic 3, and it carries through into every later calculation.

### Comparing molecules

Mr values compare the masses of molecules in the same way that Ar values compare atoms. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) and propane (C₃H₈) both have Mr = 44, so one molecule of each has the same mass on average, even though they contain different atoms. A molecule of glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ (Mr 180), is ten times as heavy as a molecule of water (Mr 18).

## Working backwards

Questions sometimes give an Mr and ask for an unknown.

**Worked example 6 (unknown element).** A metal chloride has the formula XCl₂ and Mr = 111. Identify X.

Mass of two chlorines = 2 × 35.5 = 71. So Ar of X = 111 − 71 = 40. X is **calcium**.

**Worked example 7 (water of crystallisation).** Hydrated sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄·*n*H₂O, has Mr = 322. Find *n*.

Mr of Na₂SO₄ = (2 × 23) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 142.
18*n* = 322 − 142 = 180, so *n* = **10**.

## Common mistakes

- Giving relative masses units (g or g/mol). Ar and Mr have no units. (Molar mass, in g/mol, belongs to section 3.3.)
- Defining Ar without "average" or without "1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12".
- Multiplying only the last atom inside a bracket: Ca(NO₃)₂ treated as CaNO₆.
- Forgetting a hydrogen in OH, or the water in a hydrated salt.
- Using proton numbers instead of relative atomic masses (for example 8 for oxygen instead of 16).
- Rounding Ar values the question gives, such as 35.5 for chlorine.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** Ar: learn the syllabus wording, including "average" and "carbon-12".
- **Define** Mr: "the sum of the relative atomic masses" of the atoms in the formula.
- **Calculate**: write the atom counts and the multiplications, not just the answer. A method mark is often available.
- Use the Ar values printed on the Periodic Table provided in the exam, or those given in the question.
