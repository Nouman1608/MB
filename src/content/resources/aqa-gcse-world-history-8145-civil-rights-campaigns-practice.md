Content tested: "Segregation laws; Martin Luther King and peaceful protests; Malcolm X and the Black Power Movement; Civil Rights Acts of 1964 and 1968."

## Section A: knowledge and description

**1.** Name two organisations that campaigned for civil rights in the 1950s and 1960s. **[2]**

**2.** Describe two features of segregation laws in the southern states. **[4]**

**3.** Describe two features of the Montgomery Bus Boycott. **[4]**

**4.** Describe two features of the Black Panther Party. **[4]**

## Section B: change, interpretations and judgement

**5.** In what ways were the lives of Black Americans affected by the Civil Rights Act of 1964? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**6.** In what ways was the civil rights movement affected by the ideas of Malcolm X? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**7.** In what ways were civil rights campaigns affected by television coverage? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**Interpretation A** *(invented)* -- From a biography of Martin Luther King written in 1990 by an American journalist:
"King's genius was to make America look at itself. Every time a peaceful marcher was struck down on the evening news, millions of white Americans saw segregation for what it was. Without his discipline of non-violence, there would have been no Civil Rights Act."

**Interpretation B** *(invented)* -- From an interview with a former Black Panther, published in a magazine in 1971:
"The marches won a seat at the lunch counter, but they did not put food on the table in Harlem or Oakland. Malcolm told the truth: you cannot beg for freedom. It was when Black people stood up for themselves that the government began to listen."

**8.** How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about how civil rights were won? Explain your answer using Interpretations A and B. **[4]**

**9.** Which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about how civil rights were won? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what Interpretations A and B say. **[8]**

**10.** Which of the following was the more important reason why the civil rights movement changed in the mid-1960s: the ideas of Malcolm X; poverty and discrimination in northern cities? Explain your answer with reference to both reasons. **[12]**

**11.** Which of the following was the more important achievement of the civil rights campaigns: Brown v Board of Education (1954); the Civil Rights Act of 1964? Explain your answer with reference to both achievements. **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** "Describe": each of two features identified [1] and developed [1]. "In what ways": each of two ways identified [1], evidenced [1] and explained as a change [2]. Question 1-style: difference [2], support from A [1] and B [1]. Question 3-style: each interpretation tested [3] + [3], judgement [2]. 12-mark essays: each bullet explained with evidence [4] + [4], linked judgement [4].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: NAACP; Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC); Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC); Nation of Islam; Black Panther Party; Congress of Racial Equality.

**2.** Feature one [2]: separation in public life [1]; Jim Crow laws separated Black and white people in schools, buses, restaurants, parks and hospitals, under the "separate but equal" principle of Plessy v Ferguson (1896) [1]. Feature two [2]: denial of voting rights [1]; literacy tests, poll taxes and intimidation kept most Black Southerners from voting [1].

**3.** Feature one [2]: it began after Rosa Parks was arrested on 1 December 1955 [1] for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white passenger [1]. Feature two [2]: Black residents refused to use the buses for over a year [1], organised car-sharing and walked to work, until the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional in 1956 [1]. Also creditable: King emerged as its leader.

**4.** Feature one [2]: it was founded in Oakland, California, in 1966 [1] and organised armed patrols to monitor how police treated Black residents [1]. Feature two [2]: it ran community programmes [1], such as free breakfasts for children, stressing self-reliance in Black neighbourhoods [1].

**5.** Way one [4]: segregation in public places ended in law [1]; the Act banned segregation in restaurants, hotels, cinemas and other public places [1]; so Black Americans in the South could legally use facilities that had been closed to them, ending the daily humiliation of Jim Crow [2]. Way two [4]: protection at work [1]; it outlawed job discrimination on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission [1]; so Black workers gained a legal right to challenge unfair hiring and pay, though change was slow and the Act did not tackle housing or voting [2].

**6.** Way one [4]: it challenged the aim of integration [1]; Malcolm X argued, as a leader of the Nation of Islam, that Black Americans should separate from white society rather than join it [1]; so the movement now debated its goal, and many young activists questioned whether integration was possible [2]. Way two [4]: it challenged non-violence [1]; he defended self-defence "by any means necessary" [1]; so after his death in 1965 his ideas influenced Black Power and groups such as the Black Panthers, dividing the movement over methods [2].

**7.** Way one [4]: campaigns gained national sympathy [1]; television showed Bull Connor's dogs and fire hoses used on demonstrators, including children, in Birmingham in 1963 [1]; so Americans outside the South saw the brutality of segregation, and the movement gained support from people who had ignored it [2]. Way two [4]: pressure on government increased [1]; after Birmingham Kennedy proposed a civil rights bill, and televised violence at Selma in 1965 preceded the Voting Rights Act [1]; so campaigners deliberately chose places where confrontation would be filmed, making the media part of their strategy [2].

**8.** Difference [2]: A argues that civil rights were won by King's non-violent protest, which changed white public opinion, while B argues that peaceful protest achieved little and progress came when Black people "stood up for themselves". Support from A [1]: "Without his discipline of non-violence, there would have been no Civil Rights Act." Support from B [1]: "you cannot beg for freedom".

**9.** A tested [3]: televised violence at Birmingham (1963) led Kennedy to propose a civil rights bill; the March on Washington built support; the 1964 Act followed; Selma (1965) preceded the Voting Rights Act. But A gives all the credit to King, ignoring the NAACP's court victories, student sit-ins and Johnson's role. B tested [3]: B is right that the Acts did not end poverty in northern cities such as Harlem and Oakland, where riots showed deep anger; Black Power encouraged pride and community programmes. But the major laws of 1964 and 1965 came before Black Power spread, so B's claim that the government listened only after Black people "stood up" does not fit the dates. Judgement [2]: A is more convincing about how *legal* rights were won, because the timing of the Acts matches the non-violent campaigns; B is more convincing about what remained unsolved.

**10.** The ideas of Malcolm X [4]: he rejected integration and non-violence; his speeches and autobiography inspired younger activists; after 1965 Carmichael and the Black Panthers took up self-defence and Black pride. Northern poverty and discrimination [4]: de facto segregation in housing and jobs was untouched by the 1964 Act; unemployment and police treatment caused anger; riots in Watts (1965) and other cities showed that Southern-style campaigns did not fit northern problems. Linked judgement [4]: for example, northern conditions were more important because they explained why Malcolm X's ideas found an audience after 1965; or Malcolm X's ideas were more important because they gave anger a language and a programme. The judgement should show how the two connected.

**11.** Brown (1954) [4]: overturned "separate but equal" in schools; gave the movement legal backing and confidence; but many Southern states resisted, as at Little Rock (1957), and schools stayed largely segregated for years. 1964 Act [4]: banned segregation in all public places and job discrimination; backed by federal power, including withholding funds from segregated schools; made Brown more enforceable. Linked judgement [4]: for example, the 1964 Act was more important because it applied across public life and carried federal enforcement, whereas Brown was a court ruling that depended on others to act; or Brown was more important because it destroyed the legal basis of segregation on which everything later was built.
