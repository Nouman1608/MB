## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the government objective of stability of the current account. **[2]**

**2.** Which policy is most likely to reduce a current account surplus?

A: a cut in government spending

B: a rise in income tax

C: a cut in import tariffs

D: a new subsidy for exporters **[1]**

**3.** Country P imports 400,000 units of a good at a world price of 20 each. It imposes a tariff of 25%, and imports fall to 340,000 units. The world price does not change.

**(a)** Calculate the amount paid to foreign suppliers before and after the tariff. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the tariff revenue. **[1]**

**4.** Country Q cuts government spending. National income falls by 9 billion and the marginal propensity to import is 0.2.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in imports. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the government may regard this as a costly way to improve the current account. **[2]**

**5.** Explain one way in which supply-side policy can improve a country's non-price competitiveness. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why an export subsidy may lead to retaliation by trading partners. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Country L raises its interest rates. Lower spending at home reduces imports by 4 billion. The currency appreciates and the value of exports, previously 60 billion, falls by 3%. Cheaper imports also raise import spending by 1 billion.

**(a)** Calculate the net effect on the current account. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the net effect is smaller than the first effect suggests. **[2]**

**8.** Country M has a current account deficit of 6% of GDP. Its economy is booming and inflation is above target. Explain why contractionary monetary policy may be a suitable response. **[3]**

**9.** Country N has a current account deficit caused by low productivity. Unemployment is high.

**(a)** Explain why contractionary fiscal policy may be unsuitable. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how supply-side policy could reduce the deficit. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how expansionary fiscal policy could reduce a current account surplus. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether protectionist policies are an effective way to correct a current account deficit. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Avoiding current account deficits or surpluses that are large relative to GDP [1] and that persist over many years; a zero balance every year is not required [1].

**2.** **C** [1]. Lower tariffs make imports cheaper, so import spending rises and the surplus falls. A and B reduce imports, and D raises exports, so each would enlarge the surplus.

**3.** **(a)** Before: 400,000 × 20 = **8.0 million** [1]. After: 340,000 × 20 = **6.8 million** [1].

**(b)** Tariff per unit = 0.25 × 20 = 5; revenue = 340,000 × 5 = **1.7 million** [1].

**4.** **(a)** 0.2 × 9 = **1.8 billion** [1].

**(b)** Only 1.8 billion of the 9 billion fall in income comes off imports [1]; the remaining 7.2 billion is lost output of home-produced goods, which means lower growth and fewer jobs [1].

**5.** For example, support for technological improvement helps firms develop better designs and more reliable products [1], so foreign buyers choose them for quality rather than price, raising exports and reducing the attraction of imports [1]. (Training that raises product quality or after-sales service is also accepted.)

**6.** An export subsidy lets domestic firms sell abroad at lower prices than they otherwise could [1], which takes sales from producers in partner countries, so their governments may protect their industries with tariffs or subsidies of their own [1].

**7.** **(a)** Fall in exports = 0.03 × 60 = 1.8 billion [1]. Net effect = +4 − 1.8 − 1 = **+1.2 billion** [1].

**(b)** Higher rates attract money from abroad and the currency appreciates [1], so exports become dearer and imports cheaper, which offsets much of the improvement from lower domestic spending [1].

**8.** The deficit is caused by excess spending in a boom, so imports are high [1]. Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding, reducing consumption and investment, including spending on imports [1]. Because inflation is above target, lower AD also reduces inflationary pressure, so the policy helps two objectives at once [1].

**9.** **(a)** The cause is low productivity, not excess demand, so cutting AD does not tackle it [1]; with unemployment already high, lower AD would cut output and raise unemployment further [1].

**(b)** Training and support for technology raise output per worker [1], which lowers unit costs and improves price competitiveness [1], so exports become cheaper abroad and home goods more attractive than imports, raising exports and reducing imports [1].

**10.** Lower taxes or higher government spending raise disposable income and AD [1]. Households and firms spend more, and part of the extra spending goes on imports [1]. Higher imports reduce the surplus on trade in goods and services, and so the current account surplus [1].

**11.** Effective: tariffs and quotas reduce import volumes quickly [1]; tariffs raise revenue that could fund other policies [1]; temporary protection can help a new or struggling industry until it becomes competitive [1].

Not effective: partners may retaliate, reducing exports and possibly leaving the account worse [1]; higher prices of imported inputs raise costs for exporters, harming competitiveness [1]; protection does not tackle causes such as low productivity, so the deficit returns when protection ends [1]; consumers face higher prices and less choice [1]; trade agreements may forbid new barriers.

Judgement: protection may narrow a deficit in the short run, but it is rarely effective on its own because of retaliation and because it ignores the cause; its effect depends on the elasticity of demand for imports and on partners' responses [1].
