These notes condense section **3.1.4.2 Calorimetry** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Calorimetry study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-calorimetry/) explains each step with worked examples, and the [Calorimetry practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-calorimetry-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- **q = mcΔT**, where m is the mass of the substance with a temperature change ΔT and a specific heat capacity c.
- Use it to calculate the **molar enthalpy change** and in related calculations.
- You will **not** be expected to recall c.
- Report answers to a suitable number of **significant figures**.
- Required practical 2: measurement of an enthalpy change.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specific heat capacity, c | Energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 K |
| Calorimeter | Apparatus for measuring heat changes |
| q | Heat energy transferred, in joules |
| ΔT | Temperature change, in K or °C |
| Molar enthalpy change | Enthalpy change per mole of the named substance |
| Limiting reagent | The reagent that is used up first |

## Equation and units

```
q = m c ΔT          q in J; m in g; c in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹; ΔT in K or °C
ΔH = ± q ÷ n        q in kJ (÷ 1000); n in mol; ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹
```

- m is the mass of the **water or solution** that changes temperature, not of the reactants weighed out.
- For dilute solutions, assume density 1.00 g cm⁻³ (1 cm³ = 1 g).
- Sign: temperature **rises** means exothermic, ΔH **negative**. Temperature **falls** means endothermic, ΔH **positive**.

## Method in five steps

1. Find the mass that changes temperature, m.
2. Find ΔT.
3. q = mcΔT in J, then ÷ 1000 for kJ.
4. n of the limiting reagent (or the named substance).
5. ΔH = q ÷ n, with the correct sign and unit.

## Worked reminders

**Neutralisation.** 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl + 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, temperature rise 13.4 °C.
m = 50.0 g; q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 13.4 = 2801 J = 2.801 kJ.
n(HCl) = 2.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0500 mol.
ΔH = −2.801 ÷ 0.0500 = **−56.0 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Dissolving.** 2.00 g of KCl (Mᵣ 74.6) in 40.0 g of water, temperature falls 2.8 °C.
q = 40.0 × 4.18 × 2.8 = 468 J = 0.468 kJ; n = 0.02681 mol.
ΔH = +0.468 ÷ 0.02681 = **+17.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Displacement.** 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄ with excess zinc, rise 45.0 °C.
q = 25.0 × 4.18 × 45.0 = 4.70 kJ; n(CuSO₄) = 0.0250 mol (limiting).
ΔH = **−188 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Combustion.** 100 g of water heated by 12.5 °C by burning 0.46 g of ethanol (Mᵣ 46.0).
q = 5.225 kJ; n = 0.0100 mol; ΔH = **−523 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Finding c.** 2.50 kJ raises 200 g of a liquid by 6.0 K: c = 2500 ÷ (200 × 6.0) = **2.08 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹**.

## Why combustion experiments give low values

| Error | Effect |
|---|---|
| Heat loss to surroundings | Smaller temperature rise; ΔH less negative |
| Incomplete combustion | Less energy released per mole |
| Heat absorbed by the can | Water gains less heat |
| Fuel evaporates from the wick | Mass loss overestimates fuel burned |
| Non-standard conditions | Different enthalpy from the standard value |

**Improve:** draught shield or lid, burner close to the can, stirring, insulation, repeat and average, use a bomb calorimeter.

## Solution experiments: practical points

- Use a polystyrene cup with a lid for insulation.
- Record the temperature for a few minutes before mixing.
- Stir and read at regular intervals after mixing.
- Plot temperature against time and **extrapolate** the cooling line back to the time of mixing to find the maximum temperature change.
- Measure volumes with a pipette or burette for accuracy.

## Uncertainty

- Uncertainty in ΔT from two readings of ±0.05 °C is ±0.10 °C.
- Percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ reading) × 100. For ΔT = 8.0 °C, 0.10 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 1.3%.
- A larger temperature rise reduces the percentage uncertainty. Use more concentrated solutions or more reactant (within safe limits).
- Give the final answer to the number of significant figures of the least precise measurement.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the solid reactant in q = mcΔT.
- Not dividing q by 1000 before calculating kJ mol⁻¹.
- Wrong sign of ΔH.
- Using the excess reagent for n.
- Writing "human error" instead of a named error.
- Not saying which direction an error shifts the answer.

## Self-test

1. Calculate q when 150 g of water rises in temperature by 8.0 °C (c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹).
2. A reaction releases 5.02 kJ into 120 g of solution. Calculate the temperature rise.
3. 0.0500 mol of magnesium reacts with acid, and 100 g of solution rises by 22.0 °C. Calculate ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹.
4. 2.00 g of KCl (Mᵣ 74.6) dissolves in 40.0 g of water and the temperature falls by 2.8 °C. Calculate ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹.
5. A thermometer is read to ±0.05 °C twice. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in a temperature rise of 8.0 °C.
6. 2.50 kJ raises the temperature of 200 g of a liquid by 6.0 K. Calculate c.
7. State two reasons why the enthalpy of combustion found in a school experiment is less negative than the data-book value.
8. Why is a polystyrene cup preferred to a glass beaker?
9. State what the mass in q = mcΔT represents.
10. In an experiment the temperature falls. State the sign of ΔH.
11. Why is the cooling curve extrapolated?
12. Explain why using a larger mass of reactant reduces the percentage uncertainty.

### Answers

1. q = 150 × 4.18 × 8.0 = **5016 J** (5.0 kJ).
2. ΔT = 5020 ÷ (120 × 4.18) = **10.0 °C**.
3. q = 100 × 4.18 × 22.0 = 9196 J = 9.196 kJ; ΔH = −9.196 ÷ 0.0500 = **−184 kJ mol⁻¹**.
4. q = 468 J; n = 0.02681 mol; ΔH = **+17.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.
5. Uncertainty in ΔT is ±0.10 °C; 0.10 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = **1.3%**.
6. c = 2500 ÷ (200 × 6.0) = **2.08 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹**.
7. Any two of: heat loss to the surroundings, incomplete combustion, heat absorbed by the can, non-standard conditions, evaporation of fuel.
8. Polystyrene is a better insulator, so less heat is lost and the temperature change is closer to the true value.
9. The mass of the water or solution whose temperature changes.
10. Positive (endothermic).
11. The reaction is not instant and heat is lost during mixing, so extrapolation estimates the temperature change at the moment of mixing.
12. A larger mass gives a larger temperature rise. The same ± uncertainty in the thermometer is then a smaller fraction of the reading.
