## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Derived demand | Demand for labour (or another input) that arises only because people want what it produces |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per hour) |
| Human capital | Skills, knowledge and health of the workforce |
| Occupational mobility | Ease of moving to a different type of job |
| Geographical mobility | Ease of moving to a different place for work |
| Trade union | Organisation of workers that bargains together |
| Negative externality | A cost falling on outsiders that the producer does not pay |
| Pollution permit | A right, which can be sold on, to release a set amount of waste |
| Minimum wage | Legal lowest hourly pay |
| Merger / takeover | Two firms join / one firm buys another |

## 1.2.5 a) Demand for labour

| Factor | Change | Labour demand curve |
|---|---|---|
| Demand for final product | Up | Right |
| Demand for final product | Down | Left |
| Machines cheaper or better | Substitute for workers | Left |
| Machines dearer | Workers become relatively cheaper | Right |
| Productivity of workers | Up | Right |

Chain: product demand falls, the firm makes less, it needs fewer workers, so labour demand shifts left.

Wage changes slide you along the curve; they never shift it.

## 1.2.5 b) Supply of labour

| Factor | Supply shifts | Short reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population grows | Right | More people of working age |
| Immigration | Right | More workers available |
| Emigration | Left | Fewer workers |
| Ageing population | Left | Fewer of working age |
| Retirement age raised | Right | People work longer |
| School-leaving age raised | Left (short run) | Young people stay in school |
| Female participation rises | Right | More women in paid work |
| More skills and qualifications | Right (for skilled jobs) | More suitable applicants |
| Better mobility | Right (where jobs exist) | Workers can move to jobs |

## 1.2.5 c), d) Quantity, quality and human capital

- Quantity of labour: number of workers or hours. Shortages leave orders unmet.
- Quality of labour: skill, health, motivation, which sets productivity.
- Education and training raise human capital, so output per worker rises, cost per unit falls and product quality improves.
- Costs to a firm: fees, lost output, risk of trained staff leaving.
- Benefits to the economy: higher productivity and incomes.

### Worked reminder: labour cost per unit

Wage 12 an hour. Output per hour 20 before training, 26 after.
- Before: 12 ÷ 20 = 0.60 per unit.
- After: 12 ÷ 26 = 0.46 per unit.
- Fall ≈ 23%.

## 1.2.5 e) Labour market diagrams

Method in steps:
1. Axes: wage (vertical), quantity of labour or employment (horizontal).
2. Downward-sloping demand D; upward-sloping supply S.
3. Mark the equilibrium wage and employment where they cross.
4. For a shift, add a second curve, named D1 or S1.
5. Mark the new equilibrium and state the effect on wage and employment.

| Shift | Effect on wage | Effect on employment |
|---|---|---|
| Demand right | Rises | Rises |
| Demand left | Falls | Falls |
| Supply right | Falls | Rises |
| Supply left | Rises | Falls |

### Worked reminder: Town T market (illustrative)

| Wage | Demanded | Supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 700 | 300 |
| 8 | 600 | 400 |
| 10 | 500 | 500 |
| 12 | 400 | 600 |
| 14 | 300 | 700 |

Equilibrium: wage 10, employment 500. Demand rises by 100 at every wage: new equilibrium wage 11, employment 550.

## 1.2.5 f) Trade unions

- Aims: higher wages, better conditions, job security, a voice for workers.
- Methods: collective bargaining, threat of industrial action.
- Possible gain: higher pay for members, safer conditions.
- Possible loss: if the wage is pushed above equilibrium, employment falls. At a wage of 12 in Town T, employment is 400, so 100 jobs are lost, while the 400 in work gain 2 an hour each.
- Firms may face higher costs, so higher prices or lower profit.
- Union action can also raise productivity through morale.

## 1.2.6 a), b) Policies for externalities

| Policy | How it works | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax | Raises the cost of the harmful activity | Raises revenue, polluter pays | Hard to set rate, inelastic demand limits effect |
| Subsidy | Lowers cost of cleaner or beneficial activity | Encourages green output | Costs taxpayers, dependency |
| Fine | Penalty for breaking a rule | Punishes, deters | Needs detection, harm already done |
| Regulation | Limit or ban | Certain, fast | Enforcement cost, no incentive to do better than the limit |
| Pollution permits | Cap and tradable permits | Certain cap, lower-cost cuts | Cap setting, monitoring, big firms may buy up permits |

### Worked reminder: tax

Chemical market: demand P = 100 − 2Q, private supply P = 20 + 2Q. Market output 20 at 60. External cost 16 a unit, so best output is 16 at price 68. A tax of 16 gets there. Revenue = 16 × 16 = 256. Consumers and producers each bear 128.

### Worked reminder: fine

Firm saves 24,000 by dumping. Chance of being caught 30%. Fine 60,000: 0.3 × 60,000 = 18,000, no deterrent. Fine 100,000: 30,000, deterrent.

### Worked reminder: permits

Firm X cuts at 40 a tonne, Firm Y at 15. Permit price 25. X must cut 20 tonnes: own cut costs 800, buying permits costs 500, a saving of 300. Y cuts 20 extra tonnes at 300 and earns 500, a gain of 200.

### Evaluation points for any policy

- How much does it cost and who bears it?
- How easy to enforce?
- How certain is the result?
- Short run or long run?
- Effect on jobs, prices and competitiveness.

## 1.2.6 c) Regulating competition

| Aim | Examples |
|---|---|
| Promote competition | Lower entry barriers, support small firms, stop unfair practices |
| Limit monopoly power | Ban cartels and price fixing, fine abuse of dominance, price caps, break up firms |
| Protect consumers | Safety rules, honest advertising and labelling, fair contracts, refund rights |
| Control mergers and takeovers | Investigate, block or set conditions when market share becomes too large |

### Worked reminder: merger

Shares 35%, 25%, 20%, 20%. The top two merge to 60%. Price rises from 8 to 10: +25%. On 50,000 units, consumers pay 100,000 more.

Evaluation: enforcement cost, possible economies of scale, whether new entrants can still arrive.

## 1.2.6 d) Minimum wage

Reasons: reduce poverty and inequality, stop exploitation, make work pay more than benefits.

### Method in steps: minimum wage diagram

1. Draw the market with D and S and mark the equilibrium wage.
2. Draw a horizontal line at the minimum, above equilibrium.
3. Read quantity demanded (where D meets the line) and quantity supplied (where S meets the line).
4. The gap is unemployment. Employment is the quantity demanded.
5. To show an increase, raise the line: the gap widens.

A minimum below equilibrium has no effect.

### Worked reminder: Town T

| Minimum wage | Demanded | Supplied | Surplus of labour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 400 | 600 | 200 |
| 13 | 350 | 650 | 300 |

At 12, employment falls from 500 to 400 (−20%). Wage bill: 10 × 500 = 5,000 before and 12 × 400 = 4,800 after.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Higher pay for those employed | Unemployment if above equilibrium |
| Less poverty and inequality | Higher costs and prices |
| Better motivation and spending | Informal work, enforcement cost |
| Less need for benefits | May not target poor households |

### Worked reminder: small firm

6 staff, 35 hours, 50 weeks = 10,500 hours. Wage 9.00 to 10.50 is +16.7%. Extra cost = 1.50 × 10,500 = 15,750.

## Quick self-test

1. Define derived demand.
2. Give two reasons the labour supply curve might move right.
3. A firm has 30 workers who make 360 units a day. Calculate output per worker.
4. Output per worker rises from 12 to 15. Calculate the percentage increase.
5. At a minimum wage of 11, 300 workers are demanded and 380 supplied. State the surplus of labour.
6. Which way does the supply of labour shift if the school-leaving age rises?
7. A firm has a 30% chance of a 40,000 fine. Calculate the expected fine.
8. A tax of 6 a unit is paid on 200 units. Calculate the revenue.
9. State two ways a government can regulate competition.
10. A wage rises from 8 to 9. Calculate the percentage rise.

### Answers

1. Labour demand that arises only because of demand for the product (two parts: demand for a factor, and the link to output).
2. Any two of: larger population, immigration, higher retirement age, higher female participation, more qualifications.
3. 360 ÷ 30 = 12 units.
4. (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = 25%.
5. 380 − 300 = 80 workers.
6. Left in the short run.
7. 0.3 × 40,000 = 12,000.
8. 6 × 200 = 1,200.
9. Any two of: ban cartels, block a merger, cap prices, enforce consumer protection laws.
10. (9 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = 12.5%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting a curve for a wage change instead of moving along it.
- Drawing diagrams without labels or without the new equilibrium.
- Stating a minimum wage always causes unemployment, with no mention of where it is set.
- Forgetting the winners (those who keep jobs) as well as the losers.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no development.
- Writing about taxes in general when the question names a pollution context.
- Giving percentages with the wrong base: use the original figure.
- No working on a Calculate question.
- Explaining one policy when asked to evaluate between two.
- Ending an Evaluate answer without a conclusion.
