These notes condense **Implementing policy** (topics 3.1 Fiscal policy, 3.2 Monetary policy, 3.3 Supply side policy and 3.4 Policy conflicts) from Component 02 of the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The comparisons and worked examples are in the [implementing policy study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-implementing-policy/), and the [implementing policy practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-implementing-policy-practice/) give exam-style work. All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Government budget | The plan of government revenue and spending for a year |
| Direct tax | A tax on income or wealth, paid directly to the government |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending, collected through sellers |
| Progressive tax | Average tax rate rises as income rises |
| Proportional tax | Average tax rate is constant at all incomes |
| Regressive tax | Average tax rate falls as income rises |
| Current spending | Day-to-day spending, such as salaries and medicines |
| Capital spending | Spending on long-lived assets, such as roads and schools |
| Budget deficit | Spending exceeds revenue in a year |
| Cyclical deficit | The part of the deficit caused by the economic cycle |
| Structural deficit | The part that remains when output is at trend |
| National (government) debt | The accumulated stock of government borrowing |
| Automatic stabilisers | Taxes and benefits that change with the cycle without new decisions |
| Crowding out | Government borrowing reducing private spending, for example through higher interest rates |
| Laffer curve | Beyond some tax rate, higher rates reduce tax revenue |
| Quantitative easing | Central bank creation of money to buy financial assets, lowering long-term interest rates |
| Inflation rate target | A stated rate of inflation the central bank aims to achieve |
| Supply-side policy | Measures to raise productive capacity, shifting LRAS right |

Note the difference between the deficit (a flow in one year) and the debt (the stock built up over many years).

## Tax rate method

- Average tax rate = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
- Marginal tax rate = extra tax ÷ extra income × 100.

**Quick example.** Tax is 0% on the first 10,000 cu and 25% above that. Income 40,000 cu: tax = 0.25 × 30,000 = **7,500 cu**; average rate = 7,500 ÷ 40,000 = **18.75%**; marginal rate = **25%**. Because the average rate rises with income, the tax is progressive.

## Deficit split

Cyclical deficit = size of negative output gap × response of the deficit per 1% of gap. Structural = actual − cyclical.

**Quick example.** Deficit 6% of GDP; output gap −2%; response 0.6 per 1% of gap. Cyclical = **1.2%**; structural = **4.8%**.

## Fiscal policy: effectiveness

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Can target regions and groups | Recognition, decision and implementation lags |
| Multiplier magnifies the effect | Crowding out near full capacity |
| Stabilisers act immediately | Adds to national debt and future interest payments |
| Capital spending can raise LRAS too | Higher taxes may reduce incentives (Laffer curve) |

## Monetary policy: transmission and effectiveness

Interest rate cut → cheaper borrowing, lower mortgage payments, higher asset prices, weaker currency → consumption, investment and net exports rise → AD shifts right.

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Rates can change quickly | Effect takes many months |
| Independent central bank adds credibility | Weak when confidence is low or rates are near zero |
| Inflation target anchors expectations | Affects the whole economy, not specific regions |
| QE available when rates are very low | Can inflate asset prices; exchange rate effects hit exporters |

## Supply-side policy: types and effectiveness

| Market-based | Interventionist |
|---|---|
| Privatisation, deregulation | Infrastructure investment |
| Competition policy | Education and training |
| Tax and benefit reform | Research and development subsidies |
| Labour market flexibility | |

Immigration control changes the size and skills of the labour force.

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Raises capacity, so growth without inflation | Slow: years to take effect |
| Can improve competitiveness and the current account | Uncertain size of effect |
| Can ease policy conflicts | Some measures widen inequality; others cost public money |

## Policy conflicts

| Pair of objectives | Typical conflict |
|---|---|
| Unemployment and inflation | Raising AD cuts unemployment but raises inflation |
| Growth and the current account | Faster growth raises imports |
| Inflation and growth | Higher interest rates cut inflation but slow growth |
| Growth and equality | Some market-based supply-side measures widen income gaps |

## Exam traps

- Confusing deficit and debt.
- Mixing up average and marginal tax rates.
- Treating QE as the same as a rate cut.
- Using a demand-side tool for a supply-side problem.
- Ignoring time lags or crowding out in evaluation.
- Forgetting that the exchange rate is part of monetary transmission.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between a direct and an indirect tax.
2. Why is a tax on basic food usually regressive?
3. Distinguish between current and capital government spending.
4. Tax is 0% on the first 8,000 cu and 20% above. Find the average tax rate on 28,000 cu.
5. The deficit is 4% of GDP; the output gap is −2.5%; the response is 0.4 per 1%. Find the structural deficit.
6. Give two automatic stabilisers.
7. Explain crowding out in one sentence.
8. State two channels through which an interest rate rise reduces AD.
9. What does a central bank buy under quantitative easing?
10. Give two market-based and two interventionist supply-side policies.
11. Why might supply-side policy ease the conflict between unemployment and inflation?
12. Give one reason fiscal policy may be ineffective.

### Answers

1. A direct tax is on income or wealth and paid straight to the government; an indirect tax is on spending and collected through sellers.
2. Poorer households spend a larger share of their income on basic food, so the tax takes a larger share of their income.
3. Current spending is day-to-day spending such as salaries; capital spending is on long-lived assets such as roads.
4. Tax = 0.2 × 20,000 = 4,000; average rate = 4,000 ÷ 28,000 = **14.3%**.
5. Cyclical = 2.5 × 0.4 = 1.0%; structural = 4 − 1 = **3.0%** of GDP.
6. Progressive income tax; unemployment benefits (also VAT revenue falling in a downturn).
7. Government borrowing raises interest rates or uses resources, reducing private investment or consumption.
8. Any two of: dearer borrowing cuts consumption and investment; higher mortgage payments cut disposable income; lower asset prices cut wealth; appreciation cuts net exports.
9. Financial assets, usually government bonds.
10. Market-based: any two of privatisation, deregulation, competition policy, tax and benefit reform, labour market flexibility. Interventionist: any two of infrastructure, education and training, research and development subsidies.
11. It shifts LRAS right, so output and employment can rise without pushing up the price level.
12. Any one of: time lags; crowding out; a small multiplier; households saving any tax cut.
