## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organism | One individual living thing |
| Population | Every individual of a single species living in one area |
| Community | The populations of every species living together in one area |
| Ecosystem | The community plus the non-living parts of the area |
| Abiotic factor | A non-living factor, such as temperature, light intensity, moisture or soil pH |
| Biotic factor | A factor caused by living organisms, such as predators or food supply |
| Producer | An organism that makes its own food by photosynthesis (plants, algae) |
| Consumer | An organism that gets biomass by eating other organisms |
| Trophic level | A feeding level in a food chain |
| Interdependence | Organisms in a community relying on each other |
| Competition | Organisms needing the same limited resource |
| Pyramid of biomass | A diagram showing the dry mass of each trophic level in an area |
| Efficiency of biomass transfer | Biomass passed to the next level as a percentage of the biomass at the level below |

## Levels of organisation (B4.1e)

Order from smallest to largest: organism, population, community, ecosystem.

- "All the frogs in a pond" is a population.
- "All the frogs, fish, plants and bacteria in a pond" is a community.
- "The pond including its water, light and temperature" is an ecosystem.

## Abiotic and biotic factors (B4.1f)

| Factor | Type | Effect on a community |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Abiotic | Controls enzyme activity, so growth and reproduction. Extreme values reduce which species can live there |
| Light intensity | Abiotic | Limits photosynthesis, so producer numbers, so the food supply for consumers |
| Moisture level | Abiotic | Affects germination, water uptake and survival of plants and animals that dry out easily |
| Soil pH | Abiotic | Affects mineral availability and root growth, so which plants can grow |
| Predators | Biotic | Remove prey, so more predators usually means fewer prey |
| Food | Biotic | More food allows a population to grow. A shortage reduces numbers |

Method for an explain answer:

1. Name the factor and say whether it increases or decreases.
2. Say what this does to individual organisms (growth, survival, reproduction).
3. Say what happens to the population or the community.

## Interdependence and competition (B4.1g)

| Relationship | Who benefits | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Predation | Predator gains, prey is killed | Ladybird and aphids |
| Mutualism | Both species | Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules: sugars for the bacteria, nitrogen compounds for the plant |
| Parasitism | Parasite gains, host is harmed | Tapeworm in the gut, flea on a mammal |

Competition:

- Plants: light, water, mineral ions, space.
- Animals: food, water, shelter, territory, mates.
- Within a species it is often stronger, because individuals need identical resources.
- If one species wins a resource, the other declines.

Predator-prey graph: the prey peak comes first and the predator peak follows. Prey numbers fall when predators are plentiful, and predator numbers then fall because food is scarce.

## Trophic levels (B4.1h)

| Position | Name | Feeds on |
|---|---|---|
| First | Producer | Makes its own food |
| Second | Primary consumer | Producers |
| Third | Secondary consumer | Primary consumers |
| Fourth | Tertiary consumer | Secondary consumers |

- Arrows show the direction of biomass transfer: from the food to the feeder.
- An apex predator has no natural predators.
- Removing producers affects every level. Removing a top predator can allow its prey to increase.

## Pyramids of biomass and biomass loss (B4.1i)

- Use dry mass per unit area, with producers at the bottom.
- Each bar is drawn to the same scale and the bars are centred.
- The pyramid narrows upwards because biomass is lost at each transfer.

| Loss | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Egestion | Undigested material leaves as faeces | Cellulose, bone, shell, hair. Parts not eaten at all, such as roots |
| Excretion | Metabolic waste is removed | Urea in urine, carbon dioxide from the lungs |
| Respiration | Glucose is broken down to release energy, and carbon leaves as carbon dioxide | Movement, keeping warm |

Egestion is not excretion. Egestion is leaving the gut unabsorbed. Excretion is waste made by cells.

## Calculating efficiency (B4.1j)

efficiency (%) = biomass transferred to the next level / biomass at the level below × 100

Method in steps:

1. Choose the two consecutive levels. The lower level is the denominator.
2. Check both values use the same unit and the same area and time.
3. Divide and multiply by 100.
4. Give the answer with a % sign and sensible rounding.

Worked reminder: a producer level makes 450 kg of biomass, the primary consumers gain 36 kg and the secondary consumers gain 3.24 kg.

Producer to primary: 36 / 450 × 100 = 8%.
Primary to secondary: 3.24 / 36 × 100 = 9%.
Overall producer to secondary: 3.24 / 450 × 100 = 0.72%.

If 8% is transferred, 100 - 8 = 92% is not. It is lost as egested material, excreted waste and respiration.

Working through several levels at about 10% each: multiply by 0.1 for each step up, divide by 0.1 for each step down.

Food chains are short, usually four or five levels, because so much is lost at each step that too little biomass remains to support another level.

## Small worked reminders

Reading a factor from data. A table shows the mean number of one plant species per quadrat on soils of increasing acidity. If numbers fall as pH decreases, say so with figures, name pH as the abiotic factor, and suggest a reason such as poor mineral availability or root damage. Then note that a correlation does not prove that pH is the only cause.

Reading a pyramid. If the bars read 800, 90 and 7 (g per m²), the biomass falls by roughly a factor of ten at each step. That fits the idea that most biomass is lost between levels. You can turn it into efficiency: 90 / 800 × 100 = 11.25%, and 7 / 90 × 100 = 7.8%.

Explaining a loss. Use the pattern: name the route, say what leaves the animal, and say why it cannot become new tissue. For respiration: glucose is broken down to release energy, carbon leaves as carbon dioxide, so that carbon is not added to the animal's body.

Making a pyramid of biomass: pick a scale so the widest bar fits the page, draw each bar at the same height, centre every bar on the same vertical line, put the producer at the bottom and label each bar with the organism and its biomass.

## Must-know distinctions

- Population against community: one species against many.
- Community against ecosystem: living only against living and non-living.
- Abiotic against biotic: non-living against living.
- Mutualism against parasitism: both gain against one is harmed.
- Egestion against excretion: undigested food against metabolic waste.
- Biomass transferred against biomass lost: use the denominator carefully.

## Quick self-test

1. A group of 60 owls of one species lives in a forest. Is this an organism, a population, a community or an ecosystem?
2. Is soil pH abiotic or biotic?
3. Name the type of relationship in which a tick feeds on a deer and harms it.
4. Give two resources that plants compete for.
5. In the chain algae → water flea → minnow, which organism is the primary consumer?
6. State the direction an arrow points in a food chain.
7. Name the three ways in which biomass is lost between trophic levels.
8. Why does a pyramid of biomass use dry mass?
9. A secondary consumer level has 2400 kg of biomass. The tertiary consumers gain 288 kg. What is the percentage efficiency?
10. At 10% transfer, how much producer biomass supports 30 kg of tertiary consumers?
11. Why do food chains rarely have more than five trophic levels?

### Answers

1. A population, because they are one species in one area.
2. Abiotic.
3. Parasitism.
4. Any two of light, water, mineral ions, space.
5. The water flea.
6. From the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it.
7. Egestion, excretion and respiration.
8. Water content varies, so dry mass measures the actual amount of tissue.
9. 288 / 2400 × 100 = 12%.
10. 30 / 0.1 / 0.1 / 0.1 = 30 000 kg.
11. So much biomass is lost at each transfer that too little is left to support another level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "community" for "population", or leaving the non-living part out of a definition of ecosystem.
- Giving an effect without a consequence, such as "less light" without saying fewer producers.
- Confusing predation, parasitism and mutualism. Say who benefits and who is harmed.
- Drawing arrows the wrong way round in a food chain.
- Saying "energy is lost as heat" and not naming biomass loss through egestion, excretion or respiration.
- Treating egestion as excretion.
- Using the wrong level as the divisor in an efficiency calculation.
- Quoting an answer with no working or no % sign.
- Describing a graph without using the numbers, or ignoring a time lag in predator-prey data.
