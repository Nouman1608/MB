## Polarity in two steps

1. **Bond polarity:** different electronegativities → the shared pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom (δ−); the other atom is δ+.
2. **Molecular dipole:** the molecule is polar only if the bond dipoles **do not cancel**, which depends on **shape**.

| Non-polar (dipoles cancel) | Polar (dipoles do not cancel) |
|---|---|
| CO₂, BF₃, CH₄, CCl₄, SF₆, PF₅ | HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, SO₂ |

## Intermolecular forces

**Van der Waals' forces** = generic term for **all** intermolecular forces (forces between molecular entities other than those due to bond formation).

| Type | Found between | Origin | Stronger when |
|---|---|---|---|
| id-id (London dispersion) | **all** molecules | moving electrons create an instantaneous dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbour | more electrons; larger surface area of contact |
| pd-pd | polar molecules (in addition to id-id) | δ+ of one molecule attracts δ− of another | larger permanent dipole |
| Hydrogen bonding (special pd-pd) | molecules with **N–H or O–H** | very δ+ H attracted to a **lone pair** on N or O of another molecule | more H atoms **and** lone pairs available |

General order of strength (for molecules of similar size): **hydrogen bonding > pd-pd > id-id**.

**All are weaker than ionic, covalent and metallic bonds** (in general).

## Drawing a hydrogen bond (checklist)

- δ+ on H, δ− on N or O
- **lone pair** shown on the N or O of the neighbouring molecule
- dashed line from H to that lone pair
- N/O–H···N/O roughly **linear (180°)**

## Data to quote

| Comparison | Boiling points | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| F₂ vs HCl (18 electrons each) | −188 °C vs −85 °C | similar id-id; HCl also has pd-pd |
| F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ | −188, −34, 59 °C | more electrons, stronger id-id |
| pentane vs 2,2-dimethylpropane | 36 °C vs about 10 °C | same electrons; branched isomer has less contact area |
| H₂O vs H₂S, H₂Se, H₂Te | 100 vs −60, −41, −2 °C | hydrogen bonding in water only |
| CH₄, SiH₄, GeH₄, SnH₄ | −162, −112, −88, −52 °C | id-id only; smooth rise with electrons |
| H₂O vs NH₃ | 100 vs −33 °C | water forms two hydrogen bonds per molecule on average; ammonia one (only one lone pair) |
| ethanol vs propane (26 electrons each) | 78 vs −42 °C | hydrogen bonding in ethanol |

## Water's anomalous properties

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Relatively high m.p. and b.p. | extra energy needed to overcome hydrogen bonds between molecules |
| Relatively high surface tension | surface molecules pulled inwards and sideways by hydrogen bonds |
| Ice less dense than water | in ice each molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others in an **open, ordered lattice**; on melting some hydrogen bonds break and molecules pack closer |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electronegativity | Power of an atom to attract electrons to itself |
| Polar bond | Bond with unequal sharing, giving δ+ and δ− ends |
| Dipole moment | Overall separation of charge in a molecule; zero if bond dipoles cancel |
| Van der Waals' forces | Generic term for all intermolecular forces |
| Instantaneous dipole | Temporary uneven electron distribution at a given moment |
| Induced dipole | Dipole created in a molecule by a neighbouring dipole |
| Hydrogen bond | pd-pd attraction between H bonded to N or O and a lone pair on N or O of another molecule |

## Exam traps

- **Every** molecule has id-id forces; never say a non-polar substance has "no forces".
- Van der Waals' forces **include** hydrogen bonding in this syllabus.
- Polar bonds do **not** guarantee a polar molecule; check the shape.
- Hydrogen bonding needs H **on** N or O in one molecule and a **lone pair** on N or O in the other. H bonded to C does not hydrogen bond.
- Compare like with like: when electron numbers differ a lot, id-id differences may dominate.
- Boiling a simple molecular substance breaks **intermolecular forces only**.
- Ice's lower density needs the **open structure** argument.

## Self-test

1. Define van der Waals' forces as the syllabus uses the term.
2. Explain why CCl₄ is non-polar although C–Cl bonds are polar.
3. Explain how an id-id force arises.
4. State two factors that increase the strength of id-id forces.
5. Explain why HCl has a higher boiling point than F₂.
6. State the conditions needed for a hydrogen bond between two molecules.
7. Explain why water has a higher boiling point than ammonia.
8. Explain why ice floats on water.
9. Explain why the boiling points of the Group 14 hydrides increase from CH₄ to SnH₄.
10. Which has the higher boiling point, CH₃OCH₃ or C₂H₅OH? Explain.
11. Is CHCl₃ polar or non-polar? Explain.
12. When liquid bromine boils, what is overcome?

**Answers:**

1. The intermolecular forces between molecular entities other than those due to bond formation; a generic term for all intermolecular forces.
2. It is tetrahedral and symmetrical, so the four bond dipoles cancel.
3. Moving electrons create a temporary uneven charge distribution (instantaneous dipole), which induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule; the dipoles attract.
4. More electrons in the molecule; larger surface area of contact.
5. Both have 18 electrons, so similar id-id forces, but HCl is polar and also has pd-pd forces.
6. H bonded directly to N or O in one molecule, and a lone pair on N or O in the other molecule.
7. Each water molecule has two H atoms and two lone pairs, so it forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule than ammonia, which has only one lone pair.
8. In ice the molecules are hydrogen-bonded in an open, ordered lattice, which takes up more volume than the liquid, so ice is less dense.
9. The number of electrons increases, so id-id forces become stronger; there is no hydrogen bonding.
10. C₂H₅OH: it has an O–H group and forms hydrogen bonds; CH₃OCH₃ has only pd-pd and id-id forces.
11. Polar: the H and three Cl atoms around carbon are not identical, so the bond dipoles do not cancel.
12. id-id forces between Br₂ molecules (the Br–Br covalent bonds are not broken).
