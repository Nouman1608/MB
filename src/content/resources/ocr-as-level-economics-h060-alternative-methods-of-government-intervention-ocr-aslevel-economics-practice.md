These questions cover the methods of government intervention listed in topic 2.8 Government intervention (Component 01, Microeconomics) of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), and the evaluation of their effectiveness. The mark totals are our own and every market and figure is invented. Explanations are in the [government intervention study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-alternative-methods-of-government-intervention-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [government intervention revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-alternative-methods-of-government-intervention-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

Questions 3, 4, 7 and 8 use an invented market with demand Qd = 500 − 20P and supply Qs = 30P − 100, where P is in pounds and Q in thousand units. The free market equilibrium is P = £12 and Q = 260.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by internalising an externality. **[2]**

**2.** Which intervention fixes the total quantity of pollution with the most certainty?

A: An indirect tax on output

B: A subsidy for cleaner technology

C: A system of tradable pollution permits

D: An information campaign **[1]**

**3.** The government sets a maximum price of £10. Calculate the resulting shortage. **[2]**

**4.** The government instead sets a minimum price of £14. Calculate the resulting surplus. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between legislation and regulation as methods of intervention. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one reason why a public/private partnership may cost the government more in the long run than direct provision. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** The government imposes a specific tax of £2.50 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate how much of the tax per unit is paid by consumers and how much by producers. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**

**8.** Instead, the government pays producers a subsidy of £1 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost of the subsidy to the government. **[1]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, how a buffer stock scheme can stabilise the price of a crop. **[6]**

**10.** Two firms, C and D, each emit 50 tonnes of a pollutant. The government sets a cap of 60 tonnes and gives each firm 30 permits. Cutting one tonne costs C £15 and D £40.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of meeting the cap if permits cannot be traded. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total cost if permits can be traded, and the saving. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why trading reduces the cost of meeting the cap. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate whether indirect taxation is the most effective way to reduce consumption of a good that creates external costs. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Internalising an externality means making the producer or consumer bear the external cost (or receive the external benefit) of their decisions [1], for example through a tax equal to the marginal external cost, so that private and social costs coincide [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A cap on permits sets the quantity directly; a tax sets a price and leaves the quantity uncertain. **[1]**

**3.** Qd = 500 − 200 = 300; Qs = 300 − 100 = 200 [1]. Shortage = **100 thousand units** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Qd = 500 − 280 = 220; Qs = 420 − 100 = 320 [1]. Surplus = **100 thousand units** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Legislation is a law passed by parliament that bans or requires an action, such as a ban on selling a product to under-18s [1]. Regulation is a set of rules or standards, often detailed and enforced by an agency, such as emission limits monitored by inspectors [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The government commits to payments over many years [1], and these may add up to more than building the project directly because private finance is usually more expensive than government borrowing, or because contracts are hard to change [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** New supply Qs = 30(P − 2.5) − 100 = 30P − 175 [1]. 500 − 20P = 30P − 175 gives **P = £13.50** and **Q = 230** thousand [1].

**(b)** Consumers pay £1.50 more (£12 to £13.50) [1]; producers receive £11, so they bear £1.00 [1].

**(c)** 2.50 × 230 = **£575 thousand** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Consumers bear more of the tax because demand is less price elastic than supply in this market.

**8. (a)** Qs = 30(P + 1) − 100 = 30P − 70 [1]. 500 − 20P = 30P − 70 gives **P = £11.40** and **Q = 272** thousand [1].

**(b)** 1 × 272 = **£272 thousand** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Diagram showing demand, a price band with a floor and a ceiling, and supply curves for a good harvest and a poor harvest [1]. In a good harvest supply shifts right and the free price would fall below the floor [1]; the agency buys the surplus at the floor price and stores it, keeping the price at the floor [1]. In a poor harvest supply shifts left and the price would rise above the ceiling [1]; the agency sells from its stock, adding to supply and holding the price at the ceiling [1]. Prices and farmers' incomes are therefore more stable than in a free market [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Each must cut 20 tonnes: 20 × 15 + 20 × 40 = **£1,100** [1].

**(b)** C cuts all 40 tonnes (40 × 15 = £600) and sells 20 permits to D, which cuts nothing [1]. Total cost = **£600**, a saving of **£500** [1].

**(c)** The firm with the lower cost of cutting pollution does more of the cutting [1], and is paid for its spare permits by the higher-cost firm, so the same total cut is achieved at the lowest total cost [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- A tax shifts supply up, raising price and reducing quantity [1]; set at the marginal external cost, it moves output to the social optimum [1].
- It uses the price mechanism, so consumers who value the good most still buy it [1], and it raises revenue to spend on related problems [1].
- But the effect on quantity depends on PED: if demand is inelastic, consumption falls very little [1].
- The marginal external cost is hard to measure, so the tax may be set too low or too high [1].
- Taxes on goods bought widely by low-income households can be regressive [1], and high taxes may encourage smuggling or buying abroad [1].
- Alternatives may work better in some cases: regulation or a ban for very harmful goods, permits where a certain cap on quantity is needed, information provision where the problem is lack of knowledge [1].
- Judgement: taxation is most effective where demand is reasonably elastic and the external cost can be estimated; otherwise a combination with regulation and information provision is likely to be more effective [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare with at least one named alternative; evaluation that only lists problems with a tax is less convincing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting demand instead of supply for a tax or subsidy.
- Using the old quantity to calculate revenue or subsidy cost.
- Placing a maximum price above equilibrium.
- Describing buffer stocks without saying when the agency buys and when it sells.
- Evaluating without comparing methods.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do consumers sometimes pay most of an indirect tax?**
When demand is less price elastic than supply, buyers keep buying at the higher price, so firms can pass most of the tax on.

**What is the difference between a pollution tax and tradable permits?**
A tax sets the price of polluting and lets the quantity adjust. Permits set the quantity through the cap and let the permit price adjust.
