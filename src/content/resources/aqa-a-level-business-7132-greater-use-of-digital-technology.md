This guide goes deeper into section **3.9.4 Assessing greater use of digital technology** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the pressures to adopt digital technology**, where digital technology should include **automation, e-commerce, big data and data mining**
- **the value of digital technology**.

The wider strategic decision-making requirements of the specification also ask you to consider the impact of technology on strategic decision making. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What is digital technology?

**Digital technology** means using computers, networks, software and data to carry out business activities. Four forms appear in the specification:

| Form | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Automation | Machines or software carrying out tasks with little human input | Robots in a warehouse; automated payroll; chatbots |
| E-commerce | Buying and selling over the internet | Online shops; booking sites; apps |
| Big data | Very large, fast-growing sets of data from sales, websites, sensors and social media | Purchase histories; website clicks; delivery tracking |
| Data mining | Searching large data sets for patterns and relationships | Finding which customers are likely to leave |

## Pressures to adopt digital technology

- **Competition:** rivals who have adopted it may offer lower prices, faster service or better products. Businesses that do not adopt risk losing customers.
- **Customer expectations:** customers expect to buy online, track orders, get instant replies and receive personal offers.
- **Cost pressure:** automation reduces labour and error costs.
- **Availability of data and cheaper technology:** cloud services, cheaper sensors and software as a service make it affordable even for small firms.
- **Remote working and flexible supply chains.**
- **New entrants:** digital start-ups with low costs can enter markets.
- **Regulatory and supplier requirements:** for example digital tax records or electronic ordering with large customers.
- **Shareholder pressure** for efficiency and growth.

The pressure is strongest when customers can easily switch and when technology changes quickly.

## Automation

**Value:**

- lower unit costs and fewer errors
- higher productivity and consistent quality, 24 hours a day
- frees people for more complex or creative work
- safer for dangerous tasks

**Limits:**

- high set-up cost and maintenance
- redundancies, low morale and loss of skills
- breakdowns or cyber attacks can stop production
- less flexible for custom or varied work
- needs skilled staff to run and maintain it

### Worked example: warehouse robots

Brannock Distribution buys warehouse robots for **£450,000**. They replace 9 staff earning £25,000 each, and cost £45,000 a year to run and maintain.

- Labour saved: 9 × £25,000 = **£225,000**
- Net annual saving: £225,000 − £45,000 = **£180,000**
- Payback: £450,000 ÷ £180,000 = **2.5 years**

**Judgement.** A payback of 2.5 years is attractive if the robots last for more than five years. But the figure ignores redundancy payments, retraining and the risk that demand falls. A phased roll-out and retraining of staff for supervisory roles would reduce the risks.

## E-commerce

**Value:**

- reaches customers nationally and internationally, 24 hours a day
- lower overheads than shops (but costs of delivery, returns and website)
- detailed data on customer behaviour
- allows direct-to-consumer sales and so higher margins
- new business models, such as subscriptions and marketplaces

**Limits:**

- intense price competition and easy comparison
- delivery and returns costs, especially for bulky goods
- security, fraud and data protection duties
- dependence on website performance and search engine rankings
- loss of face-to-face service
- customers who cannot or will not use the internet

### Worked example: conversion rate

Wren & Daly sells homeware online. Each month 200,000 people visit the site and 2.5% buy, spending an average £48.

- Orders: 200,000 × 2.5% = **5,000**
- Revenue: 5,000 × £48 = **£240,000**

A redesign and better delivery information raise the conversion rate to 3.0%.

- Orders: 200,000 × 3.0% = **6,000**
- Revenue: 6,000 × £48 = **£288,000**, an increase of **£48,000** a month (20%)

A half-point improvement in conversion gives a 20% rise in revenue without any additional visitors, so improving the site can be better value than buying more advertising.

## Big data and data mining

**Value:**

- **Understanding customers:** buying patterns, preferences, which products sell together.
- **Targeted marketing and personal offers** that raise sales and reduce wasted advertising.
- **Pricing:** adjusting prices to demand.
- **Operations:** predicting demand to cut inventory; predicting equipment failure.
- **Retention:** identifying customers likely to leave.
- **Risk:** spotting fraud.

**Limits:**

- cost of collecting, storing and analysing data, and of skilled staff
- data quality problems: poor data gives poor decisions
- data protection law and privacy concerns, with penalties for misuse
- patterns found are not always causes (correlation is not cause)
- security risks and loss of trust after a breach
- over-reliance on data at the expense of judgement and creativity

### Worked example: reducing customer loss

Quillfeather Subscriptions has 50,000 customers, each paying £120 a year. Data mining identifies customers likely to cancel, so the firm offers them targeted help and discounts. The annual loss rate falls from 18% to 14%.

- Customers lost before: 50,000 × 18% = **9,000**
- Customers lost after: 50,000 × 14% = **7,000**
- Customers kept: **2,000**, worth 2,000 × £120 = **£240,000** a year

The firm must compare this with the cost of the data team, software and discounts. If these cost £150,000, the net benefit is £90,000.

## Impact on functional areas

| Function | Impact |
|---|---|
| Operations | Automation, real-time stock tracking, predictive maintenance, flexible supply chains |
| Marketing | E-commerce, social media, targeted advertising, customer data |
| Human resources | New skills, retraining, remote working, redundancies, recruitment through platforms |
| Finance | Large investment, real-time data, digital payments, fraud risks |

## Digital technology and strategy

Digital technology is not only an operational tool. It can change the strategic position of a business.

- **Strategic direction (3.8.1):** e-commerce is a route into new markets (market development), and data can reveal gaps for new products.
- **Strategic positioning (3.8.2):** automation supports a low-cost strategy; data and personalisation support differentiation.
- **Scale (3.9.1):** digital platforms let small firms reach large markets, and automation lets output grow without proportionate growth in staff, which can delay diseconomies of scale.
- **The competitive environment (3.7.7):** low set-up costs for online selling reduce barriers to entry, while price comparison sites raise buyer power. Established firms may face new rivals from other industries.
- **Innovation (3.9.2):** data-driven products, such as apps or connected devices, are new forms of product innovation.

Not every business gains equally. A business whose value lies in personal service, craft or a local experience may keep its advantage by using technology to support, not replace, that experience. The question to ask is whether the technology strengthens what customers value about the business, or removes it.

### Barriers to successful adoption

- **Skills gaps:** staff cannot use the tools, and specialist recruitment is costly.
- **Legacy systems:** older systems do not connect with new ones.
- **Culture and resistance:** staff fear job losses, and managers distrust data.
- **Poor integration:** separate systems create duplicate and inconsistent data.
- **Cost overruns and delays**, common in large IT projects.
- **Customer readiness:** some customers lack access or confidence online.

Good change management (see section 3.10.1) matters: involving staff early, training them, and rolling out in stages reduce the risk of failure.

## Assessing greater use of digital technology

Ask:

1. **Fit with strategy:** does it support the objectives and the chosen position (low cost or differentiation)?
2. **Cost and return:** use investment appraisal (payback, ARR, NPV).
3. **Readiness:** does the business have the skills, culture and data?
4. **Customers and employees:** how will they respond?
5. **Risk:** security, dependence on suppliers of technology, system failure, rivals copying.
6. **Timing:** adopt early (first-mover advantage but higher risk) or follow (lower risk but possible loss of advantage)?

Technology gives a lasting advantage only when it is combined with the firm's own skills and data, because rivals can buy the same equipment.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** choose one form (for example automation), say what it changes, and trace the effect on costs, revenue or quality for the business in the case.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the benefits against cost, risk and the reaction of people, and judge in the light of the firm's size, market and finances.
- Do not write generally about technology. Name the form and the function.

## Common mistakes

- Treating digital technology as always beneficial.
- Confusing big data (the data) with data mining (finding patterns in it).
- Forgetting the human and ethical effects of automation.
- Ignoring data protection and security.
- Not linking the technology to a specific business objective.
