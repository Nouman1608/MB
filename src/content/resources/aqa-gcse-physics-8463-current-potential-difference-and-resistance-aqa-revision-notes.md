Recall notes for **4.2.1 Current, potential difference and resistance** in AQA GCSE Physics (8463), points
4.2.1.1 to 4.2.1.4. Paper 1, both tiers, no Higher-only content.

The full explanations and worked examples are in the [current, potential difference and resistance study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-current-potential-difference-and-resistance-aqa/).
Test yourself afterwards with the [current, potential difference and resistance practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-current-potential-difference-and-resistance-aqa-practice/).

## The two equations to recall

| Equation | In words | Units |
|---|---|---|
| Q = I t | charge flow = current × time | C, A, s |
| V = I R | potential difference = current × resistance | V, A, Ω |

Rearranged forms: I = Q ÷ t, t = Q ÷ I, I = V ÷ R, R = V ÷ I.

Conversions you will need: 1 mA = 0.001 A; 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω; 1 MΩ = 1 000 000 Ω; 1 min = 60 s; 1 h = 3600 s.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Electric current | The rate of flow of electrical charge |
| Charge flow (Q) | Amount of charge passing a point, in coulombs |
| Source of pd | Needed, with a closed circuit, for charge to flow |
| Resistance | Opposition to current; larger R means smaller I for the same pd |
| Ohmic conductor | Current directly proportional to pd at constant temperature; resistance constant |
| Linear element | I–V graph is a straight line through the origin |
| Non-linear element | I–V graph is curved; resistance changes with current |
| Thermistor | Resistance decreases as temperature increases |
| LDR | Resistance decreases as light intensity increases |
| Diode | Lets current flow in one direction only; very high resistance in reverse |

## Facts in one line each

- Current is the same at any point in a single closed loop.
- Ammeter in series with the component; voltmeter in parallel across it.
- To measure resistance, record V and I and calculate R = V ÷ I.
- Filament lamp resistance rises because the filament gets hotter.
- Thermistor application: thermostats. LDR application: lights that come on when it gets dark.
- "Voltage" earns credit, but questions are written with "potential difference".

## I–V graph shapes

| Component | Shape (I on y-axis, V on x-axis) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Resistor at constant temperature | Straight line through the origin, same in both quadrants | R constant |
| Filament lamp | Curve through the origin, flattening at higher pd, symmetrical in both directions | R increases with current |
| Diode | Almost zero current for negative pd; current rises steeply after a small forward pd | Very high R in reverse |

Resistance at a point = V ÷ I from that point's coordinates. A steeper line through the origin means a
**smaller** resistance.

## Required practical 3 at a glance

- Wire taped to a metre rule; crocodile clips set the length.
- Measure V and I for each length; R = V ÷ I.
- Keep temperature constant: small current, switch off between readings.
- Expected result: straight line through the origin, so R ∝ length.
- A straight line with a positive intercept points to contact resistance at the clips.
- Second part: measure the resistance of resistor combinations in series and in parallel.

## Required practical 4 at a glance

- Components: resistor at constant temperature, filament lamp, diode.
- Variable resistor in series to change pd; read V and I in steps.
- Reverse the connections to get negative values.
- Protective resistor in series with the diode.
- Plot I against V and describe the shape, including whether it passes through the origin.

## Sensor investigations

- Thermistor in a water bath with a thermometer; record V and I at each temperature and work out R.
- LDR at different light intensities; record V and I and work out R.
- Plot R against temperature (or light intensity): both curves fall.

## Worked reminders

- 0.60 A for 3.5 min: Q = 0.60 × 210 = **126 C**.
- 15 V across 750 Ω: I = 15 ÷ 750 = **0.020 A** (20 mA).
- Lamp graph point (4.0 V, 0.50 A): R = 4.0 ÷ 0.50 = **8.0 Ω**.
- 240 C in 80 s: I = 240 ÷ 80 = **3.0 A**.

## Exam traps

- Gradient trap: on a curved I–V graph, R is never "1 ÷ gradient" at a point; use V ÷ I.
- Prefix trap: 4.7 kΩ substituted as 4.7 Ω gives a current 1000 times too large.
- Time trap: hours left as hours in Q = It.
- Wording trap: "the lamp's resistance increases because the current increases" misses the cause; say the
  filament temperature rises.
- Diagram trap: a voltmeter drawn in the main loop or an ammeter across a component.
- Practical trap: "keep it a fair test" scores nothing; name the control variable (temperature) and how it is
  controlled.
- Sensor trap: confusing which way resistance changes. Both thermistor and LDR resistance go **down** as their
  condition (temperature or light) goes **up**.

## Self-test

1. Define electric current.
2. Two things a circuit needs before charge can flow.
3. A current of 0.25 A flows for 8.0 minutes. Calculate the charge.
4. A resistor has 3.0 V across it and 0.015 A through it. Calculate its resistance.
5. Calculate the current through a 6.8 kΩ resistor with 12 V across it.
6. Where is the voltmeter placed when measuring a lamp's resistance?
7. Describe the I–V graph of a resistor at constant temperature.
8. Why does a filament lamp's resistance increase as the current increases?
9. What happens to the resistance of an LDR when a room gets darker?
10. State one application of a thermistor.
11. In required practical 3, why is the circuit switched off between readings?
12. A diode is connected in reverse with 2.0 V across it. Describe the current.

### Answers

1. The rate of flow of electrical charge.
2. A closed circuit (complete loop) and a source of potential difference.
3. t = 480 s; Q = 0.25 × 480 = **120 C**.
4. R = 3.0 ÷ 0.015 = **200 Ω**.
5. I = 12 ÷ 6800 = **0.0018 A** (1.8 mA, 2 s.f.).
6. In parallel, across the lamp.
7. A straight line through the origin: current directly proportional to pd.
8. The filament gets hotter as current increases, and its resistance rises with temperature.
9. It increases, because light intensity has fallen.
10. A thermostat (temperature-controlled switching).
11. So the wire does not heat up, because a change in temperature would change its resistance.
12. Almost zero, because the diode has a very high resistance in the reverse direction.
