## Section A: the genre and the option

**1.** State the three text requirements for Option 1B. **[3]**

**2.** AQA says the comic texts are those "where the discomfort of characters generally excites
laughter rather than concern". Explain why "generally" leaves room for debate in an essay. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish romantic comedy, satire and comedy of manners, giving one set text that could be
discussed as each. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what AQA means by "rule and misrule" in the structure of comedy. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two ways in which a comedy can "draw attention to itself". **[4]**

## Section B: original extracts and essay skills

**Extract 1 (original scene).** Sir Humphrey, a country squire, has arrived at a house he believes is
a London hotel. It is in fact the home of Mrs Lacey, whose daughter he has come to court.

> **SIR HUMPHREY:** *(handing her his hat)* My good woman, a room with a view of the street, and
> none of your country cooking.
> **MRS LACEY:** *(holding the hat at arm's length)* We have only the one view, sir. It is of my
> garden.
> **SIR HUMPHREY:** Then I shall pay for the garden. What do you charge for a garden?
> **MRS LACEY:** To you, sir? A great deal.
> *(Enter LUCY, her daughter. SIR HUMPHREY bows low, then, seeing her plain dress, straightens and
> winks.)*
> **SIR HUMPHREY:** And this pretty chambermaid shall carry up my boxes.
> **LUCY:** *(aside)* Shall she, indeed? Then she shall carry them into the pond.

**6.** Analyse how comedy is created through misunderstanding in Extract 1. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how Extract 1 sets up the comic structure of disorder leading to order. **[4]**

**Extract 2 (original poem).**

> *On the Statue of a Great Man in the Market Square*
>
> He stands where once he never stood,
> in bronze, three times his real height;
> the pigeons treat him as they should
> and climb his learned head each night.
> His speeches, carved below his feet,
> have never once been read aloud;
> the traders shout across the street
> and sell their onions to the crowd.

**8.** Analyse how Extract 2 uses satire to mock pomposity. **[6]**

**9.** Explain one way Extract 1 and Extract 2 could be linked in a Section C-style argument, and one
important difference between them. **[4]**

**10.** A student writes: "*Twelfth Night* is a comedy because it is funny and ends with marriages."
Explain two ways to develop this into a debated, genre-focused point. **[4]**

**11.** Plan a Section C answer on your own two non-Shakespeare texts for the question: "Comic
endings reward the wise and punish the foolish." List five elements your plan should contain.
**[5]**

**12.** Explain how the context of an early modern audience and of a modern audience could produce
different readings of a comic humiliation. Use one set text as an example. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** One Shakespeare play [1], one pre-1900 drama text [1] and one further text [1].

**2.** "Generally" admits that some discomfort does excite concern [1], so an essay can debate
moments where cruelty, humiliation or exclusion trouble the laughter and test the genre [1].

**3.** One mark for each type correctly distinguished with a valid example: romantic comedy centres
on love and courtship ending in marriage, for example *Twelfth Night* [1]; satire ridicules folly or
vice to expose it, for example Swift's elegy [1]; comedy of manners mocks the codes of polite
society, for example *The Importance of Being Earnest* or *Emma* [1].

**4.** Misrule is a temporary overturning of normal order, hierarchy or behaviour [1]; the comic
structure moves through this disorder back to rule, often re-establishing order through marriage
or discovery [1].

**5.** For each of two ways, way identified [1] and explained [1]. For example: a frame or play
within a play [1], such as the Induction in *The Taming of the Shrew*, reminds the audience that
they are watching a performance [1]; asides and direct address [1] make the audience complicit in
the joke [1]; wit and epigram [1] put language on display, so the comedy is admired as artifice
[1]; characters commenting on how improbable events are [1] mock the conventions of the plot
[1].

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The premise of mistaking a private house for a hotel creates dramatic irony: the audience knows
  what Sir Humphrey does not.
- Handing his hat to the hostess as if to a servant reverses social rank, a visual joke.
- "None of your country cooking" exposes the snobbery of a man who is himself a country squire.
- Mrs Lacey's dry "We have only the one view" turns his demand into understatement; she controls
  the exchange.
- "What do you charge for a garden?" shows his folly in thinking money can buy anything, which is
  satirised.
- "To you, sir? A great deal" is a double meaning: a price, and a hint of revenge to come.
- His low bow to Lucy, then straightening and winking at her plain dress, shows his manners
  changing with perceived rank.
- Lucy's aside about the pond makes the audience her ally and promises comic retribution.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the mistake about the house creates initial disorder; misreading Lucy as
a chambermaid sets up a courtship based on false identity; Lucy's aside shows she will turn the
mistake to her advantage, so the trickster's plot begins; the audience anticipates discovery of
the truth, which will humiliate Sir Humphrey; the courtship purpose of his visit points towards a
final order of marriage or rejection once identities are revealed.

**8.** Any six, [1] each:

- "Where once he never stood" mocks the gap between the man's reputation and his real presence in
  the town.
- "Three times his real height" exaggerates literally, satirising inflated fame.
- The pigeons treating him "as they should" deflate dignity with a comic, bodily image.
- "Learned head" is mock-praise, made absurd by the birds.
- Speeches "never once been read aloud" show his words were ignored, so his importance is
  self-declared.
- The final image of traders selling onions sets ordinary life against monument, giving the
  market the last word.
- Regular tetrameter and alternating rhymes give a jaunty, ballad-like movement that undercuts
  the solemn subject.
- The bathos of ending on onions brings the high subject down to the everyday.

**9.** Connection [2]: both mock pride and pretension [1]; both deflate a man who thinks himself
important through a reversal of expected respect [1]. Difference [2]: Extract 1 is drama whose
comedy depends on a plot of misunderstanding moving towards resolution, while Extract 2 is a short
satirical poem with no plot [1]; Extract 1 promises the fool's correction through courtship, while
Extract 2 simply exposes its target, with no love interest or marriage [1].

**10.** Two developments, each [2] for a developed method or [1] for a partial one: test "funny" by
asking who is laughed at and whether the gulling of Malvolio stays comic; test "ends with
marriages" by noting who is excluded (Malvolio, Antonio) and whether the endings feel complete;
analyse methods that create comedy, such as Viola's disguise producing dramatic irony in scenes
with Olivia; link to AQA's aspects, such as rule and misrule or the comic villain whose power is
curtailed.

**11.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on whether rewards and punishments follow wisdom
and folly; the characters in each text who are rewarded or punished; evidence that the foolish are
sometimes rewarded or the wise mocked, to complicate the view; methods by which endings distribute
rewards (marriage, discovery, exposure); contexts for each text on social order; an alternative
interpretation, such as comedy as conservative or subversive; a conclusion comparing the two
texts.

**12.** An early modern audience might accept a humiliation as fitting punishment for someone who
breaks social or gender rules [1]; a modern audience may find the same humiliation cruel or unjust
[1]. Example [1]: Malvolio's imprisonment in darkness in *Twelfth Night*, or Katherina's taming in
*The Taming of the Shrew*, which can be read as comic correction or as troubling control.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining the joke rather than the method.
- Treating the happy ending as complete without asking who is left out.
- Ignoring marriage and the love interest.
- Section C answers that discuss one text and then the other.

For full practice, write all three 25-mark answers under timed, closed-book conditions, about 50
minutes each.
