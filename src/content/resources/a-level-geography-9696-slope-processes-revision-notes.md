Condensed recall for **3.3 Slope processes** (mass movement and water and sediment movement on slopes). For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mass movement | Downslope movement of material under gravity |
| Shear stress | Force pulling material downslope |
| Shear strength | Resistance of material to movement |
| Pore water pressure | Pressure of water in spaces between particles, reducing friction |
| Rainsplash | Raindrop impact that moves soil particles |
| Surface runoff | Water flowing over the surface: sheetwash and rills |
| Cohesion | The force binding particles together, such as clay bonds or roots |
| Friction | Resistance between particles or along a surface, which rises with the weight pressing down |
| Factor of safety | Shear strength divided by shear stress; failure is likely when it falls below 1 |
| Angle of rest (repose) | Steepest angle at which loose material stays stable |
| Regolith | Loose weathered material overlying bedrock |

Failure occurs when **shear stress exceeds shear strength**.

## Stress and strength explained

Gravity acting on slope material can be split into two parts: one pulling **along** the slope (shear stress) and one pressing **into** it (normal force, which helps friction). On a steeper slope, more of the weight acts downslope, so stress rises. Strength comes from **friction** between particles and from **cohesion**. Water acts on both sides:

- it adds **weight**, raising stress;
- it raises **pore water pressure**, which pushes particles apart and reduces the normal force, so friction falls;
- it can weaken **cohesion**, for example by softening clay.

**Factor of safety:** a value above 1 means the slope is stable; around 1 means failure is close; below 1 means failure. Any change that lowers strength or raises stress moves the factor towards 1.

## Classification of mass movement

| Group | Examples | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Heave | Soil creep, solifluction | Very slow movement; freeze-thaw and wetting-drying lift and settle particles |
| Flows | Earthflow, mudflow, debris flow | Saturated material moving as a viscous fluid; fast |
| Slides | Landslide, rotational slip (slump) | Material moves along a plane or curved surface |
| Falls | Rockfall | Free fall from a steep rock face, often after weathering |

Moisture content and speed increase from creep to flows.

## Each group in more detail

- **Heave (soil creep):** wetting or freezing lifts particles at right angles to the slope; drying or thawing lets them fall back vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a tiny distance downslope. Evidence: **terracettes**, leaning fences, walls and trees, and soil piled up behind walls. **Solifluction** is a periglacial process where a thawed active layer flows over permanently frozen ground, forming lobes.
- **Flows:** material behaves like a liquid when it holds a lot of water. **Mudflows** carry fine sediment; **debris flows** carry boulders and can travel fast along valleys. Volcanic mudflows are called **lahars**.
- **Slides:** a coherent mass moves on a slip surface. **Translational slides** move along a flat plane, such as a bedding plane dipping downslope. **Rotational slips (slumps)** move on a curved surface, typically in clay or where weak rock overlies stronger rock, leaving a back-tilted block and a curved scar.
- **Falls:** blocks detach from steep faces along joints, often after freeze-thaw or undercutting, and collect at the base as **scree (talus)** slopes at the angle of rest.

## Factors that raise stress or reduce strength

- Heavy rain or snowmelt adding weight and raising pore water pressure;
- steep slope angle;
- removal of vegetation and root support;
- undercutting by rivers or the sea;
- weak or jointed rock, or clay;
- earthquakes and other vibration;
- weathering that loosens material.

| Raises shear stress | Lowers shear strength |
|---|---|
| Steepening by undercutting or cutting | Rising pore water pressure |
| Extra weight from water, buildings or debris | Weathering of rock into weak regolith |
| Vibration from earthquakes | Loss of root cohesion |
| Removal of support at the base | Weak layers such as clay or bedding planes dipping downslope |

## Water and sediment movement

Rainsplash detaches particles. Surface runoff begins as thin **sheetwash**, then concentrates into **rills**. Runoff increases where infiltration is low: bare soil, compaction, intense rain. Vegetation reduces it.

- **Rainsplash:** each raindrop throws particles outwards; on a slope, more travel downhill than uphill, so there is a net downslope movement. It also seals the surface by filling pores with fine particles, which lowers infiltration and promotes runoff.
- **Sheetwash:** a thin, unchannelled layer of water that moves fine soil evenly across the surface.
- **Rills:** small channels, a few centimetres deep, where flow concentrates and erodes faster. If they deepen and join, they can grow into **gullies**.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain how heavy rainfall can trigger a landslide."*

> Infiltrating water adds weight to the slope material, raising shear stress (**stress**). It also raises pore water pressure, which reduces friction between particles (**strength**). In clay it can weaken cohesion (**strength**). When stress exceeds strength, the factor of safety falls below 1 and the slope fails along a slip surface (**outcome**).

## Exam traps

- State the cause of failure as an imbalance: stress greater than strength.
- Do not say water "only adds weight": it also lowers friction and cohesion.
- Slow versus rapid is about speed; flows versus slides is about how material moves.
- Soil creep moves particles by lifting and falling, not by steady sliding.
- A slump has a curved slip surface; a translational slide has a flat one.
- Name evidence in the landscape, such as terracettes or a slump scar, when describing a process.
