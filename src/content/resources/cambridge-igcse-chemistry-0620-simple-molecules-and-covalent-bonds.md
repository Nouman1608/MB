This guide covers subtopic **2.5 Simple molecules and covalent bonds** in Topic 2, Atoms,
elements and compounds, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The
overview in [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/)
shows water, methane and carbon dioxide; this page works through every molecule the
syllabus names, with an electron-counting method you can apply to unfamiliar ones.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- State that a covalent bond is formed when a **pair of electrons is shared between two
  atoms**, leading to **noble gas electronic configurations**.
- Describe covalent bonding in **H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃ and HCl**, using dot-and-cross
  diagrams for these "and similar" molecules.
- Describe the properties of simple molecular compounds: **low melting and boiling
  points**; **poor electrical conductivity**.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Describe covalent bonding in **CH₃OH, C₂H₄, O₂, CO₂ and N₂**, using dot-and-cross
  diagrams.
- **Explain** the properties: low melting and boiling points in terms of **weak
  intermolecular forces** (the specific types are not required), and poor conductivity.

"And similar molecules" matters: an exam can give you a molecule that is not on the list,
such as hydrogen sulfide or phosphorus trichloride, and expect you to draw it with the
same method.

## What a covalent bond is

Covalent bonds form between **non-metal atoms**. Neither atom can easily lose electrons, so
instead each contributes one electron to a **shared pair**. Both nuclei attract the shared
pair, and this holds the atoms together. Each shared pair is one **single covalent bond**,
drawn as a line in a displayed formula: H–Cl.

Sharing lets each atom count the shared electrons towards its own outer shell, giving it a
noble gas configuration: eight outer electrons for most atoms, two for hydrogen.

Some atoms share **two** pairs with the same partner (a **double bond**, C=C or O=O) or
**three** pairs (a **triple bond**, N≡N).

## How many bonds does each atom form?

An atom forms as many covalent bonds as the number of electrons it needs to fill its outer
shell.

| Atom | Outer electrons | Electrons needed | Usual number of bonds | Lone pairs left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 4 | 0 |
| N | 5 | 3 | 3 | 1 |
| O | 6 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Cl | 7 | 1 | 1 | 3 |

A **lone pair** is a pair of outer electrons not involved in bonding. Lone pairs must be
drawn: forgetting them is the commonest reason a diagram loses its mark.

## Dot-and-cross diagrams: the method

1. Count the outer electrons on each atom.
2. Decide which atoms bond to which (the atom that forms most bonds usually goes in the
   middle).
3. Draw one dot and one cross in the overlap for each shared pair.
4. Add the remaining outer electrons as lone pairs, in the same symbol as their atom.
5. **Check:** every atom has eight outer electrons (hydrogen two), and the total number of
   dots and crosses equals the total number of outer electrons you started with.

## The Core molecules

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Lone pairs | Electron check |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 1 (H–H) | none | each H: 2 |
| Cl₂ | 1 (Cl–Cl) | 3 on each Cl | each Cl: 6 + 2 = 8 |
| HCl | 1 (H–Cl) | 3 on Cl | H: 2; Cl: 8 |
| H₂O | 2 (two O–H) | 2 on O | O: 4 + 4 = 8 |
| NH₃ | 3 (three N–H) | 1 on N | N: 2 + 6 = 8 |
| CH₄ | 4 (four C–H) | none | C: 8 |

### Worked example 1 (Core): ammonia, NH₃

Nitrogen (2,5) has five outer electrons and needs three more, so it shares one pair with
each of three hydrogen atoms. Two of nitrogen's electrons are left as a lone pair.

```
        ××
   H •× N ×• H
        ×•
        H

N: 5 crosses (2 in the lone pair, 1 in each of 3 bonds)
each H: 1 dot.  Total = 5 + 3 = 8 electrons drawn
N has 8 outer electrons; each H has 2
```

## The Supplement molecules

| Molecule | Bonds | Lone pairs | Electron check |
|---|---|---|---|
| O₂ | one O=O double bond (2 shared pairs) | 2 on each O | each O: 4 + 4 = 8 |
| N₂ | one N≡N triple bond (3 shared pairs) | 1 on each N | each N: 2 + 6 = 8 |
| CO₂ | two C=O double bonds | 2 on each O; none on C | C: 8; each O: 8 |
| C₂H₄ | one C=C double bond and four C–H | none | each C: 4 (from C=C) + 4 (two C–H) = 8 |
| CH₃OH | three C–H, one C–O, one O–H | 2 on O | C: 8; O: 4 + 4 = 8 |

### Worked example 2 (Supplement): nitrogen, N₂

Each nitrogen atom has five outer electrons and needs three more. A single bond would leave
each atom with only six, so the atoms share **three pairs**.

- In the overlap: **three dots and three crosses**, drawn as three pairs.
- Outside the overlap: **one lone pair** on each atom — two dots on one atom, two crosses on
  the other.
- Check: 5 + 5 = 10 electrons drawn; each atom counts 6 shared + 2 lone = 8.

The same check catches errors in ethene. Each carbon has four outer electrons; two go into
the C=C double bond and one into each of two C–H bonds, so there are **no lone pairs** on
carbon. If your ethene diagram has a lone pair on carbon, you have drawn only a single C–C
bond.

In methanol, the oxygen sits between the carbon and one hydrogen. Oxygen's six outer
electrons are split into one electron in the C–O bond, one in the O–H bond and two lone
pairs.

## Properties of simple molecular substances

A **simple molecular** substance is made of separate small molecules. Two kinds of force
are involved, and you must not confuse them:

- **Covalent bonds** *within* each molecule — strong.
- **Intermolecular forces** *between* molecules — weak.

**Low melting and boiling points.** Core: describe — many simple molecular substances are
gases or liquids at room temperature, such as chlorine, methane and water. Extended:
explain — melting or boiling separates the molecules from one another; only the **weak
intermolecular forces** have to be overcome, which needs **little energy**. The covalent
bonds are **not broken**: steam is still H₂O molecules.

Intermolecular forces generally become stronger as molecules get larger. That is why, down
Group VII, chlorine is a gas, bromine a liquid and iodine a solid at room temperature.

**Poor electrical conductivity.** Simple molecules are **neutral** and there are **no ions
or free electrons** to carry charge, in the solid, the liquid or the pure substance. Some
molecular substances, such as hydrogen chloride, react with water to form ions, so their
solutions do conduct — but that is a property of the solution, not of the molecules.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (Supplement): Explain why methane has a low boiling point.* **[2]**

> Methane is made of simple molecules held together by **weak intermolecular forces** ✓.
> Only a **small amount of energy** is needed to overcome these forces when it boils ✓.

Writing "the covalent bonds are weak" scores zero: C–H bonds are strong and do not break.

## Common mistakes

- **Missing lone pairs** on N, O and Cl.
- **Single bonds in O₂, N₂, CO₂ or C₂H₄.** Count the electrons: each atom must reach eight.
- **Mixing up symbols:** an atom's own electrons must all be the same symbol.
- **"Covalent bonds break when it boils."** Intermolecular forces are overcome.
- **"All covalent substances have low melting points."** Giant covalent structures such as
  diamond do not — see
  [Giant Covalent Structures](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-giant-covalent-structures/).

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** the definition with all three parts: **pair** of electrons, **shared**,
  **between two atoms**.
- **Draw / complete** a dot-and-cross diagram: run the electron check before you move on.
- **Describe** properties: name the property and the conditions.
- **Explain** (Supplement): name the forces, say they are weak, say little energy is
  needed.
- **Predict** for an unfamiliar molecule: use the bonds-per-atom table above.

## Quick checklist

- Definition of a covalent bond
- Diagrams for H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl
- *(Supplement)* diagrams for O₂, N₂, CO₂, C₂H₄, CH₃OH
- Properties described (Core) and explained with intermolecular forces (Supplement)

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name types of intermolecular force?**
No. The syllabus says specific types are not required; "weak intermolecular forces" is the
expected phrase.

**Are lone pairs needed in a dot-and-cross diagram?**
Yes. Show all outer electrons, including lone pairs.
