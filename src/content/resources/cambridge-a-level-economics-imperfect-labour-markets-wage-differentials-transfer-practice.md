## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by a monopsony and give one example of where one could arise. **[2]**

**2.** In the market for port cleaners, demand for labour is L = 840 − 40W and supply is L = 80W − 240, where L is workers.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** A union secures a wage of 11. Calculate employment and the excess supply of labour. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the wage elasticity of demand for labour between the two wages. **[3]**
**(d)** Calculate the change in the total wage bill. **[2]**
**(e)** Using (c), explain whether the union's claim benefits its members as a group. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows a labour market for kitchen assistants.

| Wage (cu) | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Workers demanded | 90 | 80 | 70 | 60 | 50 |
| Workers supplied | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium wage. **[1]**
**(b)** A national minimum wage of 10 is introduced. State employment and the excess supply of labour. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the wage elasticity of demand over this change. **[2]**
**(d)** State the effect of a national minimum wage of 7. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate the change in the wage bill for the 10 minimum. **[1]**

**4.** A quarry is the only employer in its district. The table shows the workers it can hire.

| Workers | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wage needed (cu) | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |
| Total wage cost | 5 | 12 | 21 | 32 | 45 | 60 | 77 | 96 |
| MRP (cu) | 16 | 15 | 14 | 13 | 12 | 11 | 10 | 9 |

**(a)** Calculate the marginal cost of labour of the second, third and fourth workers. **[2]**
**(b)** State the employment and wage that maximise the quarry's profit. **[3]**
**(c)** State employment, and the range in which the wage would lie, if the labour market were competitive. **[2]**
**(d)** A minimum wage of 9 is introduced. State the effect on employment. **[3]**

**5.** Evaluate the view that a national minimum wage always reduces employment. **[8]**

**6.** Pay for tunnel inspectors, who need a rare licence, is much higher than for shelf stackers. Explain two market forces that could cause this wage differential. **[6]**

**7.** A bakery sells each loaf for 6 cu. Worker P bakes 10 loaves an hour more than without P, and worker R adds 7.

**(a)** Calculate the MRP of each worker. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage by which P's MRP exceeds R's. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain two reasons why actual pay differences might not match this gap. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Define transfer earnings and economic rent. **[2]**
**(b)** A goalkeeper is paid 48 cu an hour. The best alternative job pays 30 cu. Calculate economic rent and its share of earnings. **[2]**
**(c)** State the effect on rent if the best alternative job pays 40 cu. **[1]**

**9.** An occupation has supply W = 4 + 0.05L and demand W = 34 − 0.05L.

**(a)** Calculate equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate transfer earnings. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate economic rent. **[1]**
**(d)** Demand rises to W = 40 − 0.05L. Calculate the new wage, employment and economic rent. **[3]**
**(e)** Calculate the change in economic rent. **[1]**

**10.** Explain why a leading concert violinist earns more economic rent than a receptionist. **[6]**

**11.** Using the table in question 4, assess whether a union that negotiates a wage of 10 is likely to benefit members of the quarry workforce. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour [1]. Example: a quarry that is the only employer in an isolated district [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Make sure the definition refers to a single buyer of labour; a single seller describes a monopoly, which is a different market.

**2. (a)** 840 − 40W = 80W − 240, so 1,080 = 120W [1]. **W = 9**, L = 840 − 360 = **480** [1].
**(b)** Employment: 840 − 440 = **400** [1]. Supply: 880 − 240 = 640 [1]. Excess supply = 640 − 400 = **240** [1].
**(c)** Employment −80 ÷ 480 = −16.7% [1]. Wage +2 ÷ 9 = +22.2% [1]. Elasticity = **−0.75** [1].
**(d)** Before 9 × 480 = 4,320; after 11 × 400 = 4,400 [1]. Rise of **80** [1].
**(e)** Demand is inelastic (−0.75), so the wage rise outweighs the fall in employment [1]. Total pay to members as a group rises, although 80 jobs are lost [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (e) gains credit when it uses the elasticity value from (c) and refers to the group, not just to individuals.

**3. (a)** Demand equals supply at **8** (70 workers) [1].
**(b)** At 10 demand is 50, so employment is **50** [1]; supply is 110, so excess supply is **60** [1].
**(c)** Employment −20 ÷ 70 = −28.6% and wage +2 ÷ 8 = +25% [1]. Elasticity = **−1.14** [1].
**(d)** A minimum of 7 is below equilibrium, so it has **no effect** [1].
**(e)** 8 × 70 = 560 and 10 × 50 = 500, so the bill falls by **60** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Reading the demand figure, not the supply figure, as employment at a floor above equilibrium is the key step.

**4. (a)** Second: 12 − 5 = **7** [1]. Third: 21 − 12 = **9**; fourth: 32 − 21 = **11** [1].
**(b)** Hire while MC of labour is at most MRP [1]. Fourth: MC 11 against MRP 13, so hire. Fifth: MC 13 against MRP 12, so stop, giving **4 workers** [1]. Wage from the supply column = **8** [1].
**(c)** Hire while the wage needed is below MRP: sixth has 10 against 11, seventh has 11 against 10 [1]. **6 workers, with the wage between 10 and 11** [1].
**(d)** At 9 the quarry can attract 5 workers, and the marginal cost is a flat 9 for each of them [1]. The fifth has MRP 12, above 9; a sixth would need 10 for everyone, a marginal cost of 15 against MRP 11, so it hires **5** [1]. Employment rises from 4 to 5, though below the competitive 6 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Answers on a monopsony floor tend to be stronger when they compare employment before and after, and not only state the new figure.

**5.** Point 1: a floor above equilibrium creates excess supply and employment falls along demand [1]. Point 2: the fall is smaller if labour demand is inelastic [1]. Point 3: a floor at or below equilibrium has no effect [1]. Point 4: in a monopsony a floor between the monopsony and competitive wages can raise employment [1]. Point 5: higher pay may raise productivity and shift MRP right [1]. Point 6: more spending by low earners can raise demand for products [1]. Point 7: firms may adjust through prices, hours or substitution [1]. Judgement: the answer depends on the market structure, the elasticity and how far the floor is above the previous wage, so it does not always reduce employment [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when it ends in a supported judgement about conditions, not a list of "it depends".

**6.** Supply: the licence takes long training, so few can supply inspectors and the supply curve lies far left [1]; the wage needed to clear the market is higher [1]; stackers need little training, so supply is large [1]. Demand: an inspector's MRP is high because errors are costly [1]; firms are willing to pay up to MRP [1]; stackers add less value [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking each force to the position of a demand or supply curve is stronger than naming it.

**7. (a)** P: 10 × 6 = **60**; R: 7 × 6 = **42** [1][1].
**(b)** (60 − 42) ÷ 42 × 100 = **42.9%** [1].
**(c)** Unions may raise pay for one group above its MRP [1]. Discrimination or poor mobility can widen or narrow gaps [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show 18 ÷ 42 explicitly; the base of the percentage is the lower MRP.

**8. (a)** Transfer earnings: minimum payment to keep a factor in its present use [1]. Economic rent: payment above transfer earnings [1].
**(b)** Rent = 48 − 30 = **18** [1]; share = 18 ÷ 48 = **37.5%** [1].
**(c)** Rent falls to **8** (48 − 40) [1].
*Examiner insight:* State the opportunity cost as transfer earnings before subtracting.

**9. (a)** 30 = 0.1L, so **L = 300**; W = 4 + 15 = **19** [1][1].
**(b)** Area under supply = 300 × 4 + ½ × 300 × 15 = 1,200 + 2,250 [1] = **3,450** [1].
**(c)** Earnings 5,700 − 3,450 = **2,250** [1].
**(d)** 36 = 0.1L, so L = 360 [1]; W = 22 [1]. Transfer earnings = 1,440 + ½ × 360 × 18 = 4,680; earnings 7,920; rent = **3,240** [1].
**(e)** 3,240 − 2,250 = **990** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Define the triangle by its base (employment) and height (wage minus the supply intercept).

**10.** Supply of top violinists is highly inelastic because talent and training are rare [1]. Few substitutes exist [1]. The next best job pays little compared with performing, so transfer earnings are low [1]. High demand pushes the wage far above transfer earnings [1]. A receptionist's supply is elastic because many workers can move into the job [1]; so most pay equals transfer earnings and rent is small [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers contrast both occupations rather than describing one.

**11.** At the monopsony wage the quarry hires 4 workers at 8 [1]. A negotiated wage of 10 makes marginal cost flat at 10 up to 6 workers [1]. MRP is at least 10 up to the seventh worker, but only 6 are supplied at 10, so employment rises to 6 [1]. The wage rises 25% [1]. The bill rises from 32 to 60 [1]. This matches the competitive outcome [1]. Limits: a wage of 13.5 gives only 3 workers because the fourth worker's MRP of 13 is below it [1], so jobs fall below 4 [1]. Judgement: members gain jobs as well as pay only while the wage stays below 13 [1], and if the quarry is a monopsony [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the table figures in the argument, then state a conclusion about the range of wages that helps members.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using supply as employment when a floor is above equilibrium.
- Forgetting the sign of an elasticity.
- Reading the monopsony wage from the MC column.
- Treating total pay as rent without subtracting the alternative.
- Describing a union as raising wages with no job effect.
- Giving "it depends" in evaluation without naming the condition.
- Using a base of the new value when calculating a percentage change.
