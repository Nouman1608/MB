## Why homeostasis matters

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a **constant internal environment**, within narrow limits, despite internal or external changes. The syllabus highlights three variables, each important for a cell-level reason:

- **Temperature**: enzymes work fastest near their optimum; too high denatures them, too low slows reactions.
- **Blood glucose**: cells need a steady supply for respiration; brain cells depend heavily on it. Too high lowers the water potential of blood, so water leaves cells by osmosis.
- **Water potential**: cells swell or shrink by osmosis if the water potential of tissue fluid changes.

## The control loop

Every homeostatic mechanism follows the same pattern:

1. A **stimulus** is a change in a factor. It may be **internal** (blood glucose rises after a meal) or **external** (air temperature falls).
2. **Receptors** detect the change.
3. A **coordination system** (nervous or endocrine) sends information.
4. **Effectors** (muscles or glands) bring about a response.
5. **Negative feedback**: the response reverses the change, so the factor returns towards its set point. Once it has returned, the stimulus for the response is removed.

Negative feedback produces small oscillations about the set point, not a perfectly flat line. Having **separate mechanisms** to raise and to lower a factor (such as insulin and glucagon) gives finer control.

## Urea and the kidney

Excess amino acids cannot be stored. In the liver they are **deaminated**: the amino group is removed and converted to **urea**, which is carried in the blood to the kidneys.

**Gross structure** (outer to inner): **fibrous capsule** → **cortex** → **medulla** → **renal pelvis** → **ureter**. Branches of the **renal artery** bring blood in; branches of the **renal vein** take it out.

**The nephron in order**: glomerulus inside the Bowman's capsule (cortex) → proximal convoluted tubule (cortex) → loop of Henle (dips into the medulla) → distal convoluted tubule (cortex) → collecting duct (runs down through the medulla to the pelvis).

On a photomicrograph of the cortex, look for circular glomeruli surrounded by a space and a thin capsule wall, and many cut tubules around them. Tubules with a fuzzy inner edge (microvilli) are proximal convoluted tubules.

## Ultrafiltration in the Bowman's capsule

**What happens.** Blood in the glomerulus is under **high hydrostatic pressure**, because the **afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole**. Water and small solutes are forced out of the capillaries into the capsule space, forming **glomerular filtrate**.

**The three-layer filter**:

| Layer | Structure | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Capillary endothelium | Has many gaps (pores) between cells | Lets plasma through freely; holds back blood cells |
| Basement membrane | Mesh of protein fibres | Acts as the actual filter, holding back most plasma proteins |
| Podocytes | Cells of the capsule wall with foot-like extensions and slits between them | Leave filtration slits so filtrate passes easily into the capsule |

Filtrate therefore contains water, glucose, amino acids, urea and ions at about the same concentrations as plasma, but almost no protein and no cells.

## Selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule

All glucose and amino acids, most ions and much of the water are reabsorbed here. The mechanism is a sequence:

1. **Sodium–potassium pumps** in the **basal membrane** (facing the blood) actively move Na⁺ out of the PCT cell, using ATP. The Na⁺ concentration inside the cell falls.
2. Na⁺ diffuses into the cell from the lumen through **cotransporter proteins**, each carrying a glucose or amino acid molecule with it, against that molecule's gradient.
3. Glucose and amino acids diffuse out of the cell into the blood by **facilitated diffusion**.
4. Removing solutes lowers the water potential of the cells and blood, so **water follows by osmosis**.

**Structure to function**: **microvilli** give a large surface area on the lumen side; **many mitochondria** supply ATP for the pumps; **basal infoldings** increase the surface area for pumps and transporters; close contact with capillaries keeps diffusion distances short.

### Worked example 1: reading filtrate data

Illustrative values (g dm⁻³):

| Substance | Plasma | Glomerular filtrate | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | 80 | 0.05 | 0 |
| Glucose | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0 |
| Urea | 0.30 | 0.30 | 20 |

- Protein is almost absent from filtrate because the **basement membrane** holds back large molecules.
- Glucose is at the same concentration in filtrate as in plasma (small, filtered freely) but absent from urine, because it is **all reabsorbed in the PCT**.
- Urea is concentrated in urine. Concentration factor = 20 ÷ 0.30 = **66.7 times**. This happens mainly because so much **water** is reabsorbed, not because urea is secreted.

If glucose appears in urine, the blood glucose concentration was so high that the cotransporters in the PCT could not reabsorb it all.

## Osmoregulation and ADH

**Worked example 2: an annotated model answer.**
*Explain how the body responds to a fall in blood water potential after heavy sweating.* (6 marks)

1. **Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus** detect the fall in water potential of the blood. *(Receptor and location named.)*
2. The hypothalamus sends nerve impulses to the **posterior pituitary gland**, which releases **ADH** into the blood. *(ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.)*
3. ADH binds to receptors on the cells lining the **collecting duct**. *(Target cells named.)*
4. Vesicles containing **aquaporins** move to and fuse with the cell surface membrane facing the lumen. *(Mechanism, not just "more permeable".)*
5. The collecting duct wall becomes **more permeable to water**, so more water moves by osmosis into the tissue fluid of the medulla, which has a lower water potential, and then into the blood. *(Direction of osmosis justified.)*
6. A **smaller volume of more concentrated urine** is produced, and as blood water potential returns to normal, less ADH is released: **negative feedback**. *(Outcome and feedback both stated.)*

When blood water potential rises, the reverse happens: less ADH, aquaporins are taken back into vesicles, and a large volume of dilute urine forms.

## Glucagon as an example of cell signalling

Glucagon is a peptide hormone and cannot cross the cell surface membrane. In a liver cell:

1. Glucagon binds to a **cell surface receptor**, causing a **conformational change** in it.
2. The receptor activates a **G-protein**, which stimulates **adenylyl cyclase**.
3. Adenylyl cyclase converts ATP into the **second messenger, cyclic AMP (cAMP)**.
4. cAMP activates **protein kinase A**, which starts an **enzyme cascade**.
5. At each stage, each active enzyme **phosphorylates and activates** many molecules of the next, so the signal is **amplified**.
6. The final enzyme in the pathway is activated and catalyses the **breakdown of glycogen** to glucose, which leaves the cell into the blood.

## Insulin, glucagon and negative feedback

| | Insulin | Glucagon |
|---|---|---|
| Secreted by | β cells of the islets of Langerhans | α cells of the islets of Langerhans |
| Stimulus | Blood glucose above the set point | Blood glucose below the set point |
| Target cells in the syllabus | Muscle cells and liver cells | Liver cells |
| Main effects | Muscle: more glucose transporter proteins (GLUT4) are moved into the cell surface membrane, so more glucose enters; glucose converted to glycogen. Liver: enzymes that phosphorylate glucose and make glycogen are activated, so glucose is taken up and stored | Glycogen broken down to glucose; glucose also made from other compounds such as amino acids; glucose released into blood |
| Result | Blood glucose falls | Blood glucose rises |

As glucose returns to the set point, the α or β cells detect it and secretion falls: negative feedback.

## Test strips and biosensors

Both rely on two enzymes:

1. **Glucose oxidase** catalyses the oxidation of glucose, producing **hydrogen peroxide**.
2. **Peroxidase** catalyses the reaction of hydrogen peroxide with a colourless chromogen, producing a **coloured compound**.

On a **test strip**, the colour that develops is compared with a printed chart; the more glucose, the darker the colour. A **biosensor** uses glucose oxidase immobilised on an electrode; the reaction produces a small electric current, proportional to glucose concentration, shown as a digital reading. Biosensors give a precise number rather than a colour range. Urine strips reflect earlier high blood glucose, not the current concentration.

## Common mistakes

- Saying ADH makes the collecting duct "absorb more water" without mentioning aquaporins.
- Writing that the posterior pituitary detects water potential. The **hypothalamus** does.
- Stating that glucagon enters the liver cell.
- Mixing up glycogen (a store), glucagon (a hormone) and glycogenolysis (a process).
- Describing ultrafiltration as active. It is driven by **hydrostatic pressure**.

- **Describe** (structure, cell signalling): name each component in order; diagrams can be labelled.
