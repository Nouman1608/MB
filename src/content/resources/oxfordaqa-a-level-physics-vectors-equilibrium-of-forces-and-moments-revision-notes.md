## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scalar | Quantity fully described by its size |
| Vector | Quantity needing both size and direction |
| Resultant | Single vector equivalent to the sum of several |
| Equilibrium | Net force on the object is zero |
| Moment of a force | Force × shortest distance between the point and the force's line of action |
| Couple | Two coplanar forces, equal in size, opposite in direction, acting along different lines |
| Moment of a couple | One force × perpendicular separation of the two lines |
| Centre of mass | Point through which the whole weight can be taken to act |

## Scalars and vectors (3.2.1)

| Scalar | Vector |
|---|---|
| distance | displacement |
| speed | velocity |
| mass | weight, force |
| | acceleration |

- Distance is path length. Displacement is the shortest separation between start and finish, with a direction.
- Speed = distance/time. Velocity = displacement/time.
- Mass does not depend on place. Weight = mass × gravitational field strength and points towards the planet's centre.

## Adding vectors

| Case | Method |
|---|---|
| Two vectors at 90° | R = √(a² + b²); angle from tan θ = opposite/adjacent |
| Two vectors at angle θ, tail to tail | R² = a² + b² + 2ab cos θ |
| Several vectors, any angles | Resolve into perpendicular components, add each axis, recombine |
| Scale drawing | Choose a scale, draw tip to tail, join start to end, measure |

Resolving F at θ to the horizontal: horizontal = F cos θ, vertical = F sin θ.

### Method: add vectors by components

1. Choose two perpendicular axes.
2. Resolve every vector along them.
3. Add the components on each axis, keeping signs.
4. R = √(Rx² + Ry²); angle from tan θ = Ry/Rx. State what the angle is measured from.

### Method: scale drawing

1. State the scale (for example 1 cm : 5 N).
2. Draw vector one at its measured angle.
3. Draw vector two from the tip of vector one.
4. Join start to finish; measure length and angle.

Worked reminder: 12 N east plus 5 N north gives R = √(144 + 25) = 13 N at tan⁻¹(5/12) = 22.6° north of east.

## Equilibrium of coplanar forces at a point (3.2.1)

- Resultant force = 0.
- Two forces: equal, opposite, in line.
- Three forces: they form a closed triangle when drawn tip to tail, and components sum to zero along any direction.
- An object at rest or moving at constant velocity is in equilibrium. The converse does not hold: rest is only one case.

### Method: three forces at a point

1. Draw a free-body diagram with every force and angle marked.
2. Choose axes to put as many forces as possible on an axis (along and perpendicular to a slope, or horizontal and vertical).
3. Write the sum of components = 0 along each axis.
4. Solve. Check by confirming that the force triangle closes.

Worked reminder: a 200 N weight hangs from two equal ropes, each 60° to the horizontal. 2T sin 60° = 200, so T = 115 N.

## Moments (3.2.2)

- moment = F × d, where d is perpendicular to the line of action. Unit: N m.
- For a force at angle θ to a rod of length L: moment = F L sin θ.
- Say clockwise or anticlockwise.

### Principle of moments

When a body is in equilibrium, total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment, about any one point.

### Method: beam in equilibrium

1. Mark all forces; put a uniform beam's weight at its midpoint.
2. Take moments about a point that an unknown force passes through, which eliminates it.
3. Equate clockwise and anticlockwise moments.
4. Resolve vertically (and horizontally if needed) for the other unknowns.

Worked reminder: a uniform 2.0 m rod of mass 3.0 kg is pivoted 0.40 m from its left end. What mass m hung on the left end balances it? The rod's weight acts 0.60 m to the right of the pivot, so m × 0.40 = 3.0 × 0.60 and m = 4.5 kg. The pivot force is not needed, because it passes through the pivot.

## Couples (3.2.2)

- Equal in size, opposite in direction, on parallel but separate lines.
- The net force is zero, so the object turns without accelerating sideways.
- Moment of couple = F × perpendicular distance between the lines.
- Examples: turning a tap with finger and thumb, a steering wheel turned with both hands, a wheel brace.

Worked reminder: 18 N forces 0.25 m apart give a couple of 18 × 0.25 = 4.5 N m.

## Centre of mass (3.2.2)

- The point where the weight can be taken to act.
- A uniform regular solid has its centre of mass at its centre (middle of a rod, cube or sphere).
- It can lie outside the material, for example the centre of a ring.
- For a loaded rod, take moments about the balance point: moments from each side are equal.
- Two point masses on a light rod: the centre of mass is closer to the larger mass. For 2.0 kg and 6.0 kg placed 0.80 m apart, the distance from the 6.0 kg mass is 2.0 × 0.80 / 8.0 = 0.20 m.
- If a body hangs freely from a point, its centre of mass lies vertically below that point once it settles.

## Must-know distinctions

- Distance is not displacement; speed is not velocity.
- Mass is a scalar in kg; weight is a force in N.
- A couple has zero resultant force; an unbalanced single force has a resultant force.
- Equilibrium means zero resultant force, not zero velocity.
- Moment of a force uses distance to one line from a point; moment of a couple uses distance between two lines.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish a scalar from a vector, giving one example of each.
2. Forces of 6.0 N and 8.0 N act at a point at 90° to each other. Find the resultant and its angle from the 8.0 N force.
3. A force of 120 N acts at 25° above the horizontal. Find its horizontal and vertical components.
4. State two conditions that must hold for three coplanar forces at a point to be in equilibrium.
5. A box slides across a floor at constant velocity under a horizontal push of 45 N. What is the friction force? Is the box in equilibrium?
6. Find the moment of a 35 N force acting perpendicular to a lever 0.40 m from the pivot.
7. Two forces of 18 N, equal and opposite, act on a disc with their lines 0.25 m apart. Find the moment of the couple.
8. A uniform seesaw balances at its centre. A 30 kg child sits 1.6 m from the pivot. Where must a 24 kg child sit to balance?
9. A 40 N push on a door acts 0.80 m from the hinge, at 65° to the plane of the door. Find the moment about the hinge.
10. Where is the centre of mass of a uniform cube?

### Answers

1. A scalar has size only (for example mass); a vector also has a direction (for example force).
2. R = √(6.0² + 8.0²) = 10 N at tan⁻¹(6.0/8.0) = 36.9° from the 8.0 N force.
3. Horizontal = 120 cos 25° = 109 N; vertical = 120 sin 25° = 50.7 N.
4. The resultant force is zero (components sum to zero in any direction), and drawn tip to tail the forces form a closed triangle.
5. Friction is 45 N, opposite to the push. Yes: constant velocity means zero resultant force.
6. 35 × 0.40 = 14 N m.
7. 18 × 0.25 = 4.5 N m.
8. 30 × 1.6 = 24 × d, so d = 2.0 m on the other side of the pivot.
9. d = 0.80 sin 65° = 0.725 m; moment = 40 × 0.725 = 29.0 N m.
10. At its geometric centre, where the diagonals meet.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the full length of a lever as the distance when the force is angled to it.
- Writing "moment = force × distance" without saying perpendicular distance.
- Quoting a resultant size with no direction, or an angle with no stated reference line.
- Using cos θ for the vertical component when θ is measured from the horizontal.
- Omitting the weight of a uniform beam, or placing it at the wrong point.
- Taking moments about one point on one side and a different point on the other.
- Forgetting that a force through the pivot has zero moment, so it can be ignored.
- Treating a couple as if it produced a net force.
- Stating that equilibrium needs the object to be at rest.
- Leaving out units, or giving N m as a force.
