## What the specification asks for

- Recall and apply **density = mass ÷ volume**, ρ = m / V, including to changes where mass is conserved.
- Recognise and draw simple particle diagrams for solids, liquids and gases.
- Explain differences in density between the states in terms of the arrangement of atoms or molecules.
- Required practical 5: determine the densities of **regular and irregular solid objects and liquids**, finding
  volume from dimensions for regular shapes and by **displacement** for irregular ones, measuring dimensions with
  apparatus such as a ruler, micrometer or Vernier callipers.
- Describe how mass is conserved when substances melt, freeze, boil, evaporate, condense or sublimate, and explain
  why changes of state are physical changes.

## Density and the particle model

```
density = mass ÷ volume      ρ = m / V
ρ in kg/m³, m in kg, V in m³
```

Density measures how much mass is packed into each unit of volume. The particle model explains the differences
between states.

| State | Spacing of particles | Effect on density |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | Touching, in fixed regular positions | High: many particles per cubic metre |
| Liquid | Touching, but free to move past one another | High, usually slightly less than the solid |
| Gas | Far apart, with large spaces between | Very low: few particles per cubic metre |

The **particles themselves do not change size or mass** between states. Only their arrangement changes, so the
same mass occupies a different volume. When a liquid boils to a gas, the volume increases greatly while the mass
stays the same, so the density falls greatly.

**Drawing particle diagrams.** Use circles of equal size in all three boxes. Solid: rows touching in a regular
pattern. Liquid: touching, with no pattern and at least as many particles as in a similar solid region. Gas: a few
particles spread out, not touching. Never draw gas particles larger than solid ones.

## Units: the step most marks are lost on

Lab measurements are usually in grams and cubic centimetres. The specification's unit is kg/m³.

```
1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³      (a 1 m cube holds 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm cubes)
1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³
1 ml = 1 cm³
```

A quick route: calculate in g/cm³, then multiply by 1000 to get kg/m³.

**Worked example 1 (liquid).** An empty measuring cylinder has a mass of 82.0 g. With 50.0 cm³ of oil added, the
mass is 128.0 g. Find the density of the oil in kg/m³.

```
mass of oil = 128.0 − 82.0 = 46.0 g
ρ = 46.0 ÷ 50.0 = 0.920 g/cm³
ρ = 0.920 × 1000 = 920 kg/m³
```

**Worked example 2 (rearranging).** Aluminium has a density of 2700 kg/m³. Find the mass of a sheet with volume
0.0040 m³.

```
m = ρ × V = 2700 × 0.0040
m = 10.8 kg  (11 kg to 2 s.f.)
```

**Worked example 3 (mass conserved in a change of state).** 0.90 kg of water (density 1000 kg/m³) freezes into
ice of density 920 kg/m³. Find the volume before and after.

```
Water: V = m ÷ ρ = 0.90 ÷ 1000 = 0.000900 m³
Ice:   V = 0.90 ÷ 920 = 0.000978 m³
The mass is unchanged; the volume increases by about 9%.
```

This is the kind of change "where mass is conserved" that the specification mentions: the mass stays the same, so
any change in density must come from a change in volume.

## Required practical 5: density

**Regular solid (for example a metal cuboid).**

1. Measure each side. Use a ruler for lengths of several centimetres, Vernier callipers for a few centimetres to
   0.1 mm or better, or a micrometer for small thicknesses to 0.01 mm.
2. Calculate the volume: length × width × height (or the appropriate formula for a cylinder or sphere).
3. Measure the mass on a top-pan balance.
4. Calculate ρ = m / V.

**Irregular solid (for example a stone).**

1. Measure the mass on a balance.
2. Find the volume by displacement. Either: read the water level in a measuring cylinder, lower the stone in on a
   thread, and read the new level; or fill a displacement (eureka) can until water runs from the spout, wait for it
   to stop, lower the stone in, and collect the displaced water in a measuring cylinder.
3. The volume of water displaced equals the volume of the stone.

**Liquid.**

1. Place an empty measuring cylinder on the balance and zero it (or record its mass).
2. Pour in a measured volume of liquid, reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
3. Record the mass of the liquid and calculate ρ = m / V.

### Improving accuracy and precision

| Problem | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Reading the meniscus from above or below | Volume misread (parallax) | Read at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus |
| Stone dropped in, splashing water out | Volume too small, density too large | Lower it gently on a thread |
| Air bubbles clinging to the stone | Volume too large, density too small | Tap the cylinder to release bubbles |
| Eureka can not filled to the spout first | Volume too small | Fill until it drips, wait, then add the object |
| Using a ruler for a 2 mm thickness | Large percentage uncertainty | Use a micrometer |
| Single reading | Random errors not spotted | Repeat and calculate a mean |

## Changes of state

The six changes named in the specification:

| Change | From → to |
|---|---|
| Melting | Solid → liquid |
| Freezing | Liquid → solid |
| Boiling | Liquid → gas, throughout the liquid at the boiling point |
| Evaporating | Liquid → gas, from the surface |
| Condensing | Gas → liquid |
| Sublimating | Solid → gas directly |

**Why mass is conserved.** In a change of state, no particles are created or destroyed. They stay the same
particles with the same mass; only their arrangement, spacing and energy change. In a closed container the mass
measured before and after a change of state is the same. If a beaker of boiling water loses mass, it is because
gas particles have escaped into the room, not because mass has been destroyed.

**Why changes of state are physical changes.** A physical change can be reversed and the material **recovers its
original properties**. Melted wax that cools becomes solid wax again. In a chemical change, new substances form
and reversing the conditions does not give back the original material: burning wax produces gases, and cooling
them does not make wax.

## Common mistakes

- Using grams with cm³ but giving the answer as kg/m³ without multiplying by 1000.
- Saying gas particles are bigger or lighter than solid particles.
- Calling evaporation and boiling the same thing without saying where the change happens.
- Writing that mass decreases when ice melts. It is constant; the volume changes.
- Forgetting to subtract the starting level in a displacement method.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Calculate**: state ρ = m / V, convert units, then substitute.
- **Describe** a method: list apparatus, measurements, and how volume is found.
- **Explain** density differences: refer to particle **arrangement and spacing**, not to particle size.
- **Suggest** an improvement: name the error and say how the change reduces it.
