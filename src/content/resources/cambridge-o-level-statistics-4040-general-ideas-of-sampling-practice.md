These questions cover section 1.1 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: general ideas of sampling, including the terms population, census, sample and representative sample. Work from the [1.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-general-ideas-of-sampling/) or the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-general-ideas-of-sampling-revision-notes/) if you need to recall a method. All organisations and data are invented. Show your working.

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a census and a sample. **[2]**

**2.** A council wants to know how residents of Eastvale travel to work. Write down a precise description of a suitable population. **[2]**

**3.** For each investigation, state whether a census or a sample is more suitable and give a reason.

**(a)** Finding the mean time a brand of candle burns before going out. **[2]**

**(b)** Recording the date of birth of each of the 12 players in a football squad. **[2]**

**(c)** Finding the proportion of a country's adults who own a bicycle. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** advantages of using a sample rather than a census. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a representative sample. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A factory employs 800 people: 480 in production, 200 in offices and 120 in management. A sample of 80 employees contains 40 from production, 30 from offices and 10 from management.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the sample in each group. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain whether the sample is representative of the three groups. **[2]**

**(c)** Find how many employees from each group a representative sample of 80 should contain. **[3]**

**7.** A sample of 120 of the 2,400 commuters using a railway station found that 45 cycle to the station.

**(a)** Find the proportion of the sample who cycle. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the number of commuters at the station who cycle. **[2]**

**(c)** Give a reason why the true number may differ from your estimate. **[1]**

**8.** A farmer has 340 fields of the same crop. A representative sample of 25 fields gives a mean yield of 2.6 tonnes per field.

**(a)** Estimate the total yield of all 340 fields. **[2]**

**(b)** State one assumption you made. **[1]**

**9.** A website asks visitors to answer an online poll. 2,000 people respond. The website claims that, because the sample is large, the results represent all adults in the country. Explain why this claim may be wrong. **[3]**

**10.** A student says: "A census is always better than a sample, because it includes everyone." Give **two** reasons why this is not always true. **[4]**

**11.** A quality inspector tests 250 light bulbs from a delivery of 12,000 and finds 9 faulty.

**(a)** Estimate the number of faulty bulbs in the delivery. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a census of the delivery would not be appropriate if the test involves running each bulb until it fails. **[2]**

**12.** Explain why a precise definition of the population is needed before choosing a sample. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A census collects data from every member of the population [1]; a sample collects data from only part of the population [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Who: residents of working age who travel to work [1]; where and when: living in Eastvale, on a stated date or during a stated month [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "People in Eastvale" is too vague; the mark needs who is included and the time.

**3. (a)** Sample [1]; burning the candles destroys them, so testing all would leave none to sell [1].

**(b)** Census [1]; the population is small and every player is easy to reach [1].

**(c)** Sample [1]; the population is millions of people, so a census would be too costly and slow [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason must fit its situation; "it is cheaper" for all three earns at most one reason mark.

**4.** Any two of: cheaper [1]; quicker [1]; less data to process; the only option when testing destroys the items. Also credit fewer, better-trained collectors making fewer errors. **[2]**

**5.** A sample in which the characteristics of the population [1] appear in roughly the same proportions as in the population [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Production 40/80 = 50%, offices 30/80 = 37.5%, management 10/80 = 12.5% [2] (one mark for two correct).

**(b)** The population is 60% production, 25% offices and 15% management [1]. The sample over-represents office staff and under-represents production and management, so it is not representative [1].

**(c)** Production 80 × 480/800 = 48 [1]; offices 80 × 200/800 = 20 [1]; management 80 × 120/800 = 12 [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that your three numbers add up to the sample size: 48 + 20 + 12 = 80.

**7. (a)** 45/120 = 0.375 [1].

**(b)** 0.375 × 2400 [1] = 900 commuters [1].

**(c)** The sample is only part of the population; a different sample would probably give a different proportion [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** 2.6 × 340 [1] = 884 tonnes [1].

**(b)** That the sample fields are representative, so the mean yield of all fields is the same as the sample mean [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Only people who visit the website can respond, so many adults are excluded [1]. People choose whether to respond, and those with strong views are more likely to [1]. A large sample is not representative if it comes from an unrepresentative part of the population; size does not fix this [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Testing may destroy items, so a census would leave nothing [1], for example testing the strength of every rope [1]. A census of a large population is very costly and slow [1], so the results may be out of date or contain more recording errors than a careful sample [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** 9/250 × 12000 [1] = 432 faulty bulbs (an estimate) [1].

**(b)** Running every bulb until it fails would destroy the whole delivery [1], leaving no bulbs to sell, so only a sample should be tested [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The population decides who is eligible to be chosen [1]; without a precise definition, the sample may include people or items that should not be there, or miss some that should [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- A population with no place or time.
- The same general reason ("cheaper") used for every situation.
- Assuming a large sample is representative.
- Forgetting to check that the numbers in a representative sample add up to the sample size.
- Not calling an estimate an estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a census ever the wrong choice?**
Yes. If testing destroys the items, or the population is very large, a census is impractical or impossible, and a well-chosen sample is better.

**Which part of the 4040 syllabus do these questions cover?**
