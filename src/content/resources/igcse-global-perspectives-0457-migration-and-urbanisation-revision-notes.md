These notes condense the [Migration and urbanisation study guide](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-migration-and-urbanisation/). Read that first if anything here is unfamiliar.

They cover the topic **Migration and urbanisation** from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025): the topic list in section 3, the assessment objectives in section 2 and the component details in section 4. The topic list applies to Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report and Component 3 Team Project.

**Remember:** the syllabus states that knowledge of content is not assessed. These notes show how to use the topic to practise the assessed skills. Test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-migration-and-urbanisation-practice/), and track progress with the [0457 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and the [Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## The skills, in the syllabus's own words

| Assessment objective | What it asks for | Weighting of the qualification |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation | Research issues, causes, consequences and courses of action; use evidence; analyse and evaluate evidence, reasoning and sources; develop a line of reasoning | 68% |
| AO2 Reflection | Consider perspectives objectively and with empathy; justify a personal perspective; consider how research and teamwork influenced your learning | 15% |
| AO3 Communication and collaboration | Present arguments, evidence and perspectives clearly; cite and reference; contribute to the Team Project | 17% |

Component 1 is 100% AO1. AO2 carries no marks in the Written Exam, so save personal reflection for Components 2 and 3.

## Real-world reference points at a glance

You do not need these for marks. They are accurate anchors to reason with in coursework, where you must cite your sources.

| Who / what | What it says or does | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| UN, *World Urbanization Prospects: The 2018 Revision* | 55% of people lived in urban areas in 2018; projected 68% by 2050 | Countries define "urban" differently, so comparisons need care |
| UN DESA international migrant estimates | About 304 million international migrants in mid-2024 | A global total hides huge differences between corridors and countries |
| Sustainable Development Goal 11 | Make cities inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable | A goal states an aim; it does not show progress |
| SDG target 10.7 | Orderly, safe, regular and responsible migration | Words such as "orderly" are interpreted differently by different governments |
| Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (2018) | A non-binding international framework on migration | A small number of states voted against or abstained, showing no single global perspective |
| China's *hukou* household registration | Has linked access to some public services to a person's registered place | Shows how national policy shapes the experience of internal migrants |
| WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel (2010) | Voluntary principles for recruiting health workers from other countries | Voluntary codes depend on governments and employers choosing to follow them |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue.** "Migration and urbanisation" is a topic. "Should a city clear an informal settlement?" is an issue.
- **Perspective vs opinion.** A perspective rests on evidence and reasoning; an opinion may not.
- **Fact vs claim.** "3,000 permits were approved" is a checkable fact. "Migrants push up rents" is a claim needing evidence.
- **Generalisation vs evidence.** "Everyone who moves is better off" generalises; a study of 60 migrants is evidence about those 60.
- **Vested interest vs bias.** A vested interest is a reason someone might be biased. Bias is shown in the content itself: selective data, loaded words.
- **Opinion data vs causal evidence.** A survey shows what people believe, not why something happens.
- **Cause vs consequence.** Drought is a cause of migration; crowded housing is a consequence. Some things are both in a chain.
- **Local vs national vs global.** A school buddy scheme is local; a work-permit rule is national; an international compact is global.
- **Action vs aim.** "Reduce overcrowding" is an aim. "Fit water points and drains in the settlement" is an action.

## Cause and consequence chains

Write chains, not lists:

- Falling farm incomes -> young adults move to the city -> population grows faster than housing -> informal settlements expand.
- Skilled nurses emigrate -> origin hospitals lose staff -> waiting times rise; meanwhile remittances support families.
- New arrivals fill service jobs -> some businesses expand -> demand for housing near work rises -> rents increase for everyone.

Always ask: **who gains, who loses, and at which level?**

## Using the topic in each component

- **Component 1 Written Exam:** the sources may be on any topic in the list. Practise on migration sources by naming claims, testing evidence, comparing arguments and assessing actions, using only what the sources say.
- **Component 2 Individual Report:** pick a global issue in this topic that divides opinion, write a global research question, and research local and/or national and global perspectives before proposing one course of action.
- **Component 3 Team Project:** start from a local issue, such as how newcomers to your area find services, and plan an action whose success you can measure.

## Judging an action: four criteria

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Does it tackle the cause, or only a symptom? |
| Feasibility | Cost, time, and who must agree? |
| Sustainability | Will it last, financially and environmentally? |
| Fairness | Who benefits, and does anyone lose out? |

## Mini essay plan: assessing actions

*Typical question style:* using the sources, which of two or three proposed actions would be most effective in responding to rapid city growth?

1. **Judgement first.** Name the action you think is most effective and the main reason.
2. **Action 1 for.** Link to a specific source detail (for example a figure on unsafe water). Explain how the action tackles the cause.
3. **Action 1 against.** One limitation, with evidence (cost, land rights, time).
4. **Action 2 compared.** Its strongest point and its weakest point, with source evidence.
5. **Action 3 compared (if given).** Briefly, using the same criteria.
6. **Weigh them.** Which criterion matters most here, and why?
7. **Conditional conclusion.** "Action 1 is most effective, provided that..." Say what evidence would change your judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. How does the syllabus define a perspective?
2. Name the eight terms the syllabus says Component 1 candidates will benefit from understanding.
3. Is "The city will double in size by 2050" a fact, a prediction or a value?
4. A developer funds a survey showing support for clearing a settlement. What term describes the developer's position?
5. What does a residents' opinion survey show, and what can it not show?
6. Give one personal, one local and one global level of the issue "people moving from villages to cities".
7. Which assessment objective carries no marks in Component 1?
8. What must the Individual Report's issue do, according to the syllabus?
9. What level must the Team Project issue be at?
10. Give one benefit and one drawback of relocating informal-settlement residents.
11. Name the four criteria for judging an action.
12. Why is the 2018 Global Compact useful when discussing global perspectives?

### Answers

1. A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning.
2. Bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value and vested interest.
3. A prediction.
4. A vested interest.
5. It shows what respondents believe; it cannot show the cause of a problem.
6. Personal: a family member who moved for work. Local: rents rising in one neighbourhood. Global: the projected rise in the share of people living in cities.
7. AO2 Reflection.
8. Divide opinion (and be global, with local and/or national and global perspectives).
9. Local.
10. Benefit: safer, planned homes. Drawback: the new site may be far from work, so social ties and income are lost.
11. Effectiveness, feasibility, sustainability, fairness.
12. Some states voted against or abstained, so it shows disagreement even at the global level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding outside facts about migration in Component 1 instead of using the sources. The Written Exam does not assess prior knowledge of the topic.
- Saying "this source is biased" without quoting loaded language or naming a vested interest.
- Treating opinion data ("72% think...") as proof of a cause.
- Ignoring sample problems: one district, one shopping centre, one job centre.
- Offering two sides ("for" and "against" migration) when the sources show several groups with distinct reasons.
- Describing consequences without saying who is affected and at which level.
- Assessing an action with no criteria, or not comparing it with alternatives.
- Ending without a judgement, or with a judgement that does not follow from the reasoning.
- In the Individual Report, a title that is a topic ("Urbanisation") rather than a question.
- In the Team Project, an action that cannot be measured.
