These notes condense **A1.1 Water** from the IB DP *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. Statements A1.1.1 to A1.1.6 are SL and HL; A1.1.7 and A1.1.8 are additional higher level. Full explanations and model answers are in the [A1.1 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-1-a1-1/), and the [A1.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-biology-1-a1-1-practice/) test them. Nucleic acids, the other half of A1, are in the [A1.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-biology-1-a1-2-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polar covalent bond | Covalent bond with unequal sharing of electrons, giving partial charges |
| Polar molecule | Molecule whose partial charges do not cancel, such as bent H₂O |
| Hydrogen bond | Attraction between a δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and a δ− atom such as oxygen of another |
| Cohesion | Attraction of water molecules to each other by hydrogen bonds |
| Adhesion | Attraction of water to a different polar or charged material |
| Capillary action | Movement of water through narrow spaces by adhesion and cohesion together |
| Surface tension | Resistance of a water surface to breaking, from inward pull on surface molecules |
| Hydrophilic | Polar or charged; attracted to water and dissolves in it |
| Hydrophobic | Non-polar; not attracted to water and does not dissolve |
| Buoyancy | Upward force on a body in a fluid |
| Viscosity | Resistance of a fluid to flow |
| Thermal conductivity | Rate at which heat passes through a material |
| Specific heat capacity | Energy needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C |

## The polarity chain (A1.1.2)

Unequal sharing in O–H bonds → δ− oxygen, δ+ hydrogens → bent shape, so charges do not cancel → δ+ H attracted to δ− O of a neighbour → hydrogen bond. Weak singly, strong in vast numbers.

In a diagram: δ− on O, δ+ on each H, dashed lines *between* molecules.

## A1.1.1 Medium for life

- First cells probably arose in water.
- Cell chemistry happens in aqueous solution: cytoplasm, plasma, tissue fluid, sap.

## A1.1.3 Cohesion: two consequences

| Consequence | How cohesion causes it |
|---|---|
| Transport under tension in xylem | Evaporation from leaves pulls water; hydrogen bonds keep the column continuous |
| Surface tension as a habitat | Surface molecules pulled inwards; surface supports small animals such as pond skaters |

## A1.1.4 Adhesion: one consequence

- Water is attracted to polar or charged materials: cellulose (–OH groups), clay particles (charged).
- **Capillary action** in soil holds water between particles; in plant cell walls it moves water through the cellulose.

### Cohesion versus adhesion in one line

Cohesion is water to water; adhesion is water to something else.

## A1.1.5 Solvent properties

| Substance | Polar or charged? | Behaviour in water | How it is transported |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Polar | Dissolves | In plasma; sucrose in phloem |
| Amino acids | Polar or charged | Dissolve | In plasma |
| Mineral ions (Na⁺, K⁺, NO₃⁻) | Charged | Dissolve | Plasma; xylem sap |
| Urea | Polar | Dissolves | In plasma |
| Oxygen | Non-polar | Poorly soluble | Mostly bound to haemoglobin |
| Fats, cholesterol | Non-polar | Hydrophobic | Combined with proteins |

Two roles to name: **medium for metabolism** (dissolved enzymes and substrates collide) and **medium for transport** (in plants and animals).

## A1.1.6 Water versus air for animals

| Property | Water vs air | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Buoyancy | Much greater | Body supported; less skeletal support needed |
| Viscosity | Much greater | More drag; streamlining favoured |
| Thermal conductivity | Much greater | Faster heat loss; insulation needed |
| Specific heat capacity | Much greater | Stable water temperatures |

**Named examples**

- **Black-throated loon:** legs set far back for swimming; bones less air-filled than in most flying birds, aiding diving; clumsy on land.
- **Ringed seal:** thick blubber against heat loss; streamlined body against drag.

## A1.1.7 and A1.1.8 (HL only)

- Earth's water may have arrived from space, for example on asteroids.
- Retained because gravity holds the atmosphere and temperatures let vapour condense.
- Search for life targets places where liquid water could exist.
- **Goldilocks zone:** distances from a star at which surface water could be liquid.
- Being in the zone makes a planet a candidate, not a proof of life.

## Worked reminders

**Comparing heat loss.** Thermal conductivity of water is about 0.6 W per m per K and of air about 0.026 (supplied values). 0.6 ÷ 0.026 ≈ 23, so heat leaves a body roughly 23 times as fast in water at the same temperature difference.

**Classifying from data.** A substance with a charged group and a long non-polar chain has a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part; say which part faces water.

**Goldilocks check.** At about Earth's pressure, liquid water needs 0 °C to 100 °C. A planet with a surface at 60 °C qualifies; one at −40 °C does not.

## Exam traps

- Hydrogen bonds drawn inside one molecule.
- "Water sticks together" offered for adhesion.
- Water "pushed" up the xylem.
- Solubility explained with no mention of polarity or charge.
- Thermal conductivity confused with specific heat capacity.
- Goldilocks zone presented as evidence that life exists.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is a water molecule polar overall?
2. Name the bond between two water molecules.
3. Give the two consequences of cohesion named in A1.1.3.
4. Define adhesion.
5. Why does water rise between soil particles?
6. Classify urea and cholesterol as hydrophilic or hydrophobic.
7. Why is most oxygen in blood carried by haemoglobin?
8. Which property of water explains why aquatic animals are often streamlined?
9. Which property of water explains why lakes change temperature slowly?
10. Give one adaptation of the ringed seal and the property of water it relates to.
11. (HL) Give two reasons Earth retained its water.
12. (HL) Define the Goldilocks zone.

### Answers

1. Its O–H bonds are polar and its bent shape means the partial charges do not cancel.
2. A hydrogen bond.
3. Transport under tension in xylem; surface tension as a habitat.
4. Attraction of water to a different polar or charged material.
5. Capillary action: adhesion to charged particles and cohesion pulling more water behind.
6. Urea hydrophilic; cholesterol hydrophobic.
7. Oxygen is non-polar, so it is poorly soluble in plasma.
8. Viscosity.
9. High specific heat capacity.
10. Blubber, related to high thermal conductivity (or streamlined body, related to viscosity).
11. Gravity holds the atmosphere; temperatures allow water vapour to condense.
12. The range of distances from a star in which a planet's surface temperature allows liquid water.
