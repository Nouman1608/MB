## 1. Limited and unlimited liability

Liability is who has to pay when a business cannot pay its debts.

- **Unlimited liability.** The owner is personally responsible for all of the business debts. If the business assets do not cover what is owed, creditors can look to the owner's own wealth, such as savings.
- **Limited liability.** The owner's risk is limited to the money they put in, which is usually the value of their shares. Creditors cannot ask the owner to cover the rest from personal wealth.

### Worked example: Elmstead Ceramics

Imran runs Elmstead Ceramics as a sole trader. The business fails owing £48,000. Selling every business asset raises £31,000.

Shortfall = 48,000 - 31,000 = £17,000.

With unlimited liability, Imran must find the £17,000 from personal resources.

Now suppose the same business had been a private limited company in which Imran paid £12,000 for shares. His loss is limited to that £12,000. The £17,000 shortfall is not his to pay. The creditors lose it. That is the trade-off: limited liability protects the owner and moves risk onto the people the business owes.

**Implications for the owner.** Unlimited liability means a business failure can become a personal financial crisis. It can make an owner cautious about borrowing and about expansion. Limited liability makes it easier to take a risk, and can make it easier to persuade other people to invest. Lenders may still ask a small company owner for a personal guarantee, which means the protection is not always complete in practice. This is a general possibility, not a rule.

## 2. Types of business ownership for start-ups

### Sole trader

One person owns and runs the business.

- Advantages: the owner keeps all the profit, makes decisions quickly, has few formalities to set up and keeps close contact with customers.
- Disadvantages: unlimited liability, a small pool of capital (the owner's own money and loans), the whole workload falls on one person, and the business can struggle to cover illness or holidays.

### Partnership

Two or more people share ownership. In general, partners in an ordinary partnership have unlimited liability (general knowledge, not specification text).

- Advantages: more capital than one person can raise, a wider mix of skills, a shared workload and shared decisions.
- Disadvantages: unlimited liability, profits must be shared, partners may disagree, and a partner's poor decision can leave the others paying.

Partners often agree in writing how to share profit. A common method is to share in proportion to the capital each put in.

### Worked example: Calder and Moss Design

Aisha puts in £40,000 and Rory puts in £20,000. They agree to share profit in the ratio of their capital, which is 2 : 1. Profit for the year is £54,000.

Total parts = 2 + 1 = 3. One part = 54,000 / 3 = £18,000.
Aisha = 2 x 18,000 = £36,000. Rory = £18,000. Check: 36,000 + 18,000 = £54,000.

### Private limited company

A private limited company is owned by shareholders, and, as a general rule, its name ends in Ltd. It has limited liability, so shareholders risk only what they invested.

- Advantages: limited liability, shares can be sold to family or investors to raise capital, and the business can continue even if one owner leaves.
- Disadvantages: more rules and paperwork than a sole trader faces, the founder may share control and profit with other shareholders, and, in general, the shares cannot be offered freely to the public.

Keep legal detail general. The specification asks for advantages and disadvantages, not for statutes or filing deadlines.

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One | Two or more | Shareholders |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited (ordinary partnership) | Limited |
| Capital | Narrowest | Wider | Widest of the three |
| Control | Complete | Shared | Shared with shareholders |
| Set-up | Simplest | Simple | More formal |

## 3. Franchising

A franchise is an arrangement where a **franchisor** (the owner of a brand and business method) lets a **franchisee** run an outlet under that name. In general, a franchisee pays an initial fee and then a royalty, often a percentage of sales revenue (a common structure, not specification text).

**Advantages of franchising (mainly for the franchisee):** a known brand brings customers from the start, a proven business format lowers the risk of failure, and the franchisor may give training and support. **For the franchisor:** the brand can grow without the franchisor putting up all the capital, and the franchisee is motivated because it is their own money at stake.

**Disadvantages:** the franchisee pays fees and royalties, has less freedom over products and prices, and can be harmed by the franchisor's reputation. The franchisor loses some control over quality and shares the revenue.

### Worked example: Fresh Press Juice

Nadia buys a Fresh Press Juice franchise. She pays an initial fee of £30,000 and a royalty of 7% of sales revenue. Her annual sales revenue is £180,000 and her profit before royalty is £34,000.

Royalty = 7% x 180,000 = £12,600.
Profit after royalty = 34,000 - 12,600 = £21,400.
In year one, after also paying the fee: 21,400 - 30,000 = -£8,600.

The first year does not cover the fee. Whether the franchise is a good idea depends on how long the agreement runs and whether the brand keeps trade up.

## 4. Proximity to market, labour, materials and competitors

Location means where the business operates. Each factor pulls in a different direction, and the best site is a trade-off.

- **Proximity to market.** Being near customers matters most where people walk in or where goods are costly or difficult to deliver. It can raise sales and cut distribution cost, but rent in busy places is higher.
- **Proximity to labour.** Being near enough people with the skills you need makes recruitment easier and may keep wages competitive. A shortage of skilled labour can push wages up.
- **Proximity to materials.** Heavy, bulky or perishable inputs cost more to carry and may spoil. Being close lowers transport cost and waste.
- **Proximity to competitors.** Being close can bring customers who are comparing choices, but it also means sharing them. Being far away can give a local monopoly but fewer passing customers.

## 5. Nature of the business activity

The activity decides which factor dominates. A shop or hairdresser lives on footfall, so market comes first. A business that makes bulky goods from heavy materials leans toward the materials. A business that depends on a rare skill goes where the skilled labour is. A business that needs a lot of space may prefer cheaper land away from town centres. Always tie the factor to what the business actually does.

### Worked example: Oakfield Loaves

A bakery compares two sites for a year.

| | Site A (town centre) | Site B (industrial estate) |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | £24,000 | £14,400 |
| Cost of getting materials in | £6,000 | £11,000 |
| Expected sales revenue | £140,000 | £118,000 |

Site A location costs = 24,000 + 6,000 = £30,000. Site B = 14,400 + 11,000 = £25,400. Site A costs £4,600 more.
Site A sales are 140,000 - 118,000 = £22,000 higher, which is 22,000 / 118,000 x 100 = 18.6% higher.

Site A costs a little more but brings far more sales revenue. A shop depending on walk-in trade would probably choose it, provided the sales forecast is reliable.

## 6. The internet and location

E-commerce means selling over the internet. It changes the location decision in several ways.

- A business can reach customers anywhere, so being near the market matters less for sales.
- It can choose cheaper premises, since customers do not visit. A warehouse in a low-rent area may be enough.
- Fixed premises still matter. Goods must be stored and sent, some customers want to see and try items, and services such as repairs or hairdressing cannot be delivered online.
- Many businesses combine both, using a website alongside a shop or a depot. Delivery costs and returns become new location factors.

## Common errors

- Writing that limited liability means the business cannot lose money. It limits the owner's loss, not the business's.
- Saying that partners always have limited liability. Be exact: ordinary partners usually do not.
- Giving a franchise advantage without saying who gains. Name the franchisor or franchisee.
- Listing location factors without linking them to the business described.
- Claiming the internet removes the need for any premises.
