These notes condense content item 2.3.3 Managing quality from Topic 2.3 Making operational decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples are in the [managing quality study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-managing-quality/) and exam-style questions in the [managing quality practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-managing-quality-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

The concept of quality and its importance in:

- the production of goods and the provision of services: **quality control** and **quality assurance**
- allowing a business to **control costs** and **gain a competitive advantage**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quality | Meeting customers' expectations and being fit for purpose, consistently |
| Quality control (QC) | Checking products or services, usually at the end or at set stages, to find and remove faults |
| Quality assurance (QA) | A system of standards at every stage to prevent faults, with every worker responsible |
| Defect (fault) rate | Faulty items ÷ total items × 100 (standard form) |
| Mystery shopper | A person paid to pose as a customer and report on service quality |
| Total quality management (TQM) | A QA approach aiming for zero defects and continuous improvement (standard term) |
| Competitive advantage | Something that makes customers choose a business over rivals |

## QC versus QA

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Find faults | Prevent faults |
| When | End of process or set stages | Every stage, from suppliers onwards |
| Who | Inspectors or managers | Every worker |
| Waste | Faults found after resources are used | Fewer faults made, so less waste |
| Set-up cost | Lower | Higher: training and systems |
| Goods example | Testing a sample from each production run | Supplier standards and worker self-checks |
| Service example | Mystery shoppers; checking a cleaned room | Service standards and staff training |

## Quality in services

- Produced while the customer is present, so faults cannot be removed before the customer sees them.
- Depends on staff: knowledge, manner, speed.
- Measured with feedback surveys, reviews, complaint rates and mystery shoppers.
- Managed mainly through QA: training, clear standards, systems that prevent errors.

**Applied example.** A driving school sets a standard that every lesson starts on time and ends with a written progress note (QA), and a senior instructor sits in on one lesson per instructor each term (QC). Pupils get a consistent experience, and the checks show whether the standard is being met.

## How quality controls costs

Poor quality costs: scrapped materials, rework, refunds, replacements, return postage, complaint handling and lost future sales.

**Rule of thumb:** a quality system pays for itself when it costs less than the faults it prevents.

**Quick calculation.** Penrose Bakery makes 5,000 cakes a week; 3% are faulty and each costs £1.20 to make. Faults cost 150 × £1.20 = **£180** a week. Training costing £90 a week that cuts faults to 1% saves (150 − 50) × £1.20 = £120, a net gain of **£30** a week.

## How quality builds competitive advantage

1. Reputation and good reviews attract new customers cheaply.
2. Loyal customers return and resist cheaper rivals.
3. Trusted quality supports a premium price.
4. Lower fault costs allow competitive prices without cutting profit.

Quality supports both differentiation and low-cost advantages.

## Comparing quality fairly

Use rates, not raw numbers, when volumes change. 24 complaints from 4,000 orders = 6 per 1,000; 20 complaints from 5,000 orders = 4 per 1,000. Complaints fell by a third, not just by 4.

## Exam traps

- Defining quality as luxury.
- Swapping QC and QA.
- Answering about goods only when the business provides a service.
- Assuming quality systems only add cost.
- Comparing raw complaint numbers when volumes differ.

## Self-test

1. Define quality assurance.
2. Give one difference between quality control and quality assurance.
3. Give one method of quality control in a service business.
4. Why can quality in services be harder to manage than quality in goods?
5. 2,000 items are checked and 70 are faulty. Calculate the defect rate.
6. Give two costs to a business of poor quality.
7. Give one way good quality can help a business gain a competitive advantage.
8. Why might QA motivate employees?
9. A firm makes 10,000 units with a 2% fault rate. Each fault costs £8. Calculate the total cost of faults.
10. In question 9, a training scheme costing £900 cuts the fault rate to 1%. Calculate the net saving.
11. What does TQM aim for?
12. Why can quality help a business keep prices competitive?

### Answers

1. A system of setting standards at every stage of production or service so that faults are prevented, with every worker responsible for quality.
2. QC finds faults after or during production; QA prevents faults by building quality into every stage.
3. Mystery shoppers, checking finished work such as a cleaned room, or reviewing customer feedback.
4. Services are provided while the customer is present and depend on staff behaviour, so faults cannot be removed before the customer experiences them.
5. 70 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **3.5%**.
6. Any two: wasted materials; rework; refunds and replacements; complaint handling; lost future sales.
7. Better reputation and reviews, customer loyalty, or the ability to charge a premium price.
8. Workers are trusted to check their own work and take responsibility, which can give a sense of achievement.
9. 10,000 × 2% = 200 faults × £8 = **£1,600**.
10. New faults = 100 × £8 = £800; saving on faults = £800; net saving = £800 − £900 = **−£100**, so the scheme costs £100 more than it saves.
11. Zero defects and continuous improvement, with every employee responsible for quality.
12. Fewer faults mean less waste and lower costs, so the business can charge competitive prices without cutting profit.
