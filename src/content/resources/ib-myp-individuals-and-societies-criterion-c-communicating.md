This study guide teaches Criterion C: Communicating in IB MYP Individuals and Societies. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Individuals and societies guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion C is not about how much you know. It is about how well you present what you know: in the right style for the reader or listener, in the right shape for the format you were asked to use, and with sources credited properly. The guide says students develop skills to organise, document and communicate their learning in a variety of media and presentation formats.

Use it with the [Criterion C revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-c-communicating-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion C practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-c-communicating-practice/). The wider course is on the [IB MYP Individuals and Societies course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/).

## What the guide says about Criterion C

At the end of Year 5 the three strands are:

1. **Communicate** information and ideas effectively using an appropriate style for the audience and purpose.
2. **Structure** information and ideas in a way that is appropriate to the specified format.
3. **Document** sources of information using a recognised convention.

Criterion C has a maximum of 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8. The command term "document" means to credit sources of information used by referencing (or citing), following a recognised referencing system. References should be included in the text and also at the end of the piece of work in a reference list or bibliography.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Band | i Style | ii Structure | iii Documenting sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | information communicated in a limited way, with limited appropriateness to audience and purpose | structured according to the format in a limited way | documents sources in a limited way |
| 3-4 | satisfactorily; style somewhat appropriate | somewhat appropriate to the format | sometimes documents using a recognised convention |
| 5-6 | accurately; style mostly appropriate | mostly appropriate to the format | often documents using a recognised convention |
| 7-8 | effectively and accurately; style completely appropriate | completely appropriate to the format | consistently documents using a recognised convention |

In Year 3 the strands read "communicate information and ideas in a way that is appropriate for the audience and purpose", "structure information and ideas according to the task instructions" and "create a reference list and cite sources of information". The band words move from "not always" through "somewhat" and "mostly" to "completely", and for sources from "inconsistently" to "always cites sources" with a complete reference list.

The key words are **appropriate**, **completely** and **consistently**. They tell you that the whole piece must fit the task, not just parts of it.

## Strand i: style for audience and purpose

Before you write, answer two questions.

- **Audience**: Who will read or listen? (a council committee, classmates, younger students, the public, a historian)
- **Purpose**: What is the piece for? (to inform, persuade, explain, narrate, argue, reflect)

Then choose the **style**.

| Element | Formal (for example a report or letter to officials) | Informal (for example a blog for teenagers) |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary | subject terms, precise words | everyday words, some subject terms explained |
| Sentences | longer, complex, objective | shorter, direct |
| Person | third person ("residents report") | first and second person ("you might think") |
| Tone | measured, respectful | friendly, lively |
| Devices | evidence, logical linking words | questions, examples, humour |

### Worked example: one topic, three styles

The topic is a proposal to close the high street of a town to cars and turn it into a walking area.

**Letter to the council (formal, persuasive).**
"Dear Councillor Reyes, I am writing on behalf of Year 10 at Millbrook School to support the proposal to pedestrianise the high street. Evidence from the council's own survey shows that 62% of shoppers arrive on foot or by bus. Reducing traffic would improve air quality and make the street safer for children. I would therefore urge the committee to approve a trial period of six months. Yours sincerely, ..."

Why it works: the opening states the purpose and who is writing; the tone is polite and formal; a figure supports the claim; the ending is a clear request.

**Blog post (informal, informing and persuading peers).**
"Imagine walking down your high street without dodging cars. That could happen if the council goes ahead with its plan. So what would change? Fewer fumes, safer crossings and more space for cafés. But some shop owners worry that customers who drive will stay away. Here is what we found when we asked them."

Why it works: a hook that speaks to the reader, short sentences, a question, and a promise of evidence.

**Poster or infographic (visual, informing).**
Title: "A walking high street: what could change?" Three headed boxes: "Air" (a short fact), "Safety" (a short fact), "Shops" (a short fact). A simple diagram of the street. A final line: "Have your say by 30 June."

Why it works: very little text, headings that can be scanned, visuals doing part of the explaining.

All three carry the same information. What differs is the choice of words, tone and layout, which is exactly what strand i rewards.

## Strand ii: structure for the specified format

If the task names a format, **follow its conventions**. The guide lists response types that can appear in the examination, including creative writing, a blog, an article, a letter, a presentation, a poster or infographic and a speech.

| Format | Typical structure |
|---|---|
| Letter | sender and date, greeting, opening paragraph (purpose), body paragraphs (one idea each), closing request, sign-off |
| Article | headline, introduction that sums up the story, body in order of importance or time, conclusion or quotation |
| Blog | title, personal opening, short sections with subheadings, direct address, closing thought or question |
| Speech | greeting, attention-grabbing opening, clear points with signposts ("first", "next"), rhetorical devices, strong ending and call to action |
| Presentation | title slide, agenda, one idea per slide, key words not sentences, visual support, summary slide, sources slide |
| Poster or infographic | title, headed sections, labelled visuals, key facts, sources in small print |
| Creative writing | a setting, a viewpoint, a clear sequence, details that show the topic, a purposeful ending |

Inside any piece, structure the **ideas** too. Use paragraphs with a topic sentence, evidence and a link back to the question. Use linking words such as "however", "as a result" and "in contrast". Plan before you write: a quick list of the points in order stops you from repeating yourself.

**Follow the task instructions.** If the task says "write a speech of about two minutes to Year 8", then the length, format and audience are all part of the marking. Re-read the instructions at the end to check each requirement.

## Strand iii: documenting sources

Documenting means crediting the sources you use, with **one** recognised convention, in the text **and** in a reference list or bibliography at the end.

**Why do it?** It shows where your information came from, lets readers check it, and respects the work of others. It is also part of academic honesty.

**Practical rules.**

- Choose a recognised system (your school will name one) and use it throughout.
- In the text, cite whenever you use a fact, figure, quotation, image or idea from someone else, for example (Mensah, 2021).
- In the reference list, give every source with the details your system asks for, usually author or organisation, year, title, publisher or website and the date you accessed it.
- Put the list in the order your system uses, often alphabetical by author.
- Quote only short passages, put them in quotation marks, and cite them. Paraphrase the rest in your own words and cite.
- Cite images and data tables too.

**Illustration with invented sources.**

In the text: "Air quality improved on streets that closed to traffic (Mensah, 2021), and shop income rose in the following year (Millbrook Council, 2022)."

Reference list:

- Mensah, K. (2021) *Streets without cars: a study of five towns*. Accra: Harbour Press.
- Millbrook Council (2022) *High street annual report*. Available at: millbrookcouncil.example/reports (Accessed: 12 May 2025).

These sources are made up for the example. Use real sources in your own work.

**For the top band** the documentation must be consistent: every in-text citation matches an entry in the list, every entry is complete, and the same style is used for each.

## How Criterion C is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for individuals and societies, Criterion C is assessed in the Communicating task (12 marks) and the Thinking critically task (8 marks), which makes 20 of the 80 marks. The guide says the Communicating task requires students to engage creatively with a given topic or context, presenting information and ideas effectively using an appropriate style for the audience and purpose and in a way that is appropriate to the specified format. The response types it lists are creative writing, blog, article, letter, presentation, poster or infographic and speech. Criterion A is also assessed in that task, for 6 marks.

## Common errors

- Writing in one style for every task, whatever the audience.
- A formal letter with slang, or a blog with long, heavy sentences.
- Ignoring the format, for example a speech with no opening or a poster with a block of text.
- Ideas out of order, or one paragraph that mixes several points.
- Not answering the purpose, for example informing when the task says persuade.
- Citing in the text but leaving the reference list out, or the other way round.
- Mixing referencing styles.
- Copying text without quotation marks.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion C in IB MYP Individuals and Societies?**
Criterion C is Communicating. It assesses how well you communicate information and ideas in a style that suits the audience and purpose, structure them for the specified format, and document your sources with a recognised convention.

**Which referencing system should I use?**
The guide asks for a recognised convention and does not name one. Use the one your school teaches, and use it consistently.

**Does the style of a piece affect the mark even if the content is right?**
Yes. Strand i rewards an appropriate style for the audience and purpose, and strand ii rewards the right structure for the format.

**How many marks is Criterion C worth in the on-screen examination?**
Twenty of the 80 marks: 12 in the Communicating task and 8 in the Thinking critically task.
