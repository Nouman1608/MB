These questions cover section **3.3.3 Halogenoalkanes** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification
(7404/7405), version 1.2, July 2026, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards: 3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic
substitution, 3.3.3.2 Elimination and 3.3.3.3 Ozone depletion. All of it is AS content, so nothing here is
A-level only. The topic is examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level, and A-level Paper 3 can test any content.

## Questions

**1.** Explain why the carbon atom bonded to bromine in bromoethane is attacked by nucleophiles. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *nucleophile*. **[1]**

**3.** 2-chlorobutane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Name the organic product. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the type of reaction. **[1]**

**4.** 2-bromopropane reacts with potassium cyanide.

**(a)** State the conditions used. **[1]**
**(b)** Outline the mechanism for this reaction. **[3]**
**(c)** Name the organic product. **[1]**

**5.** 1-chloropropane reacts with excess ammonia to form propylamine.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Outline the mechanism for this reaction. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why excess ammonia is used. **[1]**

**6.** A student compares the rates of hydrolysis of 1-chloropropane, 1-bromopropane and 1-iodopropane. She
adds each halogenoalkane to ethanol in a separate test tube, warms the tubes in a water bath, then adds
aqueous silver nitrate to each.

**(a)** Identify the nucleophile in these reactions. **[1]**
**(b)** Predict the colour of each precipitate and the order in which they appear. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain this order. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why ethanol is used. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why all three tubes are kept in the same water bath. **[1]**

**7.** 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts with potassium hydroxide. Two organic products form at the same time.

**(a)** Name the two organic products. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the two roles of the hydroxide ion in forming these products. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline the mechanism for the formation of the alkene. **[3]**

**8.** 2-bromobutane is heated under reflux with potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol. Three isomeric
alkenes form.

**(a)** Name the three alkenes. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why more than one structural isomer of the alkene forms. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why two of the alkenes are stereoisomers. **[1]**
**(d)** State how the conditions should change to make butan-2-ol the main product. **[1]**

**9.** A student makes 2-chloro-2-methylpropane by shaking 7.40 g of 2-methylpropan-2-ol with excess
concentrated hydrochloric acid. After purification she obtains 6.29 g of product.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(c)** After removing the aqueous layer, the student purifies the crude product. Describe the steps. **[3]**

**10.** 2-bromopropane reacts with potassium hydroxide by elimination and by substitution:

```
elimination:  C3H7Br + KOH → C3H6 + KBr + H2O
substitution: C3H7Br + KOH → C3H7OH + KBr
```

**(a)** Calculate the percentage atom economy for making propene by elimination. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage atom economy for making propan-2-ol by substitution. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest one reason, other than an incomplete reaction, why the yield of propene is less than 100%. **[1]**

**11.** Dichlorodifluoromethane, CCl₂F₂, was once used as a refrigerant.

**(a)** State why ozone in the upper atmosphere is beneficial. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the formation of a chlorine atom from CCl₂F₂ in the upper atmosphere. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a C–Cl bond breaks rather than a C–F bond. **[1]**
**(d)** Write two equations to show how chlorine atoms decompose ozone. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain how these equations show that chlorine atoms act as a catalyst. **[2]**
**(f)** Suggest why hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) do not destroy ozone in this way. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–Br bond is polar [1]. The carbon is δ+ (electron
deficient), so it attracts a nucleophile, which donates an electron pair to it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Bromine is electronegative" on its own is weak; compare it with carbon and say what that
does to the charge on carbon.

**2.** An **electron pair donor** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Something attracted to positive charge" describes behaviour, not the definition; write
"electron pair donor".

**3. (a)** **Butan-2-ol** [1]
**(b)** CH₃CHClCH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ + NaCl (or with OH⁻ → Cl⁻) [1]
**(c)** **Nucleophilic substitution** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Substitution" without "nucleophilic" is incomplete for (c); name both parts of the type.

**4. (a)** KCN in **aqueous ethanol**, heated under reflux [1]
**(b)** Arrow from the lone pair on the **carbon** of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon of C–Br [1]. Arrow from the C–Br bond
to Br [1]. Products (CH₃)₂CHCN and Br⁻ [1].
**(c)** **2-methylpropanenitrile** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The nitrile carbon counts in the chain: (CH₃)₂CHCN has a three-carbon chain with a methyl
branch, so it is not butanenitrile.

**5. (a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Cl [1]
**(b)** Arrow from the lone pair on N of NH₃ to the δ+ carbon [1]. Arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl [1].
Intermediate CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ with the positive charge on N [1]. Arrow from the lone pair of a second NH₃ to an H
on N⁺, and arrow from that N–H bond onto N, giving CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄⁺ [1].
**(c)** The amine product has a lone pair and could act as a nucleophile; excess NH₃ makes further
substitution less likely, so the primary amine is the main product [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An equation with only one NH₃ is unbalanced unless HCl is shown as a product; using 2NH₃
and NH₄Cl is the safe form.

**6. (a)** **Water** [1]
**(b)** Yellow (AgI), cream (AgBr), white (AgCl) [1]; order of appearance: **iodo first, then bromo, chloro
last** [1]
**(c)** The C–I bond has the **lowest bond enthalpy** / is the weakest C–X bond [1], so it needs the least
energy to break and substitution is fastest [1].
**(d)** The halogenoalkane does not mix with water; ethanol is a solvent for both, so the reactants mix [1].
**(e)** Rate depends on temperature, so temperature must be the same for a fair comparison [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer to (c) based on polarity or electronegativity of the halogen cannot score: the
rate order is explained by C–X bond enthalpy.

**7. (a)** **2-methylpropan-2-ol** [1] and **2-methylpropene** [1]
**(b)** As a **nucleophile**, OH⁻ donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon, giving the alcohol [1]. As a **base**,
OH⁻ accepts H⁺ from a carbon next to the C–Br carbon, giving the alkene [1].
**(c)** Arrow from the lone pair on O of OH⁻ to an H on a CH₃ carbon [1]. Arrow from that C–H bond to between
the two carbons, forming C=C [1]. Arrow from the C–Br bond to Br; products CH₂=C(CH₃)₂, H₂O and Br⁻ [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the H removed must be on a carbon adjacent to the C–Br carbon; here the central
carbon carries no H at all, so an arrow to it is wrong.

**8. (a)** **But-1-ene** [1], **(E)-but-2-ene** [1], **(Z)-but-2-ene** [1]
**(b)** H can be removed from C1 or from C3, as both are next to the C–Br carbon (C2), so the C=C forms in two
positions [1].
**(c)** There is restricted rotation about the C=C, and each carbon of the C=C carries two different groups
(H and CH₃) [1].
**(d)** Use aqueous potassium hydroxide (water as solvent) and a lower temperature (warm) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), "restricted rotation" alone is not enough; also say each C=C carbon has two
different groups.

**9. (a)** (CH₃)₃COH + HCl → (CH₃)₃CCl + H₂O [1]
**(b)**

```
Mr (CH3)3COH = 74.0   n = 7.40 ÷ 74.0 = 0.100 mol
Mr (CH3)3CCl = 92.5   theoretical mass = 0.100 × 92.5 = 9.25 g
% yield = 6.29 ÷ 9.25 × 100 = 68.0%
```

Moles of alcohol [1]; theoretical mass of product [1]; **68.0%** [1]
**(c)** Shake with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a separating funnel to remove acid, releasing the
pressure (CO₂), then discard the aqueous layer [1]. Dry the organic layer with an anhydrous salt such as
anhydrous sodium sulfate [1]. Distil and collect the fraction boiling near the boiling point of the product [1].
**[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the theoretical mass must use the Mr of the chloroalkane (92.5), not the alcohol;
moles stay the same because the ratio is 1 : 1.

**10. (a)** Sum of Mr of reactants = 122.9 + 56.1 = 179.0 [1]; atom economy = 42.0 ÷ 179.0 × 100 = **23.5%** [1]
**(b)** 60.0 ÷ 179.0 × 100 = **33.5%** [1]
**(c)** Substitution happens at the same time, so some 2-bromopropane forms propan-2-ol instead of propene [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Atom economy uses all reactants in the balanced equation and only the desired product, so
dividing by the products' total gives the wrong answer.

**11. (a)** It absorbs ultraviolet radiation [1].
**(b)** CCl₂F₂ → •CClF₂ + Cl• [1]
**(c)** The C–Cl bond has a lower bond enthalpy (is weaker) than the C–F bond [1].
**(d)** Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ [1]; ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl• [1]
**(e)** Cl• is used in the first step and **regenerated** in the second [1]. It does not appear in the overall
equation, 2O₃ → 3O₂, so one Cl• can destroy many ozone molecules [1].
**(f)** HFCs contain no chlorine (no C–Cl bond), so no chlorine atoms are formed [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the radical dot on Cl and ClO in (b) and (d); missing dots make the species
ambiguous and can cost the mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Curly arrows starting from the H of OH⁻, or from a minus sign with no lone pair, instead of the lone pair on
  O (or on C for CN⁻, N for NH₃).
- The C–X arrow pointing from the halogen to carbon instead of from the bond to the halogen.
- The second NH₃ step left out of the ammonia mechanism.
- Nitriles named without counting the C≡N carbon.
- Reactivity explained by polarity rather than C–X bond enthalpy.
- In elimination, an H removed from the C–X carbon itself, or OH⁻ called a nucleophile.
- Aqueous and ethanolic conditions swapped between substitution and elimination.
- Ozone equations without radical dots, or with Cl• not regenerated.
- In yield calculations, the theoretical mass worked out from the wrong compound's Mr.
