These notes condense sub-topic **10.2 Antibiotics** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus (examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027). It is AS content for Paper 1 and Paper 2, assumed on Paper 4. For explanations and worked examples, use the [antibiotics study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-antibiotics/); for exam-style questions, use the [antibiotics practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-antibiotics-practice/).

## The two outcomes

1. **Outline** how penicillin acts on bacteria and why antibiotics do not affect viruses.
2. **Discuss** the consequences of antibiotic resistance and the steps that can reduce its impact.

## Penicillin in six steps

1. Bacterial wall = **peptidoglycan**: polysaccharide chains joined by peptide **cross-links**.
2. To grow, enzymes break bonds in the wall; new chains are added.
3. **Transpeptidases** form new cross-links to seal the wall.
4. Penicillin **inhibits transpeptidases**: no new cross-links.
5. Wall weakens at growth points while breaking enzymes keep working.
6. Water enters by **osmosis**; the cell **bursts** (osmotic lysis).

**So**: only growing bacteria are killed; human cells (no wall) are unharmed.

## Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

| Antibiotic target in bacteria | In a virus? |
|---|---|
| Peptidoglycan cell wall | No |
| 70S ribosomes | No ribosomes |
| Bacterial metabolic enzymes | No metabolism |

Viruses replicate inside host cells using the host's ribosomes and enzymes, so there is nothing for an antibiotic to act on.

## Resistance: arise, select, spread

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Arise | Random **mutation** gives a resistance allele (e.g. gene for **penicillinase**, which breaks down penicillin). Not caused by the antibiotic. |
| Select | Antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria; resistant survive with less competition. |
| Spread vertically | Binary fission passes the allele to offspring. |
| Spread horizontally | Genes on **plasmids** pass to other bacteria, even other species (conjugation). |
| Result | Proportion of resistant bacteria in the population rises. |

Examples: MRSA; multi-drug-resistant TB.

### Worked reminder: proportion after selection

1 resistant in 10 000; antibiotic kills 99 % of susceptible ones.

```
before: 1 ÷ 10 000 = 0.01 % resistant
after:  susceptible left = 0.01 × 9999 ≈ 100; resistant = 1
        proportion ≈ 1 ÷ 101 ≈ 1 %   (about 100 times higher)
```

## Consequences (with significance)

- Infections harder or impossible to treat → more deaths.
- Longer hospital stays → higher costs, more spread.
- Need for newer drugs → more expensive, may have more side effects.
- Surgery, cancer treatment and care of premature babies riskier.
- TB control harder with multi-drug-resistant strains.

## Steps to reduce impact (and how each works)

| Step | Works by |
|---|---|
| Prescribe only when needed; never for viruses | Less selection pressure |
| Test sensitivity; use narrow-spectrum antibiotic | Fewer bacteria exposed |
| Complete the full course; do not share leftovers | Fewer survivors to pass on resistance |
| Rotate antibiotics in hospitals | No single drug selects for long |
| Reduce use in farming | Less selection; fewer resistant bacteria reaching people |
| Hygiene, hand-washing, isolation | Less spread of resistant bacteria |
| Research into new antibiotics | Alternatives when resistance appears |

## Interpreting antibiotic disc data

- Clear zone = no bacterial growth.
- Larger zone → bacteria more sensitive (but diffusion rate also matters).
- No zone beyond the disc → resistant.
- Area = πr²; r = diameter ÷ 2. Doubling the diameter quadruples the area.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Antibiotic | Substance that kills or stops the growth of bacteria without harming the host |
| Peptidoglycan | Polysaccharide chains cross-linked by peptides; bacterial wall material |
| Transpeptidase | Enzyme forming peptide cross-links in peptidoglycan |
| Osmotic lysis | Bursting of a cell as water enters by osmosis |
| Penicillinase | Bacterial enzyme that breaks down penicillin |
| Resistance | Ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that kills others of their species |
| Selection pressure | Environmental factor favouring individuals with certain alleles |
| Plasmid | Small circular DNA in bacteria, often carrying resistance genes |
| Narrow-spectrum | Acting on a limited range of bacteria |

## Exam traps

- "Penicillin breaks down the cell wall": it stops new cross-links forming.
- Lysis explained without osmosis.
- "Viruses are not alive, so antibiotics cannot kill them": name the missing targets.
- "Bacteria become immune" or "the antibiotic causes mutations".
- Steps listed without saying how each reduces selection or spread.
- Diameter used as radius in πr².

## Self-test

1. What is the main polymer of a bacterial cell wall?
2. Name the type of enzyme penicillin inhibits.
3. Why does a bacterium treated with penicillin burst?
4. Why does penicillin not kill a bacterium that is not growing?
5. Why is penicillin harmless to human cells?
6. Give three reasons antibiotics do not affect viruses.
7. Name an enzyme that makes bacteria resistant to penicillin.
8. Explain how the use of an antibiotic increases the proportion of resistant bacteria.
9. How can resistance pass between different species of bacteria?
10. Explain why completing a course of antibiotics is advised.
11. Give two consequences of antibiotic resistance.
12. Clear zones of 10 mm and 20 mm diameter: how many times larger is the area of the second?

### Answers

1. Peptidoglycan.
2. Transpeptidases (enzymes forming cross-links).
3. Its wall is weakened, and water entering by osmosis makes it swell and burst.
4. It is not making new wall, so the lack of cross-links has no effect.
5. Human cells have no cell wall or peptidoglycan.
6. No cell wall; no ribosomes; no metabolism of their own (they use the host cell's).
7. Penicillinase.
8. Susceptible bacteria die and resistant ones survive and reproduce, passing on the resistance allele.
9. On plasmids, transferred by conjugation.
10. So that as many bacteria as possible are killed and fewer survive to pass on resistance.
11. Any two: infections harder to treat; more deaths; longer hospital stays; higher costs; surgery riskier; TB harder to control.
12. Four times (area depends on radius squared: 10² ÷ 5² = 4).
