These questions cover section **3.2.1 Notation, vocabulary and manipulation** (A1 to A7) of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification, for teaching from September 2015 with exams from May/June 2017 (version 1.0). Questions and parts on content from the specification's "Higher content only" column are labelled **(Higher)**; everything else suits both tiers. Each question says whether it is non-calculator (Paper 1 style) or calculator (Papers 2 and 3 style).

## Questions

**1.** (non-calculator) Write each of these in its simplest algebraic form.

**(a)** a × 7 × b **[1]**
**(b)** n × n × n × p **[1]**
**(c)** (x + 3) ÷ 4 **[1]**
**(d)** 0.25 × y **[1]**

**2.** (calculator)

**(a)** The time, T seconds, for one swing of a pendulum is given by T = 2π√(l/g). Calculate T when l = 0.8 and g = 9.8. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** The formula F = 9C/5 + 32 converts a temperature C in °C to F in °F. Work out F when C = −15. **[2]**

**3.** (non-calculator)

**(a)** Here are four lines of algebra.

A: 7 − 2w    B: 7 − 2w = 1    C: 7 − 2w < 1    D: 2(w − 3) ≡ 2w − 6

Write down which one is an expression, which is an equation, which is an inequality and which is an identity. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that 3 is a factor of 6w − 9. **[1]**

**4.** (non-calculator) Simplify fully.

**(a)** 5p − 3q − 2p + 7q **[1]**
**(b)** 2a⁵ × 6a³ **[1]**
**(c)** 24m⁶n⁴ ÷ 8m²n **[2]**
**(d)** (k⁴)³ **[1]**

**5.** (non-calculator) Expand and simplify.

**(a)** 4(2x − 3) − 3(x − 5) **[2]**
**(b)** (x − 6)(x + 2) **[2]**
**(c)** (3x + 1)² **[2]**

**6.** (non-calculator) Factorise fully.

**(a)** 8x²y − 20xy² **[2]**
**(b)** x² − 11x + 28 **[2]**
**(c)** w² − 121 **[1]**

**7.** (non-calculator) Expand and simplify (5 − √2)(3 + √2). Give your answer in the form a + b√2, where a and b are integers. **[3]**

**8.** (calculator)

**(a)** The area of a trapezium is A = ½(a + b)h. Make h the subject. **[2]**
**(b)** The volume of a cone is V = ⅓πr²h. Make r the subject. Hence find the radius of a cone with volume 500 cm³ and height 12 cm, to 3 significant figures. **[4]**

**9.** (non-calculator)

**(a)** Show that 5(x + 2) − 2(x − 1) ≡ 3x + 12. **[2]**
**(b)** Is 4(x − 1) = 3x + 2 an equation or an identity? Give a reason for your answer. **[2]**

**10.** (non-calculator) (Higher)

**(a)** Prove that (n + 5)² − (n + 1)² is a multiple of 8 for every integer n. **[4]**
**(b)** Prove that the sum of any three consecutive even numbers is a multiple of 6. **[3]**

**11.** (non-calculator) (Higher)

**(a)** Factorise 3x² − 5x − 12. **[2]**
**(b)** Hence simplify (3x² − 5x − 12)/(x² − 9). **[2]**
**(c)** Write 4/(x − 3) − 1/(x + 3) as a single fraction in its simplest form. **[3]**
**(d)** Expand and simplify (x + 1)(x − 2)(2x + 3). **[3]**

**12.** (non-calculator)

**(a)** A function machine multiplies the input by 4 and then subtracts 3. Find the output when the input is −2. **[1]**

The function machine in part (a) is the function f(x) = 4x − 3. Also, g(x) = x² + 2.

**(b)** (Higher) Find f⁻¹(x). **[2]**
**(c)** (Higher) Find fg(x). Give your answer in its simplest form. **[2]**
**(d)** (Higher) Work out gf(2). **[2]**
**(e)** (Higher) Explain why fg(x) can never be less than 5. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **7ab** [1]
**(b)** **n³p** [1]
**(c)** **(x + 3)/4** [1]
**(d)** **¼y** or **y/4** [1]
*Examiner insight:* On (d), 0.25y is not accepted as simplest form, because the specification asks for coefficients written as fractions rather than decimals; on (a), b7a or 7 × a × b would also lose the mark.

**2. (a)** T = 2π√(0.8 ÷ 9.8) = 1.7951... [1] **T = 1.80** (seconds) [1]
**(b)** F = 9(−15)/5 + 32 [1] = −27 + 32 = **5** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write down the unrounded calculator value (1.7951...) before rounding: if the rounding is wrong, the first line still earns the method mark, and 1.8 alone is not 3 significant figures.

**3. (a)** Two or three correctly named [1]; all four correct: **A expression, B equation, C inequality, D identity** [1]
**(b)** 6w − 9 = **3(2w − 3)**, so 3 divides exactly into it [1]
*Examiner insight:* For (b), saying "6 and 9 are both in the 3 times table" without the factorised form is weaker; writing 3(2w − 3) shows the factor directly.

**4. (a)** **3p + 4q** [1]
**(b)** **12a⁸** [1]
**(c)** Two of the three parts correct (3, m⁴, n³) [1]; **3m⁴n³** [1]
**(d)** **k¹²** [1]
*Examiner insight:* (k⁴)³ = k⁷ is a common wrong answer that scores zero: raising a power to a power multiplies the indices, so there is no method mark for adding them.

**5. (a)** 8x − 12 − 3x + 15 [1] = **5x + 3** [1]
**(b)** x² + 2x − 6x − 12 [1] = **x² − 4x − 12** [1]
**(c)** (3x + 1)(3x + 1) = 9x² + 3x + 3x + 1 [1] = **9x² + 6x + 1** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), −3 × −5 = +15 is where the sign slips; one correct expansion of all four terms usually earns the method mark even if the final collection is wrong.

**6. (a)** A partial factorisation such as 4x(2xy − 5y²) or 2xy(4x − 10y) [1]; **4xy(2x − 5y)** [1]
**(b)** Numbers −4 and −7 identified (product 28, sum −11) [1]; **(x − 4)(x − 7)** [1]
**(c)** **(w + 11)(w − 11)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Factorise fully" means the highest common factor; a correct but incomplete factorisation in (a) earns only the first mark.

**7.** 15 + 5√2 − 3√2 − √2 × √2 [1]; √2 × √2 = 2, so 15 + 2√2 − 2 [1] = **13 + 2√2** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Four terms must appear in the expansion; giving 15 − 2 = 13 and forgetting the surd terms, or writing √2 × √2 = 4, loses the accuracy marks.

**8. (a)** 2A = (a + b)h [1]; **h = 2A/(a + b)** [1]
**(b)** 3V = πr²h, so r² = 3V/(πh) [1]; **r = √(3V/(πh))** [1]
r = √(3 × 500 ÷ (π × 12)) = √39.788... [1] = 6.3078... so **r = 6.31 cm** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A follow-through mark is usually available for substituting into your own rearranged formula, but only if the rearrangement is written down.

**9. (a)** 5x + 10 − 2x + 2 [1] = 3x + 12, which is the right-hand side for every x ✓ [1]
**(b)** It is **an equation** [1], because it is true only for one value: 4x − 4 = 3x + 2 gives x = 6 [1]
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that", the answer is given, so both marks depend on the expansion line being written out, including −2 × −1 = +2.

**10. (a)** (n + 5)² = n² + 10n + 25 and (n + 1)² = n² + 2n + 1 [1]
Difference = n² + 10n + 25 − n² − 2n − 1 [1] = 8n + 24 [1]
= 8(n + 3), and n + 3 is an integer, so the expression is a multiple of 8 [1]
**(b)** Let the numbers be 2n, 2n + 2 and 2n + 4 [1]
Sum = 6n + 6 [1] = 6(n + 1), which is a multiple of 6 [1]
*Examiner insight:* The final mark in each proof is for the concluding statement linked to the factorised form; 8n + 24 on its own, or checking n = 1, 2, 3, does not complete the proof.

**11. (a)** ac = −36; use −9 and 4: 3x² − 9x + 4x − 12 [1] = **(3x + 4)(x − 3)** [1]
**(b)** x² − 9 = (x + 3)(x − 3) [1]; cancelling (x − 3) gives **(3x + 4)/(x + 3)** [1]
**(c)** [4(x + 3) − (x − 3)] / ((x − 3)(x + 3)) [1]
= (4x + 12 − x + 3) / ((x − 3)(x + 3)) [1] = **(3x + 15)/((x − 3)(x + 3))**, or 3(x + 5)/(x² − 9) [1]
**(d)** (x + 1)(x − 2) = x² − x − 2 [1]
(x² − x − 2)(2x + 3) = 2x³ + 3x² − 2x² − 3x − 4x − 6 [1] = **2x³ + x² − 7x − 6** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Hence" in (b) means the factorisation from (a) must be used; in (c), the most common lost mark is writing − x − 3 instead of − x + 3 when subtracting the second numerator.

**12. (a)** 4 × (−2) − 3 = **−11** [1]
**(b)** y = 4x − 3, so x = (y + 3)/4 [1]; **f⁻¹(x) = (x + 3)/4** [1]
**(c)** fg(x) = 4(x² + 2) − 3 [1] = **4x² + 5** [1]
**(d)** f(2) = 5 [1]; gf(2) = g(5) = 25 + 2 = **27** [1]
**(e)** x² ≥ 0 for every x, so 4x² + 5 ≥ 5 [1]
*Examiner insight:* For fg(x), the function written next to x acts first; working out g(f(x)) instead gives 16x² − 24x + 11 and usually scores nothing for (c).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using decimal coefficients such as 0.25y, or leaving × signs in an answer.
- Squaring a negative without brackets during substitution.
- Adding indices when one power is raised to another.
- Expanding (3x + 1)² as 9x² + 1 with no middle term.
- Stopping at a partial factorisation when the question says "factorise fully".
- In a surd expansion, forgetting that √2 × √2 = 2 and not 4, or dropping the surd terms.
- Rearranging a formula but not writing the rearranged version before substituting, which loses follow-through credit.
- Subtracting a bracketed numerator without changing every sign inside it (Higher).
- Missing the concluding sentence in a proof, or testing numbers instead of using n (Higher).
- Composing functions in the wrong order (Higher).
