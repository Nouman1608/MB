## Questions

**1.** A freelance photographer in Country C has an accounting profit of 14,000 cu in a year. She could have earned 16,000 cu as an employee. Explain whether she is earning normal profit and what she is likely to do. **[3]**

**2.** A tile maker in Country C has these long-run average costs (LRAC).

| Output (thousand tiles a week) | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRAC (cu per tile) | 14.00 | 11.20 | 9.80 | 9.10 | 9.10 | 9.80 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in LRAC when output rises from 5 to 20 thousand tiles. **[2]**
**(b)** State the minimum efficient scale and explain your answer. **[2]**

**3.** Market demand for tiles is 100 thousand a week at the going price.

**(a)** Calculate the largest number of firms that could each produce at the minimum efficient scale in question 2, and the number if new technology doubled that scale. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the change implies for competition in the market. **[2]**

**4.** Name and explain the type of internal economy of scale shown in each case.

**(i)** A hospital group orders 5,000 pairs of gloves at once and pays 12% less per pair. **[2]**
**(ii)** A fast-growing software company can now afford a full-time finance specialist. **[2]**
**(iii)** A farm co-operative sells vegetables, dairy and eggs, so a poor vegetable harvest hurts it less. **[2]**

**5.** Twelve firms cluster in one town and a training college opens a course for their industry. Each firm's LRAC falls by 6%.

**(a)** One firm's LRAC at its minimum efficient scale was 9.10 cu. Calculate the new value. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether this is a movement along the LRAC curve or a shift, and name the external economy. **[3]**

**6.** The possible links between n people in a call centre are n(n - 1)/2.

**(a)** Calculate the links for 8 staff and for 16 staff, and compare the increases. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how this and one other source of diseconomies of scale can raise LRAC. **[3]**

**7.** A phone repair shop sells 800 repairs a month at 15 cu. Total fixed cost is 6,000 cu (a lease it must pay either way) and average variable cost (AVC) is 12 cu.

**(a)** Show that it should keep trading rather than close in the short run. **[3]**
**(b)** The price falls to 11 cu. Decide what it should do and justify your answer. **[3]**

**8.** A farm sells 25,000 kg at 8.40 cu. Explicit costs are 150,000 cu. The owner's time and land could earn 50,000 cu elsewhere.

**(a)** Calculate accounting profit and supernormal profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the long-run effect on the industry if entry is free. **[2]**

**9.** A firm's average cost falls from 10.00 cu to 8.00 cu as output doubles from 40,000 to 80,000 units. The price is 12.00 cu. Evaluate whether consumers will benefit. **[8]**

**10.** A small hotel in Country D sells 600 room-nights in the low season at 40 cu. Average variable cost is 34 cu and fixed costs are 24,000 cu a month (lease and loan payments).

**(a)** Calculate the monthly loss if it stays open and if it closes. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate what it should do in the short run and in the long run. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Normal profit is the least return that stops her switching to another job, an opportunity cost counted as a cost [1]. Economic profit = 14,000 - 16,000 = -2,000 cu [1]. She earns less than normal profit, so if this continues she is likely to take employment instead [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that subtract the opportunity cost and then state the consequence usually show more than a definition alone does.

**2. (a)** (9.10 - 14.00)/14.00 [1] = **-35.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The minimum efficient scale is **20 thousand tiles** [1], the lowest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum of 9.10 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the lowest output on the flat section is the one to give, since 25 gives the same cost but is not the first.

**3. (a)** 100/20 = 5 firms [1]. With MES 40: 100/40 = 2.5, so at most **2 firms** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A higher scale means fewer firms can reach the lowest cost [1]. Smaller firms have higher costs, so the market tends to become concentrated and entry is harder [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* rounding 2.5 down to 2 and saying why shows you understand the idea of whole firms.

**4. (i)** Purchasing economy [1]: a bulk order earns a discount, lowering cost per unit [1]. **[2]**
**(ii)** Managerial economy [1]: a specialist makes better decisions and the salary is spread over more output [1]. **[2]**
**(iii)** Risk-bearing economy [1]: several products spread risk, so revenue is steadier [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a correct label with no reason linking to lower average cost usually earns only part of the credit.

**5. (a)** 9.10 x (1 - 0.06) [1] = 9.10 x 0.94 = **8.55 cu** (8.554) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It is a shift down of the LRAC curve [1] because growth of the industry, not of one firm's output, lowers costs for every firm [1]. The external economy is the availability of skilled labour, as the college trains workers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* saying whose output has changed is the clearest way to separate a movement from a shift.

**6. (a)** 8 x 7/2 = 28 links [1]; 16 x 15/2 = 120 links [1]. Staff double but links rise 4.3 times (120/28 = 4.29) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** More links make messages slower or distorted, so decisions are late and errors costly [1]. X-inefficiency (or coordination): with weaker monitoring, waste is tolerated [1]. Total cost therefore rises faster than output, so LRAC rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* ending the chain at higher average cost, not at a vague problem, is what links the cause to the curve.

**7. (a)** TR = 15 x 800 = 12,000; TC = 6,000 + 12 x 800 = 15,600 [1]. Loss if open = 12,000 - 15,600 = -3,600; loss if closed = -6,000 [1]. AR 15 exceeds AVC 12, and the contribution (15 - 12) x 800 = 2,400 covers part of fixed cost [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** TR = 11 x 800 = 8,800, below total variable cost 12 x 800 = 9,600 [1], because AR 11 is below AVC 12 [1]. Opening loses 8,800 - 15,600 = -6,800, closing loses 6,000, so **shut down** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* comparing the two losses, not just stating the rule, shows the reasoning behind the decision.

**8. (a)** TR = 8.40 x 25,000 = 210,000; accounting profit = 210,000 - 150,000 = **60,000 cu** [1]. Supernormal profit = 60,000 - 50,000 = **10,000 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Supernormal profit attracts new firms [1]. Supply rises and price falls until AR equals AC and only normal profit remains [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the phrase "if entry is free" matters, so say how the answer would change if barriers existed.

**9.** Profit before = (12.00 - 10.00) x 40,000 = 80,000 cu [1]. If the price stays at 12.00, profit after = (12.00 - 8.00) x 80,000 = 320,000 cu, four times higher [1]. Cost falls 20.0%, so the firm could cut the price [1]. At a price of 10.00 consumers save 2.00 x 80,000 = 160,000 cu, and profit is still (10.00 - 8.00) x 80,000 = 160,000 cu [1]. Evaluation: the gain reaches consumers only if competition forces the price down; a firm with market power may keep it [1]. The result depends on the minimum efficient scale: if few firms can reach it, competition is weak [1]. Further growth could bring diseconomies and reverse the gain [1]. Higher profit could fund investment that benefits consumers later, though quality or choice may fall [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* using the figures to compare two possible prices makes the evaluation specific and keeps it from being a list of generalities.

**10. (a)** TR = 40 x 600 = 24,000; total variable cost = 34 x 600 = 20,400; TC = 24,000 + 20,400 = 44,400 [1]. Loss if open = 24,000 - 44,400 = **-20,400 cu** [1]. Loss if closed = **-24,000 cu** (fixed costs) [1]. Contribution (40 - 34) x 600 = 3,600 is the difference [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Short run: AR 40 exceeds AVC 34, so staying open reduces the loss by 3,600 [1]. Staying open is stronger if the low season is temporary, since closing risks lost customers and the cost of rehiring [1], and the lease must be paid either way [1]. Long run: AC = 44,400/600 = 74.00, far above AR 40 even before normal profit [1]. If low-season prices persist the hotel should leave once commitments end [1], unless high-season profit makes the year as a whole profitable; so on balance it should stay open now but review the business over the full year [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* a conclusion that separates the short-run and long-run decisions and rests on a calculated figure tends to read as a judgement rather than a summary.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating accounting profit as supernormal profit without deducting the owner's opportunity cost.
- Using average cost, not average variable cost, to decide on short-run shutdown.
- Stating a type of economy without explaining why average cost falls.
- Drawing or describing an external economy as a movement along the curve.
- Giving percentage changes with no working, or the wrong base.
- Forgetting that fixed costs are still paid if the firm closes in the short run.
- Writing a list of evaluation points with no judgement or no use of the data.
- Confusing diseconomies of scale with diminishing returns.
