This guide goes deeper into **1.2.2 Use of apparatus and techniques** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026). The statement lists the apparatus and techniques, (a) to (l), through which you show the 1.2.1 skills and the CPAC across "a minimum of 12 assessed practicals". They are assessed in the Practical Endorsement, and written papers can ask about any of them.

The [practical endorsement study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-practical-skills-practical-endorsement/) gives the standard method for each technique. This page covers *which* apparatus, *why* each step is there, and *what happens to the result* when a step is skipped. Condense it with the [1.2.2 revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-2-2-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.2.2 practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-2-2-practice/).

## Choosing measuring apparatus: resolution and uncertainty

Statement (a) is about more than reading a scale. You must pick apparatus whose uncertainty is small compared with the quantity measured, which links to 1.1.4(d), percentage errors and uncertainties in apparatus.

**Rule for a single reading.** The uncertainty of an analogue scale reading is usually taken as half the smallest division. A burette graduated every 0.10 cm³ is read to ±0.05 cm³.

**Rule for a difference.** A titre, a mass by difference or a temperature change uses two readings, so the two uncertainties add. A titre has ±0.10 cm³.

**Percentage uncertainty** = (uncertainty ÷ quantity measured) × 100.

| Apparatus | Uncertainty used | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Two-decimal-place balance, mass by difference | ±0.01 g | Two readings of ±0.005 g |
| Three-decimal-place balance, mass by difference | ±0.001 g | Two readings of ±0.0005 g |
| Burette, titre | ±0.10 cm³ | Two readings of ±0.05 cm³ |
| Pipette or volumetric flask | Tolerance printed on the glass | Given in a question if needed |
| Measuring cylinder (1 cm³ divisions) | ±0.5 cm³ | Fine for adding excess reagent |
| Thermometer (1 °C divisions), temperature change | ±1.0 °C | Two readings of ±0.5 °C |
| Gas syringe (1 cm³ divisions) | ±0.5 cm³ | Volume read once from zero |

### Worked example 1: is the apparatus good enough?

A student weighs 0.20 g of magnesium ribbon on a two-decimal-place balance by difference and measures a temperature rise of 6.0 °C on a thermometer with 1 °C divisions.

1. Mass: 0.01 ÷ 0.20 × 100 = **5.0%**.
2. Temperature change: 1.0 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **16.7%**.
3. The temperature change dominates. A thermometer reading to 0.1 °C (±0.05 °C per reading, ±0.10 °C for the change) gives 0.10 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = 1.7%.
4. Using more magnesium in the same volume of acid would raise ΔT and reduce both percentages. Increasing the measured quantity is often the better improvement.

## Heating: matching the heat source, statement (b)

- **Water bath.** Cannot exceed about 100 °C and heats evenly: warming test-tubes in qualitative tests, or gentle heating of low-boiling organic liquids.
- **Electric heater** such as a heating mantle. No flame, so it suits flammable mixtures that must boil, as in reflux.
- **Sand bath.** Even heat above 100 °C, where a water bath is not hot enough.

## Measuring pH, statement (c)

| Method | Resolution | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| pH chart with universal indicator | About 1 pH unit | Judging colour is subjective |
| pH meter | Usually 0.01 pH unit | Must be calibrated |
| pH probe on a data logger | As for a meter | Records pH against time or volume automatically |

**Calibration.** Place the rinsed probe in a buffer of known pH and adjust the reading to that value; repeat with a second buffer. Rinse with distilled water between solutions. A data logger suits pH that changes continuously, as when plotting a pH curve.

## Volumetric work: why each step is there, statements (d)(i), (e) and (f)

The method for a titration is in the endorsement guide. The table below gives the *reason* for each step and the effect if it is missed, which is what written questions test.

| Step | Reason | If missed |
|---|---|---|
| Rinse burette with the titrant | Water left inside would dilute it | Titre too large |
| Rinse pipette with the solution it delivers | Water dilutes the aliquot | Fewer moles delivered; titre too small |
| Rinse conical flask with distilled water only | Water does not change moles in the flask | Rinsing with the analyte adds extra moles; titre too large |
| Remove funnel and fill the tip of the burette | Funnel drips or an air bubble change the reading | Drips: recorded titre too small; escaping bubble: too large |
| Read at eye level, bottom of meniscus | Avoids parallax | Systematic reading error |
| Transfer all washings to the volumetric flask | Every mole of solute must reach the flask | Standard solution too dilute |
| Invert the stoppered flask several times | Solution must be uniform | Aliquots differ; titres not concordant |

**Indicator choice (f).** The colour change must lie on the steep part of the pH curve, so a weak acid–strong alkali titration needs phenolphthalein.

### Worked example 2: tracing a fault to the answer

A student finds the concentration of a sodium hydroxide solution by titrating it into 25.0 cm³ of a standard acid. The burette was rinsed with water but not with the alkali.

1. Water in the burette dilutes the alkali.
2. More alkali solution is needed to neutralise the same moles of acid, so the titre is too large.
3. Concentration of NaOH = moles of acid ÷ titre volume. A larger titre gives a **smaller calculated concentration** than the true value.

State the direction of change in the reading *and* the answer.

## Preparation and purification: statements (d)(ii), (d)(iv), (g), (h) and (i)

**Setting up glassware.** The specification asks for glassware set up "using retort stand and clamps". Clamp the flask neck and the condenser. Never heat a sealed system: a reflux condenser stays open at the top.

**Which filtration?** Use **fluted filter paper** for fast gravity filtration when you want the *filtrate*, for example removing insoluble impurities from a hot solution. Use **filtration under reduced pressure** when you want the *solid*, because suction leaves crystals drier.

**Which layer in a separating funnel?** The denser liquid forms the lower layer. If densities are not given, add a few drops of water: the layer that grows is aqueous. Release pressure through the tap often, because shaking with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution releases carbon dioxide.

**Melting point and chromatography.** An impure or damp solid melts lower and over a wider range than the pure compound. Draw a chromatography baseline in pencil (ink would itself separate) and above the solvent, so spots do not dissolve into the reservoir.

## Electrochemical cells, statement (j)

A cell needs two half-cells, a **salt bridge** (lets ions move to complete the circuit; a wire would set up extra electrode reactions) and a **high-resistance voltmeter** (draws almost no current, so the reading is the cell potential). Clean metal electrodes with emery paper first.

## Rates by two methods, statement (l)

The specification requires **at least two different methods**: an initial rate method such as a clock reaction, and a continuous monitoring method. Continuous monitoring follows one property during a single run: gas volume in a syringe, mass of a flask on a balance as gas escapes, or colour in a colorimeter.

### Worked example 3: rate from mass loss

Marble chips react with hydrochloric acid in an open flask on a balance. Carbon dioxide escapes. Invented data:

| Time / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass / g | 152.40 | 152.16 | 151.98 | 151.84 |

1. Mass lost in the first 30 s = 152.40 − 152.16 = 0.24 g.
2. Mean rate over the first 30 s = 0.24 ÷ 30 = **0.0080 g s⁻¹**.
3. From 60 s to 90 s: 0.14 g ÷ 30 s = 0.0047 g s⁻¹. The rate falls as the acid is used up.
4. Cotton wool in the neck stops acid spray, which would exaggerate the mass loss.

## Handling hazardous substances, statement (k)

Statement (k) is covered across all your activities. Give the hazard and a control matched to it: cyclohexane is flammable, so heat it with an electric heater, no flames. "Wear goggles" alone is not a risk assessment.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a burette uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³ for a titre; a titre is two readings, ±0.10 cm³.
- Stating that a fault gives "an inaccurate result" instead of the direction of the change.
- Choosing a water bath for a reaction that must exceed 100 °C.
- Drawing a chromatography baseline in pen, or below the solvent level.
- Describing a low-resistance voltmeter or a wire in place of a salt bridge.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Explain**: give the reason *and* its effect, as in the table of steps above.
- **Suggest**: apply the technique to an unfamiliar set-up, using its chemistry.
- **Evaluate**: compare percentage uncertainties, identify the largest, and propose a change that reduces it.
- **Calculate**: show the uncertainty used for each reading.
