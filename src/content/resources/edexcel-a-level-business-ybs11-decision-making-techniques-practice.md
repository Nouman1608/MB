## Sources

> **Source 1: Wren Garden Centres**
>
> Wren is deciding whether to build a new greenhouse complex for $150,000.
> Net cash flows are expected to be $40,000, $60,000, $70,000 and $50,000 in
> years 1 to 4. The discount factors at 10% are 0.909, 0.826, 0.751 and
> 0.683. Monthly sales in the existing centre ($000) from April to August
> were 28, 31, 37, 34 and 40.
>
> **Source 2: Orlan Builders**
>
> Orlan fits wardrobes. It sells at $900 each with variable costs of $600
> and fixed costs of $90,000 a year. It sells 450 a year and can make 600.
> A hotel has offered to buy 100 wardrobes at $700 each. Orlan is also
> considering bidding for a council contract. The bid would cost $8,000 to
> prepare, with a 0.4 chance of winning and a profit of $60,000 if it wins.
>
> The activities for the hotel fit-out are:
>
> | Activity | Days | Preceded by |
> |---|---|---|
> | J | 2 | none |
> | K | 4 | J |
> | L | 3 | J |
> | M | 5 | K and L |
> | N | 2 | K |
> | O | 3 | M and N |

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "net present value". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the three-period moving averages for Wren's monthly sales. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate the payback period of the greenhouse project. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate the average rate of return of the project. **[4]**

**5.** Calculate the net present value of the project at 10%. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse one limitation of using net present value to decide on this project. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "contribution". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate Orlan's contribution per wardrobe and its break-even output. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the extra profit from accepting the hotel order, and state any assumption you make. **[4]**

**10.** Calculate the earliest start time and total float for each activity, and state the critical path. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss whether Orlan should bid for the council contract. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Orlan should accept the hotel's offer. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The sum of the discounted (present) values of a project's cash flows [1] minus the initial cost [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (28 + 31 + 37) / 3 = **32.0** [1]; (31 + 37 + 34) / 3 = **34.0** [1]; (37 + 34 + 40) / 3 = **37.0** [1]. Units given ($000) [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Cumulative cash flow: $40,000; $100,000; $170,000 [1]. $50,000 is still needed after year 2 [1]. Payback = 2 + 50,000 / 70,000 [1] = **2.71 years** (about 2 years 9 months) [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Total return = 40 + 60 + 70 + 50 = $220,000 [1]. Net return = 220,000 - 150,000 = $70,000, so the annual average = 70,000 / 4 = $17,500 [1]. ARR = 17,500 / 150,000 x 100 [1] = **11.7%** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Present values: 40,000 x 0.909 = $36,360; 60,000 x 0.826 = $49,560; 70,000 x 0.751 = $52,570; 50,000 x 0.683 = $34,150 [2 for all four, 1 for two or three correct]. Total = $172,640 [1]. NPV = 172,640 - 150,000 = **+$22,640** [1]. **[4]**

**6.** The NPV depends on the 10% discount rate, which is an estimate [1]. If the true cost of finance were higher, for example 15%, the present values would fall and the NPV of $22,640 could become negative [1]. The cash flows are also forecasts, and Wren's sales in the existing centre are changing from month to month, from 28 to 40 [1], so the greenhouse flows could be quite different from those predicted [1]. A positive NPV on estimated figures can give false confidence [1]. Wren should test the result with different assumptions and consider non-financial factors such as the effect on its brand and risk [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Selling price minus variable cost [1] per unit, which contributes towards fixed costs and profit [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Contribution per wardrobe = 900 - 600 [1] = **$300** [1]. Break-even = fixed costs / contribution = 90,000 / 300 [1] = **300 wardrobes** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Contribution on each hotel wardrobe = 700 - 600 = $100 [1]. Total = 100 x 100 = **$10,000** [1] extra profit, because fixed costs are already covered [1]. Assumes that Orlan has spare capacity (450 + 100 = 550, within 600) and that the sale does not affect its normal sales [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Earliest starts: J 0, K 2, L 2, M 6, N 6, O 11 [2]. Total float: J 0, K 0, L 1, M 0, N 3, O 0 [1]. Critical path = **J, K, M, O**, taking 14 days [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Expected value of winning = 0.4 x 60,000 = $24,000; net of bid cost = 24,000 - 8,000 = **$16,000** [1].
**For:** positive expected value suggests that bidding is worthwhile on average [1]; the contract could bring a steady flow of future work and lift Orlan's reputation [1].
**Against:** there is a 60% chance of losing $8,000 [1]; the probability is a guess, and a lower chance of winning, such as 0.1, would give an expected value of 6,000 - 8,000 = -$2,000 [1]; the work might also use capacity that could earn money from the hotel [1].
**Judgement:** Orlan should bid, because the expected value is positive and the maximum loss of $8,000 is small compared with its current profit of $45,000 [1]. The decision depends on how reliable its estimate of 0.4 is and on whether it has the capacity to take on the contract [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **For accepting:** each wardrobe adds $100 of contribution, so total profit rises by $10,000, from $45,000 (450 x 300 - 90,000) to $55,000 [1]; there is spare capacity (550 of 600) [1]; the hotel may become a regular customer or lead to other orders [1]; fixed costs are already covered, so any positive contribution adds to profit [1].
**Against:** the price of $700 is $200 below the normal price, and if other customers find out, they may demand discounts [1]; the contribution per wardrobe is only a third of the usual $300 [1]; the order uses 100 units of the spare 150, leaving little room for the council contract, which could be worth $24,000 in expected value [1]; the hotel fit-out may need the critical path activities J, K, M and O to be done over 14 days and could stretch the workforce [1].
**Judgement:** Orlan should probably accept, because $10,000 of extra profit for no extra fixed cost is a significant gain and it has the capacity [1]. It should do so only if it is confident that its normal customers will not learn of the price, and that the work will not compromise the council bid [1]. The decision would be different if the hotel work stopped Orlan from meeting its usual orders [1], or if the hotel would pay a higher price [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the moving average as a total rather than dividing by three.
- Working out payback from the wrong cumulative year.
- Forgetting to subtract the $150,000 cost when finding NPV.
- Using profit per unit instead of contribution for the special order.
- Leaving out the bid cost when finding the expected value.
- Naming the critical path without checking that its activities have zero float.
