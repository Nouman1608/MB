## Legislation (1.2.1 to 1.2.3)

- **Parliament Act 1911**: Lords lose the veto over most public Bills; power to delay only. **Parliament Act 1949**: delay cut to about one year. Money Bills: one month.
- Used rarely: War Crimes Act 1991, Hunting Act 2004. Validity upheld in *Jackson v Attorney General* [2005].
- **Royal Assent Act 1967**: assent can be notified to each House.
- **Reform influences**: parties (manifestos), pressure groups (Snowdrop campaign, Firearms (Amendment) Act 1997), Law Commission (1965; Fraud Act 2006), judges (*R v R* [1991]), media ("Sarah's Law").
- **Interpretation extras**: *ejusdem generis* (*Powell v Kempton Park* [1899]); presumption of *mens rea* (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970]); *Hansard* (*Pepper v Hart* [1993]); Interpretation Act 1978 (masculine includes feminine, singular includes plural); HRA 1998 s 3 compatible reading (*Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* [2004]), s 4 declaration of incompatibility.

**Why the Parliament Acts matter:** they confirm that the elected Commons can, in the end, override the unelected Lords. In *Jackson* the claimants argued that the 1949 Act, passed using the 1911 procedure, was invalid, so the Hunting Act 2004 was too; the House of Lords held both were valid Acts of Parliament. Evaluation: the procedure protects democratic will, but the Lords' delaying power still lets it force the government to reconsider.

**More interpretation tools with cases:**

- *Noscitur a sociis* (a word is known by the company it keeps): *Inland Revenue Commissioners v Frere* [1965], where "interest" in a list with "annuities or other annual" payments was read as annual interest only.
- *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius* (naming one thing excludes others): *R v Inhabitants of Sedgley* [1831], where a list naming "lands, houses, coal mines" was held not to cover other kinds of mine.
- *Ejusdem generis* in *Powell v Kempton Park*: "house, office, room or other place" was read as indoor places, so an outdoor betting enclosure was not covered.
- *Pepper v Hart* allows *Hansard* only where the Act is ambiguous or obscure, or its literal meaning leads to absurdity, and the ministerial statement relied on is clear.

## Delegated legislation (1.2.4 to 1.2.6)

Orders in council, statutory instruments, bylaws. Controlled by Parliament (resolution procedures, scrutiny committees) and the courts (*ultra vires*). **Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006**: ministers may reduce regulatory burdens by order, with safeguards; criticised as expanding executive power.

- **Procedural ultra vires**: *Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms* [1972]: an order was not binding on a mushroom growers' association that should have been consulted but was not.
- **Unreasonableness**: *Strickland v Hayes Borough Council* [1896]: a bylaw banning singing or reciting obscene material in any public or private place was unreasonable, because it went too far.
- **Advantages**: saves parliamentary time; uses technical and local expertise; quick to make and amend. **Disadvantages**: made by unelected people; huge volume and limited scrutiny; sub-delegation can blur who is responsible.

## Judicial law making (1.2.7 to 1.2.9)

- Precedent: *stare decisis*, ratio binds, obiter persuades, 1966 Practice Statement.
- **Equity in contract**: discretionary remedies. Specific performance (*Beswick v Beswick* [1968]); rescission, lost by delay (*Leaf v International Galleries* [1950]).
- For: flexible, responds to real cases. Against: unelected judges, retrospective effect, depends on a case reaching court.
- **Equity's discretion**: remedies are not available as of right. Specific performance is refused where damages would be an adequate remedy, so it is mainly used for unique subject matter such as land or a rare item. Rescission is barred by **delay** ("delay defeats equity", as in *Leaf*, where the buyer of a painting waited years before trying to rescind) and where restoring the parties to their original position is impossible.
- **Judges and law reform**: *R v R* shows judges changing the law when it was out of step with society (removing the marital exemption for rape), but critics say such a large change should come from Parliament.

## EU law (1.2.10 to 1.2.12)

**Regulations** apply directly; **directives** bind as to the result; the **Court of Justice** interprets EU law; the **Commission** proposes law and enforces treaties. *Marshall* [1986]: directive enforced against a state body. *Francovich* [1991]: state liable for non-implementation. The UK left the EU in 2020.

- **Supremacy**: *Costa v ENEL* [1964] established that EU law takes priority over conflicting national law; in *R v Secretary of State for Transport, ex parte Factortame* [1990] UK courts disapplied part of the Merchant Shipping Act 1988.
- **Direct effect**: *Van Gend en Loos* [1963]: clear, precise and unconditional Treaty provisions give individuals rights they can rely on in national courts. Directives have **vertical** direct effect only (against the state or an emanation of the state, as in *Marshall*).
- **After withdrawal**: the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 kept EU-derived law in force as "retained EU law"; the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 renamed what remains "assimilated law" from the start of 2024. Check with your teacher how far this content is examined.

## Civil justice (1.2.13 to 1.2.19)

- **Courts**: County Court, High Court, Court of Appeal, Supreme Court. **Civil Procedure Rules 1998**: tracks by value (small claims up to £10,000; fast track up to £25,000; multi-track above, with an intermediate track added in 2023); overriding objective of dealing with cases justly and at proportionate cost.
- **Tribunals**: Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007; First-tier and Upper Tribunal; judge plus lay members.
- **Tribunals evaluated**: cheaper, quicker and more informal than courts, with expert members; but legal aid is rarely available, so an unrepresented claimant may face a represented employer, and the Upper Tribunal can be slow.
- **ADR evaluated**: cheaper, faster, private and less confrontational, which can preserve relationships (useful in family and business disputes); but there may be an imbalance of power between the parties, there is no precedent, and agreements in mediation are only binding if the parties make them so. Arbitration awards can be enforced through the courts and appeals are very limited.

## People (1.2.20 to 1.2.22)

Judges: Judicial Appointments Commission (Constitutional Reform Act 2005), wider eligibility (Courts and Legal Services Act 1990). Solicitors (SRA) and barristers (Bar Standards Board). Magistrates: lay, usually three with a legal adviser. Jurors: random from the electoral register (Juries Act 1974).

- **Judges**: the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 replaced the old "rights of audience" test with years of post-qualification legal experience, opening appointment to a wider range of lawyers. Selection is on **merit**, with the JAC also required to encourage diversity.
- **Magistrates**: appointed by the Lord Chancellor on the advice of local advisory committees; unpaid volunteers with no legal qualification required; they hear most criminal cases and sit with a legally qualified adviser on law and procedure.
- **Jurors**: aged 18 to 75, registered to vote and resident in the UK for at least five years since age 13; some people are disqualified, for example because of certain criminal convictions. Evaluation: random selection brings public participation and independence from the state, but jurors give no reasons and may not understand complex evidence.

## Costs and funding (1.2.23 to 1.2.24)

Court fees, lawyers' fees, loser pays, reputational cost. Funding: insurance, legal aid (cut by LASPO 2012), conditional fees, trade unions, Citizens Advice, pro bono.

LASPO 2012 took many civil areas (for example most private family, employment and welfare benefit matters) out of the scope of legal aid, which is now run by the **Legal Aid Agency**. It also stopped a winning claimant recovering the lawyer's **success fee** under a conditional fee agreement from the losing side, so the fee usually comes out of the damages. Evaluation: cuts saved public money but are criticised for reducing **access to justice** and increasing the number of unrepresented litigants.

## Model evaluation paragraph

*Topic: evaluate the use of ADR rather than the courts for civil disputes.*

> ADR is usually cheaper and quicker than going to court, and methods such as mediation are private and less confrontational, which can help parties such as neighbours or business partners keep working together. Arbitration also gives a decision that is binding under the Arbitration Act 1996 and can be enforced through the courts. However, where one party is much more powerful, such as a large company against an individual, mediation may produce an unfair agreement, because the mediator does not impose a result. ADR also creates no precedent, so it does not develop the law. Overall, ADR suits disputes where the parties are of similar strength and want to preserve a relationship, while the courts remain necessary where a binding ruling on the law is needed.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Saying the Lords can veto Bills: since 1911 they can only delay most public Bills.
- Giving a rule of language without a case or the effect on the outcome.
- Treating equitable remedies as automatic: they are discretionary.
- Saying directives have horizontal direct effect: they bind the state, not private individuals.
- Calling conciliation and mediation the same: a conciliator suggests terms; a mediator only facilitates.
- Saying magistrates are legally qualified: they are lay people advised by a legal adviser.
