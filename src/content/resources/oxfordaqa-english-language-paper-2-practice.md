## Section A — Reading

**1.** Explain what **R2 (collating and synthesising detail)** requires a reader to do that goes beyond simply finding a single piece of information stated in a source. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **one** skill needed to read a solely visual source, such as an infographic or chart, that is not needed when reading continuous prose. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** A campaign leaflet on reducing plastic waste includes this passage: *"Every plastic bottle you refuse today outlives you by four hundred years. That's not a fact to feel guilty about — it's a reason to act now, while it still makes a difference."*

**(a)** Analyse the effect of "outlives you by four hundred years". **[3]**
**(b)** Analyse the effect of the short sentence "That's not a fact to feel guilty about". **[3]**
**(c)** Comment on the structural effect of ending the passage with "while it still makes a difference". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **four** features you could look for when analysing a visual or graphic source, such as an infographic, in Section A. **[8]**

**5.** Explain how to synthesise detail effectively across two source texts covering the same modern-world issue, giving **three** things to look for and **one** common mistake. **[6]**

**6.** You are writing a letter to a local newspaper, aimed at persuading readers in your town to support a new recycling scheme.

**(a)** Identify the audience and the purpose of this task in one sentence each. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **three** techniques you would use to adapt your writing to this specific audience and purpose. **[6]**

**7.** Explain why technical accuracy (W4) is credited equally alongside content and organisation in Section B, and name **three** features it covers. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. R2** requires the reader to **draw connections between details found in more than one place** (often across separate sources), rather than reporting each piece of information in isolation [1] [1]; a strong answer **synthesises** these details into a combined point — for example, noting that two sources agree on a problem but propose different solutions — rather than simply listing what each source says one after another [1].

**2.** Any one, worth 3 marks: reading a visual source requires identifying **what has been emphasised through size, position or colour**, and equally what has been **omitted**, since a chart or infographic conveys meaning through **implicit framing** as well as explicit data [1] [1] [1]. (Accept also: interpreting numerical or graphical data directly, without the scaffolding of connected prose sentences.)

**3. (a)** "Outlives you by four hundred years" creates a **jarring contrast between the brief moment of using a bottle and its near-permanent environmental consequence** [1], personifying the bottle as something that will continue to exist long after the reader is gone [1], which makes an abstract statistic feel **personally and unsettlingly relevant** [1].
**(b)** The short sentence creates an abrupt, **conversational pivot** immediately after the alarming statistic [1], its brevity giving it the weight of a direct address to the reader [1]; it also **pre-empts and dismisses a defensive reaction** (guilt) before the reader can settle into it, redirecting them toward action instead [1].
**(c)** Ending on **"makes a difference"** leaves the reader with a **note of agency and hope** rather than despair [1], which is a deliberate structural choice for a persuasive campaign text — closing on impact rather than the earlier, more troubling statistic increases the likelihood the reader acts rather than feels merely alarmed [1].

**4.** Any four, 2 marks each: **what is emphasised through size or position** — the largest or most central element is usually the source's key message [1] [1]. **What has been omitted** — data or context left out can reveal a source's bias or intended framing [1] [1]. **Colour and visual contrast** — used to draw the eye to specific figures or to create positive/negative associations [1] [1]. **The relationship between images and any accompanying text or caption**, which can reinforce, or sometimes subtly shift, the meaning of the data shown [1] [1]. **The scale or axis choices in a chart**, which can make a change look more or less dramatic than the raw numbers alone suggest [1] [1].

**5.** Look for: where the two sources **agree**, which strengthens a synthesised point [1]; where they **disagree or offer different perspectives** on the same issue, which is often the most valuable material to compare [1]; and how each source's **purpose or intended audience** shapes what it chooses to include or emphasise [1]. Common mistake: **summarising source A in full, then summarising source B in full**, rather than drawing the two together into synthesised points that reference both sources within the same idea [2] [1].

**6. (a) Audience:** readers of the local newspaper — a general local adult readership, likely including both supporters and sceptics of a new scheme [1]. **Purpose:** to persuade them to support the recycling scheme [1].
**(b)** Any three, 2 marks each: **local, specific examples** (a nearby street, a familiar local landmark or existing problem) make the issue feel immediate and relevant to this particular readership, rather than abstract [1] [1]. **Rhetorical questions** invite readers to reach the pro-scheme conclusion themselves, which tends to be more persuasive than simply telling them [1] [1]. **Acknowledging a likely objection** (such as inconvenience or cost) before addressing it directly shows the writer is reasonable and reduces resistance from sceptical readers [1] [1]. **A confident, direct call to action** in the closing lines, appropriate to a newspaper letter's persuasive purpose [1] [1].

**7.** Technical accuracy is credited equally because **AO2 assesses communication as a whole**, and errors in grammar, punctuation or spelling can **undermine clarity and credibility** regardless of how well-organised or persuasive the content is [1] [1]. Three features it covers: **sentence demarcation** (including avoiding comma splices) [1]; **consistent tense** throughout the response [1]; **accurate use of apostrophes and correct spelling of frequently-used vocabulary** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering R2 questions by listing what each source says separately, rather than synthesising a combined point across them.
- Treating a visual source as containing less to analyse than a prose source, and so under-answering questions based on it.
- Analysing a persuasive technique without explaining its effect on the specific reader it targets.
- Drafting Section B without first fixing the audience and purpose, producing writing that drifts into a generic register partway through.
- Neglecting technical accuracy under time pressure, despite it carrying equal weight to content and organisation within AO2.

## Approaching Section A and Section B together

Because Paper 2 tests reading and writing skills that reinforce each other, the strongest preparation treats them as one connected paper rather than two separate halves. Every technique identified and analysed in Section A — a rhetorical question, a short sentence for impact, a structural choice to end on hope rather than alarm — is a technique that can be deliberately deployed in Section B's directed writing, and examiners reward candidates who can do both: recognise a writer's craft when reading, and apply the same craft with control when writing for their own specified audience and purpose.
