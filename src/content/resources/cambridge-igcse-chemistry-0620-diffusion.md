## What the syllabus asks for

| Core | Supplement (Extended only) |
|---|---|
| 1 Describe and explain diffusion in terms of kinetic particle theory | 2 Describe and explain the effect of relative molecular mass on the rate of diffusion of gases |

Both outcomes say **describe and explain**. A description says what you would see. An explanation says why, using particles.

## What diffusion is

Diffusion is the **net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random motion**. It continues until the particles are evenly spread out.

Three parts of that definition matter in an answer:

1. **Net** movement: individual particles move in every direction, but overall more move from the crowded region to the less crowded one.
2. **Higher to lower concentration**: the direction of the overall movement.
3. **Random motion**: nothing pushes or pulls the particles; diffusion happens because they move about randomly on their own.

## Building the particle explanation

Kinetic particle theory says all particles are in constant, random motion. Picture a gas jar of a coloured gas placed mouth to mouth with a jar of air.

- The coloured gas particles move randomly in all directions and collide with air particles and with each other.
- At first there are many coloured particles in the lower jar and none in the upper jar.
- By chance, more coloured particles move from the lower jar into the upper jar than move back, simply because there are more of them in the lower jar to make the move.
- Air particles diffuse the other way for the same reason.
- When the particles are evenly mixed, as many move up as down, so there is no further net movement. The colour is even throughout. The particles are still moving.

This is why diffusion does not need stirring, and why it never stops the particles moving; only the net movement stops.

## Diffusion in gases and liquids

| | Gases | Liquids |
|---|---|---|
| Does diffusion happen? | Yes | Yes |
| Speed | Fast: a smell crosses a room in seconds or minutes | Slow: a colour spreads through still water over hours |
| Particle reason | Particles move fast, with large spaces between them, so they travel far between collisions | Particles move more slowly and are close together, so they collide very often and travel only a short distance each time |

Solids are not included. In a solid the particles vibrate about fixed positions and cannot move through the substance, so there is no net movement of particles at this level.

### Two classic demonstrations

**A coloured crystal in water.** A crystal of a coloured, soluble solid, such as potassium manganate(VII), is placed carefully at the bottom of a beaker of still water. Over several hours the purple colour spreads upwards until the whole beaker is evenly coloured. The water is not stirred, so the spreading is caused only by the random motion of the particles.

**A coloured gas.** A gas jar of brown bromine vapour is placed under a jar of air, separated by a cover. When the cover is removed, the brown colour spreads upwards, even though bromine is denser than air, until both jars are the same pale brown. The fact that it spreads upwards, against what gravity alone would do, is evidence for random particle motion. Bromine is toxic, so this is done in a fume cupboard.

## Relative molecular mass and rate (Extended only)

At the same temperature, particles of different gases have the same average kinetic energy. A particle with a smaller mass must therefore move faster to have the same kinetic energy. So:

**the lower the relative molecular mass, Mᵣ, the faster the gas diffuses.**

You need to describe the effect (lighter gases diffuse faster) and explain it (lighter particles move faster at the same temperature). Calculating exact rates is not required.

### Worked example 1: ranking gases

Rank these gases from fastest to slowest diffusion at the same temperature: carbon dioxide, hydrogen, sulfur dioxide, methane. (Relative atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, S = 32.)

| Gas | Formula | Mᵣ |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H₂ | 2 × 1 = 2 |
| Methane | CH₄ | 12 + 4 × 1 = 16 |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | 12 + 2 × 16 = 44 |
| Sulfur dioxide | SO₂ | 32 + 2 × 16 = 64 |

Fastest to slowest: **hydrogen, methane, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide**.

Always show the Mᵣ calculation. It is the evidence for your ranking, and a mistake such as using O = 8 is easy to spot.

### Worked example 2: the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube

A long glass tube is clamped horizontally. Cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is placed in one end, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid in the other, at the same moment. Both ends are sealed with bungs. After a few minutes, a white ring forms inside the tube, closer to the hydrochloric acid end.

**Describe**: ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas diffuse along the tube towards each other. Where they meet, they react to form a white solid, ammonium chloride:

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

**Explain**: Mᵣ of ammonia, NH₃ = 14 + 3 = 17. Mᵣ of hydrogen chloride, HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5. Ammonia particles are lighter, so they move faster and travel further along the tube in the same time. The two gases meet nearer the hydrogen chloride end.

Points that earn marks in a write-up of this experiment:

- the bungs stop the gases escaping and prevent air currents, so movement is by diffusion only
- the cotton wool is placed at both ends at the same time, so the comparison is fair
- the experiment is carried out in a fume cupboard because both gases are harmful.

### Worked example 3: predicting a result

The ammonia is replaced by a gas with Mᵣ = 73, and the hydrogen chloride is kept. Would the ring form nearer the hydrogen chloride end, nearer the other end, or in the middle?

The new gas (Mᵣ 73) is heavier than hydrogen chloride (Mᵣ 36.5), so it diffuses more slowly. Hydrogen chloride would travel further, and the product would form **nearer the end where the new gas started**.

## Temperature

Diffusion is faster at a higher temperature, because the particles have more kinetic energy and move faster. This is a direct application of kinetic particle theory: the same idea explains why a smell spreads more quickly in a warm room.

## Common mistakes

- Defining diffusion without "random motion", or saying particles move "because they want to spread out".
- Writing "from low to high concentration".
- Saying diffusion stops because the particles stop moving. Only the net movement stops.
- Saying heavier gases diffuse faster because they are "stronger".
- In the tube experiment, saying ammonia is faster because it is "more reactive". The reason is its lower Mᵣ.
- Stating the effect of Mᵣ without explaining it in terms of particle speed.

## Exam technique

- **Describe**: what you would observe, such as "the colour spreads until it is even".
- **Explain**: random motion, collisions, net movement from higher to lower concentration; for Mᵣ, lighter particles move faster.
- **Predict**: compare Mᵣ values first, then state where or how fast, with the reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is diffusion slower in liquids than in gases?**
Liquid particles move more slowly and are close together, so they collide very often and travel only short distances between collisions.

**Why does the white ring form nearer the hydrochloric acid end?**
Ammonia has a lower relative molecular mass (17) than hydrogen chloride (36.5), so its particles move faster and travel further in the same time.
