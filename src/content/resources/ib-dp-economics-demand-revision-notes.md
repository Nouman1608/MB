## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand | The amounts buyers wish and can afford to purchase at each price over a period |
| Law of demand | Other things unchanged, a higher price means a lower quantity demanded |
| Demand curve | Line or curve of price against quantity demanded, sloping down |
| Market demand | Sum of all consumers' quantities at each price |
| Extension / contraction | Slide down / up the curve after a fall / rise in the good's own price |
| Increase / decrease in demand | Curve moves right / left because of any non-price determinant |
| Normal good | Demand rises when income rises |
| Inferior good | Demand falls when income rises |
| Substitutes | Goods that can replace each other |
| Complements | Goods used together |

## The law and its explanation

The law says price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. On a diagram, the curve slopes downward. Always put price up the side, quantity along the bottom, and name the curve D.

**HL only: why the law holds**

- **Substitution effect:** a lower price makes the good cheaper relative to alternatives, so buyers switch towards it.
- **Income effect:** a lower price raises purchasing power, so a normal good is bought in larger quantity.
- **Diminishing marginal utility:** each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so buyers take more only if the price falls.

Quick numbers. A budget of 60 om on a fare that falls from 4 to 3 om: trips possible rise from 15 to 20, an increase in purchasing power of 33.3%. Slice values 9, 7, 5, 3, 1 om: at a price of 4 om, three slices are worth buying (9, 7, 5). At 2 om, four are (9, 7, 5, 3).

## Method: finding market demand

1. List the price points.
2. At each price, read off each consumer's quantity. A consumer with no demand at that price contributes zero.
3. Add the quantities across consumers (horizontal sum).
4. Plot or state market quantity against price.

Reminder: in an illustrative market with Aiko, Bram and Cato, the quantities at a price of 4 om are 4, 4 and 2, which give 10. At 6 om, Cato drops out, and Aiko and Bram give 2 + 3 = 5. When a consumer drops out as price rises, the market curve changes slope at that price.

## Non-price determinants: direction of shift

| Determinant | Change | Effect on demand |
|---|---|---|
| Income (normal good) | Rises | Shift right |
| Income (inferior good) | Rises | Shift left |
| Tastes and preferences | Move in favour | Shift right |
| Expected future price | Expected to rise | Current demand shifts right |
| Expected future price | Expected to fall | Current demand shifts left |
| Price of a substitute | Rises | Shift right |
| Price of a complement | Rises | Shift left |
| Number of consumers | Rises | Shift right |

Each row says what to write: the cause, the direction, and a reason.

## Movements versus shifts

```
Price
 |
 |  \    \
 |   \    \
 |    a    \
 |     \    b
 |      \    \
 |  D1   \  D2
 +-------------------- Quantity
```

Point a to a lower point on D1 is a movement. Moving from D1 to D2 at the same price is a shift.

- Own price changes: slide along the same curve (from a to another point on D1).
- Any other determinant changes: draw a new curve (D2) and label the new quantity at the original price.

Worked reminder (illustrative flask market, Qd = 70 − 5P). At P = 6, Qd = 40. If P falls to 5, Qd = 45: an extension of 5. If tastes shift so that Qd = 85 − 5P, then at P = 6, Qd = 55: demand has increased by 15 at the same price. If 10% more consumers join, quantity at P = 6 is 40 × 1.10 = 44. If a normal good gains a 20% rise in demand at each price from higher incomes, quantity at P = 6 is 40 × 1.20 = 48.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Extension versus increase.** An extension needs a price fall. An increase needs a non-price cause.
- **Substitute versus complement.** Substitute price up: demand up. Complement price up: demand down.
- **Normal versus inferior.** The sign of the income effect on demand differs.
- **Current demand versus future price.** Expecting a higher future price raises demand now.
- **Individual versus market.** Add quantities, not prices.
- **Substitution versus income effect (HL only).** Relative price versus purchasing power.

## A model diagram answer

Question type: "Using a demand diagram, explain the effect of a rise in household incomes on the market for a normal good."

1. Draw price (vertical) and quantity (horizontal). Draw D1 sloping down.
2. State that income is a non-price determinant and the good is normal.
3. Draw D2 to the right of D1. Label it.
4. Pick one price and show that D2 gives a larger quantity than D1 at that price.
5. Write one sentence of reasoning: higher incomes raise the ability to pay at every price.

For an inferior good, steps 3 to 5 reverse: D2 sits to the left, because richer buyers trade up to goods they prefer.

## Quick self-test

1. State the law of demand.
2. A traveller has a budget of 60 om and the fare per trip falls from 4 om to 3 om. How many more trips are possible, and what is the percentage rise in purchasing power? (HL only)
3. Dara's slice values are 9, 7, 5, 3 and 1 om. How many slices will she buy at a price of 6 om? (HL only)
4. At a price of 2 om, three consumers buy 6, 5 and 6 flasks. State the market quantity.
5. Demand is Qd = 70 − 5P. Find Qd at P = 6 and at P = 4.
6. Demand shifts to Qd = 85 − 5P. Find Qd at P = 6, and say whether this is a movement or a shift.
7. A rise in the price of tea shifts the demand curve for biscuits left. Are the two goods substitutes or complements?
8. Shoppers are told a price rise is coming next week. What happens to demand this week?
9. Name the five non-price determinants listed in the guide.
10. Why is a price fall not a shift of the demand curve?

### Answers

1. When other things are unchanged, a lower price raises the quantity demanded (and a higher price lowers it).
2. 60 ÷ 4 = 15 trips and 60 ÷ 3 = 20 trips. That is 5 more trips, a rise of 33.3%.
3. Values of at least 6 om: 9 and 7. She buys 2 slices.
4. 6 + 5 + 6 = 17 flasks.
5. At P = 6: 70 − 30 = 40. At P = 4: 70 − 20 = 50.
6. 85 − 30 = 55. It is a shift (the same price gives a different quantity).
7. Complements: a rise in the price of tea reduces the demand for biscuits that are bought with it.
8. It shifts right now, because buyers bring purchases forward.
9. Income; tastes and preferences; future price expectations; prices of related goods; number of consumers.
10. The own price is already on the vertical axis, so its change is shown by moving along the curve.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing a diagram without labelling both axes, the curve names or the new quantity.
- Saying "demand rises" when the cause is a fall in own price.
- Explaining a shift with no reason for its direction, such as "income rises so the curve moves" with no mention of normal or inferior.
- Adding prices rather than quantities when building market demand.
- Treating a complement like a substitute, or the reverse.
- Ignoring the "willing and able" part of the definition of demand.
- Mixing up the income effect and the substitution effect in HL answers.
- Not showing working in a calculation that asks for it. The guide defines "calculate" as obtaining a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working.
- Giving only a definition when the command term is "explain", which needs causes or reasons.
