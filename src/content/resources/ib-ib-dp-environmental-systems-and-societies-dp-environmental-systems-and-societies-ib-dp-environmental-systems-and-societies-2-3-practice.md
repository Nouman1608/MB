## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a carbon sink and a carbon source. **[2]**

**2.** Outline how the solubility pump transfers carbon to the deep ocean. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how increasing atmospheric carbon dioxide can affect coral reefs through ocean
chemistry. **[3]**

**4.** A forest records: GPP 1,800 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹; plant respiration 950 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹; decomposer
respiration 680 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the NPP. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the net ecosystem uptake and state whether the forest is a sink or a source.
**[2]**
**(c)** Convert the net uptake into tonnes of CO₂ per hectare per year. **[2]**
**(d)** Predict how a severe fire would change the forest's role in the carbon cycle in the
following years. **[2]**

**5.** A lake's sediments and water hold 4,800 t of carbon. Carbon enters at 160 t per year and
leaves at the same rate. Calculate the residence time. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why thawing permafrost is an example of positive feedback. **[3]**

**7.** State the nitrogen cycle process that matches each description: (i) carried out by bacteria in
legume root nodules; (ii) needs well-aerated soil and produces nitrate; (iii) returns nitrogen gas
to the air from waterlogged soil. **[3]**

**8.** A farm field's nitrogen budget (kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹): fertiliser 150, manure 60, fixation 10,
deposition 10; removed in crop 120, leaching 55, denitrification 25, ammonia loss 20.

**(a)** Calculate the net change in the soil nitrogen store. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of the nitrogen inputs lost by leaching. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** changes in management that would reduce leaching. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why burning fossil fuels changes the carbon cycle more than an equal release of carbon
from burning this year's crop residues. **[3]**

**10.** Outline the role of decomposers in the carbon and nitrogen cycles. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why including legumes in a crop rotation can reduce the need for synthetic
fertiliser. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate strategies for increasing the amount of carbon stored on land. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** A sink takes up more carbon than it releases over a given period [1]; a source releases more
carbon than it takes up [1]. **[2]**

**2.** CO₂ dissolves in surface ocean water, more readily where the water is cold [1]; cold, dense
water sinks at high latitudes, carrying the dissolved carbon into the deep ocean [1]. **[2]**

**3.** More CO₂ dissolves in seawater, forming carbonic acid [1]. This releases hydrogen ions, lowering
pH and reducing the concentration of carbonate ions [1]. Corals have less carbonate to build their
calcium carbonate skeletons, so growth slows and reefs become weaker [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 1800 − 950 = **850 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 850 − 680 = **170 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹** [1]; positive, so a **sink** [1]
**(c)** 170 × 10,000 = 1,700,000 g = 1.7 t C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [1]; 1.7 × 44/12 = **6.23 t CO₂ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹**
(3 s.f.) [1]
**(d)** Burning releases stored carbon immediately [1]; afterwards, photosynthesis is low while
decomposition of dead wood continues, so the forest acts as a source until regrowth restores uptake
[1]. **[7]**

**5.** 4800 / 160 = **30 years** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Warming thaws permafrost [1]; organic matter that was frozen decomposes, releasing CO₂ and, in
waterlogged ground, methane [1]; these greenhouse gases increase warming, which thaws more
permafrost, amplifying the original change [1]. **[3]**

**7.** (i) Nitrogen fixation [1]; (ii) nitrification [1]; (iii) denitrification [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Inputs = 150 + 60 + 10 + 10 = 230; outputs = 120 + 55 + 25 + 20 = 220 [1]; net change =
**+10 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 55 / 230 × 100 = **23.9%** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** Any two: reduce or split fertiliser doses to match crop need [1]; plant cover crops to take
up nitrate over winter [1]; avoid spreading fertiliser or manure before heavy rain [1]; plant buffer
strips beside drains [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Fossil fuel carbon has been stored in the slow cycle for millions of years [1], so burning it
adds carbon that the fast cycle has not held for a very long time, raising atmospheric
concentration [1]. Crop residue carbon was taken from the atmosphere by photosynthesis within the
last year, so its release returns carbon recently removed and is balanced by next year's growth [1].
**[3]**

**10.** Decomposers break down dead organic matter and waste [1], releasing carbon dioxide to the
atmosphere through respiration [1] and converting nitrogen in proteins and urea into ammonium
(ammonification), making it available for nitrification and plant uptake [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Legumes host nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules that convert N₂ into ammonium
[1]; when the legume residues decompose, this fixed nitrogen remains in the soil for the next crop,
so less fertiliser is needed [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Award up to 7: at least two named strategies, each with a benefit and a limitation (up to 6),
and a judgement [1].
- *Reforestation and afforestation:* growing trees take up carbon for decades [1]; but land is
  needed for food, monoculture plantations offer little biodiversity, and stored carbon can be lost
  to fire [1].
- *Peatland rewetting:* raising water tables slows decomposition, stopping losses and allowing slow
  accumulation [1]; but drained peat may be valuable farmland, and accumulation is very slow [1].
- *Soil carbon in farming (reduced ploughing, cover crops, adding organic matter):* increases soil
  organic carbon while improving fertility [1]; but gains are small per hectare and can be reversed
  if practices change [1].
- *Protecting existing forests:* keeps large existing storages intact, avoiding emissions [1]; but
  needs enforcement and compensation for lost income [1].
- *Judgement:* for example, protecting existing stores is the fastest and most certain option, with
  new planting and rewetting as long-term additions; none replaces cutting fossil fuel emissions [1].
  **[7]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a forest a sink without comparing uptake with release.
- Forgetting the 44/12 conversion, or applying it the wrong way.
- Mixing up nitrification and denitrification conditions.
- Evaluating sequestration with benefits only.
