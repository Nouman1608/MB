## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by capacity utilisation. **[2]**

**2.** Tarn Kayaks can make 2,500 kayaks a month. In March it made 1,800. Calculate its capacity utilisation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business may choose not to operate at 100% capacity utilisation. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by capacity management. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following would reduce a business's capacity?

A: Subcontracting some production.

B: Selling spare machinery.

C: Adding a night shift.

D: Hiring temporary staff.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Tarn Kayaks has fixed costs of £30,000 a month. Each kayak sells for £40 and has variable costs of £22.

**(a)** Calculate the fixed cost per kayak when 1,800 kayaks are made. Give your answer to the nearest penny. **[2]**

**(b)** Tarn Kayaks sets a target of 85% capacity utilisation. Calculate the output needed and the fixed cost per kayak at that output, to the nearest penny. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the break-even output and express it as a percentage of capacity, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**7.** In summer Tarn Kayaks receives orders for 2,900 kayaks a month. In winter it receives orders for 1,000.

**(a)** Calculate the capacity utilisation in each season. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse the problems that this pattern creates for Tarn Kayaks. **[6]**

**8.** Summer demand for Tarn Kayaks is expected to stay at 2,900 kayaks a month for several years. The directors are considering two options.

Option A: rent a second unit that adds 600 kayaks of monthly capacity at an extra fixed cost of £5,400 a month.

Option B: subcontract the 400 extra kayaks to another firm at a cost of £34 each.

**(a)** Calculate the extra monthly profit from each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend which option Tarn Kayaks should choose and justify your answer. **[8]**

**9.** Evaluate the importance of effective capacity management to a hospital that offers both emergency and planned operations. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Capacity utilisation is the proportion of maximum possible output that a business actually produces in a period [1], usually shown as a percentage of the maximum [1]. **[2]**

**2.** 1,800 ÷ 2,500 [1] × 100 = **72%** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** At 100% there is no spare capacity for an urgent order [1], and no time for planned maintenance, so one breakdown or absence cuts output immediately [1]. Accept: overworked staff and machines cause errors and accidents. **[2]**

**4.** Capacity management is planning and controlling the capacity of a business [1] so that it matches demand at the lowest cost without losing customers or quality [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** £30,000 ÷ 1,800 [1] = **£16.67** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Output = 85% × 2,500 = **2,125 kayaks** [1]. Fixed cost per kayak = £30,000 ÷ 2,125 [1] = **£14.12** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Contribution per kayak = £40 − £22 = £18 [1]. Break-even = £30,000 ÷ £18 = 1,666.7, so **1,667 kayaks** [1]. As a percentage of capacity: 1,666.7 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **66.7%** [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Summer: 2,900 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **116%** [1]. Winter: 1,000 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **40%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** In summer Tarn cannot meet demand from normal capacity, as it is 400 kayaks short [1], so it loses sales and customers may turn to rivals [1]. It may use overtime or temporary staff, which raise cost and may lower quality [1].
In winter utilisation is 40%, below the break-even level of 66.7%, so the business makes a loss on the month [1]. Fixed costs of £30,000 are paid whether or not the capacity is used, so the fixed cost per kayak is very high [1].
The average of the two seasons hides the problem: capacity suited to one season is wrong for the other [1]. Accept staff morale and retention linked to short-time working in winter. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Option A: contribution on 400 kayaks = 400 × £18 = £7,200 [1], less extra fixed cost £5,400 = **£1,800** extra profit [1]. Option B: contribution per kayak = £40 − £34 = £6 [1]; 400 × £6 = **£2,400** extra profit [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** On the figures, Option B gives £600 a month more profit than Option A [1], and it has no extra fixed cost, so it is safer if demand falls [1]. The break-even between the options is where £18x − £5,400 = £6x, so x = 450 kayaks above capacity [1]. Demand of 400 is below that, so B is better now, but if demand grew beyond 450 above capacity, Option A would be better [1].
Option A gives Tarn control over quality and delivery and leaves spare capacity of 200 kayaks (2,900 out of 3,100 is 93.5% utilisation) [1]. Subcontracting means a rival or supplier might copy the design, quality is harder to check, and the other firm might refuse the work or raise its price [1].
Demand is expected to stay high for several years, which supports Option A in the long term, but the extra rent is a fixed commitment if the market weakens [1].
Judgement: Tarn should subcontract first, because it earns more at the current level and carries less risk, and then rent the unit if demand rises well above 2,950 kayaks a month or if quality control with the subcontractor proves poor [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Importance: the hospital must keep spare capacity for emergencies, which cannot be predicted [1], so a utilisation near 100% on beds or theatres would delay or endanger emergency patients [1]. Planned operations can be scheduled, so waiting lists and booking systems can smooth demand [1]. Low utilisation of expensive theatres and staff wastes resources, since fixed costs are high and funding is limited [1].
Capacity management affects patients (waiting times, safety), staff (workload, stress, overtime), and taxpayers or funders (value for money) [1].
Ways to manage it: forecasting seasonal demand such as winter illness, and flexible staffing, including bank staff and annualised hours [1].
Evaluation: the cost of idle capacity is smaller than the cost of failing to treat an emergency patient, so a lower target utilisation is justified than in a factory [1]. Judgement: capacity management is vital here because the consequences of a mistake are serious, so the hospital should plan for an average utilisation well below 100% [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by demand or by last month's output instead of maximum output.
- Saying "100% is best" without considering maintenance or emergencies.
- Giving a list of ways to change capacity without choosing one and justifying it.
- Forgetting to recalculate costs when fixed costs change.
- Ignoring whether the demand change is permanent.
