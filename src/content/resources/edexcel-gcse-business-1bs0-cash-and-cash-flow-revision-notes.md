## The specification in brief

- The importance of cash to a business: to pay suppliers, overheads and employees; to prevent business failure (insolvency); the difference between cash and profit.
- Calculation and interpretation of cash-flow forecasts: cash inflows, cash outflows, net cash flow, opening and closing balances.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash | Money held in the bank or the till that the business can spend now |
| Cash flow | The movement of cash into and out of a business |
| Cash inflow | Money coming in: cash sales, payments from credit customers, loans, owner's capital |
| Cash outflow | Money going out: materials, wages, rent, bills, equipment, loan repayments |
| Net cash flow | Cash inflows − cash outflows in a given period |
| Opening balance | The closing balance of the previous period |
| Closing balance | Opening balance + net cash flow |
| Cash-flow forecast | A prediction of future inflows, outflows and balances |
| Insolvency | Being unable to pay debts when they fall due |
| Credit sale | A sale where the customer pays later |
| Profit | Revenue − costs for a period, whenever the cash moves |

## Formulae and rearrangements

| You want | Use |
|---|---|
| Net cash flow | inflows − outflows |
| Closing balance | opening balance + net cash flow |
| Opening balance | previous closing balance |
| Missing outflows | inflows − net cash flow |
| Missing inflows | net cash flow + outflows |
| Missing net cash flow | closing balance − opening balance |

The first three are Appendix 3 formulae; the rest are rearrangements.

## Condensed recall

### Why cash matters
- Suppliers: late payment risks losing credit terms or supplies.
- Overheads: rent and bills fall due on fixed dates.
- Employees: wages must be paid on time.
- No cash to pay debts → insolvency → possible closure.

### Why cash and profit differ
1. Customers pay after the sale (credit).
2. Equipment bought with a large cash payment is used for years.
3. Loans and owner's capital are cash in but not revenue.
4. A growing business often pays for materials before customers pay, so growth can drain cash.

### Mini forecast: Pebble Cove Café

| | Jul | Aug | Sep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inflows | 7,000 | 6,500 | 9,000 |
| Outflows | 7,800 | 7,200 | 7,400 |
| Net cash flow | −800 | −700 | 1,600 |
| Opening balance | 2,000 | 1,200 | 500 |
| Closing balance | 1,200 | 500 | 2,100 |

Reading it: two negative months shrink the balance to £500 in August, close to running out; September recovers.

### Fixing a cash-flow problem (link to 1.3.4)
- Short-term finance: overdraft, trade credit.
- Ask customers to pay sooner or pay deposits.
- Delay large purchases.
- Cut or postpone non-essential outflows.

## Exam traps

- Using sales made instead of cash received when customers pay later.
- Carrying the opening balance forward instead of the closing balance.
- Treating a negative balance as a loss.
- Counting a loan as revenue or profit.
- Forgetting the minus sign when net cash flow is negative.
- Saying a forecast is certain; it is a prediction.

## Self-test

1. Give the three things the specification says cash is needed to pay.
2. Define insolvency.
3. State the formula for net cash flow.
4. State the formula for closing balance.
5. Inflows are £8,400 and outflows are £9,100. Calculate net cash flow.
6. The opening balance is £1,200 and net cash flow is −£1,650. Calculate the closing balance.
7. A closing balance is £2,300 and the opening balance was £3,000. Calculate net cash flow.
8. Net cash flow is £400 and outflows are £6,600. Calculate inflows.
9. Give one reason why a business could be profitable but short of cash.
10. Why is a bank loan a cash inflow but not revenue?
11. Explain one way a business could improve a forecast negative balance.
12. Why might a start-up's cash-flow forecast be less reliable than an established firm's?

### Answers

1. Suppliers, overheads and employees.
2. Being unable to pay debts when they fall due.
3. Net cash flow = cash inflows − cash outflows.
4. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
5. £8,400 − £9,100 = −£700.
6. £1,200 + (−£1,650) = −£450.
7. £2,300 − £3,000 = −£700.
8. £400 + £6,600 = £7,000.
9. Customers pay on credit, so revenue is earned before cash arrives (or a large equipment purchase uses up cash).
10. It must be repaid, so it is not money earned from selling the product.
11. For example, arranging an overdraft for the months the balance is negative, so bills can be paid until customers' payments arrive.
12. It has no past sales records, so its forecasts of sales and timing of payments are estimates with little evidence.
