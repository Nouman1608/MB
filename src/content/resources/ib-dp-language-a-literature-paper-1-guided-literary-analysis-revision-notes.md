## Method in steps

```
1  Read the guiding question. Underline the aspect and the verb.
2  Reading 1 (literal): who, where, when, what happens.
3  Reading 2 (form and style): mark features that answer the
   question; write the effect in the margin, not just the label.
4  Reading 3 (argument): one-sentence thesis that answers the
   question and suggests an order.
5  Plan 3 body paragraphs by idea, 2-3 quotations each.
6  Write: short introduction -> body -> short conclusion.
7  Check: every paragraph links back to the guiding question.
```

**Paragraph pattern:** claim → evidence (short, embedded quotation) → effect → link to the question.

**Thesis pattern:** "The writer presents [aspect in the question] as [your judgement], through
[feature group 1], [feature group 2] and [feature group 3]."

**Introduction (3–4 sentences):** the form and situation in one sentence, the voice, then the
thesis. No biography, no dictionary definitions, no "since the dawn of time".

**Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** how the features work together to answer the question. No new
quotations and no paragraph-by-paragraph repeat.

**SL timing (one analysis, 75 min):** 15 reading · 10 planning · 5 introduction · 35 body ·
5 conclusion · 5 checking.

## Terms by form

### Poetry

| Term | What it is | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker | The voice of the poem, not the poet | Who speaks, to whom, in what situation? |
| Stanza | A group of lines | Does a new stanza bring a shift in time, place or tone? |
| Enjambment | A sentence runs over the line break | What word is delayed, and what does the pause add? |
| End-stopped line | The line ends with punctuation | Does it make the idea feel settled or final? |
| Caesura | A pause inside a line | What is interrupted or isolated? |
| Rhyme scheme | Pattern of end rhymes (ABAB, AABB) | Does rhyme bind ideas or create closure? |
| Iambic pentameter | Ten syllables, five unstressed-stressed pairs | Where does the rhythm break, and why there? |
| Free verse | No regular rhyme or metre | How are line breaks used instead? |
| Volta | A turn in thought, often late in the poem | What changes after it? |

### Prose

| Term | What it is | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| First-person narrator | "I" tells the story | Can we trust them? Are they looking back? |
| Third-person limited | "She/he", close to one character | Whose view is the reader held inside? |
| Omniscient narrator | Knows every character's thoughts | What does the narrator reveal that characters don't know? |
| Free indirect style | Third-person narration takes on the character's own voice | Whose words are these, really? |
| Pace | Speed of the telling | Where does the writer slow down or skip ahead? |
| Foreshadowing | A hint of what will happen | What does the reader expect that the character doesn't? |

### Drama

| Term | What it is | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Stage directions | Set, props, movement, pauses | What do they show that no one says? |
| Subtext | Meaning under the spoken words | What are the characters avoiding? |
| Dramatic irony | The audience knows more than a character | How does the gap change our response? |
| Soliloquy / aside | Speech to the audience, unheard by others | What private truth is revealed? |

## Time and space on an unseen passage

- Use what the text shows: period details (a telegram, a gas lamp, a mobile phone), place, social
  roles and the way people speak.
- Say how the setting shapes the meaning: a missed bus at night means more in a world without easy
  help.
- Keep claims about context modest. "The detail suggests a small town" is safe; a date for the
  passage is a guess unless the paper gives one.

## Must-know distinctions

| Don't confuse | With |
|---|---|
| The **speaker** of a poem | The **poet** who wrote it |
| The **narrator** | The **author** |
| **Tone**: the attitude of the voice (bitter, tender, ironic) | **Mood**: the atmosphere the reader feels (tense, calm) |
| **Subject**: what the passage is about (a missed bus) | **Theme**: the idea it explores (pride, dependence) |
| **Simile/metaphor**: a comparison | **Symbol**: an object that stands for a wider idea through the whole text |
| **Analysis**: why a feature matters | **Description**: what happens, or what a feature is called |
| **Answering the guiding question** | **General commentary** on everything you noticed |

## Worked reminder: from label to analysis

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- prose fiction extract
>
> I was nine when the river froze. Everyone says that winter was the coldest, but everyone says
> that about the winter they were nine. What I remember is my brother's boots on the ice, the sound
> under them like a door closing somewhere far off, and my own voice, calling him back, much
> smaller than I meant it to be.

**Guiding question:** How does the writer present the power of memory?

| Level | Example sentence |
|---|---|
| Label only | The writer uses a simile. |
| Label + quotation | The writer uses a simile, "like a door closing somewhere far off". |
| Analysis | The sound of the ice is "like a door closing somewhere far off", so a child's memory of a noise already carries an adult's sense of loss, as if something in the family was shutting. |

The third sentence names no device at all and still earns more, because it explains the effect
and links it to memory.

Two more points worth making: the adult narrator admits that everyone exaggerates "the winter they
were nine", which makes memory seem unreliable just before the vivid details prove how strongly it
holds; and "much smaller than I meant it to be" shows the child's helplessness through the adult's
hindsight.

## Quick self-test

Questions 6–8 and 11 use this short poem.

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- poem
>
> *Harbour, March*
>
> The boats lie tilted on the mud,
> each one leaning on its shadow.
> The gulls complain. The tide is out
> the way a debt is out: it will be back.

1. What is the difference between a speaker and a poet?
2. In the river extract above, what kind of narrator is used, and from what point in time?
3. Quote the sentence from the river extract that makes the reader question memory.
4. Define free indirect style in one sentence.
5. What is the difference between tone and mood?
6. Give one example of personification in *Harbour, March*.
7. Which line of *Harbour, March* is enjambed, and what does the run-on do?
8. Name the technique in "the way a debt is out" and explain its effect.
9. A student writes "The poet uses personification." Name two things the sentence still needs.
10. Why should a body paragraph be planned by idea rather than by device?
11. Where is the caesura in line 3 of *Harbour, March*, and what does it do?
12. What two things should a Paper 1 conclusion avoid?

### Answers

1. The speaker is the voice inside the poem; the poet is the real person who wrote it. They may
   differ, so write "the speaker".
2. A first-person narrator looking back as an adult on being nine: retrospective narration.
3. "Everyone says that winter was the coldest, but everyone says that about the winter they were
   nine."
4. Third-person narration that takes on the character's own thoughts and way of speaking, without
   quotation marks.
5. Tone is the attitude of the voice; mood is the atmosphere the reader experiences.
6. "The gulls complain" (or the boats "leaning on its shadow").
7. Line 3 ("The tide is out"). It pushes the reader over the line break before the comparison
   arrives, so "out" seems simple until the last line changes its meaning.
8. A simile. Comparing the tide to a debt makes the calm, empty harbour feel temporary and
   threatening: what has gone must return, and it is owed.
9. A quotation as evidence, and the effect of the feature linked to the guiding question.
10. Planning by idea keeps each paragraph answering the guiding question; planning by device
    produces a list of techniques with no argument.
11. In line 3, after "The gulls complain." The full stop isolates the gulls' noise as a short
    sentence and splits the line, so the second half turns to the tide and the real subject.
12. New evidence, and a repeat of each paragraph in turn.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Summary instead of analysis.** Retelling the passage in order, with a device named now and
  then.
- **Drifting from the guiding question.** Strong observations that answer a different question.
- **No thesis.** An introduction that only says what the passage is about.
- **Labels with no effect.** "Enjambment is used in line 3" and nothing more.
- **Speaker and poet confused.** Treating the "I" of a poem as the poet's own life.
- **Invented context.** Guessing at the writer's biography instead of working from the text.
- **Form ignored.** Discussing only word choice in a poem, never line breaks or stanzas; or
  ignoring stage directions in drama.
- **Quotations dropped in.** A long quotation on its own line, then a separate sentence about it.
- **Unfinished answer.** No conclusion because planning or timing slipped.
