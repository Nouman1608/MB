## 1.3.1 (1): The market

**Mass markets** sell a standardised product to a large number of customers, so unit costs can be low but competition is intense. **Niche markets** serve a small, specialised group, so volumes are lower but customers may accept higher prices and rivals are fewer. Characteristics to compare: customer numbers, price levels, competition, and how easily the firm can be replaced.

**Market size** is the total sales (by value or volume) of all firms. **Market share** is one firm's slice of it:

```
market share (%) = firm's sales / total market sales x 100
market growth (%) = change in market size / original size x 100
```

**Worked example.** Mirabeau Kites sells £1.35m in a £9.0m market, so its share is 1.35 / 9.0 x 100 = **15%**. The market grows 20% to £10.8m. Mirabeau's sales grow only 12% to £1.512m. New share = 1.512 / 10.8 x 100 = **14%**. Sales rose, yet share fell. A market can grow while a firm loses ground.

**Brands** are names, symbols or images that identify a product and set it apart. A strong brand can support a higher price, build loyalty and make customers less willing to switch, which links directly to PED below.

**Dynamic markets** keep changing. Online retailing widens customer choice and lets small firms reach buyers directly. Markets change through new technology, shifting tastes, new entrants and changes in income. **Innovation** can create growth by opening new uses or customers. **Adapting to change** means monitoring the market, updating products and channels, and being flexible about costs.

**Competition** affects the market through prices (rivals constrain a firm's ability to raise price), quality and service (firms compete on more than price), and profit (intense competition squeezes margins). Fewer rivals give a firm more pricing power.

**Risk and uncertainty.** A **risk** can be measured because there is data to estimate its likelihood, such as seasonal sales that Mirabeau has recorded for five years. **Uncertainty** cannot be measured because there is no reliable basis for estimating the outcome, such as an unforeseen invention that replaces kites as a hobby. Research reduces risk but cannot remove uncertainty.

## 1.3.1 (2): Market research

Research is used to **identify and anticipate customer needs and wants**, **quantify likely demand** and **gain insight into consumer behaviour**.

* **Primary research** is collected first-hand for the firm's own purpose. It is specific and up to date but costly and slow.
* **Secondary research** already exists. It is cheaper and quicker but may be dated or not fit the question.
* **Quantitative** data is numerical (how many, how much). **Qualitative** data is about opinions, attitudes and reasons (why).

| Primary methods | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Surveys/questionnaires | Reach many people cheaply; results are easily quantified |
| Focus groups/consumer panels | Explore attitudes in depth; small groups may not be representative |
| Face-to-face/telephone interviews | Probe answers; interviewer time is costly |
| Product trials/test marketing | Test real purchasing behaviour before a full launch |

**Secondary sources:** websites and social media; newspapers, magazines, TV and radio; reports; databases.

**Sampling.** A sample is a subset of the target population. Three methods are listed:

* **Random:** every member has an equal chance of selection. It limits bias but may miss small groups.
* **Quota:** interviewers fill set numbers from chosen groups (such as 40 under-30s). It is quick, but because choice within each quota is not random, bias is possible.
* **Stratified:** split the population into groups, then draw randomly from each in proportion to its size.

**Worked example.** Oakhampton Preserves has 2,400 customers on file: 480 under 30, 1,200 aged 30-54, 720 aged 55 or over. For a stratified sample of 100: under 30 = 480 / 2,400 x 100 = **20**; 30-54 = 1,200 / 2,400 x 100 = **50**; 55+ = 720 / 2,400 x 100 = **30**. These total 100, and each group is then picked at random.

## 1.3.1 (3): Market positioning

**Product orientation** starts from what the firm can make, then looks for buyers. **Market orientation** starts from what customers want, using research, then designs the product. Product orientation can work for genuinely new inventions; market orientation reduces the risk of making something nobody wants.

**Market mapping** plots competitors on two axes (for example price and quality). It shows where rivals cluster and where **gaps** appear. A gap is not automatically an opportunity, because there may be no demand there.

**Market segmentation** divides a market into groups with similar characteristics or needs, such as age, income, location, lifestyle or behaviour. Segmentation lets the firm target a group precisely, at the cost of extra research and product variants.

**Competitive advantage** is something that lets a firm outperform rivals persistently, such as lower costs, a stronger brand, better service or a unique product. **Product differentiation** makes a product distinct from similar ones. Its purpose is to reduce the pull of rivals' prices, build loyalty, and support a higher price (a less price-elastic demand). **Adding value** means increasing the gap between the cost of bought-in inputs and the selling price, through design, service, convenience, branding or finishing.

## 1.3.2: Demand and supply

**Demand** is the quantity customers are willing and able to buy at each price. Price changes cause movement along the curve. The listed factors shift the whole curve:

* prices of **substitutes** (a rise makes the firm's product relatively cheaper, so demand rises) and **complements** (a rise in the price of one lowers demand for the other)
* **consumer incomes**
* **fashions, tastes and preferences**
* **marketing, advertising and branding**
* **demographics** (such as an ageing population)
* **external shocks** (such as a health scare or a sudden shortage)
* **seasonality**

**Supply** is the quantity firms will offer at each price. The listed shifters are changes in **costs of production**, **new technology**, **indirect taxes** (which raise costs and reduce supply), **government subsidies** (which lower costs and increase supply) and **external shocks**.

**Interaction.** The **equilibrium price** is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Above it there is a surplus, which pushes price down. Below it there is a shortage, which pushes price up.

**Worked example (invented linear figures).** In the market for city bicycles, Qd = 800 - 40P and Qs = 200 + 20P, with P in £. Setting them equal: 600 = 60P, so P = **£10** and Q = **400**. Draw demand sloping down and supply sloping up, crossing at (400, £10).

* A rival's price rises, so demand becomes Qd = 920 - 40P. Then 720 = 60P, so P = **£12**, Q = **440**. The demand curve shifts right; price and quantity both rise.
* Instead, a cost rise shifts supply to Qs = 80 + 20P. Then 720 = 60P, so P = **£12**, Q = **320**. The supply curve shifts left; price rises, quantity falls.

When interpreting a diagram, say what moved, which way, the new equilibrium, and what that means for the business.

## 1.3.2: Price elasticity of demand (PED)

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price
```

PED is normally negative, because price and quantity move in opposite directions. Interpret the size, ignoring the sign.

| Value (ignoring sign) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Greater than 1 | Elastic: quantity changes proportionally more than price |
| Less than 1 | Inelastic: quantity changes proportionally less than price |
| Equal to 1 | Unitary |

**Influences on PED:** availability and closeness of substitutes; whether the good is a necessity or a luxury; the share of income spent on it; brand loyalty and differentiation; the time period (demand usually becomes more elastic over time).

**Worked example (elastic).** Rusholme Cycles raises a model from £400 to £440 (+10%) and sales fall from 1,500 to 1,200 (-20%). PED = -20 / 10 = **-2.0**, elastic. Revenue falls from 400 x 1,500 = **£600,000** to 440 x 1,200 = **£528,000**.

**Worked example (inelastic).** Oakhampton raises a jar from £4.00 to £4.40 (+10%) and sales fall from 30,000 to 28,500 (-5%). PED = -5 / 10 = **-0.5**. Revenue rises from **£120,000** to **£125,400**.

| Demand is | Price rise | Price fall |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | Total revenue falls | Total revenue rises |
| Inelastic | Total revenue rises | Total revenue falls |

**Significance for pricing.** A firm with inelastic demand can raise price to lift revenue, which is why it works to differentiate and build its brand. A firm facing elastic demand may gain by cutting price, but must check what happens to profit, because costs rise with volume and rivals may respond.

## 1.3.2: Income elasticity of demand (YED)

```
YED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income
```

Here the sign matters. A **normal good** has positive YED (demand rises with income); if YED is above 1, it is income elastic (often a luxury), and between 0 and 1 it is income inelastic. An **inferior good** has negative YED (demand falls as income rises).

**Worked example.** Average income rises from £30,000 to £31,500 (+5%). Rusholme's premium bikes rise from 2,000 to 2,200 (+10%): YED = **+2.0**. Oakhampton's budget jam falls from 10,000 to 9,600 (-4%): YED = -4 / 5 = **-0.8**, so it is inferior.

**Influences:** how much of a necessity or luxury the good is, its price level relative to alternatives, and how quickly consumers upgrade. **Significance:** firms selling high-YED goods gain most in a boom and lose most in a recession, so they may add lower-priced lines or spread risk across products. A firm selling inferior goods may benefit when incomes fall.

## Common errors

* Confusing market size with market share.
* Dropping the minus sign on YED, or reading PED's size as its sign.
* Concluding on revenue when the question asks about profit.
* Treating a gap on a market map as a guaranteed opportunity.
* Confusing a movement along a curve (price change) with a shift (other factors).
* Using "risk" and "uncertainty" as synonyms.
