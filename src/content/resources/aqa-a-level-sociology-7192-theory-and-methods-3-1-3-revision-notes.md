These notes condense the research-methods side of AQA A-level Sociology (7192) section 3.1.3 Theory
and Methods, assessed by 10 marks of extended writing at the end of Paper 1. The same eleven-point
list reappears as 3.3.2 in Paper 3; the theory side is condensed in the

## Condensed recall

**Research design sequence:** topic → literature review → hypothesis or research question →
operationalisation → sampling → pilot study → data collection → analysis → report.

- **Hypothesis:** testable prediction of a relationship between variables (positivist starting point).
- **Research question:** open question letting meanings emerge (interpretivist starting point).
- **Operationalisation:** turning a concept into measurable indicators; different indicators can
  give different results.
- **Pilot study:** small trial run to test wording, timing and coding.

**Sampling:** research population → sampling frame → sample. A faulty frame defeats even a random
selection. Random, systematic and stratified random sampling aim at representativeness; quota,
snowball and opportunity sampling trade representativeness for speed or access.

**Methods: the one-line case for and against**

| Method | Best argument for | Best argument against |
|---|---|---|
| Closed questionnaire | Large, reliable, comparable data | Imposes the researcher's categories |
| Open questionnaire | Respondents' own words | Hard to code and compare |
| Structured interview | Standardised and quick to analyse | Interviewer effects on a fixed script |
| Semi-structured interview | Comparable core plus follow-up probing | Probes differ between interviews |
| Unstructured interview | Rapport and depth (Oakley, 1981) | Small samples; hard to replicate |
| Group interview | Shows how views form in interaction | Dominant voices; peer pressure |
| Overt participant observation | Ethical; informant can open doors (Whyte, 1943) | Hawthorne effect |
| Covert participant observation | Natural behaviour | Deception; recording problems (Patrick, 1973) |
| Laboratory experiment | Control; cause and effect | Artificial; narrow scope |
| Field experiment | Natural setting (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968) | Less control; consent problems |
| Content analysis of documents | Turns media output into numbers (*Bad News*, 1976) | Coding involves interpretation |
| Official statistics | Cheap, national, trends over time | Soft statistics are socially constructed |

**Interview interaction:** Labov's 1960s research showed a child silent in a formal interview but
fluent in a relaxed one, so the interview situation shapes the data.

**PET:**
- *Practical:* time, cost, funding, access, gatekeepers, researcher characteristics, skills.
- *Ethical:* informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, harm, vulnerable groups, deception.
- *Theoretical:* reliability, validity, representativeness, perspective.

**Social facts:** Durkheim, "treat social facts as things". Interpretivists reply that rates are
produced by acts of definition and recording.

**Theory and methods:** perspective shapes method, but practical factors and triangulation
(Willis, 1977) blur the link.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Research population | The whole group the study is about |
| Sampling frame | The list from which the sample is drawn |
| Stratified random sample | Random sampling within sub-groups, in proportion |
| Quota sample | Set numbers per category, chosen by the interviewer |
| Snowball sample | Participants recruit further participants |
| Representativeness | Sample shares the characteristics of the population |
| Generalisability | Findings can be applied beyond the sample |
| Response rate | Share of those approached who take part |
| Interviewer bias | Interviewer's characteristics or manner shape answers |
| Social desirability | Giving the answer that seems acceptable |
| Hawthorne effect | Behaviour changes because people know they are studied |
| Going native | Over-identifying with the group observed |
| Gatekeeper | Person who controls access to a group or setting |
| Content analysis | Systematic counting of themes in documents |
| Hard and soft statistics | Fairly complete counts versus counts shaped by reporting and recording |
| Triangulation | Checking findings with a second method |

## Exam traps

- **Wrong question type.** The Paper 1 Theory and Methods question does not need an education
  context. The 20-mark Methods in Context question does.
- **Reliability versus validity.** Reliability is about replication; validity is about truth.
  Unstructured interviews are usually more valid but less reliable, not "both".
- **Overt and covert are not participant and non-participant.** They are two separate choices.
- **Quota is not random.** Interviewers choose whom to approach.
- **"Cheap" is not an evaluation.** Say cheap compared with what, and why it matters for this
  research aim.
- **One point, developed.** In an "outline and explain two" answer, a second reason that repeats the
  first in new words earns little.
- **Name studies accurately.** Whyte was overt; Patrick and Humphreys were covert.

## Self-test

1. What is the difference between a sampling frame and a sample?
2. Why does a pilot study improve validity?
3. Give one reason why interpretivists prefer research questions to hypotheses.
4. What is operationalisation, and why can it change results?
5. Which sampling technique suits a hidden group, and what is its weakness?
6. Name the effect that occurs when people know they are being observed.
7. What did Labov's research show about interviews?
8. Give one ethical problem with Rosenthal and Jacobson's field experiment.
9. Distinguish hard from soft official statistics.
10. What does content analysis do to documents?
11. Name the four ethical principles stressed in BSA guidance.
12. Give an example of triangulation in a single study.

### Answers

1. The frame is the list of the population; the sample is the subset actually selected from it.
2. It shows whether respondents understand the questions as intended, so data measure what the
   researcher thinks they measure.
3. An open question lets participants' meanings emerge instead of testing the researcher's
   assumptions.
4. Turning a concept into measurable indicators; a different indicator (income rather than
   occupation for class) can classify people differently.
5. Snowball sampling; participants share networks, so the sample is unrepresentative.
6. The Hawthorne effect.
7. That the interview situation shapes the data: a child who said little formally spoke fluently in
   a relaxed setting.
8. Teachers were deceived about pupils' ability (no informed consent), and other pupils may have been
   disadvantaged.
9. Hard statistics (births, deaths) are fairly complete counts; soft statistics (recorded crime)
   depend on reporting and recording decisions.
10. Codes and counts themes, turning qualitative material into quantitative data.
11. Informed consent, confidentiality and anonymity, avoiding harm, and care with vulnerable groups.
12. Willis (1977) combined observation, interviews and group discussions.
