Section 10.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Media representations of class,
gender, ethnicity, and age groups*, opens heading 10, *Media representation and effects*, in Paper
4. The syllabus lists four points: how different groups are represented in the media; ways that the
media contributes to gender socialisation; moral panics around class, gender, ethnicity and age
groups; and the relationship between the media and popular culture. Heading 10 links these to
*Socialisation, culture and identity* and *Structure and human agency*.

The [media representation and media effects guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-representation-and-effects/)
gives a table of the main findings for each group. This page explains the concepts behind them,
adds further evidence and shows how to analyse a moral panic. Use it with the
[media representations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-media-representations-revision-notes/)
and the [media representations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-media-representations-practice/).

## Concepts for analysing representation

- **Representation** is the process by which the media construct images of groups, places and
  events. It is always selective: something is shown, something left out.
- **Stereotype**: a simplified, generalised image applied to all members of a group.
- **Under-representation**: a group appears less often than its share of the population.
- **Misrepresentation**: a group appears, but in distorted or narrow roles.
- **Othering**: presenting a group as fundamentally different from "us", often as a problem or a
  threat.
- **Normalisation**: presenting one group's lifestyle as ordinary, so others look like deviations.

Use these terms to move from description ("women are shown as housewives") to analysis ("women are
misrepresented in a narrow range of domestic roles, which normalises a gendered division of
labour").

## How different groups are represented

### Class

- The middle class is often presented as the norm in drama, advertising and news, while the
  working class appears as comic, criminal or dependent.
- **"Poverty porn"** is a term critics applied to documentary series such as *Benefits Street*
  (Channel 4, 2014), which they argued presented people on low incomes as entertainment and
  encouraged blame rather than understanding.
- Imogen Tyler's *Revolting Subjects* (2013) argued that groups such as the poor, migrants and
  Gypsies and Travellers are made into objects of public disgust ("social abjection"), which helps
  justify harsher policies towards them.
- The wealthy may be shown as aspirational, celebrating consumption rather than questioning
  inequality.

### Gender

- R.W. Connell's concept of **hegemonic masculinity** (developed in *Gender and Power*, 1987)
  describes the dominant, culturally idealised form of masculinity, associated with strength,
  control and heterosexuality. Media images of action heroes and sports stars often reproduce it.
- The **Bechdel test**, which began in Alison Bechdel's comic strip in 1985, asks whether a film has
  two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man. It is a simple way of
  showing how often women's stories are marginalised.
- Women's bodies are more often sexualised and judged on appearance; older women are particularly
  under-represented.

### Ethnicity

- Stuart Hall's essay "The Whites of Their Eyes" (1981) distinguished **overt racism**, which
  openly expresses racist views, from **inferential racism**, where racist assumptions are built
  into apparently neutral reporting, for example by treating the presence of minorities as itself a
  "problem".
- Elizabeth Poole's *Reporting Islam* (2002) analysed British press coverage of Muslims and found
  them frequently framed in relation to conflict, threat and cultural difference.
- Minorities have been under-represented in positions of authority on screen and in newsrooms,
  though representation has diversified in some media.

### Age

- **Youth** are often represented through risk: crime, drugs, anti-social behaviour, online
  dangers. Positive stories of young people's achievements receive less attention.
- **Older people** are under-represented, or shown through stereotypes of frailty, confusion or
  dependence, with exceptions such as the "active, affluent pensioner" in advertising.
- **Children** may be shown as innocent and vulnerable, which supports both child-protection
  campaigns and moral panics about threats to them.

## How the media contributes to gender socialisation

- **Role models**: repeated images of what men and women do teach norms of masculinity and
  femininity.
- **Advertising**: Jean Kilbourne's film *Killing Us Softly* (first made in 1979) analysed
  advertisements that present idealised, often sexualised images of women and treat their bodies
  as objects.
- **Performativity**: Judith Butler (*Gender Trouble*, 1990) argued that gender is something people
  do repeatedly rather than something they are. Media provide scripts for these performances.
- **Active audiences**: people negotiate and resist media messages, and online media allow users to
  share alternative images of gender.

**Evaluation:** socialisation theories risk treating audiences as passive; postmodern and
interactionist views stress choice, while feminists reply that choice operates within a narrow
range of profitable images.

## Analysing moral panics

Stanley Cohen's concept of the moral panic is covered in the overview page. To analyse a panic in
depth, use Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda's five criteria (*Moral Panics: The Social
Construction of Deviance*, 1994):

| Criterion | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Concern** | Heightened public worry about a group's behaviour |
| **Hostility** | The group is seen as an enemy of society (folk devils) |
| **Consensus** | Widespread agreement that the threat is real and serious |
| **Disproportionality** | The reaction is greater than the actual harm justifies |
| **Volatility** | Panics appear suddenly and may fade just as quickly |

**Moral panics by group (examples to analyse):**
- **Class:** reporting of "welfare scroungers" or "chavs" that blames poverty on individuals.
- **Gender:** concern about lone mothers or about young women's drinking ("ladettes").
- **Ethnicity:** Hall et al. on "mugging" (1978); coverage of asylum seekers as a threat.
- **Age:** the 1980s panic over "video nasties" in the UK, which contributed to the Video
  Recordings Act 1984; later concern about online gaming and social media harming children.

**Critique:** Angela McRobbie and Sarah Thornton (1995) argued that in a multi-mediated world
folk devils can answer back through their own media and supportive groups, and some youth
subcultures welcome hostile coverage as publicity. The classic model may therefore be too simple
for today's media.

## Media and popular culture

- **Mass culture critique:** Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment*
  (1944) described a **culture industry** producing standardised entertainment that keeps audiences
  passive.
- **Cultural capital:** Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) showed that tastes are linked to
  class; preferring "high" culture signals status, so the distinction between high and popular
  culture is itself a form of power.
- **Popular culture as active:** John Fiske (*Understanding Popular Culture*, 1989) argued that
  audiences use popular texts creatively and can make resistant meanings from them.
- **Global popular culture:** the international spread of South Korean pop music shows that
  popular culture no longer flows only from the West, linking 10.1 to globalisation (7.2).

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that the media misrepresent the working class.*

> Tyler (2013) supports the view, arguing that poor people are turned into objects of disgust,
> while critics described *Benefits Street* (2014) as "poverty porn" that invited viewers to blame
> people on low incomes. **[AO1 and AO2]** Such images normalise middle-class lifestyles and
> present poverty as a moral failing, which Marxists would see as legitimating inequality.
> **[AO3: analysis]** However, audiences do not simply accept these images; some viewers of such
> programmes respond with sympathy or criticism, and there are dramas that treat working-class
> lives seriously. **[AO3: counter-argument]** Misrepresentation is common, but its effect
> depends on how audiences read it. **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Listing stereotypes for each group without concepts such as under-representation or othering.
- Writing about gender only as women; masculinity is represented too.
- Using "moral panic" for any media story; check the criteria.
- Ignoring change: representations have diversified in some media, which needs evaluating.
- Treating popular culture as trivial; the syllabus asks how it relates to the media.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: concept, mechanism, example. For instance: "inferential racism" →
  assumptions built into reporting → coverage that treats minorities as a problem.
- **Evaluate** questions: contrast feminist, Marxist and neo-Marxist accounts with pluralist and
  postmodern views of choice and diversity.
- Link forward to 10.2 and 10.3: representations matter because of their possible effects.
