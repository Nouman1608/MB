Quick recall for section 5.3 Factors of production of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1), Issue 2, February 2026, point 5.3.1. Worked cost comparisons, a break-even wage and an annotated evaluation are in the [factors of production study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-factors-of-production/); questions are in the [factors of production practice set](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-factors-of-production-practice/). Figures are invented, in dollars.

## 1. The specification wording

**5.3.1** Changing relationships between enterprise, capital, land, and labour:
- difference between capital-intensive and labour-intensive activities.

## 2. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital, enterprise |
| Land | Natural resources (soil, water, minerals, timber) and the sites a business uses |
| Labour | The physical and mental effort and skills of people at work |
| Capital | Man-made resources used in production: machinery, equipment, vehicles, tools, software, fitted-out buildings |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors, making decisions and taking the risk of running a business |
| Capital-intensive | Using a high proportion of capital relative to labour |
| Labour-intensive | Using a high proportion of labour relative to capital |
| Substitution | Replacing one factor with another, such as machines replacing workers |

Definitions are standard teaching; the specification names the factors without defining them.

## 3. How the factors relate

- They work **together**: every product needs some of each.
- They can partly **replace** one another: the same output can come from different mixes.
- The **best mix changes** when costs, technology, skills, scale or customer expectations change.
- Enterprise carries the risk: a move towards capital usually raises fixed costs and so raises the owner's risk.

## 4. Drivers of change

| Driver | Usual direction |
|---|---|
| New technology | Towards capital |
| Wages rising faster than machine costs | Towards capital |
| Shortage of suitable workers | Towards capital |
| Business growth (higher output) | Towards capital, as fixed costs are spread |
| Expensive land | Use land more intensively or relocate; online selling reduces need for shop sites |
| Customers valuing personal or handmade products | Keeps labour central |
| Low wages relative to machinery | Towards labour |

## 5. Capital-intensive versus labour-intensive

| | Capital-intensive | Labour-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Biggest cost | Machinery and technology | Wages |
| Typical activity | Bottling, car assembly, oil refining | Hairdressing, care work, tailoring, restaurants |
| Output | High volume, standardised | Lower volume, varied or personal |
| Cost per unit | Low at high output | Changes little with output |
| Flexibility | Low | High |
| Risk | High fixed costs | Wage rises and staff shortages |

**Test:** compare cost shares (cost ÷ total cost × 100). Do not decide from the size of the business.

## 6. Worked reminders

**Cost shares.** A firm's total costs are 160,000, with wages 96,000 and equipment costs 24,000. Wages = 96,000 ÷ 160,000 × 100 = **60%**; equipment = 24,000 ÷ 160,000 × 100 = **15%**. Labour-intensive.

**Comparing methods.** Method A: 6 workers at 22,000 each = **132,000** a year. Method B: a machine costing 30,000 a year plus 2 operators at 24,000 each = 30,000 + 48,000 = **78,000** a year. Saving from Method B = **54,000**.

**Wage at which the methods cost the same.** Method B costs 78,000. With 6 workers, Method A costs the same when each earns 78,000 ÷ 6 = **13,000**. Below that wage, Method A is cheaper.

## 7. Exam traps

- **Capital is not money** in this topic: it is equipment and other man-made resources.
- Calling a business capital-intensive because it is large.
- Forgetting that land includes natural resources.
- Ignoring enterprise and the change in risk when the mix changes.
- Recommending machinery on cost alone: also weigh quality, flexibility, risk and the effect on workers.
- Writing that labour-intensive firms are "inefficient": they may suit the product and the customers.

## 8. Answer frames

- **Explain** why a firm becomes more capital-intensive: driver (wages rise) → response (buys machinery) → effect (lower cost per unit but higher fixed costs).
- **Analyse** the effect on a workforce: fewer jobs → retraining for machine roles → possible fall in morale.
- **Evaluate** switching methods: cost comparison → quality and flexibility → risk to the owner → judgement depending on demand or wages.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four factors of production.
2. Define capital in this topic.
3. Which factor takes the risk of running the business?
4. Define labour-intensive.
5. Give one example of a capital-intensive activity.
6. Give one reason a business might become more capital-intensive.
7. A firm's costs are 250,000 and its wages are 50,000. Calculate wages as a percentage of costs.
8. Give one disadvantage of capital-intensive production.
9. Why do labour-intensive businesses usually find it easier to adjust to a fall in demand?
10. Give one example of land other than premises.
11. A machine costing 20,000 a year plus one operator at 25,000 replaces 3 workers at 18,000 each. Calculate the yearly saving.
12. How does a move towards capital affect the entrepreneur?

### Answers

1. Land, labour, capital, enterprise.
2. Man-made resources used to produce goods and services, such as machinery, equipment, vehicles and software.
3. Enterprise.
4. Using a high proportion of labour relative to capital, so wages are a large share of costs.
5. Any one of: bottling, car assembly, oil refining, automated printing.
6. Any one of: new technology; rising wages; shortage of workers; business growth.
7. 50,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = 20%.
8. Any one of: high fixed costs; large investment; less flexible; a fault affects all output.
9. Wages can be cut more easily, for example by reducing hours, whereas machinery costs continue.
10. Any one of: soil, water, minerals, timber, fish.
11. Workers cost 3 × 18,000 = 54,000; machine method costs 20,000 + 25,000 = 45,000; saving = 9,000.
12. They commit more money up front and face more fixed costs, so they carry more risk.
