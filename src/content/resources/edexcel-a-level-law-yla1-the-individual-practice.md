## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between libel and slander. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights is described as a qualified right. **[3]**

**3.** State **two** steps an applicant must take before the European Court of Human Rights will consider the merits of their case. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the right given by the Freedom of Information Act 2000. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by saying that trespass to land is "actionable per se". **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A local blogger, Imran, posts that the owner of a family restaurant "serves rotten meat and should be shut down". The claim is untrue. The restaurant's bookings fall sharply.

Advise the owner on whether she can bring a claim in defamation and which defences Imran might raise. **[8]**

**7.** A celebrity is photographed through the window of a private clinic receiving treatment. A newspaper plans to publish the photographs.

Explain how the courts would decide whether publication should be stopped. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the effect on the UK government of a successful application to the European Court of Human Rights, using **two** examples. **[6]**

**9.** Gareth is an electrician called to repair wiring at a shopping centre. He is injured by a live wire he was repairing. Later the same day, Priya, 7, wanders away from her parents into a staff-only area and is injured by a falling shelf that staff knew was loose.

Advise the shopping centre on its liability to Gareth and to Priya. **[10]**

**10.** Evaluate whether the law strikes the right balance between protecting privacy and protecting freedom of expression. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **Libel** is defamation in a **permanent form**, such as writing, a broadcast or an online post [1]. **Slander** is defamation in a **transient form**, such as speech, and usually requires proof of financial loss [1].

**2.** A qualified right may be **lawfully restricted** by the state [1] if the restriction is **prescribed by law** and pursues a **legitimate aim** listed in Article 10(2), such as protecting the reputation or rights of others or preventing disorder [1], and is **necessary in a democratic society**, meaning proportionate to that aim [1].

**3.** Any two [1] each: **exhaust all domestic remedies**; apply **within four months** of the final domestic decision; show the application is **admissible** (for example, that the applicant is a victim of a violation by a Convention state).

**4.** Any person may make a **request for recorded information** held by a **public authority** [1]. The authority must say whether it holds the information and, if so, provide it, generally **within 20 working days** [1], unless an **exemption** applies, such as national security or information that is someone else's personal data [1].

**5.** The claimant can sue **without proving any damage or loss** [1]; the interference with their possession of land is itself the wrong [1].

**6.** **Elements**: the statement is **defamatory**, lowering the owner in the estimation of right-thinking people (*Sim v Stretch*) [1]; it **refers** to her, through her restaurant [1]; it was **published** online to readers [1]. As a blog post, it is **libel** [1]. **Serious harm (s 1)**: she must prove serious harm to her reputation; as the restaurant is a business trading for profit, a claim in its name would need **serious financial loss**, which the fall in bookings may show (*Lachaux*) [1] [1]. **Defences**: **truth (s 2)** fails because the claim is untrue [1]; **honest opinion (s 3)** is unlikely because "serves rotten meat" is a statement of fact, and **public interest (s 4)** fails unless Imran reasonably believed publication was in the public interest, which is hard where he did not check his facts [1].

**7.** The celebrity would sue for **misuse of private information**, seeking an **interim injunction** [1]. The court asks first whether she had a **reasonable expectation of privacy**: medical treatment in a private clinic is clearly private (*Campbell v MGN*) [1] [1]. It then **balances Article 8 against Article 10**, considering whether there is a genuine **public interest** in publication rather than mere public curiosity [1] [1]. Where there is no public interest, privacy is likely to prevail and an injunction may be granted (*PJS v News Group*) [1].

**8.** A judgment **binds the UK in international law**, and the Committee of Ministers supervises compliance [1], but it does **not automatically change UK law**: Parliament must act [1]. Examples, 2 marks each: *Malone v UK* [1984] led to the **Interception of Communications Act 1985** [1] [1]; *Goodwin v UK* [2002] led to the **Gender Recognition Act 2004** [1] [1]. Also credit *Sunday Times v UK* and the Contempt of Court Act 1981, or *Hirst v UK (No 2)* as an example of slow and limited compliance.

**9.** **Gareth** is a **lawful visitor**, so the **OLA 1957** applies [1]. The centre owes the common duty of care (s 2(2)) [1], but under **s 2(3)(b)** it may expect a person in the exercise of his calling to **appreciate and guard against special risks** ordinarily incident to it [1]. A live wire is exactly the risk an electrician guards against, as in *Roles v Nathan* [1963] [1], so the centre is probably not liable. **Priya** entered a staff-only area, so she is probably a **trespasser** and the **OLA 1984** applies [1]. A duty arises because staff **knew of the danger** and could reasonably have expected someone to come near it in a busy public building [1], and it is a risk the occupier could reasonably be expected to protect against (s 1(3)) [1]. The occupier must be prepared for children to be less careful [1]. Securing the shelf or locking the area was easy, so breach is likely, though the court may consider whether her parents should have been supervising her (*Phipps*) [1]. **Conclusion**: likely liable to Priya for her personal injury, not to Gareth [1].

**10.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: Article 8 and Article 10; misuse of private information; reasonable expectation of privacy; balancing; Data Protection Act 2018 and s 170; Freedom of Information Act 2000.
- **Privacy well protected**: *Campbell*, *Mosley* and *PJS* show the courts protecting private life where there is no real public interest; injunctions can stop publication before harm.
- **Expression well protected**: the public interest defence and Article 10's weight in the balance; *Handyside* principle; FOIA promotes open government.
- **Concerns**: law built case by case by judges rather than by Parliament; injunctions can be undermined by online publication abroad; cost of litigation means only wealthy claimants can protect privacy; the press argue the balance chills investigative journalism.
- **Evaluation**: a reasoned conclusion, for example that the balancing test is sound in principle but access to remedies is uneven.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing the serious harm requirement or the special rule for businesses.
- Treating a factual allegation as honest opinion.
- Saying a Strasbourg ruling directly changes UK law.
- Applying the 1957 Act to a child in a prohibited area without asking whether she became a trespasser.
- Listing privacy cases without the Article 8 versus Article 10 balance.
