These notes condense key question 1.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What types of data and
evidence do sociologists use?* In brief: data is either **primary** (collected by the researcher) or
**secondary** (already existing), and either **quantitative** (numbers) or **qualitative** (words and
meanings). The four named kinds of secondary evidence are official statistics, historical and personal
documents, digital sources and media content. You must also be able to read and criticise summaries,
diagrams, tables, charts and graphs.

Full explanations and a worked table are in the
[data and evidence study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-sociology-0495-kq-1-2/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Primary data** | Data collected first-hand by the researcher for the current study |
| **Secondary data** | Data that already exists, produced by others for different purposes |
| **Quantitative data** | Numerical data that can be counted, measured and compared |
| **Qualitative data** | Non-numerical data in words, images or descriptions, rich in meaning |
| **Official statistics** | Numerical data produced by governments and their agencies |
| **Census** | A government count of the whole population at a fixed date |
| **Personal documents** | Diaries, letters, autobiographies and photos created by individuals |
| **Historical documents** | Records from the past such as parish registers and archives |
| **Digital sources** | Online material such as websites, social media posts and forums |
| **Content analysis** | Systematic counting or categorising of media content |
| **Authenticity** | Whether a document is genuine |
| **Credibility** | Whether a document is sincere and accurate |
| **Dark figure of crime** | Crime that is never reported or recorded |
| **Socially constructed statistics** | Figures shaped by the decisions of people who record them |

## Recall grid: the four kinds of secondary evidence

| Evidence | Example | Main strength | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Official statistics | Census, recorded crime, exam results | Large scale; regular, so trends visible | Only counts what the state chooses; definitions change; may be socially constructed |
| Historical and personal documents | Parish registers, diaries, letters | Only route to the past; personal meaning | Authenticity, credibility, representativeness |
| Digital sources | Social media posts, forums, blogs | Huge, current, naturally produced | Hard to verify; users unrepresentative; consent and privacy |
| Media content | News reports, adverts, films | Shows how groups are represented | Reflects producers' agenda; audiences interpret differently |

## Named studies worth using

- **Atkinson, *Discovering Suicide* (1978):** coroners' decisions shape suicide statistics, so the
  figures reflect interpretation as well as behaviour.
- **Laslett, *The World We Have Lost* (1965):** parish records suggested pre-industrial English
  households were mostly small.
- **Thomas and Znaniecki (1918–1920):** letters of Polish migrants used to study migration and
  adjustment.
- **Glasgow University Media Group, *Bad News* (1976):** content analysis of television news on
  industrial disputes.

## Primary versus secondary: quick comparison

- Primary fits the aims exactly but costs time and money and may face access problems.
- Secondary is cheap and wide-ranging but may not measure what the researcher needs.
- Both can be quantitative or qualitative — learn the four-box grid (own questionnaire; own
  interviews; census; diaries).

## How to read a source in Question 1(a)

1. Read title, labels, units, dates, producer and sample size.
2. Quote the exact figure asked for, with its unit.
3. Describe the pattern: highest, lowest, trend, gap.
4. Explain one problem with the evidence and its effect.

**Problems to look for:** small or unclear sample; one place only; old data; volunteers or online
only; self-reported behaviour; unclear definitions; percentages without totals; correlation shown but
cause claimed.

## Exam traps

- "Secondary data is qualitative" — false; a census is secondary and quantitative.
- Writing "statistics are unreliable" with no reason.
- Missing the unit: "per 1,000" and "%" are not the same.
- Copying the whole table instead of selecting one figure.
- Treating a huge digital data set as representative — size does not fix who is missing.
- Forgetting that documents can be qualitative **and** historical **and** personal at the same time;
  choose the label the question asks for.

## Worked example: a 4-mark "explain" answer

*Explain **two** limitations of using official crime statistics.* (practice)

- **Under-reporting (2):** many victims do not report crimes, for example because they think the
  police will not act, so the statistics show only part of real crime — the dark figure is hidden.
- **Recording practices (2):** police decide whether an incident counts as a crime and under which
  category, and these rules change, so a rise in figures may reflect recording decisions rather than
  more crime.

Each point names the limitation and explains its effect on what the statistics show.

## Self-test

1. Define secondary data.
2. Give one example of primary qualitative data.
3. Give one example of secondary quantitative data.
4. State two strengths of official statistics.
5. What is the dark figure of crime?
6. What did Atkinson argue about suicide statistics?
7. Explain what authenticity means when judging a document.
8. Give one ethical problem with using social media posts as data.
9. What is content analysis?
10. A table says "rate per 1,000 people". Why must you not write the figure as a percentage?
11. Give two questions you should ask about any source before using its figures.
12. Why might an online survey give an unrepresentative picture of older people?

## Answers

1. Data that already exists, produced by someone else for another purpose.
2. Transcripts of the researcher's own unstructured interviews (or field notes from observation).
3. Census figures, recorded crime figures or exam results.
4. Any two: large scale; collected regularly so trends can be tracked; free; allow comparison
   between areas.
5. Crime that happens but is never reported to or recorded by the police.
6. That they depend on coroners' interpretations of deaths, so they are socially constructed.
7. Whether the document is genuine — really produced by the person and at the time claimed.
8. Users have not given informed consent; their privacy may be breached.
9. A method that systematically counts or categorises words, images or themes in media.
10. A rate per 1,000 is a different unit; 15 per 1,000 equals 1.5%, so quoting it as 15% would be
    wrong.
11. Any two: how big was the sample; how were people chosen; when was it collected; who produced it;
    how were terms defined.
12. Older people are less likely to use the internet, so they would be under-represented.
