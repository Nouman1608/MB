This guide goes deep on sub-topic **2(b) Mains electricity**, statements
2.2 to 2.6 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Physics (4PH1),
Issue 4. None of the statements carries a "P", so all can be assessed on
both papers. For the whole of Topic 2 in overview, see the
[Electricity study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-electricity/).

## What the specification asks

- **2.2** understand how insulation, double insulation, earthing, fuses
  and circuit breakers protect the device or user in a range of domestic
  appliances
- **2.3** understand why a current in a resistor results in the electrical
  transfer of energy and an increase in temperature, and how this can be
  used in a variety of domestic contexts
- **2.4** know and use power = current × voltage (P = I × V) and apply it
  to the selection of appropriate fuses
- **2.5** use energy transferred = current × voltage × time (E = I × V × t)
- **2.6** know the difference between mains electricity being alternating
  current (a.c.) and direct current (d.c.) being supplied by a cell or
  battery

## How appliances are connected

An appliance is connected to the mains through a cable. The **live wire**
carries the alternating voltage; the **neutral wire** completes the
circuit and stays close to zero volts. Many appliances with a metal case
also have an **earth wire**, which connects the case to the ground. In a
working appliance, current flows in through the live wire and back
through the neutral; the earth wire carries no current.

The danger from mains electricity is a large current passing through a
person, for example if the live wire touches something the person holds.
Each safety feature in 2.2 deals with this in a different way.

## The five safety features

**Insulation.** Each wire is covered in plastic, an electrical insulator,
so the live wire cannot touch the user, another wire or the case. Cracked
or worn insulation exposes the conductor and is a serious hazard.

**Double insulation.** The appliance has a casing made of an insulating
material, such as plastic, as well as insulated wires, so no metal part
that a user can touch could become live. A double-insulated appliance,
such as many hairdryers and food mixers, does not need an earth wire. It
carries a symbol of a square inside a square.

**Earthing.** A metal case is connected to the earth wire. If a fault lets
the live wire touch the case, a large current flows from the live wire
through the case and down the low-resistance earth wire to the ground,
rather than through a person touching the case. This large current also
blows the fuse or trips the circuit breaker, disconnecting the supply.

**Fuses.** A fuse is a short, thin piece of wire in a holder, placed in
the live side of the circuit. If the current becomes larger than the fuse
rating, the fuse wire heats up and melts, breaking the circuit. This stops
the current before the cable overheats and possibly starts a fire. A blown
fuse must be replaced.

**Circuit breakers.** A circuit breaker is an automatic switch in the live
side of the circuit. When the current exceeds a set value, it opens
(trips) and breaks the circuit. Unlike a fuse it can be reset once the
fault is fixed, and it generally acts faster than a fuse.

Fuses and circuit breakers belong in the **live** side so that, once they
break the circuit, the appliance is disconnected from the high voltage.
A fuse in the neutral would stop the current but leave the appliance
connected to the live wire.

| Feature | Protects | How |
|---|---|---|
| insulation | user | prevents contact with conductors |
| double insulation | user | no touchable metal part can become live |
| earthing | user | fault current flows to earth, then fuse blows |
| fuse | appliance and wiring (and user, with earthing) | melts when current exceeds rating |
| circuit breaker | appliance and wiring (and user, with earthing) | switch trips when current is too large; can be reset |

## Why a current heats a resistor (2.3)

In a metal, current is a flow of electrons. As the electrons move through
a resistor they collide with the vibrating ions of the metal and transfer
energy to them. The ions vibrate more, so the temperature of the resistor
rises. Electrical energy is transferred to thermal energy, and the
resistor heats its surroundings.

This is useful in kettles, toasters, electric heaters, irons, immersion
heaters, hairdryers and electric showers, which use a heating element of
high-resistance wire. It is also why a fuse works. The same heating is
wasteful in cables, which is why cables are made from low-resistance
copper and why a cable carrying too large a current becomes hot.

## Power and fuse selection (2.4)

```
power = current × voltage
P = I × V
```

To choose a fuse:

1. Calculate the normal operating current using I = P ÷ V.
2. Choose the fuse with the **smallest rating above** that current.

**Worked example 1.** A hairdryer is rated 1.4 kW, 230 V. Fuses of 3 A,
5 A and 13 A are available.

```
I = P / V = 1400 / 230 = 6.1 A
3 A and 5 A would blow in normal use; choose 13 A.
```

**Worked example 2.** A lamp is rated 60 W, 230 V.

```
I = 60 / 230 = 0.26 A
choose 3 A, the smallest rating above 0.26 A
```

A 13 A fuse would also let the lamp work, but a fault current of, say,
10 A would not blow it, and the thin lamp cable could overheat. A fuse
rated equal to the normal current is also wrong: it would blow in normal
use.

## Energy transferred (2.5)

```
energy transferred = current × voltage × time
E = I × V × t
```

with E in J, I in A, V in V and t in s.

**Worked example 3.** An electric heater takes 8.7 A from the 230 V mains
for 2.0 hours.

```
t = 2.0 × 3600 = 7200 s
E = 8.7 × 230 × 7200 = 14 407 200 J ≈ 1.4 × 10⁷ J
```

*Commentary.* Convert the time first. The large answer is best given in
standard form, or as 14 MJ.

## a.c. and d.c. (2.6)

**Direct current (d.c.)** flows in one direction only. Cells and batteries
supply d.c.; a graph of their voltage against time is a horizontal line.

**Alternating current (a.c.)** repeatedly reverses direction. Mains
electricity is a.c.; a graph of voltage against time is a wave that goes
positive, then negative, then positive again.

When you **know the difference**, give both halves: "a.c. changes direction
continually; d.c. flows in one direction only."

## Common mistakes

- Saying a fuse "stops the current getting too high". It breaks the
  circuit (by melting) when the current exceeds its rating.
- Saying the earth wire "absorbs" the current or makes the case safe on
  its own. The earth wire provides a low-resistance path, and the large
  current then blows the fuse.
- Choosing a fuse equal to, or far above, the operating current.
- Forgetting to convert kW to W or minutes to seconds.
- Explaining heating as "electrons rubbing", rather than collisions of
  electrons with ions that make the ions vibrate more.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Explain** how a feature protects the user: name the fault, what flows
  where, and the result ("the fuse melts, breaking the circuit and
  disconnecting the live wire").
- **Calculate** then **justify** a fuse choice: give the current and the
  reason for the rating chosen.
- **Describe** a.c. and d.c.: one clear statement about direction for
  each.
- **Suggest** why an appliance is double insulated: link the plastic case
  to there being no metal part that could become live.
