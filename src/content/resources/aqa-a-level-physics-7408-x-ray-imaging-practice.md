These questions cover section **3.10.5 X-ray imaging** of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), part of the A-level only Medical physics option. Learn the content first in the [X-ray imaging study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-x-ray-imaging/) and the [X-ray imaging revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-x-ray-imaging-revision-notes/).

Data: e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Attenuation coefficients are illustrative values for practice.

## Section A

**1.** Explain how the continuous part of an X-ray spectrum is produced. **[2]**

**2.** An X-ray tube operates at 60 kV. Calculate the maximum photon energy in joules and the minimum wavelength of the X-rays. **[3]**

**3.** State one advantage of a flat panel detector compared with photographic film. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why a patient is given a barium meal before an X-ray of the stomach. **[2]**

**5.** State the purpose of an anti-scatter grid. **[1]**

**6.** The half-value thickness of a tissue is 12 mm for an X-ray beam. Calculate the linear attenuation coefficient. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An X-ray tube operates at 120 kV with a tube current of 4.0 mA.

**(a)** Calculate the power delivered to the anode. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of electrons striking the anode each second. **[1]**
**(c)** About 99% of this power heats the anode. Calculate the energy transferred as heat in a 0.50 s exposure. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the anode is made to rotate. **[2]**

**8.** This question is about the spectrum of an X-ray tube with a tungsten target.

**(a)** Describe the main features of a graph of intensity against photon energy for the beam. **[3]**
**(b)** The tube voltage is reduced from 100 kV to 80 kV with the current unchanged. Describe how the graph changes. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the characteristic lines stay at the same photon energies. **[1]**

**9.** For the X-rays used, soft tissue has a half-value thickness of 12 mm and density 1050 kg m⁻³.

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the intensity transmitted through 5.0 cm of soft tissue. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass attenuation coefficient of soft tissue. **[1]**
**(c)** The half-value thickness of lead for the same beam is 0.10 mm. Calculate the thickness of lead that reduces the intensity to 1.0%. **[2]**

**10.** For a different beam, muscle has μ = 20 m⁻¹ and bone has μ = 80 m⁻¹. One beam passes through 6.0 cm of muscle; a second beam passes through 4.5 cm of muscle and 1.5 cm of bone.

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of intensity transmitted along each path. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the ratio of the two transmitted intensities and explain how this produces an image of the bone. **[2]**

**11.** An X-ray tube has a focal spot 0.60 mm wide. The source-to-object distance is 1.2 m and the object-to-detector distance is 0.30 m.

**(a)** Calculate the width of the blurred edge of the image. **[2]**
**(b)** State two changes that would make the image sharper. **[2]**

**12.** This question is about X-ray detection.

**(a)** Describe how a flat panel detector produces an image. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why an intensifying screen reduces the dose needed when film is used. **[2]**
**(c)** State why a fluoroscopic image intensifier is used. **[1]**

**13.** **(a)** Describe the basic principles of a CT scanner. **[4]**
**(b)** Compare CT with a plain X-ray image in terms of resolution, cost and safety. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Electrons are decelerated in the electric fields of target nuclei [1]; the kinetic energy they lose is emitted as photons with a continuous range of energies up to eV [1]

**2.** E = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.0 × 10⁴ = **9.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ J** [1]; λ = hc/E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸/9.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ [1]; = **2.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1]

**3.** Any one: image available immediately; digital so it can be stored, sent and enhanced; lower dose; no chemical processing [1]

**4.** The stomach absorbs X-rays about as much as the tissue around it, so it would barely show [1]; barium is X-ray opaque and coats the stomach lining, increasing contrast so it shows clearly [1]

**5.** To absorb scattered X-rays so they do not reach the detector and reduce contrast [1]

**6.** μ = ln 2/x½ = 0.693/0.012 [1]; = **58 m⁻¹** [1]

**7. (a)** P = VI = 1.2 × 10⁵ × 4.0 × 10⁻³ = **480 W** [1]
**(b)** 4.0 × 10⁻³/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.5 × 10¹⁶ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** 0.99 × 480 × 0.50 = **240 J** [1]
**(d)** The heat is spread over a ring around the rotating anode instead of one spot [1]; so a small focal spot (for sharp images) can be used without melting the target [1]

**8. (a)** A continuous curve rising from near zero at low energy (low-energy photons absorbed in the tube window or filter) [1]; falling to zero at a maximum energy equal to eV [1]; with sharp characteristic peaks superimposed at particular energies [1]
**(b)** The maximum photon energy falls from 100 keV to 80 keV [1]; the intensity falls at all energies (the curve is lower) [1]
**(c)** The line energies equal differences between energy levels of the target atoms, which depend only on the target element [1]

**9. (a)** Number of half-value thicknesses = 50/12 = 4.17 [1]; fraction = (1/2)^4.17 = **0.056** [1]
**(b)** μ = 0.693/0.012 = 57.8 m⁻¹; μm = 57.8/1050 = **0.055 m² kg⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** (1/2)ⁿ = 0.010 gives n = ln 100/ln 2 = 6.64 [1]; thickness = 6.64 × 0.10 = **0.66 mm** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Working in half-value thicknesses avoids finding μ for lead; both routes score if the logarithm is handled correctly.

**10. (a)** Muscle only: e^(−20 × 0.060) = **0.30** [1]; exponent for the second path = 20 × 0.045 + 80 × 0.015 = 2.1 [1]; e^(−2.1) = **0.12** [1]
**(b)** Ratio = 0.30/0.12 = **2.5** [1]; less intensity reaches the detector behind the bone, so the bone shows as a light region against darker muscle (differential absorption) [1]

**11. (a)** By similar triangles, blur = 0.60 × 0.30/1.2 [1]; = **0.15 mm** [1]
**(b)** Any two: use a smaller focal spot; increase the source-to-patient distance; place the patient closer to the detector; keep the patient still or use a shorter exposure [2]

**12. (a)** An X-ray scintillator converts X-ray photons into visible light [1]; an array of photodiode pixels converts the light into electric charge [1]; the pixels are read by electronic scanning to build a digital image [1]
**(b)** Film is much more sensitive to light than to X-rays [1]; the screen's phosphor emits many light photons for each X-ray absorbed, so fewer X-rays (less dose) are needed for the same exposure [1]
**(c)** To give bright, real-time (moving) images, for example during a procedure [1]

**13. (a)** The X-ray tube rotates around the patient [1]; it produces a narrow, monochromatic beam [1]; an array of detectors measures the transmitted intensity at many angles [1]; a computer processes the signals to produce cross-sectional images that can be combined in 3D [1]
**(b)** Resolution: CT gives higher resolution and better soft-tissue contrast, and 3D images [1]; cost: CT is much more expensive [1]; safety: CT gives the patient a much higher dose [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the tube current changes the maximum photon energy.
- Using log instead of ln with half-value thickness.
- Leaving mm and m⁻¹ mixed in the exponent.
- Describing the filter as reducing the intensity of all photons equally.
- Describing CT without naming the computer processing.
