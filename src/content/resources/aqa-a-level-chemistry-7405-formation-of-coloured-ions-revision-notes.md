These notes condense section **3.2.5.4 Formation of coloured ions** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Formation of coloured ions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-formation-of-coloured-ions/), and exam-style questions are in the [Formation of coloured ions practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-formation-of-coloured-ions-practice/). The wider topic is in the [Transition metals revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- Explain how **colour arises** from absorption of visible light.
- Use **ΔE = hν = hc/λ**.
- Explain how **oxidation state, co-ordination number and ligand** change the colour.
- Describe how a **colorimeter** finds the concentration of coloured ions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ground state | Lowest-energy arrangement of the d electrons |
| Excited state | d electron has moved to a higher-energy d orbital |
| ΔE | Energy gap between the d orbitals, in J per ion |
| Absorbance | Measure of how much light a solution absorbs |
| Complementary colour | The colour seen when a colour is absorbed |
| Calibration graph | Absorbance of standards against concentration |

## Origin of colour

1. White light passes through the solution.
2. A d electron absorbs a photon with energy equal to ΔE and moves to a higher d orbital.
3. The light that is **not** absorbed is transmitted.
4. The eye sees the transmitted (complementary) colour.

| Colour absorbed | Colour seen |
|---|---|
| Red | Blue-green |
| Orange | Blue |
| Yellow | Violet |
| Green | Purple |
| Blue | Orange |
| Violet | Yellow-green |

## Which ions are colourless?

- Sc³⁺ (3d⁰): no d electrons.
- Cu⁺ and Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰): full d sub-level, nowhere to be promoted.
- Coloured ions need an incomplete d sub-level.

## The equation

```
ΔE = hν = hc/λ
h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s     c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹     L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
```

| To find | Rearrangement | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| ΔE (one ion) | hc ÷ λ | λ in metres |
| ΔE (per mole) | ΔE × L ÷ 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹ | Convert J to kJ |
| λ | hc ÷ ΔE | Answer in m; × 10⁹ for nm |
| ν | c ÷ λ | Units Hz (s⁻¹) |

Worked examples:

```
λ = 600 nm:  ΔE = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
             per mole: 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.022 × 10²³ = 200 kJ mol⁻¹
ΔE = 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ J:  λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 4.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.85 × 10⁻⁷ m = 485 nm
```

## What changes ΔE (and so the colour)

| Change | Example |
|---|---|
| Oxidation state | Fe²⁺(aq) pale green; Fe³⁺(aq) yellow-brown |
| Co-ordination number | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ blue; [CuCl₄]²⁻ yellow-green |
| Ligand | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ pale blue; [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ deep blue |

Model answer: the change alters the energy gap ΔE between the d orbitals, so a different wavelength of light is absorbed and a different colour is seen.

## Colorimetry

1. Select a filter of the **complementary colour** to the solution.
2. Zero the instrument with a blank.
3. Measure the absorbance of **standard solutions** of known concentration.
4. Plot a **calibration graph** (absorbance against concentration).
5. Measure the unknown's absorbance and read off the concentration.
6. Multiply by any dilution factor.

Worked example: standards give a gradient of 55 dm³ mol⁻¹. An absorbance of 0.385 gives c = 0.385 ÷ 55 = 0.0070 mol dm⁻³. If this solution was made by diluting 5.00 cm³ to 50.0 cm³, the original is 0.070 mol dm⁻³.

If the ion is not strongly coloured, add a ligand that forms a strongly coloured complex before measuring.

## Common colours to know

| Ion or complex | Colour |
|---|---|
| [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | Pale blue |
| [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | Deep blue |
| [CuCl₄]²⁻ | Yellow-green |
| [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | Pink |
| [CoCl₄]²⁻ | Blue |
| Fe²⁺(aq) | Pale green |
| Fe³⁺(aq) | Yellow-brown |
| Mn²⁺(aq) | Very pale pink |

## Good practice in colorimetry

- Use the same cuvette (or matched cuvettes) for every reading, held with clean faces towards the light.
- Make standards by accurate dilution from a stock solution, using a pipette and a volumetric flask.
- Read the unknown within the range of the standards. If it is too concentrated, dilute it and remember the dilution factor.
- Rinse the cuvette with the solution being measured.

## Exam traps

- Colour seen described as the colour absorbed.
- Electrons said to emit light.
- Colour change explained as "different ligand" with no mention of ΔE.
- Wavelength not converted from nm to m.
- Energy per ion given when per mole is asked.
- Wrong filter colour in colorimetry.
- Zn²⁺ colourless "because it is not a metal".
- Electron movement between shells rather than d orbitals.

## Self-test

1. What happens to a d electron when light is absorbed?
2. Why is Zn²⁺ colourless?
3. Write the equation linking ΔE and λ.
4. Calculate ΔE for light of λ = 500 nm.
5. A complex absorbs orange light. What colour is it?
6. Name three factors that change the colour of a complex.
7. Why does [CuCl₄]²⁻ have a different colour from [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺?
8. What is plotted on a colorimeter calibration graph?
9. Which filter should be used for a blue solution?
10. Why is a blank used?
11. How do you convert nm to m?
12. Why are Sc³⁺ compounds colourless?

### Answers

1. It moves from the ground state to a higher-energy d orbital (an excited state).
2. Its 3d sub-level is full, so no electron can be promoted.
3. ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
4. ΔE = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
5. Blue.
6. Oxidation state, co-ordination number and ligand.
7. The change in ligand and co-ordination number changes ΔE, so a different wavelength is absorbed.
8. Absorbance against concentration of standard solutions.
9. A red or orange filter (the complementary colour).
10. To set the zero so that only absorption by the coloured ion is measured.
11. Multiply by 10⁻⁹.
12. Sc³⁺ has no d electrons.
