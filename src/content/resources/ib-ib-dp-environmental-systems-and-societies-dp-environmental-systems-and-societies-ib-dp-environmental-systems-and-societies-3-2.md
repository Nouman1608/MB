## What 3.2 asks you to do

- Explain how human activities reduce biodiversity at the genetic, species and habitat levels.
- Distinguish the direct drivers of loss from the indirect drivers behind them.
- Compare current extinction rates with the background rate, and explain why both are uncertain.
- Explain why particular species, and particular ecosystems, are more vulnerable than others.
- Apply all of this to named examples: a species driven extinct by humans, a species now threatened, and an ecosystem under pressure.

## Direct and indirect drivers

A **direct driver** acts on organisms or habitats physically: clearing a forest, catching a fish, releasing a pollutant. An **indirect driver** is the social or economic cause that makes the direct driver happen: population growth, rising consumption, trade, technology, governance and the value systems behind them.

The 2019 global assessment by the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) ranked five direct drivers by their global impact, from largest to smallest:

1. Changes in land and sea use (habitat loss, fragmentation and degradation).
2. Direct exploitation of organisms (hunting, fishing, logging, collection for trade).
3. Climate change.
4. Pollution.
5. Invasive alien species.

The order differs between ecosystems. On land, land-use change dominates; in the oceans, direct exploitation is the largest driver. A common memory aid for the direct drivers is **HIPPO**: Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, human Population, Overexploitation. Some teachers add C for climate change. Note that "population" in HIPPO is really an indirect driver: more people only harm biodiversity through more land use, harvest or pollution.

Why the distinction matters in an answer: a strategy that tackles only a direct driver often fails. A ban on logging in one forest can push clearance into another forest if demand for timber and farmland (the indirect driver) does not change. This is called **leakage**.

## How habitat change removes species

Habitat loss is more than a smaller area. Three linked processes follow when a large habitat is broken into fragments:

- **Reduced area.** Each fragment supports a smaller population. Small populations are more likely to die out through chance events such as a bad breeding year, a disease or a fire.
- **Isolation.** Roads, farmland and settlements block movement. Individuals cannot reach mates or recolonise a fragment after a local extinction, so gene flow stops and genetic diversity falls through inbreeding and genetic drift.
- **Degradation of what remains.** Edge effects, pollution, hunting along new access roads and invasive species penetrate the remaining habitat.

Species do not disappear at once. A fragment may hold a species for decades after it has become too small to support it in the long term. This delay is called an **extinction debt**: the extinctions have been "paid for" by habitat loss but have not yet happened. It is one reason current species counts can understate the damage already done.

## Measuring extinction rates

The **background extinction rate** is the rate at which species disappear in the absence of major catastrophes or human influence, estimated from the fossil record. It is often expressed as **extinctions per million species-years** (E/MSY): the number of extinctions expected if a million species were observed for one year.

### Worked example: comparing an observed rate with background

A fictional survey of a group of 4000 species records 12 extinctions over 150 years. Assume, for this example only, a background rate of 1 extinction per million species-years.

```
species-years observed = 4000 species × 150 years = 600 000 species-years
observed rate = 12 ÷ 600 000 = 0.00002 extinctions per species-year
in E/MSY      = 0.00002 × 1 000 000 = 20 E/MSY
ratio to background = 20 ÷ 1 = 20 times the background rate
```

**Interpretation.** The observed rate is twenty times the assumed background rate. Always add why such figures are uncertain:

- Most species have never been described, so their extinctions go unrecorded.
- A species is declared extinct only after long searches fail to find it, so recent losses are under-counted.
- Fossil records favour organisms with hard parts, so background rates are estimated mainly from a few groups.
- Extinction debt means some losses caused now will only appear later.

## Why some ecosystems lose more

The Topic 3 study guide lists the features that make a *species* vulnerable. At the ecosystem level, look for these features:

| Feature | Why it raises vulnerability | Example ecosystem |
|---|---|---|
| High endemism | Species found nowhere else are lost globally when the local habitat goes | Oceanic islands |
| Nutrients held in biomass | Clearing removes the nutrient store, so recovery is slow | Tropical rainforest |
| Narrow tolerance limits | Small changes in temperature or chemistry cause mass mortality | Coral reefs |
| Accessible and valuable land | Flat, fertile or coastal land is converted first | Lowland grassland, mangroves |
| Slow growth and reproduction | Populations cannot replace losses quickly | Deep-sea communities, polar ecosystems |

**Islands** show human impact most clearly. Island species often evolved without large predators, so many are flightless, nest on the ground or are tame. The dodo of Mauritius was extinct by the late seventeenth century, within a century of sailors arriving with rats, pigs and monkeys and hunting the birds directly.

## Case studies to learn

You need at least one named example for each kind of question. Learn the mechanism, not just the name.

**A species driven extinct: the passenger pigeon.** Once one of the most numerous birds in North America, it nested in huge colonies in eastern deciduous forests. Commercial hunting for cheap meat, helped by railways and the telegraph that let hunters find colonies, combined with clearance of the forests it nested in. Because it bred in dense colonies, small scattered groups may not have bred successfully. The last known individual, Martha, died at Cincinnati Zoo in 1914. The case shows that even an abundant species can be lost when exploitation and habitat loss act together on a species with specialised breeding behaviour.

**A threatened species: the vaquita.** This small porpoise lives only in the northern Gulf of California, Mexico, and is listed as Critically Endangered. Its main threat is **bycatch**: it drowns in gillnets set for fish, including the illegal fishery for the totoaba, whose swim bladder is traded. The vaquita combines several vulnerability features: a tiny range, a very small population and slow reproduction.

**An invasive species: the brown tree snake on Guam.** Introduced accidentally to the Pacific island of Guam around the end of the Second World War, probably in military cargo, the snake spread across the island and caused the loss of most of Guam's native forest bird species. The birds had evolved without snake predators and had no defensive behaviour.

**An ecosystem under pressure.** For a tropical rainforest or a coral reef that you have studied, know: the drivers acting on it, which species or services are lost, which features make it vulnerable, and what is being done about it. Use the same headings in an exam answer.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain how the introduction of an invasive species can reduce biodiversity on an island. **[4]**

**Answer.** The brown tree snake was introduced to Guam by human transport, probably in cargo [1]. The native birds had evolved without snake predators, so they had no behaviour to avoid it, such as nesting out of reach [1]. With abundant prey and no natural predators or diseases to control it, the snake population grew very large [1]. Predation caused the loss of most native forest bird species, which were endemic or locally restricted and could not recolonise from elsewhere, so species diversity fell [1].

*Why it scores.* Each sentence links a cause to an effect with "so" or "because". A list of facts about the snake would earn one or two marks.

## Common mistakes

- Naming "human population growth" as a direct cause of extinction without the mechanism (more land cleared, more fish caught).
- Treating climate change as the main driver of loss everywhere. Globally, land-use change and direct exploitation rank higher at present.
- Stating an extinction rate without units or without saying what it is compared with.
- Assuming that a species still present in a fragment is safe; extinction debt may still be due.
- Using a vague case study ("animals in the Amazon"). Name the species or area, the driver and the mechanism.
- Confusing an invasive species with any non-native species. A non-native species becomes invasive only when it spreads and causes harm.

## Exam technique for this subtopic

- **Outline** (short account): give the driver and its effect in one or two sentences each.
- **Explain** (reasons or mechanisms): every point needs a "because" or "so"; aim for one marking point per link in the chain.
- **Distinguish**: write one sentence for each term and a final sentence that states the difference directly.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss**: weigh the importance of different drivers in a named case, then reach a judgement, for example that habitat loss was the root cause and hunting the final blow.
- **To what extent**: in essays about the "sixth mass extinction", weigh the evidence for very high current rates against the uncertainties listed above, and conclude.
