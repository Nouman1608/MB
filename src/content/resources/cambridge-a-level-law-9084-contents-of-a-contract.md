Sub-topic 3.2 asks what a contract actually contains once it has been formed: which statements are
binding terms, which terms the law adds whether the parties mention them or not, how serious each
term is, and whether a clause excluding liability will work. It is assessed on Paper 3 Law of
Contract through scenario problems and essays. This guide goes deeper than the
[Topic 3 overview](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-law-of-contract-study-guide/) on each of the four
syllabus strands.

## What the syllabus lists

- **3.2.1 Express terms:** the distinction between representations and terms and why it matters;
  written terms, incorporation by signature and the parol evidence rule.
- **3.2.2 Terms implied by the Consumer Rights Act 2015:** for goods, s9, s10 and s11 with the
  remedies in s20, s22, s23 and s24; for services, s49 and s52 with the remedies in s55 and s56.
- **3.2.3 Status of terms:** conditions, warranties and innominate terms, their nature and the
  effects of breach.
- **3.2.4 Control of exemption clauses:** common-law incorporation and the contra proferentem rule;
  the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (business to business only) and the Consumer Rights Act 2015
  (trader and consumer contracts).

## 3.2.1 Terms and representations

A **representation** is a statement of fact that induces a contract but is not a promise within it.
A **term** is part of the contract, so if it proves untrue there is a breach of contract. The test is
the parties' objective intention, guided by:

- **Importance attached to the statement.** In Bannerman v White (1861) the buyer said he would not
  buy hops treated with sulphur; the assurance was a term. In Couchman v Hill (1947) an oral
  assurance that a heifer was unserved was a term even though the auction conditions said there
  were no warranties.
- **Timing.** In Routledge v McKay (1954) a statement about a motorcycle's date made a week before
  a written contract that did not repeat it was only a representation.
- **Special knowledge.** A private seller who relied on the logbook was not giving a term in Oscar
  Chess v Williams (1957); a dealer who should have known the true mileage was, in Dick Bentley
  Productions v Harold Smith Motors (1965).
- **Invitation to verify.** In Ecay v Godfrey (1947) a seller who told the buyer to have a boat
  surveyed was making a representation, whereas in Schawel v Reade (1913) a seller who told a
  buyer not to bother examining a horse because it was perfect gave a term.

**Why it matters.** Breach of a term gives an automatic claim for breach of contract, with damages
measured to put the claimant where performance would have put them. An untrue representation is
not a breach; the claimant must prove misrepresentation, and the remedies differ. Classify the
statement first.

### Written terms

Where a party signs a contractual document, they are bound by its terms whether or not they read
them (L'Estrange v Graucob, 1934). The exception is where the other party misrepresents the effect
of the document: in Curtis v Chemical Cleaning and Dyeing Co (1951) an assistant told the customer a
clause covered only beads and sequins, so the cleaners could not rely on the wider wording.

The **parol evidence rule** says that oral or other extrinsic evidence cannot be used to add to,
vary or contradict a written contract. It protects certainty but does not apply where the writing
was not meant to contain the whole agreement, and oral evidence may show
a condition precedent that the contract was not to operate until an event happened (Pym v Campbell,
1856) or a **collateral contract**, as in City and Westminster Properties v Mudd (1959), where a
tenant signed a lease banning residence in reliance on an oral promise that he could continue to
sleep on the premises.

## 3.2.2 Terms implied by the Consumer Rights Act 2015

The Act applies to contracts between a trader and a consumer. For **goods**:

- **s9 satisfactory quality:** the standard a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, taking
  account of description, price and other relevant circumstances. Aspects include fitness for all
  the purposes for which goods of that kind are usually supplied, appearance and finish, freedom from
  minor defects, safety and durability. Defects drawn to the consumer's attention before the
  contract, or which their own examination ought to have revealed, do not count.
- **s10 fit for a particular purpose:** where the consumer makes known a particular purpose, the
  goods must be reasonably fit for it, unless the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to
  rely, on the trader's skill and judgement.
- **s11 as described:** goods must match their description.

The consumer's remedies are tiered. The **short-term right to reject** (s20 and s22) must normally be
exercised within 30 days and gives a full refund. Alternatively, or afterwards, the consumer may
require **repair or replacement** (s23), which the trader must carry out within a reasonable time and
without significant inconvenience. If repair or replacement is impossible, or fails, the consumer can
claim a **price reduction or the final right to reject** (s24).

For **services**, s49 requires the service to be performed with **reasonable care and skill**, and s52
requires performance within a **reasonable time** where no time is fixed. Breach gives a right to
**repeat performance** (s55) or, where that is impossible or not done within a reasonable time and
without significant inconvenience, a **price reduction** (s56).

## 3.2.3 Conditions, warranties and innominate terms

A **condition** goes to the root of the contract; breach allows the innocent party to treat the
contract as ended and claim damages. A **warranty** is subsidiary; breach gives damages only. The
contrast is shown by Poussard v Spiers and Pond (1876) and Bettini v Gye (1876).

Calling a term a condition does not settle the matter. In Schuler v Wickman Machine Tool Sales (1974)
a clause described as a condition required weekly visits to six named firms; the House of Lords held
the parties could not have meant that one missed visit allowed termination.

An **innominate term** is classified by the effect of the breach, not in advance. In Hong Kong Fir
Shipping v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha (1962) a seaworthiness obligation was breached, but the delay did not
deprive the charterers of substantially the whole benefit, so they could not terminate. The approach
was applied to a sale of goods in Cehave v Bremer (The Hansa Nord, 1976). The tension is between
**flexibility**, which stops a party escaping a bad bargain over a trivial breach, and **certainty**:
in Bunge Corporation v Tradax Export (1981) the House of Lords treated a time clause in a commercial
contract as a condition, because traders need to know at once whether they can terminate.

## 3.2.4 Exemption clauses

### Common law

A clause must first be **incorporated**: by signature; by reasonable notice given before or at the
time of contracting (Parker v South Eastern Railway, 1877; Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel, 1949;
Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, 1971); or by a consistent course of dealing (contrast McCutcheon v
David MacBrayne, 1964, where previous dealings were inconsistent, and Hollier v Rambler Motors, 1972,
where three or four dealings over five years were not enough). The more onerous or unusual the
clause, the clearer the notice must be (Interfoto Picture Library v Stiletto Visual Programmes, 1989).

Next the clause must **cover the breach** on its true construction. Under the **contra proferentem
rule**, ambiguity is read against the party relying on the clause. In Houghton v Trafalgar Insurance
(1954) an exclusion for an "excess load" was held not to cover carrying too many passengers.

### Statute

**Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** (business to business): s1(3) confines the Act to business
liability. Under s2(1) liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence cannot be
excluded; under s2(2) other negligence liability can be excluded only if reasonable. Section 3
applies the reasonableness test where one party deals on the other's written standard terms. The
s11 test asks whether the term was fair and reasonable given the circumstances known to, or
contemplated by, the parties when the contract was made. The clause failed in George Mitchell v
Finney Lock Seeds (1983) and St Albans City and District Council v International Computers (1996), but
the Court of Appeal in Watford Electronics v Sanderson (2001) stressed that experienced businesses of
equal bargaining power should usually be left to allocate their own risks.

**Consumer Rights Act 2015** (s61 trader and consumer contracts; s2 definitions): under s31 a term
cannot exclude or restrict the s9 to s11 rights, and under s57 a term cannot exclude s49. Section 65
bars excluding liability for negligently caused death or personal injury. Section 62 makes an unfair
term not binding on the consumer: a term is unfair if, contrary to good faith, it causes a significant
imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations to the consumer's detriment. Section 68 requires
written terms to be transparent: plain, intelligible and legible.

## Annotated model answer

*Scenario: Priya buys a laptop for £900 from TechHub Ltd after the salesperson says, "This model
runs for ten hours on one charge." The receipt states: "TechHub accepts no liability for battery
performance." After five weeks the battery lasts two hours.*

1. **Status of the statement.** The salesperson has specialist knowledge and the statement was
   important to the purchase, so it is likely to be a term (Dick Bentley). It also forms part of the
   description and is relevant to satisfactory quality.
2. **Implied terms.** Priya is a consumer and TechHub a trader (s2, s61). A two-hour battery is
   arguably not of satisfactory quality (s9: durability, fitness for usual purposes) and does not
   match the description given (s11).
3. **Remedy.** Five weeks is outside the 30-day short-term right to reject, so Priya should require
   repair or replacement (s23). If that fails, she can claim a price reduction or exercise the final
   right to reject (s24).
4. **Exclusion clause.** A receipt handed over after the sale is too late to be incorporated
   (Olley). Even if incorporated, s31 makes it not binding because it restricts s9 and s11 rights.
5. **Conclusion.** The clause is ineffective.

## Common mistakes

- Applying UCTA 1977 to a consumer contract, or the CRA 2015 to a sale between two businesses.
- Forgetting that the clause must be incorporated before asking whether it is reasonable or fair.
- Assuming a term labelled "condition" is always a condition (Schuler).
