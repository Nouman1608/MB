## Section A

**1.** Define power and state its SI unit. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by the efficiency of a system, and explain why it can never be greater than 100%. **[2]**

**3.** A porter holds a 20 kg suitcase stationary at shoulder height for 30 s. Explain why the porter does no work on the suitcase during this time. **[2]**

**4.** Show that the joule is equivalent to kg m² s⁻². **[2]**

**5.** A sledge is pulled 40 m across level snow by a rope. The tension in the rope is 55 N and the rope is at 25° to the horizontal. Calculate the work done by the tension. **[2]**

**6.** Starting from the definition of power, derive the equation P = Fv for a force F moving an object at velocity v in the direction of the force. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** A lift and its passengers have a total mass of 800 kg. The lift rises 30 m at constant speed in 20 s.

**(a)** Calculate the work done against gravity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the useful output power of the lift motor. **[1]**

**(c)** The motor's input power is 16 kW. Calculate the efficiency of the system. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the energy dissipated by the system in one minute of operating in this way. **[2]**

**8.** A car of mass 1400 kg has a constant engine output power. Its maximum speed on a level road is 40 m s⁻¹, and the total resistive force is 1500 N, which you may assume stays constant.

**(a)** Calculate the output power of the engine. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the acceleration of the car at the moment its speed is 15 m s⁻¹. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the acceleration decreases as the car's speed increases. **[2]**

**9.** A box of mass 12 kg is initially at rest on a level floor. It is pushed 6.0 m by a horizontal force of 50 N. The frictional force on the box is 32 N.

**(a)** Calculate the work done by the pushing force. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the work done against friction. **[1]**

**(c)** Use the principle of conservation of energy to calculate the speed of the box after 6.0 m. **[3]**

**(d)** State what happens to the energy transferred by doing work against friction. **[1]**

**10.** An electric motor of efficiency 0.85 drives a water pump of efficiency 0.70. The pump raises water through a vertical height of 18 m at a rate of 25 kg every second.

**(a)** Calculate the useful power output of the pump. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the overall efficiency of the motor and pump together. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the electrical power input to the motor. **[2]**

**11.** A boat is towed through water at a constant speed of 3.0 m s⁻¹ by a cable. The tension in the cable is 2.5 kN and the cable makes an angle of 20° with the direction of motion.

**(a)** Calculate the rate at which the tension does work on the boat. **[3]**

**(b)** State and calculate the total resistive force acting on the boat along its direction of motion. **[2]**

**12.** A student reports that a machine lifted a 50 kg load through a vertical height of 2.0 m while taking in only 820 J of energy, and concludes that the machine has an efficiency of about 120%.

**(a)** Calculate the gain in gravitational potential energy of the load. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain, with reference to a physical principle, why the student's conclusion must be wrong. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Power is the **work done per unit time** (or rate of energy transfer) [1]. Unit: the **watt** (W), equal to J s⁻¹ [1].

**2.** Efficiency is the ratio of the **useful energy output** to the **total energy input** [1]. A value above 100% would mean more energy out than in, so energy would have been **created**, which contradicts the principle of conservation of energy [1].

**3.** Work done = force × displacement in the direction of the force [1]. The suitcase does **not move**, so the displacement is zero and **no work is done on it**, although the porter's muscles transfer energy internally [1].

**4.** W = Fs, and F = ma, so 1 J = 1 N × 1 m [1] = (kg m s⁻²) × m = **kg m² s⁻²** [1].

**5.** W = Fs cos θ = 55 × 40 × cos 25° [1] = **1990 J** (2.0 × 10³ J) [1].

**6.** P = W/t [1]; W = Fs, so P = Fs/t [1]; s/t = v, so **P = Fv** [1].

**7. (a)** W = mgh = 800 × 9.81 × 30 [1] = **2.35 × 10⁵ J** [1].

**(b)** P = W/t = 2.35 × 10⁵ ÷ 20 = **1.18 × 10⁴ W** (11.8 kW) [1].

**(c)** Efficiency = 11 772 ÷ 16 000 [1] = **0.74** (74%) [1].

**(d)** Power dissipated = 16 000 − 11 772 = 4228 W [1]; energy in 60 s = 4228 × 60 = **2.5 × 10⁵ J** [1].

**8. (a)** At maximum speed, driving force = resistive force = 1500 N [1]; P = Fv = 1500 × 40 = **6.0 × 10⁴ W** (60 kW) [1].

**(b)** Driving force at 15 m s⁻¹: F = P/v = 60 000 ÷ 15 = 4000 N [1]. Resultant force = 4000 − 1500 = 2500 N [1]. a = 2500 ÷ 1400 = **1.8 m s⁻²** [1].

**(c)** With constant power, the **driving force F = P/v falls** as the speed increases [1], so the **resultant force** (driving force minus resistive force) **decreases**, and so does the acceleration, reaching zero at maximum speed [1].

**9. (a)** W = 50 × 6.0 = **300 J** [1].

**(b)** W = 32 × 6.0 = **192 J** [1].

**(c)** Kinetic energy gained = 300 − 192 = 108 J [1]. ½ × 12 × v² = 108 [1], so v = √18 = **4.2 m s⁻¹** [1].

**(d)** It is **dissipated** as internal (thermal) energy of the box, the floor and the surroundings (and some sound) [1].

**10. (a)** Useful power = (mass per second) × g × h = 25 × 9.81 × 18 [1] = **4.4 × 10³ W** (4410 W) [1].

**(b)** Overall efficiency = 0.85 × 0.70 = **0.595** [1].

**(c)** Input = useful output ÷ efficiency = 4414.5 ÷ 0.595 [1] = **7.4 × 10³ W** (7.4 kW) [1].

**11. (a)** Component of tension along the motion = 2500 cos 20° = 2349 N [1]. P = Fv = 2349 × 3.0 [1] = **7.0 × 10³ W** (7.0 kW) [1].

**(b)** The speed is constant, so the resultant force along the motion is zero and the resistive force equals the forward component of tension [1] = **2.3 × 10³ N** (2349 N) [1].

**12. (a)** ∆Eₚ = mgh = 50 × 9.81 × 2.0 = **981 J** [1].

**(b)** The useful output (981 J) cannot exceed the total input, because by the **principle of conservation of energy** energy cannot be created [1]. So at least one measurement is wrong: the energy input must have been at least 981 J, and in practice more, since some energy is always dissipated [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out cos θ when a rope or cable is at an angle to the motion (Questions 5 and 11).
- Using the resistive force rather than P/v as the driving force during acceleration (Question 8).
- Forgetting the work done against friction in an energy balance (Question 9).
- Adding efficiencies in a chain instead of multiplying them (Question 10).
- Defining power using symbols only, without stating what W and t represent.
- Saying that holding a load still "does work on the load" because the arm tires.
