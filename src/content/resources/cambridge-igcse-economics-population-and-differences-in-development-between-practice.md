## Questions

**1.** State two factors that could reduce a country's birth rate. **[2]**

**2.** Country S has a population of 8,000,000. In one year there are 152,000 births and 64,000 deaths.

**(a)** Calculate the birth rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the death rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the natural increase as a percentage of the population. **[1]**

**3.** Country T has a population of 15,000,000. Natural increase is 45,000. Immigration is 90,000 and emigration is 25,000.

**(a)** Calculate net migration. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total change in population. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the total change as a percentage of the population, to 2 decimal places. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the closing population. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why a country with good healthcare may have a higher death rate than a country with weaker healthcare. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows the age and gender distribution of Country J (percentage of total population).

| Age group | Males | Females |
|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | 12 | 10 |
| 15-64 | 35 | 27 |
| 65+ | 7 | 9 |

**(a)** State the percentage of the population aged 65 or over. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the dependency ratio, (aged 0-14 + aged 65+) ÷ aged 15-64 × 100, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe one feature of the gender distribution of the working-age group and suggest one reason for it. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one effect of this age structure on government spending. **[3]**

**6.** The table shows total output at different populations in Country K.

| Population (millions) | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total output (units, billions) | 12 | 30 | 54 | 60 |

**(a)** Calculate output per head at each population. **[2]**
**(b)** State the optimum population and give a reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how new technology could change the optimum population. **[2]**

**7.** Discuss whether an ageing population is a problem for an economy. **[8]**

**8.** In Country U, GDP grows by 2.6% and population by 1.8%.

**(a)** Calculate the growth in GDP per head, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why most people's living standards might not improve even if GDP per head rises. **[2]**

**9.** Country M has 4 million workers and GDP of 20 billion units. Country N has 9 million workers and GDP of 270 billion units.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why productivity might differ. **[4]**

**10.** Country P has income of 600 million units and saves 4% of it. Country Q has income of 1,000 million units and saves 20% of it.

**(a)** Calculate saving in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the difference in saving could widen the income gap. **[4]**

**11.** In Country R the shares of workers in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors change from 55%, 15% and 30% to 40%, 20% and 40%.

**(a)** Identify the sector whose share grew most. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how this shift could raise average income. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate whether improving education is the best way for a low-income country to narrow its income gap with a higher-income country. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: higher incomes; more women in education or paid work; wider access to contraception; higher costs of raising children; reliable pensions. First factor [1]; second factor [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two separate factors. A repeated idea in different words is likely to earn one mark at most.

**2. (a)** 152,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **19 per 1,000** [1]
**(b)** 64,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **8 per 1,000** [1]
**(c)** (152,000 − 64,000) ÷ 8,000,000 × 100 = **1.1%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* State the unit. Rates are per 1,000, while natural increase here is a percentage.

**3. (a)** 90,000 − 25,000 = **+65,000** [1]
**(b)** 45,000 + 65,000 = **110,000** [1]
**(c)** 110,000 ÷ 15,000,000 × 100 = **0.73%** [1]
**(d)** 15,000,000 + 110,000 = **15,110,000** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show each step. A final figure alone gives no way to credit a correct method.

**4.** Death rate depends on age structure as well as healthcare [1]. A country with good healthcare often has long life expectancy and many elderly people [1]. The elderly are more likely to die in any year [1]. A younger population in a weaker-healthcare country has fewer deaths per 1,000, so its rate can be lower [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain that reaches "so the crude rate can be higher" shows the full link. Saying only "old people die" stops short.

**5. (a)** 7 + 9 = **16%** [1]
**(b)** 0-14 share = 12 + 10 = 22 and 15-64 share = 35 + 27 = 62 [1]. (22 + 16) ÷ 62 × 100 = **61.3** [1]
**(c)** Males (35%) outnumber females (27%) in the working ages [1]. Possible reason: male workers migrating in for jobs, or women emigrating [1]
**(d)** Large share aged 65+ (16%) [1]. This raises spending on pensions and healthcare [1]. With a smaller working-age share paying tax, the government may have to raise taxes or borrow [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe" needs figures from the table; "suggest" needs a plausible reason. Each part needs its own point.

**6. (a)** 4 and 5 units per head [1]; 6 and 5 units per head [1]
**(b)** The optimum is 9 million [1], because output per head is highest at 6 units [1]
**(c)** Technology raises output per worker [1], so output per head is highest at a larger population and the optimum rises [1]
*Examiner insight:* Compare output per head, not total output. Total output is highest at 12 million, but that is not the optimum.

**7.** Costs: more pension and healthcare spending [1]; fewer workers per pensioner, so higher taxes or borrowing [1]; possible labour shortages [1]. Benefits and offsets: older people spend, volunteer and pass on experience [1]; many can work longer, and care services create jobs [1]; immigration or a later retirement age can fill gaps [1]. Evaluation: the effect depends on whether the economy has high productivity and savings to fund pensions [1]. A judgement that it is a manageable challenge with sound policy, but a serious problem where the working population is shrinking and productivity is low [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers with only costs read as description. Evaluation gains credit when it gives conditions and a supported judgement.

**8. (a)** 1.026 ÷ 1.018 − 1 [1] = **0.79%** [1]
**(b)** Averages hide how income is shared, and gains may go to a small group [1]. Prices may also rise as fast as incomes, so real purchasing power does not grow [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A rise in GDP minus a rise in population is a rough shortcut. Dividing the growth factors is the safer method.

**9. (a)** M: 20 billion ÷ 4 million = **5,000** [1]; N: 270 billion ÷ 9 million = **30,000** [1]
**(b)** Capital: N may have more machinery and technology [1], so each worker produces more [1]. Skills and health: better-educated, healthier workers use equipment well and lose fewer days [1], which raises output per worker [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a link to output per worker. "N is richer" is not a reason.

**10. (a)** P: 4% × 600 = **24 million** [1]; Q: 20% × 1,000 = **200 million** [1]
**(b)** Saving funds investment [1]. Q can buy more capital and technology [1], so its productivity and output rise faster [1]. P has little to invest, so its income grows slowly and the gap widens [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the chain from saving to investment to productivity to income. Stopping at "Q saves more" earns little.

**11. (a)** The tertiary sector (up 10 percentage points) [1]
**(b)** Output per worker is often higher in secondary and tertiary work than in farming or extraction [1], so moving workers raises average output [1]. Wages tend to be higher and less affected by swings in raw-material prices [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the change in each sector with figures, then say why the new jobs raise income.

**12.** For education: raises skills and productivity [1]; helps workers use technology and attract investment [1]; higher incomes then raise saving and tax revenue [1]. Against: results take years, and the cost uses up money that could go to healthcare or capital [1]; skilled workers may emigrate [1]; if there are no jobs or machinery, extra skills raise little output [1]. Evaluation: education works best alongside investment and healthcare [1]. Judgement: it is one of the best long-run options but not the single best, because its value depends on jobs, capital and good health [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Best way" asks for a comparison. Name at least one alternative and say why it might do better or worse.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "per 1,000" or the percentage sign.
- Treating immigration as net migration without subtracting emigration.
- Choosing the largest total output as the optimum, not the highest output per head.
- Describing a pyramid without numbers from the table.
- Stating a cause of the development gap without its impact.
- Writing "more people means more growth" with no mention of resources or capital.
- Evaluation that lists points but gives no judgement.
