This page is **higher level (HL) only**. Technology is the third HL extension and is assessed in **Paper 3**, which SL students do not sit. The guide describes the growth of technology, including mobile phones, computers, video games and artificial intelligence, as a significant influence on human behaviour and relationships. It is studied inside the four contexts. The guide prints areas of inquiry and no named studies, so the research below consists of examples.

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology-practice/).

## What the guide says

The guide links technology to all four contexts: we rely on technology for health and well-being advice and for communication in relationships, we are familiar with screen time concerns in development and with the effects of technology in the classroom on learning and cognition. It is clearly linked to **change**, and also to **perspective** and **responsibility**.

It then lists the difficulties of studying technology.

- It is hard to **isolate variables** under natural conditions: people who use technology more differ in many other ways.
- Research often shows **sampling bias** (for example volunteer or student samples), which limits generalisability.
- Much research is **correlational**, so **bidirectional ambiguity** arises when interpreting findings.
- There is high potential for **researcher bias** or **confirmation bias**, especially in **action research** in schools.
- Because the topic is new, there is **publication bias**, and there are questions about the **funding** of research.
- Researchers may make assumptions about the positive or negative effects of technology, and findings from various studies have **not been replicated**, which contributes to the **replication crisis** in psychology.

## Example evidence by context

These are examples. Select ones you know well and always note the design.

**Health and well-being.**
- *Screen time and well-being.* Twenge and colleagues (2018) used large national surveys of adolescents and reported that higher screen time was associated with lower psychological well-being. Orben and Przybylski (2019) reanalysed large datasets using a method that tests many reasonable analysis choices (specification curve analysis) and concluded that technology use explained only a very small part of the variation in adolescent well-being, about 0.4 per cent in their main results. The contrast shows how analysis decisions and effect size change the conclusion. Both are correlational.
- *Tele-therapy.* Delivering therapy by video or phone widens access, particularly for people in remote areas, and evidence suggests that for several conditions it can work about as well as face-to-face therapy. Limits include differences in how studies measure outcomes, digital exclusion and drop-out.
- *Technology in prevention.* Apps and reminders that support exercise or sleep depend on engagement; evidence of lasting change is mixed.

**Human development.**
- Screen time and attachment: concern that parents' phone use reduces responsive interaction. Most evidence is correlational and self-reported.
- Artificial intelligence and simulation can be used to test models of development, for example by simulating how a learner might develop language from input. Simulations show what is possible, not what actually happens in children.

**Human relationships.**
- Kramer, Guillory and Hancock (2014) altered the emotional content of the news feeds of roughly 689,000 Facebook users for a week and found small shifts in the emotional tone of the users' own posts (emotional contagion). It is an experiment on a very large sample with a measure based on language. Evaluation: very small effect sizes, language analysis is an indirect measure of emotion, and there were serious ethical objections because users did not give informed consent or have a debrief.
- Social media and group behaviour: online communities can spread norms and support, but also polarisation. Technology can help acculturation, for example by connecting migrants to a community in their host country, but may also reduce contact with the host culture.

**Learning and cognition.**
- Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011) reported that when people expect information to be available on a computer, they are less likely to remember the information itself and more likely to remember where to find it.
- Ward and colleagues (2017) reported that the mere presence of one's smartphone reduced available cognitive capacity in a lab setting. Attempts to replicate this have been mixed, so it is a useful example of the replication problem.
- Technology in the classroom: studies of laptop use during lectures have often found a negative association with learning, but results depend on how the technology is used.

## Research problems for Paper 3

1. **Correlation and causation.** A graph showing that more hours of social media use go with lower well-being does not show that use causes lower well-being. Poorer well-being may lead to more use, or a third variable such as loneliness may affect both.
2. **Measurement.** "Screen time" is often self-reported, which is inaccurate; "well-being" varies with the scale. Logging apps are more objective but see only one device.
3. **Effect size.** A statistically significant result can still be trivially small.
4. **Sampling bias.** Studies with students, or with volunteers recruited online, do not represent everyone.
5. **Researcher and confirmation bias.** Researchers may expect harm or benefit. Action research by the school's own teacher may be biased.
6. **Publication bias and funding.** Studies finding effects are published more often. Technology companies may fund or restrict access to data.
7. **Replication.** Check whether the effect has been found again by other teams.
8. **Responsibility.** Large-scale online experiments without consent; advice to parents that overstates certainty; the stigma attached to people described as "addicted".

## Worked mini-analysis (constructed for practice)

A researcher surveyed 500 teenagers about the hours they spent on a video-sharing app per day and their score on a sleep quality scale from 0 to 21 (higher = worse). She reported a correlation coefficient of r = +0.45 and concluded that "the app damages sleep".

- **What the data show:** a moderate positive correlation, so more app use goes with worse sleep.
- **What they do not show:** direction or cause. Teenagers who cannot sleep may turn to the app (bidirectional ambiguity), or anxiety may cause both.
- **Measurement:** hours of use are self-reported; the sleep scale is also self-report.
- **Better conclusion:** among these teenagers, heavier use of the app was moderately related to poorer reported sleep; an experiment or longitudinal study would be needed to test whether the app causes the difference.

## Common mistakes

- Concluding that technology is "bad" or "good" without the design of the evidence.
- Treating all technology as one thing. Messaging, video games, passive scrolling and tele-therapy differ.
- Not mentioning ethical issues in online experiments.
- Quoting a correlation coefficient without saying what it can and cannot show.
- Ignoring replication.
