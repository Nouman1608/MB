## Section A

**1.** Identify the EU institution that is directly elected. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** functions of the Commission. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by a regulation being "directly applicable". **[2]**

**4.** Identify the date on which the United Kingdom left the European Union. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain the composition and functions of the Council of the European Union. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the role of the Court of Justice of the European Union in giving preliminary rulings. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the role of the European Parliament in making EU law, and how its role has changed. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how treaty articles can create rights for individuals, using **one** case. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **three** ways in which an individual could rely on a directive that a member state had failed to implement. **[6]**

**10.** In 2015, before withdrawal, a (fictional) directive required member states to give all workers a right to paid leave for caring responsibilities. The UK did not implement it. Sana works for a county council and Jamal for a private company; both are refused leave. Advise each on their rights at that time. **[6]**

**11.** Explain the impact of the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 and the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 on EU law in England and Wales. **[5]**

**12.** "Membership of the European Union had a major impact on the law of England and Wales." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The European Parliament [1].

**2.** Any two: proposing legislation [1]; implementing EU policies and managing the budget; enforcing the treaties against member states; negotiating trade agreements [1].

**3.** It becomes law in every member state automatically [1], without any national legislation being needed [1].

**4.** 31 January 2020 [1].

**5.** The Council consists of one government minister from each member state, with the minister depending on the subject being discussed [1]. It adopts EU legislation jointly with the Parliament [1], coordinates member states' policies and approves the budget with the Parliament [1]. Many decisions use qualified majority voting, though sensitive areas such as taxation require unanimity [1].

**6.** Under Article 267 TFEU a national court can refer a question on the interpretation of EU law to the CJEU [1]. The CJEU interprets the law, and the national court then applies that interpretation to the facts of the case [1]. This ensures EU law is interpreted uniformly across all member states [1]. A national court of final appeal must normally refer unless the answer is already clear, as held in *CILFIT* (1982); Lord Denning gave guidelines on referral for English courts in *Bulmer v Bollinger* (1974) [1].

**7.** Under the ordinary legislative procedure the Parliament shares law-making power equally with the Council [1] and can amend or reject proposals made by the Commission [1]. It also approves the budget and supervises the Commission [1]. Its role has grown from a largely consultative body to a co-legislator, increasing the democratic accountability of EU law making [1].

**8.** A treaty article that is clear, precise and unconditional has direct effect [1], meaning individuals can rely on it in national courts [1]. It can be enforced against the state and against private parties [1]. In *Defrenne v Sabena (No 2)* (1976) the Court of Justice held the treaty article on equal pay could be relied on against private employers as well as the state (also accept *Macarthys v Smith*, 1980) [1].

**9.** Vertical direct effect: if the directive is clear and unconditional and the deadline has passed, the individual can rely on it against the state or an emanation of the state, as in *Marshall* (1986) [1] [1]. Indirect effect: the national court must interpret national law, as far as possible, in the light of the directive, even in a dispute between private parties, as in *Marleasing* (1990) [1] [1]. State liability: the individual can claim damages from the state for loss caused by the failure to implement, as in *Francovich* (1991) [1] [1].

**10.** Sana: a county council is a public body and an emanation of the state, applying the test in *Foster v British Gas* (1990) [1]. If the directive is clear and unconditional and the deadline has passed, she can rely on vertical direct effect against her employer, as in *Marshall* (1986) [1]. Jamal: his employer is private, and directives do not have horizontal direct effect, as confirmed in *Faccini Dori* (1994) [1]. He could ask the court to interpret any relevant national law consistently with the directive under indirect effect [1]. If that is impossible, he could claim damages from the state under *Francovich* (1991) [1]. Sana therefore has a direct claim, while Jamal's routes are indirect [1].

**11.** The 2018 Act repealed the European Communities Act 1972 from exit day, 31 January 2020 [1], and kept EU-derived law in force at the end of the implementation period (31 December 2020) as retained EU law, so existing rights continued [1]. The 2023 Act renamed this body of law assimilated law from 1 January 2024 [1] and ended the principle of supremacy of EU law and the general principles of EU law in relation to it [1]. The Court of Appeal and Supreme Court can depart from retained EU case law, and new CJEU decisions do not bind UK courts [1].

**12.** For the statement: EU law was supreme, so in *Factortame* (1990) courts disapplied an Act of Parliament, a major change to parliamentary supremacy [1]; courts adopted a purposive approach to interpretation, as in *Pickstone v Freemans* (1988) [1]; large areas of substantive law, such as equal pay, working time and consumer protection, came from EU law [1] [1]; English courts referred questions to the CJEU and were bound by its rulings [1]; individuals gained rights enforceable through direct effect and state liability [1]. Against: large areas, such as most criminal law and much of tort and family law, were largely unaffected [1]; Parliament retained the legal power to withdraw, which it exercised [1]. Conclusion: the impact was major, especially on the constitution and employment and consumer law, and much of it continues as assimilated law [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the composition of an institution without its functions, or the reverse.
- In directive scenarios, failing to check whether the defendant is the state or a private party.
- Forgetting indirect effect and state liability as alternatives to direct effect.
- Writing about the EU only in the past tense, without explaining what remains after withdrawal.
