Together they test every learning outcome from 4.1.3(a) to (l) in Module 4, Core organic chemistry. H432 is the full A level. Questions 6, 8 and 11 are longer, mixed problems.

Data for this set: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0, F = 19.0, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40.1. Molar gas volume = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at RTP.

## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define the term electrophile. **[1]**
**(b)** State what happens to the bonding electrons when a covalent bond undergoes heterolytic fission. **[1]**

**2.** This question is about the C=C bond in alkenes.

**(a)** Describe how the σ-bond and the π-bond in C=C are formed. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why there is no rotation about a C=C bond. **[1]**
**(c)** State the bond angle around each carbon of a C=C bond and explain this value. **[2]**

**3.** **(a)** State whether each compound has E/Z isomers.
(i) 2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃
(ii) 1-chloro-2-methylbut-1-ene, ClCH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃
(iii) hept-3-ene, CH₃CH₂CH=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃ **[3]**
**(b)** In one isomer of 1-chloro-2-methylbut-1-ene, the Cl atom and the CH₃ group are on the same side of the double bond. Use CIP priority rules to decide whether this is the E or the Z isomer. Explain your answer. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why this compound cannot be described using cis–trans names. **[1]**

**4.** Octa-2,5-diene has the structural formula CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH=CHCH₂CH₃. Deduce the number of stereoisomers it has. Explain your answer. **[3]**

**5.** **(a)** Explain, in terms of bonding, why alkenes are much more reactive than alkanes. **[2]**
**(b)** Use these average bond enthalpies, in kJ mol⁻¹, to estimate the enthalpy change when HBr adds to an alkene: C=C 612, H–Br 366, C–C 347, C–H 413, C–Br 290. **[3]**

**6.** 2,5-dimethylhex-3-ene, (CH₃)₂CHCH=CHCH(CH₃)₂, reacts with each reagent below. For each, give any catalyst needed and name the organic product.

**(a)** hydrogen **[2]**
**(b)** chlorine **[2]**
**(c)** steam **[2]**
**(d)** hydrogen bromide **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Describe how bromine water could show whether a liquid hydrocarbon, Z, is unsaturated. Give the result you would see in each case. **[2]**
**(b)** A different hydrocarbon, C₈H₁₂, contains no ring. A 1.62 g sample reacts completely with 720 cm³ of hydrogen, measured at RTP. Deduce the number of C=C bonds in each molecule. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why the test in (a) cannot distinguish hex-1-ene from hex-2-ene. **[1]**

**8.** 3-methylhex-2-ene, CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃, is treated with hydrogen chloride.

**(a)** Describe the mechanism for this reaction, stating where each curly arrow starts and finishes and the intermediate formed. **[4]**
**(b)** Name the main product. Use the intermediates to explain your choice. **[3]**
**(c)** Name the minor product. **[1]**

**9.** Butan-2-ol can be made by reacting but-2-ene with steam.

**(a)** Write an equation for this reaction using structural formulae and name a suitable catalyst. **[2]**
**(b)** 14.0 g of but-2-ene gives 12.6 g of butan-2-ol. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why this reaction gives only one alcohol. **[1]**

**10.** **(a)** 3,3,3-trifluoropropene is CH₂=CHCF₃. Draw the repeat unit of its addition polymer. **[1]**
**(b)** One molecule of this polymer has Mr = 1.44 × 10⁵. How many monomer molecules joined to make it? **[2]**
**(c)** A section of a different polymer is –CF₂–CH(CH₃)–CF₂–CH(CH₃)–. Give the repeat unit and name the monomer. **[2]**

**11.** A council must decide what to do with waste plastics, including PVC, poly(chloroethene).

**(a)** Explain two benefits for sustainability of processing waste polymers instead of sending them to landfill. **[4]**
**(b)** When PVC burns, each repeat unit, –[CH₂–CHCl]–, releases one HCl molecule. Calculate the mass of calcium oxide needed to neutralise the HCl from burning 250 g of PVC. CaO + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how photodegradable and biodegradable polymers each benefit the environment. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** An electron pair acceptor [1].
**(b)** Both electrons go to one of the atoms, forming a cation and an anion [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Something that attacks a double bond" describes behaviour, not the definition; the phrase "electron pair acceptor" is what earns the mark.

**2. (a)** σ-bond: direct, head-on overlap of orbitals on the line joining the carbons [1]. π-bond: p-orbitals on neighbouring carbons overlap sideways, putting electron density above and below the bonding carbons [1].
**(b)** Rotation would break the sideways p-orbital overlap of the π-bond [1].
**(c)** About 120° [1]; three regions of electron density around each carbon repel equally as far apart as possible [1].
*Examiner insight:* Hybridisation is not required, so an answer based on sp² orbitals alone, without describing p-orbital overlap, risks missing the π-bond mark.

**3. (a)** (i) no, C2 carries two CH₃ groups [1]; (ii) yes [1]; (iii) yes [1].
**(b)** C1: Cl has a higher atomic number than H, so Cl has priority [1]. C2: both groups start with C; ethyl's carbon carries (C, H, H) and methyl's (H, H, H), so ethyl has priority [1]. Cl is on the same side as CH₃, so it is opposite the ethyl group: **E** [1].
**(c)** Cis–trans naming needs two of the groups on each C=C carbon to be the same; here the groups are Cl, H and CH₃, CH₂CH₃, with none in common [1].
*Examiner insight:* A bare "E" without both priority comparisons is unlikely to gain full credit; state the atomic numbers or the atoms compared at each carbon.

**4.** Each C=C has two different groups on each carbon, so each can be E or Z [1]. The molecule is not symmetrical (CH₃ at one end, CH₂CH₃ at the other), so (2E,5Z) and (2Z,5E) are different compounds [1]. Stereoisomers: (2E,5E), (2E,5Z), (2Z,5E), (2Z,5Z), so **4** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check for symmetry before you count; doubling for each C=C only works when the two ends of the molecule differ.

**5. (a)** The π-bond has a relatively low bond enthalpy, so it breaks more easily than a σ-bond [1]. Its electron density lies above and below the plane, exposed to attack by electrophiles [1].
**(b)** Bonds broken: C=C + H–Br = 612 + 366 = 978 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. Bonds formed: C–C + C–H + C–Br = 347 + 413 + 290 = 1050 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. ΔH = 978 − 1050 = **−72 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Only the bonds that change need counting; include the sign, because a positive value here would describe an endothermic addition.

**6. (a)** Nickel catalyst (with heat) [1]; **2,5-dimethylhexane** [1].
**(b)** No catalyst needed [1]; **3,4-dichloro-2,5-dimethylhexane** [1].
**(c)** Acid catalyst such as phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄ [1]; **2,5-dimethylhexan-3-ol** [1].
**(d)** No catalyst needed [1]; **3-bromo-2,5-dimethylhexane** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each addition here gives one product because the alkene is symmetrical; check that locants in each name are as low as possible.

**7. (a)** Shake a few drops of Z with bromine water [1]. If Z is unsaturated the orange colour disappears (colourless); if saturated it stays orange [1].
**(b)** Mr C₈H₁₂ = 108.0, so amount = 1.62 ÷ 108.0 = 0.0150 mol [1]. Amount of H₂ = 0.720 ÷ 24.0 = 0.0300 mol [1]. Ratio H₂ : hydrocarbon = 2 : 1, so **two C=C bonds** per molecule [1].
**(c)** Both contain a C=C bond, so both decolourise bromine water [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Goes clear" describes transparency, not colour; the expected wording is a change from orange to colourless.

**8. (a)** In H–Cl, H is δ+ and Cl is δ− [1]. Curly arrow from the C=C bond to the H, and from the H–Cl bond to the Cl [1]. A carbocation forms, with Cl⁻ [1]. Curly arrow from a lone pair on Cl⁻ to the positive carbon [1].
**(b)** **3-chloro-3-methylhexane** [1]. H adding to C2 puts the positive charge on C3, a tertiary carbocation; H adding to C3 gives a secondary carbocation on C2 [1]. The tertiary carbocation is more stable, so more product forms through it (Markownikoff's rule) [1].
**(c)** **2-chloro-3-methylhexane** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the carbocation types for both routes; stating "Markownikoff's rule" alone, without comparing tertiary and secondary carbocations, gives little to credit.

**9. (a)** CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + H₂O → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ [1]; phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄ [1].
**(b)** Amount of but-2-ene = 14.0 ÷ 56.0 = 0.250 mol [1]. Maximum mass of butan-2-ol = 0.250 × 74.0 = 18.5 g [1]. Yield = 12.6 ÷ 18.5 × 100 = **68.1%** [1].
**(c)** But-2-ene is symmetrical, so adding OH to either C=C carbon gives the same alcohol [1].
*Examiner insight:* Set out the theoretical mass as a separate line; if the final percentage is wrong, visible working is the only thing left that can earn credit.

**10. (a)** –[CH₂–CH(CF₃)]–, with an extension bond on each side and CF₃ as a side group [1].
**(b)** Mr C₃H₃F₃ = 96.0 [1]. Monomer molecules = 1.44 × 10⁵ ÷ 96.0 = **1500** [1].
**(c)** Repeat unit –[CF₂–CH(CH₃)]– [1]; monomer CF₂=CHCH₃, **1,1-difluoropropene** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A repeat unit must contain exactly two main-chain carbons from each monomer and no C=C; extension bonds should cross the brackets.

**11. (a)** Burning waste polymers releases their stored energy for electricity [1], saving fuel and reducing landfill [1]. Using waste as an organic feedstock for new plastics and chemicals [1] reduces the use of crude oil, a finite resource [1].
**(b)** Mr repeat unit = 62.5; amount = 250 ÷ 62.5 = 4.00 mol, so 4.00 mol HCl [1]. Amount of CaO = 4.00 ÷ 2 = 2.00 mol [1]. Mass = 2.00 × 56.1 = **112 g** [1].
**(c)** Photodegradable polymers break down when they absorb light [1]; biodegradable polymers are broken down by microorganisms; both reduce persistent plastic waste [1].
*Examiner insight:* For "explain two benefits", each benefit needs the action and its consequence; listing "recycling" and "burning" without saying what each saves earns little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the π-bond as lying between the nuclei rather than above and below them.
- Missing that a carbon with two identical groups rules out E/Z isomerism.
- Ranking CIP groups by size instead of comparing atomic numbers atom by atom.
- Ignoring symmetry when counting stereoisomers of a diene.
- Forgetting the sign on an enthalpy change from bond enthalpies.
- Drawing the first curly arrow from a carbon atom or to the wrong end of H–X.
- Leaving out the lone pair on the halide ion in the final step.
- Naming the minor product as the major one in Markownikoff questions.
- Keeping C=C in a repeat unit or putting a side group into the main chain.
