## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Human resource management (HRM) | Planning, recruiting, developing, rewarding and looking after employees so the organisation meets its objectives |
| Human resource planning | Forecasting the number and type of employees needed and matching them to those available |
| Workforce audit | A record of current staff: numbers, skills, ages, contracts and locations |
| Demand for labour | The staff the business will need, set by its plans and expected output |
| Supply of labour | The staff available, from the current workforce and the outside labour market |
| Labour mobility | How easily workers move between jobs (occupational) or places (geographical) |
| Flexi-time | Staff work agreed hours but choose start and finish times, usually around core hours |
| Gig economy | Short-term, freelance or platform-based work paid by task or job |
| Resistance to change | Opposition to, or slowing of, a change by employees or managers |
| Force field analysis (HL tool) | Scoring driving forces for a change against restraining forces |

## Condensed recall

**Role of HRM.** Labour is both a cost to control and the source of the skills the business needs. Every HR decision serves an objective such as growth, cost control, quality or reputation.

**HR planning steps.**

1. Audit the current workforce.
2. Forecast demand for labour from the business plan.
3. Forecast supply: who stays, who retires or leaves, and what the market offers.
4. Find the gap, skill by skill.
5. Act (recruit, train, redeploy or reduce) and review.

**Internal factors:** business objectives and growth plans, the existing workforce (ages, skills, expected leavers), the HR budget, structure and culture, and working patterns such as flexi-time.

**External factors:** demographic change, labour mobility, immigration, the gig economy and wider STEEPLE changes such as new employment laws or technology.

**Two-way factors.** Higher labour mobility helps a business recruit but also helps rivals poach its staff. The gig economy gives flexible labour for peaks but weak loyalty and limited firm-specific skills.

**Reasons for resistance:** fear of job loss or reduced pay, loss of status, fear of the unknown, lack of skills or confidence, low trust in management, genuine disagreement with the change, extra workload during the switch, and loss of established teams.

**Matching strategy to cause.**

* Fear of job loss: job security promises, redeployment, voluntary redundancy.
* Misunderstanding or low trust: early, honest communication of reasons and timescale.
* Skills gap: training before the change starts.
* Disagreement: consultation and participation.
* Disrupted routine: phased introduction or pilot.

## Worked mini-example

Brackenmoor Freight, an invented haulage firm, employs 120 drivers. 30 are aged 58 or over, which is 25% of the workforce. It plans a new depot needing 20 more drivers. If 15 older drivers retire within two years and 8 others leave, supply from current staff is 120 − 15 − 8 = 97. Demand is 140, so the gap is 43 drivers, more than double the 20 the depot alone suggests.

External factor: few young people hold heavy-vehicle licences (a demographic and skills factor), so supply from the market is tight. A sensible response is a funded licence-training scheme, with gig drivers covering seasonal peaks only.

## Force field reminder (HL tool)

* List driving forces and restraining forces, and score each, for example 1 to 5.
* If driving forces total more, the change can go ahead, but the plan is fragile if a restraint is strong.
* Weakening the strongest restraining force usually works better than adding pressure.

## Exam traps

* Writing "HRM hires and fires staff" as the whole role. Link activities to objectives.
* Forgetting supply from the current workforce when planning. Retirements and leavers widen the gap.
* Calling a factor internal when it comes from outside, such as a change in immigration rules.
* Treating all resistance as irrational. Experienced staff may have spotted a real flaw.
* Giving the same strategy for every cause of resistance.
* Listing strategies with no limitation in an AO3 question.
* Ending "recommend" or "evaluate" answers without a judgement and its condition.

## Quick self-test

1. State the four content points of section 2.1.
2. Define human resource planning.
3. What is a workforce audit?
4. Give two internal and two external factors that influence HR planning.
5. Explain how higher labour mobility can help and harm the same business.
6. A firm has 80 staff, expects 6 retirements and 4 other leavers, and needs 90 staff next year. What is the gap?
7. Why might a supervisor resist a restructure more than a shop-floor worker?
8. Which strategy best fits resistance caused by a skills gap, and why?
9. Give one limitation of consultation as a strategy.
10. What is flexi-time, and how does it affect HR planning?
11. In force field analysis, why reduce restraining forces rather than add driving forces?
12. What does AO3 require in a 2.1 strategies answer?

### Answers

1. Role of HRM; internal and external factors influencing HR planning; reasons for resistance to change; HR strategies for reducing the impact of change and resistance to change.
2. Forecasting the number and type of employees a business will need and matching them to the staff available.
3. A record of the current workforce, such as numbers, skills, ages, contracts and locations.
4. Internal: growth plans, ageing workforce, HR budget, flexi-time. External: demographic change, immigration, labour mobility, gig economy (any two of each).
5. It can attract workers from other regions or jobs, but rivals can also attract its staff away, so retention becomes harder.
6. Supply = 80 − 6 − 4 = 70; gap = 90 − 70 = 20 staff.
7. A restructure may remove or merge supervisory roles, so the supervisor fears losing status, authority or the job itself.
8. Training before the change, because it removes the cause of anxiety rather than rewarding compliance.
9. Any one: it slows decisions; staff ideas may be rejected, causing frustration; it takes time away from work.
10. Staff choose start and finish times around core hours. Planners must make sure enough people are present at busy times, but it can widen the pool of applicants.
11. Pressure can harden opposition, while removing a restraint lowers resistance directly and makes the change more secure.
12. Weigh at least two strategies in context, give limitations, and reach a justified judgement stating what it depends on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is section 2.1 SL or HL in IB Business Management?**
Section 2.1 applies to both SL and HL. Force field analysis, which the guide suggests as a tool for Unit 2, is HL only.

**What is the difference between demand for labour and supply of labour in HR planning?**
Demand is the staff the business will need, based on its plans. Supply is the staff available, from the current workforce after leavers and retirements, and from the outside labour market.
