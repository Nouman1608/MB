## The bank statement

A bank statement is a record, produced by the bank, of every payment into and out of the business's bank account over a period, with a running balance. The business did not write it, which is exactly why it is useful.

**Use.** The business compares the statement with its own cash book, line by line.

**Purpose.** It gives an independent check on the cash book. Through it the business can:

- confirm that receipts and payments in the cash book really reached or left the account;
- find entries the bank has made that the firm has not yet written up, such as charges or a transfer from a customer;
- spot mistakes in the cash book or by the bank;
- find out how much money the business can safely spend;
- deter and detect fraud, since a payment nobody authorised will show on the statement.

A statement may label columns "paid out" and "paid in" or use debit and credit from the bank's own point of view. Read the words and the direction of the money, not just the heading. A favourable balance on your statement is money the bank holds for you.

## Why the balances differ

The cash book bank column and the bank statement describe the same account, yet their closing balances are rarely equal. There are four causes, and each needs a different response.

| Cause | Example | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| The bank has recorded it and you have not | Bank charges, interest, credit transfer, direct debit, standing order | Enter it in the cash book |
| You recorded it wrongly | Cheque written for $315 but entered as 351 | Correct the cash book |
| Timing: you recorded it and the bank has not yet | Unpresented cheques, uncredited deposits | Show on the reconciliation statement only |
| The bank recorded it wrongly | A payment taken from your account by mistake | Show on the reconciliation statement and tell the bank |

An **unpresented cheque** is a cheque you have issued and entered in the cash book which the bank has not yet paid. An **uncredited deposit** (also called an outstanding lodgement) is money you have paid in and entered in the cash book which the bank has not yet added to your account. Neither is a mistake. Both disappear without any action once the bank catches up.

## Updating the cash book

Before reconciling, bring the cash book up to date. Each item below is entered in the cash book on the side shown.

| Item | Cash book side | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Bank charges | Credit | Money leaves the account |
| Bank interest paid (for example on borrowing) | Credit | Money leaves the account |
| Bank interest received | Debit | Money enters the account |
| Credit transfer received (a customer pays direct) | Debit | Money enters the account |
| Direct debit (a supplier collects an amount) | Credit | Money leaves the account |
| Standing order (the bank pays a fixed sum on set dates) | Credit | Money leaves the account |
| Correction of an error in the cash book | Debit if the cash book showed too little received or too much paid; credit in the opposite case | Make the cash book match what really happened |

Each entry also needs its other half in the ledger (for example the insurance expense for a standing order, or the customer's account for a credit transfer). That second entry is part of the same double entry, so do not leave it out when a question asks for ledger or journal entries.

**Step 1: tick off.** Match every line in the cash book with the statement. Cheques 0418 to 0421, and the receipts from Ormerod Ltd and Penhale Ltd, appear in both.
Cheque 0422 and the $1,740 deposit appear only in the cash book. The transfer, standing order, direct debit, interest and charges appear only on the statement.
Cheque 0421 is $315 on the statement
but 351 in the cash book. Cheque 0431 is not one Larkhaven issued: its cheques stop at 0422.

**Step 2: update the cash book.**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance b/d (from above) | 3,794 | Standing order, insurance | 95 |
| Credit transfer, Calder Nurseries | 640 | Direct debit, electricity | 164 |
| Interest received | 9 | Bank charges | 32 |
| Error: cheque 0421 (351 less 315) | 36 | Balance c/d | 4,188 |
| | **4,479** | | **4,479** |

The corrected balance is **$4,188**. Check: 3,794 + 640 + 9 + 36 − 95 − 164 − 32 = 4,188. Cheque 0422, the deposit and cheque 0431 stay out of the cash book.

**Step 3: reconcile.**

| Bank reconciliation statement at 30 April 2027 | Amount |
|---|---|
| Balance per updated cash book | 4,188 |
| Add: unpresented cheque 0422 | 520 |
| | 4,708 |
| Less: uncredited deposit | 1,740 |
| Less: bank error (cheque 0431 debited to the account in error) | 85 |
| **Balance per bank statement** | **2,883** |

The result equals the closing balance on the statement, so every difference is explained. If it did not, a difference would remain to hunt for. Larkhaven should now tell the bank about the $85.

## Which way does each item go?

The statement above starts from the cash book and works towards the statement. In that direction, ask what the bank statement is missing or has got wrong:

- An unpresented cheque has not yet reduced the statement, so the statement is too high. Add it to reach the statement balance.
- An uncredited deposit has not yet increased the statement, so the statement is too low. Deduct it.
- A payment the bank took in error has made the statement too low. Deduct it.
- Money the bank added in error has made the statement too high. Add it.

If you work the other way, from the statement balance to the cash book, every sign reverses. Pick one direction and use it consistently. Label the first line and the last line clearly, so it is obvious which balance is which.

**Overdrawn balances.** The same method works if the business owes the bank. Treat an overdrawn cash book balance as a negative figure, then add and deduct exactly as before. Charges and interest paid on the overdraft are entered on the credit side and make the overdraft larger.

## Digital business transactions

The syllabus asks about the impact of digital business transactions, such as card payments, online payments and bank transfers, on bank reconciliation. The effects to be able to explain:

- Many payments clear within hours, so fewer unpresented cheques and uncredited deposits build up and the two balances often start closer together.
- Bank feeds and online banking let a business reconcile daily or weekly rather than once a month, so errors and unauthorised payments are found sooner.
- Software can suggest matches between the bank feed and the records, which saves time on routine ticking off.
- Direct debits, standing orders, charges and interest still reach the bank first, so the cash book still needs updating.
- Mistakes such as a duplicated or wrongly keyed payment, or an automated match to the wrong item, still need a person to review them.

## Common errors

- Entering unpresented cheques or uncredited deposits in the cash book. They are already there.
- Adding bank charges to the cash book debit side. They are an outflow, so they go on the credit side.
- Beginning the reconciliation with the old, un-updated cash book figure.
- Using the wrong sign for a bank error. Ask whether the statement is too high or too low.
- Treating a cash book error as a bank error. A cash book error is corrected in the cash book.
- Leaving the reconciliation without a heading, or without stating which balance is which.
