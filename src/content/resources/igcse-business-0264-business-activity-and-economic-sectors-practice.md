## Questions

**1.** Identify the factor of production shown in each of the following. **(a)** Three hectares of rented farmland. **(b)** The programmers employed by a software firm. **(c)** A packing machine in a factory. **(d)** The owner who takes the risk of starting a business and organises production. **[4]**

**2.** Sandrowe Scarves sells each scarf for 18.00. The wool, dye and packaging it buys in cost 11.20 per scarf. It sells 340 scarves a month.

**(a)** Define added value. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total added value for the month. **[2]**

**3.** Sandrowe Scarves finds that the cost of bought-in inputs rises to 12.40 per scarf. The price stays at 18.00 and it still sells 340 scarves.

**(a)** Calculate the new added value per scarf. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate by how much the total monthly added value falls. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one way Sandrowe Scarves could increase added value. **[3]**

**4.** Ottersby Bakery can spend its savings on one of three projects: a new oven (expected extra profit 4,200 a year), a second van (3,600 a year) or a shop extension (3,100 a year). It chooses the oven.

**(a)** Define opportunity cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the opportunity cost of choosing the oven and justify your answer. **[2]**

**5.** Gurnard Fisheries catches fish at sea. Nashgrove Foods buys the fish, freezes it and packs it into family meals. A shop sells the packed meals to the public.

**(a)** State the economic sector (primary, secondary or tertiary) in which each of the three businesses mainly operates. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a business such as Gurnard Fisheries might decide to operate in more than one sector. **[3]**

**6.** **(a)** Define the private sector. **[2]**
**(b)** Define the public sector. **[2]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the usual aims of a private sector business differ from those of a public sector organisation. **[3]**

**8.** Mirabane Cafe sells 2,500 drinks a month at 3.60 each. The coffee, milk and cups it buys in cost 1.35 per drink. Rent, wages and other costs come to 4,100 a month. The owner is thinking of raising the price to 3.90, and expects sales to fall to 2,300 drinks a month.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per drink at the current price. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total monthly added value at the current price. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how much of the added value is left after the rent, wages and other costs are paid. **[1]**
**(d)** Analyse the effect of the price rise on the total monthly added value. **[3]**

**9.** Caldmoor Farm Shop has 20,000 to invest and can do only one of the following. Project A is a greenhouse, expected to give extra profit of 5,400 a year and to let the farm grow crops through more of the year. Project B is a cold store, expected to give 4,800 a year and to reduce wasted produce. Project C is a shop extension, expected to give 6,100 a year but only if more customers visit.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost if the owner chooses Project C. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate which project the owner should choose. **[6]**

**10.** Drumlin Timber owns a forest and a workshop. The workshop can make one product line this year, not both. Option 1 is flat-pack plank sets, sold at 95 each, with bought-in fixings, finish and packaging costing 38 per set, and 600 sets a month. Option 2 is finished tables, sold at 210 each, with bought-in hardware and finish costing 120 per table, and 250 tables a month. The wood comes from the firm's own forest.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per unit for each option. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total monthly added value for each option. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate which option Drumlin Timber should choose. **[6]**

**11.** For each organisation, state both its sector by type of activity (primary, secondary or tertiary) and its sector by ownership (private or public). **(a)** A hospital run by the government. **(b)** An iron-ore mine owned by a group of private investors. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** Land [1]. **(b)** Labour [1]. **(c)** Capital [1]. **(d)** Enterprise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* One word per part is enough. "Money" for the machine or "wages" for the programmers names a payment, not a factor.

**2. (a)** Added value is the difference between the selling price of a product [1] and the cost of the bought-in materials or inputs used to make it [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Added value per scarf = 18.00 - 11.20 = 6.80 [1]. Total = 6.80 x 340 = **2,312** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the per-unit step before multiplying, so a slip in the second step can still be seen as sound method.

**3. (a)** 18.00 - 12.40 = **5.60** per scarf [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New total = 5.60 x 340 = 1,904 [1]. Fall = 2,312 - 1,904 = **408** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Raise the selling price, for example to 21.00 [1], by improving the design or the brand so that customers are willing to pay more [1]. With input costs of 12.40, added value per scarf becomes 8.60, which is above the original 6.80 (provided sales hold up) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A "way" gains most when followed by its effect on price or input cost. "Make more scarves" changes quantity, not added value per unit.

**4. (a)** Opportunity cost is the benefit of the next best alternative [1] that is given up when a choice is made [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The second van, worth 3,600 a year of extra profit [1], because it is the next best alternative to the oven; the extension (3,100) is lower and so is not counted [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason that ranks the options shows you know only the next best alternative counts, not the sum of 3,600 and 3,100.

**5. (a)** Gurnard Fisheries is primary [1]. Nashgrove Foods is secondary [1]. The shop is tertiary [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** A business can operate in more than one sector [1]. For example, Gurnard Fisheries could freeze and pack some of its own catch, or open its own shop [1]. It would then add value at more than one stage and keep that value for itself, instead of leaving it to other firms [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify by what the business mainly does. Links to the chain of production and to adding value tie the explanation to this business.

**6. (a)** The private sector is made up of businesses and organisations owned and controlled by individuals or groups of individuals [1] and not by the government (such as owners or shareholders) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The public sector is made up of organisations owned and controlled by the government [1], national or local [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Ownership and control separate the two definitions. Examples alone, with no statement of who owns the organisation, leave a definition incomplete.

**7.** Private sector businesses usually aim mainly to make a profit [1], whereas public sector organisations usually aim mainly to provide a service to the public [1]. For example, a private clinic may set fees to earn a profit, while a government clinic may aim to treat everyone who needs care [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The word "usually" matters, since private firms can have social aims and public bodies must control costs.

**8. (a)** 3.60 - 1.35 = **2.25** per drink [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 2.25 x 2,500 = **5,625** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 5,625 - 4,100 = **1,525** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** New added value per drink = 3.90 - 1.35 = 2.55 [1]. New total = 2.55 x 2,300 = 5,865 [1]. This is 240 more than 5,625, so total added value rises even though sales fall, because the extra 0.30 on each drink outweighs the 200 lost sales [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs the comparison with the old figure and a reason. Stating 5,865 alone is only a calculation.

**9. (a)** The next best alternative to Project C is Project A, at 5,400 a year [1]. The opportunity cost is therefore **5,400** a year of extra profit given up [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Project C has the highest expected return, 6,100, which is 700 more than Project A [1]. However, it only brings that return if more customers visit, so it is the riskiest [1]. Project A returns less but the greenhouse lets the farm grow crops for more of the year, so it depends less on visitors [1]. Project B returns least, 4,800, and is worth choosing only if the saving from less waste is large, which the data does not show [1]. On the figures alone I would choose Project C, because the return is highest and the 700 gain over the opportunity cost is the largest margin [1]. However, if the owner cannot be confident of more visitors, Project A is the safer choice [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement gains credit when it weighs both sides and rests on a reason such as risk or the forgone return. Choosing Project C only because it is largest stays descriptive.

**10. (a)** Option 1: 95 - 38 = **57** per set [1]. Option 2: 210 - 120 = **90** per table [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Option 1: 57 x 600 = **34,200** [1]. Option 2: 90 x 250 = **22,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Option 2 adds more value per unit (90 against 57) [1], but Option 1 gives the higher monthly total, 34,200 against 22,500, a difference of 11,700 [1]. Choosing Option 2 would mean giving up the 34,200 of Option 1, which is its opportunity cost [1]. Added value is not profit: tables may need more skilled labour and machine time, and these costs come out of the added value [1]. The figures also assume all 600 sets or 250 tables sell [1]. On balance, Option 1 is the better choice because its total is far higher, unless its extra labour costs are much larger than those of the tables [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the total as well as the per-unit figure, and notice that the better unit figure does not give the better total.

**11. (a)** Tertiary [1] and public [1]. **(b)** Primary [1] and private [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each organisation needs two labels. Activity and ownership are different classifications, so one label answers half the question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "money" for capital, or naming wages or rent when asked for a factor.
- Including rent and wages in the cost of bought-in inputs when finding added value.
- Giving added value per unit when a monthly total is asked for.
- Defining opportunity cost as the money spent, or listing every rejected option.
- Mixing up the primary, secondary and tertiary classification with private and public.
- Evaluating one side only, or deciding with no reason.
