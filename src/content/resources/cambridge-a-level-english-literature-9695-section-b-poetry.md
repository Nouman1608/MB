Section B of Paper 1 asks for one essay on a poetry set text: a collection or a selection of poems by
one poet, or the *Songs of Ourselves* anthology selection. It carries 25 marks, half of the paper, and
is assessed on AO1 to AO4. This guide focuses on the reading skills the section rewards and on how to
shape each type of answer. For the paper as a whole, see the
[Paper 1 overview](/resources/a-level-english-literature-paper-1-drama-and-poetry/).

## What the section asks of you

On each set text there is a choice. The **(a) question** is an essay that asks you to discuss a theme,
attitude or method across the poems you have studied; usually you choose which poems to write about. The
**(b) question** prints one poem from the selection and asks you to analyse it closely. Texts are not
allowed in the exam room, so for (a) you need short quotations from several poems in memory.

For 2026 the set poetry texts are Maya Angelou's *And Still I Rise*, selected poems from Blake's *Songs of
Innocence and of Experience*, selected poems from Plath's *Ariel* (1965) and a selection from *Songs of
Ourselves, Volume 2*. For 2027 they are selected poems from Zaffar Kunial's *England's Green*, Plath's
*Ariel*, Christina Rossetti's poems and *Songs of Ourselves, Volume 2*. The syllabus names the individual
poems in each selection; learn those poems, not the whole book. Lists change by year, so check yours.

## The key idea: form is part of the meaning

AO2 asks how a poet's choices of **language, form and structure** shape meaning. In poetry, form is
unusually visible: line length, stanza shape, rhyme and rhythm are choices you can see and hear. The
strongest answers treat these as part of the argument of the poem, not as a separate checklist at the
end.

### Voice and address

Start every poem with three questions: **who is speaking, to whom, and in what situation?** The speaker
is a constructed voice (a persona), even when the poem sounds autobiographical. Look for shifts in
address: a poem that moves from "I" to "you", or from describing someone to speaking to them, changes the
relationship between speaker and reader.

### Imagery and diction

- **Patterns, not single images.** A cluster of related images (light, water, enclosure, machinery)
  builds an attitude across a poem or a whole selection.
- **Register.** A sudden technical, childlike or archaic word stands out against the surrounding
  diction, and often marks the poem's emotional centre.
- **Connotation.** Discuss what a word suggests, not just what it denotes.

### Sound, rhythm and metre

You do not need to scan every line, but you should recognise a regular metre and, more importantly,
notice **when it is disturbed**.

- **Iambic** rhythm (unstressed, stressed) is close to natural English speech; **iambic pentameter** has
  five such feet in a line.
- A **trochaic** or stressed opening can give a line force, urgency or a chant-like quality.
- **Caesura** (a pause within a line) and **enjambment** (a sentence running over a line break) control
  pace and emphasis. The word placed at the end of a line, or just after a break, gains weight.
- **Rhyme** can bind ideas together, give closure, or, when it is half-rhyme or broken, suggest something
  incomplete.

### Form and structure

Know the conventions of the forms you are likely to meet, so you can say how a poem uses or departs from
them:

| Form | Convention | What a departure can suggest |
|---|---|---|
| Sonnet | 14 lines; a turn (volta) in argument | A delayed or missing turn: an argument that will not resolve |
| Ballad | Quatrains, regular rhyme, narrative, refrain | Dark content in a song-like form: unsettling innocence |
| Dramatic monologue | One speaker addressing a silent listener | The speaker reveals more than they intend |
| Free verse | No fixed metre or rhyme | Line breaks carry the shaping work |
| Villanelle | Two repeated refrains, tight rhyme | Obsession, or a feeling that keeps returning |

Structure is also the **movement of thought**: where does the poem turn, and how does its ending reframe
its opening?

## Writing the (b) answer on a printed poem

1. **Read the question's focus first** and keep it in every paragraph.
2. **Gist in one sentence**: the speaker, situation and what changes by the end.
3. **Follow the poem's movement**: opening, turn, ending. This keeps you analysing structure as you go.
4. **Select small evidence**: a word or phrase, then its effect.
5. **Link outward** once or twice to other poems in the selection, briefly, to show AO1.

### Annotated model paragraph

The stanza below is **original**, written for this guide.

> The house is sold. They've taken down the gate.
> I walk the hedge-line where the garden was
> and count the posts, as if the count could wait
> for one more summer, one more hour. It does.

A strong paragraph might read:

> The stanza opens with two flat, end-stopped statements, so the loss is presented as accomplished fact
> before the speaker can resist it. The enjambment that follows ("could wait / for one more summer")
> stretches the sentence across the line break, enacting the wish to delay. The regular rhyme
> ("gate"/"wait") seems to promise order, but the final, abrupt "It does." breaks the rhythm with a
> caesura and a short sentence, an ambiguous answer: the count waits, but the speaker's hope is left
> unconfirmed.

Notice that each technique is tied to an effect on meaning at that exact point.

## Writing the (a) essay across the selection

An (a) question typically asks you to discuss ways in which the poet presents a subject or uses a method
**in two poems** or **in your selection**. Follow the wording exactly: if it says two poems, write on two.

- **Choose poems that let you argue.** Two poems that treat the focus differently give you a comparison
  with direction.
- **Integrate rather than alternate.** Avoid writing all of one poem then all of the other; move between
  them by point of similarity or contrast.
- **Keep analysis at line level.** Range does not excuse generality; each paragraph still needs short
  quotation and comment on form.
- **Prepare flexibly.** Learn four or five short, versatile quotations for each poem in the selection,
  plus one feature of its form.

## Command words and question wording

The syllabus's command words include **discuss** (write about a topic in depth in a structured way),
**analyse** (examine in detail to show meaning and how elements relate) and **comment** (give an informed
opinion), alongside phrases such as "In what ways…?" and "How far, and in what ways…?". A question that says "considering form and structure" or
"paying close attention to language" is telling you where the AO2 marks are; make that feature visible in
every paragraph.

## Common mistakes

- **Paraphrase.** Explaining what each stanza "is saying" in turn, with no analysis of how.
- **The technique tour.** Listing alliteration, a metaphor and some enjambment with no shared argument.
- **Poet equals speaker.** Assuming every "I" is the poet's own life, which narrows interpretation.
- **Metre without effect.** Labelling iambic pentameter correctly and then saying nothing about it.
- **Ignoring the ending.** Many poems turn in their final lines; an answer that stops early misses the
  turn.
- **Too many poems in (a).** Brief mentions of five poems are weaker than developed analysis of two.
