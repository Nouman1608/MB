## Answers

**How the parts are marked.** One mark for each correct answer in the 1- and 2-mark questions. In the 4-mark questions, one mark for each point identified and one for explaining it. The 6-mark questions give four AO1 marks on that pattern plus up to two AO2 marks: **one** for a general, accurate use of a source of authority, **two** for a specific use applied to the question. The 12-mark question is marked by levels:

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Thorough examination of a range of points; logical reasoning with detailed evidence; excellent, direct use of the passage |
| 3 | 7–9 | Clear examination of more than one point; reasoning with some detail; good use of the passage |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some accurate examination; reasoning not always supported; general use of the passage. **Maximum Level 2 if only one point is made** |
| 1 | 1–3 | Limited examination with little reasoning. **Maximum Level 1 if the passage is not used** |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit |

**1.** Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i [1].

**2.** Kufa [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: the Qur'an; the Sunnah; Tawhid (belief in one God); the Five Pillars.

**4. (a)** The principles of jurisprudence, or how rulings are drawn from the sources [1].

**(b)** One reason identified [1] and explained [2].

- **First systematic account** [1]: it is often called the first work on the principles of jurisprudence [1], so later scholars of all schools could use its order of reasoning [1].
- **Order of sources** [1]: it fixed Qur'an, Sunnah, Ijma and Qiyas [1], which became the standard Sunni account of sources [1].
- **Authority of Hadith** [1]: it argued that an authentic Hadith outweighs local practice [1], which strengthened the place of the Sunnah in law [1].

**5.** For each of two reasons: reason identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Wording with more than one meaning** [1]: for example *quru'* in 2:228 can mean menstrual periods or periods of purity, so scholars reached different rulings [1].
- **Hadith known in different places** [1]: companions settled in different cities, so scholars knew different reports [1].
- **Different methods** [1]: some used the practice of Madinah, others analogy or the texts alone [1].
- **Expansion of the Muslim world** [1]: new places raised new questions that scholars had to answer [1].

**6.** For each of two features: feature identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Texts first** [1]: Ahmad ibn Hanbal preferred a Hadith or a companion's view to reasoning by analogy [1].
- **Al-Musnad** [1]: his large collection of Hadith shows his expertise in the reports of the Prophet (pbuh) [1].
- **Steadfastness** [1]: he was imprisoned in the mihna for refusing to say the Qur'an was created [1].
- **Regions** [1]: the school is followed mainly in Saudi Arabia and Qatar [1].

**7.** AO1 **[4]**: for each of two ways, way identified [1] and explained [1]. AO2 **[2]**: one source of authority used generally [1] or specifically and applied [2].

Ways (AO1):

- **Practical guidance** [1]: ordinary Muslims can pray, fast and trade correctly without studying the sources themselves [1].
- **Consistency** [1]: courts and communities have a settled body of law [1].
- **Flexibility** [1]: different methods help the law answer new questions [1].
- **Unity** [1]: the schools recognise one another, as shown by the Amman Message (2005) [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you" (Qur'an 4:59), which many scholars take to include the learned [2].
- Mu'adh ibn Jabal (RA) said he would judge by the Qur'an, then the Sunnah, then his reasoning, and the Prophet (pbuh) approved (Abu Dawud) [2].

**8.** AO1 **[4]**: as in question 7. AO2 **[2]**: as in question 7.

Differences (AO1):

- **Founder and Imams** [1]: the Ja'fari school follows Ja'far al-Sadiq, regarded by Twelver Shi'a Muslims as the sixth Imam, and uses the teaching of the Imams of the Prophet's family [1].
- **Use of reason** [1]: it relies on reason ('aql) where Sunni schools use qiyas [1].
- **Ongoing ijtihad** [1]: living senior scholars (marja') continue to give rulings that followers accept [1].
- **Practice** [1]: for example, Ja'fari Muslims regularly combine prayers and prostrate on natural earth [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- Qur'an 4:59: the sources every school refers to [2].
- The Hadith of Mu'adh ibn Jabal (RA) (Abu Dawud): the Sunni schools follow its order of Qur'an, Sunnah, then reasoning, while the Ja'fari school also gives authority to the Imams' teaching [2].

**9.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **For the statement:** following one school gives consistency; it avoids picking easy rulings from each school; it follows scholars who studied the whole system; communities can pray and judge cases in the same way.
- **Against the statement:** the schools are valid ways of following the sources, so Muslims may benefit from the flexibility to follow another school when there is real difficulty (for example, when travelling); many scholars allow a Muslim to follow different schools in different matters if done sincerely; the Qur'an expects disagreement and tells believers to refer to Allah and the Messenger.
- **Balanced view:** following a school is usually safer for ordinary Muslims, but flexibility has a place when it is done with knowledge and sincerity, not to avoid duty.

*Use of the passage*

- "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger" shows that every school starts with the Qur'an and Sunnah.
- "Those in authority among you" supports reliance on qualified scholars.
- "If you disagree over anything" accepts diversity.
- "Refer it to Allah and the Messenger" is the method that all schools share.

Level 4 answers use the passage directly, give reasoning on both sides, and reach a reasoned judgement.
