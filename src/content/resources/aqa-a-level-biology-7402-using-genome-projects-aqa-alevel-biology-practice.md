These questions cover section 3.8.3 of the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), for first teaching 2015: *Using genome projects* (A-level only). All organisms and data are invented. Revise first with the [study guide on using genome projects](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-using-genome-projects-aqa-alevel-biology/) and the [revision notes on using genome projects](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-using-genome-projects-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is the definition of the proteome? **[1]**

A: The complete set of genes in a cell

B: The full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce

C: The proteins being made by a cell at a given moment

D: The amino acid sequence of one polypeptide

**2.** Which **one** of the following is **not** a reason why the genome of a mammal cannot easily be translated into its proteome? **[1]**

A: Many genes contain introns

B: Regulatory genes control the expression of other genes

C: The genetic code is not universal

D: Much of the genome is non-coding DNA

**3.** A gene from a bacterium is 963 bases long, from the first base of the start triplet to the last base of the stop triplet. Calculate the number of amino acids in the polypeptide it codes for. Show your working. **[2]**

**4.** Sequencing methods "have become automated". Explain what this means and give **one** consequence for genome projects. **[2]**

**5.** A eukaryotic gene is 3,000 bases long from its start triplet to its stop triplet. Explain why the polypeptide it codes for may be much shorter than 999 amino acids. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Scientists sequenced the genomes of eight strains of a bacterial pathogen and predicted four proteins as possible vaccine antigens.

| Protein | Location in the bacterium | Number of strains with an identical sequence |
|---|---|---|
| J | Cell surface | 8 of 8 |
| K | Cell surface | 3 of 8 |
| L | Cytoplasm | 8 of 8 |
| N | Cell surface | 6 of 8 |

**(a)** Which protein is the best candidate for a vaccine? Explain your choice using the table. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the chosen protein must still be tested before it is used in a vaccine. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows information about the genomes of three organisms.

| Organism | Genome size (million base pairs) | Number of protein-coding genes | Percentage of genome coding for protein |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 2.1 | 1,950 | 89 |
| Q | 120 | 13,500 | 25 |
| R | 2,400 | 21,000 | 2 |

**(a)** Calculate the number of base pairs in organism Q that code for protein. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of genes per million base pairs for P and for R. **[2]**

**(c)** Use the data to explain why the proteome of R would be harder to determine from its genome than the proteome of P. **[3]**

**8.** A student writes: "The genome of a crop plant has been sequenced, so scientists now know every protein its leaf cells are making." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

**9.** A virus mutates rapidly. Its proteome was used to choose an antigen for a vaccine. Explain why the vaccine may need to be updated in later years. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why the genome of a bacterium can be used to determine its proteome, but the genome of a mammal cannot easily be used in the same way. **[4]**

**11.** A mutation occurs in a regulatory gene in a plant cell. No other gene is changed. Explain how this mutation could change the proteins the cell makes. **[3]**

**12.** A laboratory used an older method to sequence the genome of one bacterial strain in 30 days. An automated machine sequenced 24 strains in 6 days.

**(a)** Calculate how many times faster, per strain, the automated machine was. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** way that sequencing many strains quickly helps the development of vaccines. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**

**2.** C: the genetic code is universal, so this is not a reason [1]. **[1]**

**3.** 963 ÷ 3 = 321 triplets [1]; the stop triplet codes for no amino acid, so 321 − 1 = **320 amino acids** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* forgetting to subtract the stop triplet is the most common error in this type of question.

**4.** Machines and computers carry out the sequencing and read the base order, rather than people doing it by hand [1]; consequence: whole genomes can be sequenced faster and more cheaply, so many organisms or individuals can be sequenced [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The gene contains introns [1], which are removed from pre-mRNA by splicing, so only the exons are translated and the mRNA has fewer triplets than the gene [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** J [1]. It is on the cell surface, so antibodies can reach and bind to it [1]; it has an identical sequence in all eight strains, so the vaccine should protect against every strain [1].

**(b)** The protein is only a potential antigen predicted from the genome [1]; it must be shown to trigger an immune response (and to be safe) before use [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* in (a), use both columns of the table. L fails on location and K and N fail on strains; naming why J beats each shows full understanding.

**7. (a)** 120 million × 0.25 = **30 million** base pairs [1].

**(b)** P: 1,950 ÷ 2.1 = **929** genes per million base pairs [1]. R: 21,000 ÷ 2,400 = **8.75** genes per million base pairs [1].

**(c)** Only 2% of R's genome codes for protein, compared with 89% of P's, so most of R's DNA is non-coding [1]. R's genes are spread out, at about 9 per million base pairs compared with about 929 in P, so locating each gene is harder [1]. R's genes probably contain introns and are controlled by regulatory genes, so the sequence does not directly show which proteins are made [1]. **[6]**

**8.** The genome shows the genes the plant has, so it can help predict proteins the plant is able to make [1]. But leaf cells express only some of their genes, and regulatory genes control which, so the genome does not show which proteins leaf cells are making [1]. Plant genes contain introns and non-coding DNA, so even the full proteome cannot easily be read from the genome; the statement is incorrect [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Mutations change the base sequence of the gene for the antigen [1], so the amino acid sequence and shape of the antigen change [1]; antibodies made in response to the vaccine may no longer bind, so a new antigen must be chosen from the new strain's genome [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Bacterial genes have no introns [1] and the genome has little non-coding DNA, so coding sequences can be translated directly using the genetic code [1]. A mammal's genome is mostly non-coding DNA and its genes contain introns, so genes are hard to identify [1]. Regulatory genes control which genes are expressed, so the sequence does not show which proteins are made [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The regulatory gene codes for a protein, such as a transcription factor, that controls the expression of other genes [1]. The mutation may change this protein so it no longer binds, or binds differently [1]. Genes it controls may be switched on or off, so the cell makes different proteins even though those genes are unchanged [1]. **[3]**

**12. (a)** Older method: 30 days per strain. Automated: 6 ÷ 24 = 0.25 days per strain [1]. 30 ÷ 0.25 = **120 times** faster [1].

**(b)** Any one: antigens shared by all strains can be found quickly; new strains can be sequenced and checked against the vaccine antigen soon after they appear [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining the proteome as proteins being made at one time, rather than those the cell is able to make.
- Forgetting the stop triplet in amino acid calculations.
- Not using the data in the table when asked to explain with reference to it.
- Saying genome projects make vaccines, instead of identifying potential antigens that must be tested.
- Confusing non-coding DNA with regulatory genes.
