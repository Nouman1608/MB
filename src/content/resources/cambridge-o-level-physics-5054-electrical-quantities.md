## 4.2.1 Electrical charge

There are two kinds of charge, **positive** and **negative**, measured in
**coulombs (C)**. **Unlike charges attract; like charges repel.**

**Charging by friction.** Rubbing a polythene rod with a dry cloth moves
electrons from the cloth onto the rod: the rod becomes negative and the
cloth is left equally positive. Rubbing an acetate rod moves electrons
from the rod to the cloth, so the rod becomes positive. Only **negative
charge (electrons)** moves; positive charges stay in the nuclei of the
atoms. An object becomes positive by *losing* electrons, never by gaining
positive charge.

**Experiment.** Hang a charged rod in a paper stirrup on a thread. Bring a
second rod, charged the same way, close to one end: the hanging rod turns
away (repulsion). Bring an oppositely charged rod: it turns towards it
(attraction).

**Electric fields.** An electric field is a **region in which an electric
charge experiences a force**. The direction of a field line at a point is
the direction of the force on a **positive** charge there.

| Arrangement | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Positive point charge | Straight radial lines pointing outwards |
| Negative point charge | Straight radial lines pointing inwards |
| Charged conducting sphere | Radial lines starting at 90° to the surface, outwards if positive |
| Oppositely charged parallel plates | Evenly spaced parallel lines from + plate to − plate (a uniform field) |

**Conductors and insulators.** Metals such as copper and aluminium, and
graphite, are **conductors**; plastics, rubber, glass and dry wood are
**insulators**. To test a material, connect it between two crocodile clips
in a series circuit with a cell and a lamp or ammeter: a lit lamp or a
current reading means a conductor. In the **electron model**, a conductor
contains **free electrons** that can move through the material; in an
insulator the electrons are held to their atoms and cannot move through it.

## 4.2.2 Electrical current

Current is the **rate of flow of charge**: the charge passing a point per
unit time.

```
I = Q / t        1 A = 1 C/s
```

**Worked example 1.** 180 C passes a point in 2.0 minutes. Find the
current.

```
t = 2.0 × 60 = 120 s
I = Q / t = 180 / 120 = 1.5 A
```

**Worked example 2.** A current of 0.25 A flows for 40 s. Find the charge.

```
Q = I t = 0.25 × 40 = 10 C
```

In metals, current is the movement of **free electrons**. By the
convention adopted before electrons were discovered, **conventional
current** flows from **positive to negative** round the circuit; the
electrons themselves flow from **negative to positive**.

**Direct current (d.c.)** flows in one direction only, as from a cell.
**Alternating current (a.c.)** repeatedly reverses direction, as from the
mains.

**Ammeters** are connected **in series**. Choose a range just above the
expected current: a 0–10 A range for a 0.3 A current gives a reading too
small to read precisely, while a 0–100 mA range would be overloaded. On an
analogue meter, read the scale with your eye directly in front of the
needle; on a digital meter, check it reads zero before the circuit is
switched on.

## 4.2.3 E.m.f. and p.d.

Both are energy per unit charge, measured in **volts**, where
**1 V = 1 J/C**. The difference is where the energy goes.

- **E.m.f.** is the electrical work done **by a source** in moving unit
  charge round a complete circuit: E = W / Q.
- **P.d.** is the work done by unit charge passing **through a
  component**: V = W / Q.

**Worked example 3.** A 9.0 V battery drives 20 C round a circuit. A lamp
in the circuit has a p.d. of 3.0 V across it.

```
work done by the battery = E Q = 9.0 × 20 = 180 J
energy transferred in the lamp = V Q = 3.0 × 20 = 60 J
```

**Voltmeters** are connected **in parallel** across the component, and the
range is chosen as for ammeters.

**Sources in series and parallel.** E.m.f.s of sources in series add when
they point the same way; a reversed cell subtracts. Three 1.5 V cells in
series give 4.5 V; with one reversed, 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V. Identical
sources in parallel give the e.m.f. of **one** source: four 1.5 V cells in
parallel still give 1.5 V.

## 4.2.4 Resistance

```
R = V / I        unit: ohm (Ω)
```

**Measuring resistance.** Connect the component in series with an ammeter,
a variable resistor and a supply, with a voltmeter in parallel across the
component. Change the variable resistor to obtain several pairs of V and I,
calculate R = V / I for each (or plot the graph), and average. Switch off
between readings so the wire does not heat up.

**Length and area.** For a wire of one material at constant temperature:

- R is **directly proportional to length**: double the length, double R.
- R is **inversely proportional to cross-sectional area**: double the area,
  halve R.

**Worked example 4.** A 2.0 m length of wire has resistance 8.0 Ω. Find the
resistance of (a) 5.0 m of the same wire, (b) 1.0 m of wire of the same
material with three times the area.

```
(a) R = 8.0 × (5.0 / 2.0) = 20 Ω
(b) R = 8.0 × (1.0 / 2.0) ÷ 3 = 4.0 ÷ 3 = 1.3 Ω
```

Watch questions that give **diameter**: doubling the diameter multiplies
the area by four, so R falls to a quarter.

**Ohm's law.** The current in a metallic conductor is directly
proportional to the p.d. across it, **provided the temperature remains
constant**.

**I–V graphs** (current on the vertical axis, p.d. horizontal):

| Component | Shape | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Resistor (constant temperature) | Straight line through the origin | Constant resistance; obeys Ohm's law |
| Filament lamp | Curve that flattens as V increases, same shape for negative V | Resistance increases as the filament heats up |
| Diode | Almost zero current in reverse; current rises steeply once a small forward p.d. is passed | Lets current through in one direction only |

**Worked example 5.** A filament lamp takes 0.20 A at 2.0 V and 0.30 A at
6.0 V.

```
at 2.0 V: R = 2.0 / 0.20 = 10 Ω
at 6.0 V: R = 6.0 / 0.30 = 20 Ω
```

Tripling the p.d. did not triple the current, because the hotter filament
has a higher resistance.

**Temperature.** As the temperature of a metal such as a lamp filament
rises, its resistance increases: the metal ions vibrate more, so the free
electrons collide with them more often.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a rod becomes positive by gaining positive charge. It loses
  electrons.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds in I = Q / t.
- Drawing field lines on a point charge that curve or point the wrong way.
  Use the force on a *positive* test charge.
- Treating e.m.f. and p.d. as identical. Same unit, different energy
  transfer: e.m.f. is energy given *by* the source, p.d. is energy
  transferred *in* a component.
- Adding the e.m.f.s of identical cells in parallel.
- Stating Ohm's law without "constant temperature".
- Reading resistance from the *gradient* of an I–V graph. Use R = V / I at
  the point; for a straight line through the origin, the gradient is 1 / R.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Define** current, e.m.f. or p.d.: use the syllabus words (charge per
  unit time; work done per unit charge) and name the unit.
- **Explain** charging by friction: name the material that gains
  electrons, the one that loses them, and state that only electrons move.
- **Describe an experiment** to find R: circuit with ammeter in series and
  voltmeter in parallel, a way of varying the current, repeat readings, and
  the calculation.
- **Sketch** an I–V graph: label both axes with quantity and unit, start at
  the origin, and show the correct curvature.
