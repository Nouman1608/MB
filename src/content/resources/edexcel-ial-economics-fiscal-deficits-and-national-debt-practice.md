## Questions

**1.** Country P has revenue of 380, total spending of 412 and GDP of 1,600. Its debt at the start of the year is 700. Next year GDP is 1,650.

**(a)** Calculate the fiscal balance and express it as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the debt at the end of the year and compare the debt ratio with the starting ratio, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why a government running a fiscal surplus can still have a large national debt. **[3]**

**3.** Classify each as an automatic stabiliser or discretionary fiscal policy, and state the difference between the two types. **[4]**
- (i) Unemployment benefit payments rise as firms lay off workers.
- (ii) Parliament votes for a one-year cut in the sales tax rate.
- (iii) Income tax receipts fall as household incomes fall.

**4.** Country R has potential output of 1,200 and actual output of 1,140. Its fiscal deficit is 54. Each unit of output gap changes the balance by 0.30 through automatic stabilisers.

**(a)** Work out how far output is below potential, as a percentage of potential output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cyclical and structural deficits. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the structural deficit as a percentage of potential output. **[1]**
**(d)** State what happens to the deficit if output returns to potential with no policy change. **[1]**

**5.** Country T owes 900 at an average interest rate of 4%. Its revenue is 300.

**(a)** Calculate interest payments and their share of revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** One-third of the debt is refinanced at 7%. Calculate the new interest bill and the rise. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one opportunity cost of higher debt servicing. **[1]**

**6.** Country U has a debt ratio of 75%, an average interest rate of 5% and nominal GDP growth of 3%. Use the formula d(r - g)/(1 + g).

**(a)** Calculate the primary surplus, as a percentage of GDP, that keeps the debt ratio stable. **[3]**
**(b)** Nominal growth rises to 6%. Calculate the new figure and interpret the sign. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how a rise in government borrowing could lead to higher interest rates. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two factors that could increase the size of a country's fiscal deficit. **[6]**

**9.** Country V borrows 400 at 3% to build a water-treatment network. It brings benefits worth 17 a year to users.

**(a)** Calculate the annual interest and the annual net benefit to users. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why borrowing for current spending raises a concern about intergenerational equity. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why borrowing in a deep recession may still be defended on grounds of fairness between generations. **[3]**

**10.** Country S is illustrative. Figures are in billions, with debt of 800 at the start of Year 1.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 540 | 560 | 520 |
| Spending excluding interest | 520 | 560 | 585 |
| Interest payments | 30 | 34 | 37 |
| GDP | 2,000 | 2,040 | 1,980 |

**(a)** Find each year's fiscal deficit relative to GDP, giving percentages to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the debt ratio at the end of Year 3. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse why the deficit rose sharply in Year 3. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that a national debt of 95% of GDP always requires the government to cut its deficit quickly. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Balance = 380 - 412 = **-32** [1]. 32 / 1,600 = **2.0%** deficit [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Debt = 700 + 32 = **732** [1]. Ratio rises from 700 / 1,600 = 43.8% to 732 / 1,650 = **44.4%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the sign or the word deficit, and divide each debt by the GDP of its own year when comparing ratios.

**2.** The debt is a stock built up from past deficits [1]. A surplus reduces it only by the size of the surplus [1], so a small surplus against a large accumulated debt leaves most of the debt in place [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name debt as a stock and the balance as a flow; the contrast is what the question tests.

**3.** (i) **Automatic** [1]. (ii) **Discretionary** [1]. (iii) **Automatic** [1]. Automatic stabilisers change the balance through existing rules with no new decision, while discretionary policy is a deliberate government choice [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-year tax cut is a decision, so it is discretionary even though it also changes the balance; the test is whether anyone had to decide.

**4. (a)** Gap = 1,200 - 1,140 = 60 [1]. 60 / 1,200 = **5.0%** below potential [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cyclical = 0.30 x 60 = **18** [1]. Structural = 54 - 18 = **36** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 36 / 1,200 = **3.0%** of potential output [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** The deficit falls from 54 to **36**, not to zero [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide the gap by potential output, not actual output, and say the structural part remains after recovery.

**5. (a)** Interest = 0.04 x 900 = **36** [1]. 36 / 300 = **12%** of revenue [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New bill = 0.07 x 300 + 0.04 x 600 = 21 + 24 = **45** [1]. Rise = 45 - 36 = **9** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The 9 extra cannot be spent on health or education, or must be financed by higher taxes or more borrowing [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the new rate only to the refinanced part; applying 7% to the whole debt overstates the bill.

**6. (a)** Formula d(r - g)/(1 + g) [1] = 0.75 x 0.02 / 1.03 [1] = **1.46% of GDP** primary surplus [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 0.75 x (0.05 - 0.06) / 1.06 = **-0.71%** [1]. The negative sign means a primary deficit of 0.71% of GDP is consistent with a stable ratio, because growth now exceeds the interest rate [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Interpret the sign; a bare negative number does not say that a primary deficit can be sustained.

**7.** The government sells bonds to cover the deficit, raising the demand for loanable funds [1]. If the supply of savings is limited, the price of borrowing, the interest rate, rises [1]. Lenders may also demand a risk premium if repayment looks doubtful [1]. Higher rates reduce private investment, so crowding out occurs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each link in the chain is separate credit; stopping at "borrowing raises interest rates" leaves out the mechanism.

**8.** Factor one, for example a recession: identified [1]; revenue falls as incomes and spending fall [1], and benefit spending rises through the stabilisers [1]. Factor two, for example an ageing population: identified [1]; more people draw pensions and use health care while fewer workers pay income tax [1], so spending rises faster than revenue [1]. Accept other valid factors such as higher interest rates or a financial rescue. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two different factors and develop each; two versions of the same cause earn little extra.

**9. (a)** Interest = 0.03 x 400 = **12** a year [1]. Net benefit = 17 - 12 = **5** a year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Future taxpayers must pay the interest and repay the debt [1]. Spending that is consumed now leaves no lasting asset [1], so later generations pay for benefits they never receive [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Without borrowing, the government might cut spending or raise taxes in a deep recession, deepening unemployment [1]. Lost output and skills would also harm the same and later generations [1]. Borrowing spreads the cost of an unusual shock and may protect future incomes, so it can be fair [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs the missing asset; "future generations pay" alone does not explain why it is unfair.

**10. (a)** Spending = 550, 594, 622 [1]. Deficits = 10, 34, 102 [1]. Percentages = **0.5%, 1.7%, 5.2%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Debt = 800 + 10 + 34 + 102 = 946 [1]. 946 / 1,980 = **47.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The primary balance swung from +20 to 0 to -65, so higher interest is not the main cause, since interest rose only from 30 to 37 [1]. Revenue fell from 560 to 520 as GDP fell 2.9% [1]. Spending excluding interest rose from 560 to 585, consistent with benefits rising [1]. Much of this is likely cyclical, but potential output is not given, so the structural share cannot be judged [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the primary balance to separate interest from other causes, and say what extra information would settle the cyclical-structural split.

**11.** Debt of 95% of GDP means the stock is almost one year's output [1]. A large debt means large interest payments, which take revenue from other uses and risk a higher risk premium [1], so lenders may raise yields and crowd out investment [1]. Future taxpayers bear repayment, raising an equity concern [1]. However, the debt ratio depends on r and g: with r = 5% and g = 2%, the primary surplus needed to stabilise it is 0.95 x 0.03 / 1.02 = 2.79% of GDP [1], but if r is below g the ratio can stay stable or fall without cuts [1]. Holders and currency matter: domestic debt in the home currency keeps interest in the economy and cannot force default through the exchange rate [1]. If the deficit is cyclical, rapid cuts in a slump reduce AD and revenue, so the ratio may not improve [1], whereas a structural deficit does need action [1]. The purpose matters: borrowing for investment may raise future output and revenue [1]. Judgement: not always; the urgency depends on the gap between r and g, the share that is structural and who holds the debt [1]; cuts are most justified when the deficit is mainly structural and lenders' yields are rising [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 95% figure and a calculated condition in the evaluation, and end with a judgement that names what it depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the deficit and the national debt as one measure.
- Leaving a deficit in cash terms with no percentage of GDP.
- Dividing an output gap by actual rather than potential output.
- Calling automatic stabilisers a policy decision.
- Applying a new interest rate to all debt rather than the refinanced part.
- Stating crowding out without the link through interest rates.
- Writing "future generations pay" without saying what they receive.
- Giving an evaluation with no figures from the question and no conditional judgement.
