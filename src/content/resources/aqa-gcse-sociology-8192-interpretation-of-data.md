This guide covers the "interpretation of data" row of section 3.7 Sociological research methods in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification. The specification asks you to "demonstrate the ability to interpret graphs, diagrams, charts and tables to discern patterns and trends in statistical data". Data items can appear in Paper 1 or Paper 2, set in any topic: families, education, crime or stratification.

The [research methods study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods/) shows one worked example of percentage points. This guide gives a full routine for reading any data item, covers each common calculation with worked answers, and explains the traps that make students misread data. Revise with the [interpretation of data revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-interpretation-of-data-revision-notes/) and practise with the [interpretation of data practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-interpretation-of-data-practice/). All data on this page are fictional.

## Patterns and trends

- A **pattern** is a regular relationship in the data at one point in time, such as one group scoring consistently higher than another.
- A **trend** is a general direction over time: rising, falling, stable or fluctuating.

Good descriptions say what the pattern or trend is, **quote figures with units**, and note any **exceptions**.

## A routine for any data item

1. **Read the title.** What is being measured, where and when?
2. **Check the units.** Numbers, percentages, rates per 1,000, or thousands?
3. **Check the source and sample.** Who produced the data, and how many people were included?
4. **Find the overall pattern or trend.** Describe it in one sentence.
5. **Quote figures.** Use the highest, lowest, first and last values.
6. **Note exceptions.** Is there a year or group that does not fit?
7. **Ask what the data cannot show.** Causes, meanings, groups not included.

## Types of presentation

| Form | Best for | Read carefully |
|---|---|---|
| Table | Exact figures for several groups or years | Row and column headings; totals |
| Bar chart | Comparing groups | Axis scale; whether bars show numbers or percentages |
| Line graph | Trends over time | Gaps between years; where the axis starts |
| Pie chart | Shares of a whole | Only shows proportions, not the size of the total |
| Diagram | Relationships or processes | Labels and arrows |

## Calculations you should be able to do

### Percentage of a total

**Example:** in a survey of 250 parents, 85 said they had visited more than three schools before choosing.

85 ÷ 250 × 100 = **34%**.

### Percentage points and percentage change

**Example (fictional Westbridge household survey):**

| Household type | 2005 | 2015 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Couple with dependent children | 38% | 34% | 30% |
| Lone parent with dependent children | 9% | 11% | 12% |
| One person | 28% | 30% | 31% |
| Other | 25% | 25% | 27% |

- Lone-parent households rose from 9% to 12%: a rise of **3 percentage points**.
- As a proportion of the starting figure: 3 ÷ 9 × 100 = a **33% increase** (to the nearest whole number).
- Couples with dependent children fell from 38% to 30%: a fall of **8 percentage points**.

**Trend description:** "Between 2005 and 2025 the share of couple households with dependent children fell steadily from 38% to 30%, while lone-parent households rose from 9% to 12% and one-person households rose from 28% to 31%."

### Rates

A **rate** compares a number with the size of a population, so groups of different sizes can be compared fairly.

**Example:** a district with 24,000 pupils recorded 360 exclusions. 360 ÷ 24,000 × 1,000 = **15 exclusions per 1,000 pupils**. A second district with 9,000 pupils recorded 180 exclusions: 180 ÷ 9,000 × 1,000 = **20 per 1,000**. The second district had fewer exclusions but a **higher rate**.

### "Times more likely"

**Example:** 60% of pupils from professional families entered university, compared with 20% from routine manual families. 60 ÷ 20 = **3 times as likely**.

### Averages

- **Mean:** add the values and divide by how many there are.
- **Median:** the middle value when the values are put in order.
- **Mode:** the most common value.

Income data often use the median, because a few very high earners pull the mean upwards.

## Traps when reading data

- **Numbers versus rates.** A larger town may have more crimes simply because it has more people.
- **Percentage versus percentage points.** A change from 20% to 30% is a rise of 10 percentage points, which is a 50% increase.
- **Truncated axes.** A graph whose vertical axis starts at 40 rather than 0 makes small differences look large.
- **Missing years.** A line graph with data for 2000, 2010 and 2025 cannot show what happened in between.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Two things rising together does not prove one causes the other. Areas with more police may record more crime because more is detected.
- **Small samples.** A percentage based on 20 people may change greatly with one or two answers.
- **Definitions.** If "poverty" or "crime" is defined differently in two years, a trend may be false.
- **Who is missing.** A survey of school pupils says nothing about young people not in school.

## Worked example: a full data answer

> **Item (fictional):** Recorded violent incidents per 1,000 residents in Haverford: 2015: 18; 2017: 21; 2019: 24; 2021: 19; 2023: 26; 2025: 28.

**Question:** describe the trend and explain one reason why it may not show the true level of violence.

> **Answer:** Overall, recorded violent incidents rose from 18 per 1,000 residents in 2015 to 28 per 1,000 in 2025, an increase of 10 incidents per 1,000. The rise was steady until 2019, when the rate reached 24, but it fell to 19 in 2021 before rising again to 26 in 2023 and 28 in 2025. These figures may not show the true level of violence because they are recorded incidents: some victims do not report violence, especially within families, and changes in police recording practices could increase the figures even if violence itself did not rise.

Notice that the answer gives the overall direction, quotes the first and last figures with units, picks out the exception (2021), and explains a limitation using a sociological concept.

## Linking data to sociology

Examiners reward students who connect numbers to theory. A table showing rising lone-parent households might be used by the New Right as evidence of family breakdown, while others would point to greater choice and acceptance of family diversity. Data rarely settle a debate on their own; they are interpreted through perspectives.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a trend with no figures.
- Giving figures with no units.
- Calling a fall in percentage points a percentage fall.
- Describing every number in a table instead of the overall pattern.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
- Forgetting to show working when a calculation is asked for.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a figure: read it precisely from the item, with its unit.
- **Describe** a pattern or trend: direction, first and last figures, exceptions.
- **Calculate:** write the sum, the answer and the unit.
- **Explain** a limitation: link to validity, representativeness, definitions or recording.
