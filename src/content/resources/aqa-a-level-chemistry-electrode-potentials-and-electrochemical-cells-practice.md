These questions cover section 3.1.11, Electrode potentials and electrochemical cells, of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams from June 2016 onwards. The whole section is marked "A-level only", so none of it is AS content. It is listed under A-level Paper 1, and Paper 3 can assess any content, including required practical 8. Use only the E⦵ data given in each question.

## Questions

**1.** State the three conditions that apply when a standard electrode potential, E⦵, is measured. **[3]**

**2.** A student writes the half-equation for a nickel electrode as Ni → Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻. Rewrite it using the IUPAC convention, and state what a negative E⦵ value tells you about nickel compared with hydrogen. **[2]**

**3.** The standard hydrogen electrode is the reference for electrode potentials.

**(a)** Describe the standard hydrogen electrode. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why platinum is used as the electrode. **[1]**

**4.** Data: Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg, E⦵ = −2.37 V; Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni, E⦵ = −0.25 V.

**(a)** Write the conventional representation of the cell made from these two half-cells. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the overall equation for the cell reaction. **[1]**

**5.** Data: Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn²⁺, E⦵ = +0.15 V; Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag, E⦵ = +0.80 V.

**(a)** Explain why the tin half-cell needs a platinum electrode. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the conventional representation of the cell. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the EMF. **[1]**
**(d)** Write the overall cell equation. **[1]**

**6.** Data: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ ⇌ 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O, E⦵ = +1.33 V; Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻, E⦵ = +1.36 V; Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻, E⦵ = +1.07 V; Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn, E⦵ = −0.76 V.

**(a)** Deduce whether acidified dichromate(VI) ions can oxidise chloride ions under standard conditions. Explain. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce whether acidified dichromate(VI) ions can oxidise bromide ions. Calculate the EMF. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the strongest reducing agent in the data. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one reason why a reaction predicted to be feasible may not be observed. **[1]**

**7.** A standard copper half-cell (E⦵ = +0.34 V) is used as a reference.

**(a)** Metal X is connected to it. X is the negative electrode and the EMF is 1.04 V. Calculate E⦵ for X²⁺/X. **[2]**
**(b)** Metal Y is the positive electrode and the EMF is 0.46 V. Calculate E⦵ for Y⁺/Y. **[1]**

**8.** The cell Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s) has an EMF of 1.10 V under standard conditions. The copper(II) sulfate solution is replaced by one of concentration 0.10 mol dm⁻³. Explain the effect on the EMF. The Nernst equation is not required. **[3]**

**9.** This question is about required practical 8.

**(a)** Explain why the metal strips are rubbed with emery paper before use. **[1]**
**(b)** State the function of the salt bridge. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why potassium chloride is not used in a salt bridge for a silver half-cell. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why a high-resistance voltmeter is used. **[1]**

**10.** The simplified electrode reactions in a lithium cell are:

Positive electrode: Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻

**(a)** Give the oxidation state of cobalt in CoO₂ and in Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the overall equation for the reaction during discharge. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe what happens at each electrode when the cell is recharged. **[2]**
**(d)** State one risk associated with lithium cells. **[1]**

**11.** A lead–acid rechargeable cell uses these half-equations:

PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ PbSO₄ + 2H₂O, E⦵ = +1.69 V
PbSO₄ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Pb + SO₄²⁻, E⦵ = −0.36 V

**(a)** Deduce the reaction at the negative electrode during discharge. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the overall equation for discharge. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[1]**
**(d)** Give the oxidation state of lead in PbO₂, PbSO₄ and Pb, and use them to show that the overall reaction is a redox reaction. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain how the cell is recharged. **[1]**
**(f)** Explain how the electrode reactions generate an electric current. **[2]**

**12.** Data: Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn, −0.76 V; Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe, −0.44 V; Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, +0.77 V; Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag, +0.80 V.

**(a)** Choose the two half-cells that give the cell with the largest EMF. Calculate the EMF and write the conventional representation. **[3]**
**(b)** Iron filings are added to a solution of iron(III) ions. Predict whether a reaction occurs. Write an equation and calculate the EMF. **[3]**
**(c)** Ag⁺ ions are mixed with Fe²⁺ ions under standard conditions. Calculate the EMF and suggest why the reaction may not go as predicted if conditions change. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Temperature 298 K [1]; pressure 100 kPa [1]; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ concentration of ions [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the specification's values; "room temperature" or "1 atm" does not match them.

**2.** **Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni(s)** [1]; negative E⦵ means nickel loses electrons more readily than hydrogen, so Ni is a better reducing agent than H₂ [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The half-equation must be a reduction with electrons on the left; reversing only the arrow is not enough.

**3. (a)** Hydrogen gas at 100 kPa [1]; bubbled over platinum in 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq) [1]; at 298 K [1]
**(b)** Platinum is inert and conducts electrons to and from the H₂/H⁺ equilibrium [1]
*Examiner insight:* A labelled-diagram description still needs the three conditions written; a sketch alone shows no concentration or pressure.

**4. (a)** Mg is more negative so goes on the left [1]; **Mg(s) | Mg²⁺(aq) || Ni²⁺(aq) | Ni(s)** [1]
**(b)** EMF = −0.25 − (−2.37) [1] = **+2.12 V** [1]
**(c)** **Mg + Ni²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Ni** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A correctly written cell always has a positive EMF, so a negative answer in (b) signals the electrodes are the wrong way round.

**5. (a)** Both Sn²⁺ and Sn⁴⁺ are ions in solution, so an inert conductor is needed to transfer electrons [1]
**(b)** Tin half-cell on the left, as the negative electrode [1]; **Pt(s) | Sn²⁺(aq), Sn⁴⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)** [1]
**(c)** 0.80 − 0.15 = **+0.65 V** [1]
**(d)** **2Ag⁺ + Sn²⁺ → 2Ag + Sn⁴⁺** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (d) the silver half-equation is doubled but its E⦵ is not; doubling 0.80 V in (c) gives a wrong EMF.

**6. (a)** Cl₂/Cl⁻ (+1.36 V) is more positive than Cr₂O₇²⁻/Cr³⁺ (+1.33 V) [1]; so dichromate **cannot** oxidise Cl⁻ (EMF would be −0.03 V) [1]
**(b)** +1.33 V is more positive than +1.07 V, so dichromate **can** oxidise Br⁻ [1]; EMF = 1.33 − 1.07 = **+0.26 V** [1]
**(c)** **Zn** [1]
**(d)** The activation energy may be high so the reaction is too slow; or conditions are non-standard [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the answer is the species Zn, not Zn²⁺; naming the ion confuses reducing agent with oxidising agent.

**7. (a)** EMF = E(Cu) − E(X), so 1.04 = 0.34 − E(X) [1]; E(X) = **−0.70 V** [1]
**(b)** E(Y) = 0.34 + 0.46 = **+0.80 V** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Decide from the polarity which electrode is subtracted before substituting; a sign slip gives +1.38 V.

**8.** Lowering [Cu²⁺] shifts Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu to the left [1]; the copper electrode potential becomes less positive [1]; so the EMF **decreases** below 1.10 V [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare "decreases" is not an explanation; link the equilibrium shift to the copper electrode potential.

**9. (a)** To remove the oxide layer, which would affect the measured potential [1]
**(b)** Completes the circuit [1]; lets ions move to balance charge without the solutions mixing [1]
**(c)** Chloride ions would precipitate silver chloride, changing [Ag⁺] [1]
**(d)** So that almost no current flows and the reading is the EMF [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) "lets electrons flow" is wrong: only ions move through the salt bridge.

**10. (a)** CoO₂: **+4** [1]; Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻: **+3** [1]
**(b)** **Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻** [1]
**(c)** An external supply drives the reactions in reverse [1]; Li⁺ is reduced back to Li at one electrode while Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻ is oxidised back to Li⁺ + CoO₂ at the other [1]
**(d)** Lithium is reactive; a damaged cell may catch fire [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the charge on [CoO₂]⁻ must be used; ignoring it gives +4 twice.

**11. (a)** The more negative half-equation runs in reverse: **Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻** [1]
**(b)** Add the positive-electrode reduction to (a), 2e⁻ each [1]; **Pb + PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2SO₄²⁻ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O** [1]
**(c)** 1.69 − (−0.36) = **+2.05 V** [1]
**(d)** PbO₂ +4, PbSO₄ +2, Pb 0 [1]; Pb is oxidised (0 → +2) and PbO₂ is reduced (+4 → +2) [1]
**(e)** An external supply forces current backwards, reversing both electrode reactions to regenerate Pb and PbO₂ [1]
**(f)** Oxidation of Pb at the negative electrode releases electrons [1]; they flow through the external circuit to the positive electrode, where PbO₂ is reduced [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) an equation with uncancelled electrons, or SO₄²⁻ on both sides, is not a finished overall equation.

**12. (a)** Largest gap is Zn and Ag [1]; EMF = 0.80 − (−0.76) = **+1.56 V** [1]; **Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)** [1]
**(b)** Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77) is more positive than Fe²⁺/Fe (−0.44), so reaction occurs [1]; **Fe + 2Fe³⁺ → 3Fe²⁺** [1]; EMF = 0.77 − (−0.44) = **+1.21 V** [1]
**(c)** EMF = 0.80 − 0.77 = **+0.03 V** [1]; the EMF is so small that a change in concentration could make it negative [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) use Fe²⁺/Fe for the iron metal and Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ for the iron(III) ions; swapping them reverses the prediction.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing IUPAC half-equations as oxidations.
- Multiplying E⦵ when a half-equation is scaled up.
- Giving a negative EMF for a cell written the right way round.
- Missing platinum or state symbols in a cell representation.
- Saying the salt bridge carries electrons.
- Treating feasibility as a statement about rate.
- Ignoring the charge on [CoO₂]⁻ when working out oxidation states.
- Explaining a concentration change without saying how the equilibrium shifts.
- Leaving electrons or spectator ions on both sides of an overall equation.
