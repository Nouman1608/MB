All firms, countries and figures are invented for practice. For explanations see the [study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-business-objectives-costs-revenue-and-profit/) and [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-business-objectives-costs-revenue-and-profit-revision-notes/), the [AQA GCSE Economics hub](/boards/aqa/gcse/economics/), the [checklist](/checklists/aqa/gcse/economics/) and the [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/).

## Questions

**1.** A toy-car maker in Country B (illustrative) sells £208,000 of cars a year in a market worth £1.6 million. **(a)** State what is meant by market share. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the firm's market share. **[2]**
**(c)** The firm wants a 16% share of an unchanged market. Calculate the extra sales it needs. **[2]**

**2.** A sandwich shop (illustrative) has these costs. State whether each is fixed or variable. **[4]**
- The shop's monthly rent.
- Bread and fillings.
- The manager's annual salary.
- Paper bags used for each order.

**3.** A phone-case printer (illustrative) has fixed costs of £1,200 a month and an average variable cost of £6. It makes 400 cases. **(a)** Calculate total variable cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate total cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate average cost. **[1]**
**(d)** Each case sells for £11. Calculate the profit. **[2]**

**4.** A candle-wax recycler (illustrative) has total fixed costs of £800 at every output and sells at £20 a unit.

| Output | TFC (£) | TVC (£) | Price (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 800 | 1,500 | 20 |
| 200 | 800 | 2,600 | 20 |
| 300 | 800 | 4,000 | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate total cost and average cost at an output of 200. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit or loss at each output. **[3]**
**(c)** Identify which output earns the most. **[1]**

**5.** Explain two ways in which a firm may increase its profit. **[4]**

**6.** A market gardener in Region C (illustrative) has an average cost of £10 for a box of vegetables. The market price rises from £12 to £15 a box. **(a)** Calculate the profit per box before and after the rise. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the price rise gives producers an incentive to expand production. **[3]**

**7.** A small theatre (illustrative) sells 180 tickets at £14 and 60 tickets at £10. **(a)** Calculate total revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate average revenue per ticket. **[2]**

**8.** A bicycle-lamp firm's sales rise from £250,000 to £285,000. **(a)** Calculate the percentage growth in sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a firm might aim for sales growth even if profit per unit falls. **[3]**

**9.** A newly opened pastry firm must choose between maximising profit now and winning market share. Discuss which objective it should prioritise. **[6]**

**10.** A clothing firm in Country D (illustrative) sells 20,000 shirts at £16. Its average cost is £14. It saves money by letting dye waste run into a stream, which cuts average cost to £11.50. **(a)** Calculate the firm's profit before and after the change. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way in which the firm's motive conflicts with ethical interests. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the firm should continue this practice. **[6]**

**11.** A juice-bar owner (illustrative) has fixed costs of £6,000 a month and an average variable cost of £2.50. The bar sells 4,000 drinks at £5. Two options are considered. Option A: a new supplier cuts average variable cost to £2.20. Option B: the price rises to £5.50 and sales fall to 3,600. **(a)** Calculate the current profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit under option A. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit under option B. **[3]**
**(d)** Evaluate which option the owner should choose. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The firm's share of total sales in its market [1].
**(b)** 208,000 / 1,600,000 [1] x 100 = **13%** [1].
**(c)** Target sales = 0.16 x 1,600,000 = £256,000 [1]. Extra = 256,000 - 208,000 = **£48,000** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Put the firm's sales over the whole market's sales, in the same units, and show that division before the answer.

**2.** Rent: fixed [1]. Bread and fillings: variable [1]. Annual salary: fixed [1]. Paper bags: variable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Test each item by asking whether the bill changes when more orders are made.

**3. (a)** 6 x 400 = **£2,400** [1].
**(b)** 1,200 + 2,400 = **£3,600** [1].
**(c)** 3,600 / 400 = **£9** per case [1].
**(d)** TR = 11 x 400 = £4,400 [1]. Profit = 4,400 - 3,600 = **£800** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each stage of working, so a slip in one early figure does not hide the rest of your method.

**4. (a)** TC = 800 + 2,600 = **£3,400** [1]. AC = 3,400 / 200 = **£17** [1].
**(b)** At 100: TC 2,300, TR 2,000, profit **-£300** (a loss) [1]. At 200: TR 4,000, profit 4,000 - 3,400 = **£600** [1]. At 300: TC 4,800, TR 6,000, profit **£1,200** [1].
**(c)** **300 units** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative profit is a loss, so keep the minus sign or write the word "loss".

**5.** First way: reduce average costs, for example by buying materials from a cheaper supplier [1], so the same revenue leaves a larger gap above costs [1]. Second way: increase revenue, for example by advertising to sell more units [1], so total revenue rises by more than any extra cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a method and a link to profit; naming "cut costs" and "raise revenue" alone is thin.

**6. (a)** Before: 12 - 10 = **£2** [1]. After: 15 - 10 = **£5** [1].
**(b)** A higher price widens the gap between price and average cost [1], so profit on each box rises [1]. Producers therefore want to supply more, and new firms may enter [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the price rise to profit per unit before you mention output, so the chain runs in order.

**7. (a)** 180 x 14 = 2,520 and 60 x 10 = 600 [1]. TR = **£3,120** [1].
**(b)** Tickets sold = 240 [1]. AR = 3,120 / 240 = **£13** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by all 240 tickets, not by the number of price categories.

**8. (a)** Change = 285,000 - 250,000 = 35,000 [1]. 35,000 / 250,000 x 100 = **14%** [1].
**(b)** More sales may build brand awareness and customer loyalty [1]. Selling more units spreads fixed costs, which lowers average cost [1]. A larger firm may then earn more total profit later even with a smaller margin [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide the change by the starting figure; dividing by the new figure is a common slip.

**9.** For profit: profit now gives the firm cash to pay bills and invest [1]; a new firm with high fixed costs may fail if it makes losses for long [1]. For market share: lower prices and promotions attract first customers and build loyalty [1], and more sales spread fixed costs, lowering average cost [1]. Judgement: share is the better early aim if the firm can fund a short period of low profit, because loyal customers support later profit [1]; if the firm has little cash, it should seek enough profit to survive first [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A clear decision with a condition tends to be stronger than listing both objectives and stopping.

**10. (a)** Before: (16 - 14) x 20,000 = **£40,000** [1]. After: (16 - 11.50) x 20,000 = **£90,000** [1]. Increase £50,000 [1].
**(b)** The firm gains extra profit by not paying to dispose of waste safely [1]. Local people lose clean water and wildlife is harmed [1], so profit is put ahead of others' interests [1].
**(c)** For continuing: profit rises by £50,000 and owners gain [1]. Against: fines, bad publicity and lost customers could outweigh the saving [1], and harm to residents is hard to justify [1]. Judgement: the firm should stop, as the short-term gain is likely to be smaller than long-term costs and the harm, unless laws are weak and customers do not mind [1]. Conclude with a stated condition [1], and refer to the figures [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the £50,000 in your evaluation and weigh it against a named cost; unsupported opinion about right and wrong earns little.

**11. (a)** TR = 5 x 4,000 = £20,000 [1]. TC = 6,000 + 2.50 x 4,000 = £16,000 [1]. Profit = **£4,000** [1].
**(b)** TC = 6,000 + 2.20 x 4,000 = £14,800 [1]. Profit = 20,000 - 14,800 = **£5,200** [1].
**(c)** TR = 5.50 x 3,600 = £19,800 [1]. TC = 6,000 + 2.50 x 3,600 = £15,000 [1]. Profit = **£4,800** [1].
**(d)** Option A gives £5,200 and option B £4,800, so A earns £400 more [1]. Option B depends on customers accepting the higher price, and sales could fall further [1]. Option A depends on the new supplier keeping quality [1]. Judgement: choose A, as it gives higher profit and relies on one supplier's quality, which can be checked [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the final profit figures and give a reason that goes beyond the numbers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total variable cost as average cost, or leaving fixed costs out of total cost.
- Dividing revenue by the number of prices rather than the number of units.
- Dividing a change by the new figure when finding percentage growth.
- Dropping the minus sign on a loss.
- Saying a price rise always raises total profit.
- Describing an ethical issue without naming who is harmed.
- Evaluating with a list and no judgement.
- Missing units such as £ or "per unit".
