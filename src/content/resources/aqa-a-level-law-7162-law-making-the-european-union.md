This guide covers the **Law making: the European Union** row of AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1. The specification lists three things: **the institutions of the European Union: the Council, the Commission, the Parliament and the Court of Justice of the European Union and their functions**; **the different sources of European Union law: treaties, regulations and directives**; and **the impact of European Union law on the law of England and Wales**. The United Kingdom left the EU on 31 January 2020, but the content remains on the specification because EU law shaped large parts of current English law. The [Rule of Law, Law Reform, the EU and Civil Justice revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-rule-of-law-reform-eu-and-civil-justice-revision-notes/) give a summary table of the institutions and key cases. This page explains how each institution works, adds further cases on each source, and organises the impact for an extended answer.

## The institutions and their functions

### The Council (Council of the European Union)

- Made up of **one government minister from each member state**; which minister attends depends on the subject (agriculture ministers for farming, finance ministers for budgets).
- **Functions:** adopts legislation jointly with the Parliament; coordinates member states' policies; concludes international agreements; approves the budget with the Parliament.
- **Voting:** many decisions use **qualified majority voting**, which requires the support of a set proportion of member states representing a set proportion of the EU's population; sensitive areas such as taxation require unanimity.
- The **presidency** of the Council rotates between member states every six months.
- Do not confuse it with the **European Council**, the meetings of heads of state or government that set the EU's overall direction but do not pass legislation.

### The Commission

- **One commissioner from each member state**, each responsible for a policy area, led by the Commission President. Commissioners must act in the interests of the EU, **independently** of their own governments.
- **Functions:**
  - **proposes legislation**: it has the main right of initiative;
  - **implements** EU policies and manages the budget;
  - acts as **"guardian of the treaties"**, bringing enforcement proceedings against member states that fail to meet their obligations (now Article 258 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU). In *Commission v United Kingdom (Tachographs)* (1979) the Court of Justice held the UK in breach for failing to implement a regulation requiring tachographs in lorries;
  - negotiates trade agreements on the EU's behalf.

### The Parliament

- Members (MEPs) are **directly elected** by citizens of member states every five years.
- **Functions:** under the **ordinary legislative procedure** it shares law-making power equally with the Council, and can amend or reject proposals; approves the budget with the Council; supervises the Commission, including approving the appointment of the Commission and having the power to dismiss it through a vote of censure.
- Its powers grew steadily, from a largely consultative body to a co-legislator, which is often presented as an answer to criticism that the EU lacked democratic accountability.

### The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)

- **One judge from each member state**, assisted by **Advocates General**, who give independent written opinions on cases before judgment. The CJEU also includes the **General Court**, which hears many cases at first instance.
- **Functions:**
  - **preliminary rulings** under Article 267 TFEU: national courts refer questions on the meaning of EU law; the CJEU interprets the law and the national court applies it to the facts. A national court of final appeal must normally refer unless the answer is already clear or settled (*CILFIT*, 1982). In *Bulmer v Bollinger* (1974) Lord Denning set out guidelines for when English courts should refer;
  - **enforcement actions** against member states brought by the Commission;
  - reviewing the legality of acts of the EU institutions.

## The sources of EU law

### Treaties

The treaties are the **primary** source, setting out the EU's aims, institutions and powers: the Treaty of Rome (1957) established the European Economic Community; the Maastricht Treaty (1992) created the European Union; and the Treaty of Lisbon (2007) produced the current Treaty on European Union and Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Treaty articles that are clear, precise and unconditional can have **direct effect**, giving rights individuals can enforce in national courts, both against the state (**vertical** direct effect) and against other individuals (**horizontal** direct effect). In *Defrenne v Sabena (No 2)* (1976), brought by an air hostess paid less than male cabin stewards, the Court of Justice held that the treaty article on equal pay could be relied on against private employers as well as the state. In *Macarthys Ltd v Smith* (1980) a woman paid less than her male predecessor relied on the same article in the English courts.

### Regulations

Regulations are **binding in their entirety** and **directly applicable** in all member states: they become law automatically, without any national legislation. In *Leonesio v Italian Ministry of Agriculture* (1972) a farmer was entitled to a payment for slaughtering dairy cows under a regulation, even though Italy had not passed national legislation to fund it. Their purpose is uniformity: the same rule in every state at the same time.

### Directives

Directives are **binding as to the result to be achieved** but leave each member state to choose the form and method, by a deadline. The UK usually implemented directives through statutory instruments: the **Working Time Directive** was implemented by the Working Time Regulations 1998.

When a state fails to implement a directive properly:

- **Vertical direct effect:** an individual can rely on a clear, unconditional directive against the state or an **emanation of the state**. *Marshall v Southampton Area Health Authority* (1986) applied this; *Foster v British Gas* (1990) set out the test for which bodies count as emanations of the state.
- **No horizontal direct effect:** a directive cannot be enforced directly against a private individual or company: *Faccini Dori v Recreb* (1994).
- **Indirect effect:** national courts must interpret national law, as far as possible, in the light of the directive, even in disputes between private parties: *Von Colson* (1984), *Marleasing* (1990).
- **State liability:** an individual can claim damages from the state for loss caused by failure to implement: *Francovich v Italy* (1991).

## The impact of EU law on the law of England and Wales

### During membership (1973 to 2020)

- **Legal basis:** the **European Communities Act 1972** gave EU law effect in the UK (section 2(1)) and required national law to be read and applied subject to it (section 2(4)).
- **Supremacy:** where there was a conflict, EU law prevailed. In *Factortame* (1990) the House of Lords disapplied parts of the Merchant Shipping Act 1988, a major qualification of parliamentary supremacy.
- **Statutory interpretation:** courts moved towards a purposive approach to make national law compatible with EU law.
- **Rights in substantive law:** equal pay and anti-discrimination law, working time and holiday rights, consumer protection, product safety, data protection and environmental standards all developed under EU law.
- **Courts and precedent:** English courts referred questions to the CJEU and were bound by its rulings on EU law.

### After withdrawal

- The **European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018** repealed the 1972 Act from exit day (31 January 2020), and an implementation period ran until 31 December 2020.
- EU-derived law in force at the end of that period was kept as **retained EU law**, so rights such as those in the Working Time Regulations 1998 continued.
- The **Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023** renamed it **assimilated law** from 1 January 2024 and ended the principle of supremacy of EU law and the general principles of EU law for this body of law.
- The Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court can depart from retained EU case law, and UK courts are no longer bound by new CJEU decisions.
- **Lasting effect:** much of English employment, consumer and equality law still has EU origins, and the purposive approach remains dominant.

## Worked example: an annotated paragraph

**Question:** Explain the difference between regulations and directives as sources of EU law. (6 marks)

> Regulations are binding in their entirety and directly applicable in every member state, so they become law without national legislation *[definition]*. In *Leonesio* (1972) a farmer could claim a payment under a regulation even though Italy had not passed laws to fund it *[case]*. Directives bind states only as to the result to be achieved and leave the method to each state *[definition]*; the UK implemented the Working Time Directive through the Working Time Regulations 1998 *[example]*. If a state fails to implement a directive, an individual may rely on it against the state, as in *Marshall* (1986), but not against a private party, as *Faccini Dori* (1994) confirmed *[development]*.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the Council of the EU with the European Council, or with the Council of Europe (which is not an EU body).
- Saying the Parliament proposes legislation; that is mainly the Commission's role.
- Saying directives are directly applicable.
- Writing as if EU law still binds UK courts in full.
- Describing impact without specific areas of law or cases.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions on an institution, give its composition and two or three functions with an example. On sources, define each and add a case. For **discuss** questions on impact, cover membership and withdrawal, with examples from substantive law and the constitution.
