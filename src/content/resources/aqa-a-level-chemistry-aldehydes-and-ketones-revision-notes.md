## Checklist for 3.3.8 (all A-level only)

- Aldehydes are readily oxidised to carboxylic acids.
- Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution distinguish aldehydes from ketones.
- NaBH₄ in aqueous solution: aldehyde → primary alcohol, ketone → secondary alcohol (nucleophilic addition).
- Overall reduction equations with [H].
- Mechanism for NaBH₄ reduction, with H⁻ as the nucleophile.
- KCN followed by dilute acid gives hydroxynitriles; overall equations with HCN.
- Mechanism for KCN followed by dilute acid.
- Why aldehydes and unsymmetrical ketones give a mixture of enantiomers.
- Hazards of using KCN.

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carbonyl group | C=O. Polar, Cδ+=Oδ−, because O is more electronegative |
| Aldehyde | C=O at the end of a chain, –CHO; name ends in -al |
| Ketone | C=O bonded to two carbons; name ends in -one |
| Nucleophile | Electron-pair donor; attacks the δ+ carbon |
| Nucleophilic addition | A nucleophile adds to the C=O carbon and the C=O becomes C–O, then O is protonated |
| Hydroxynitrile | OH and C≡N on the same carbon; nitrile carbon is C1 in the name |
| Racemic mixture (racemate) | Equal amounts of two enantiomers; optically inactive |

## Reactions summary

| Reaction | Reagent and conditions | Aldehyde gives | Ketone gives | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation | Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, or Tollens', or Fehling's (warm) | Carboxylic acid (carboxylate in the alkaline reagents) | No reaction | Oxidation |
| Reduction | NaBH₄ in aqueous solution | Primary alcohol | Secondary alcohol | Nucleophilic addition |
| Add cyanide | KCN, then dilute acid | Hydroxynitrile | Hydroxynitrile | Nucleophilic addition |

## Tests (warm in a water bath)

| Reagent | Contains | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tollens' | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ | Silver mirror (Ag⁺ reduced to Ag) | No change |
| Fehling's | Blue Cu²⁺ complex, alkaline | Red precipitate of Cu₂O | Stays blue |

Ratios for calculations: **1 mol aldehyde → 2 mol Ag**; **1 mol aldehyde → 1 mol Cu₂O** (two Cu²⁺ each gain one
electron).

Ketones resist oxidation because the C=O carbon has no H; oxidation would need a C–C bond to break.

## Equations to learn the shape of

```
Oxidation:   RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
Reduction:   RCHO + 2[H] → RCH₂OH              (primary alcohol)
             RCOR' + 2[H] → RCH(OH)R'          (secondary alcohol)
Cyanide:     RCHO + HCN → RCH(OH)CN
             RCOR' + HCN → RC(OH)(CN)R'
```

Examples not used in the study guide:

```
CH₃CH₂CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH                       propanal → propan-1-ol
CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃           pentan-3-one → pentan-3-ol
CH₃CH₂CHO + HCN → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CN                     → 2-hydroxybutanenitrile
CH₃COCH₂CH₃ + HCN → CH₃C(OH)(CN)CH₂CH₃               → 2-hydroxy-2-methylbutanenitrile
```

## Method in steps: the two mechanisms

**Reduction by NaBH₄ (nucleophile H⁻):**

1. Show the C=O with δ+ on C and δ− on O.
2. Arrow from the lone pair on **H⁻** to the carbonyl carbon.
3. Arrow from the C=O bond to the oxygen. Intermediate: an alkoxide, C–O⁻.
4. Arrow from a lone pair on O⁻ to H of H₂O (or to H⁺). Product: the alcohol.

**KCN followed by dilute acid (nucleophile CN⁻):**

1. Show the C=O with δ+ on C and δ− on O.
2. Arrow from the lone pair on the **carbon** of :CN⁻ to the carbonyl carbon.
3. Arrow from the C=O bond to the oxygen. Intermediate: C(CN)–O⁻.
4. Arrow from a lone pair on O⁻ to **H⁺**. Product: the hydroxynitrile.

Both mechanisms have the same shape: attack on C, electrons to O, then protonate O.

## Racemic mixtures in four lines

1. The C=O group is **planar** (trigonal planar carbon).
2. CN⁻ attacks from **either side** of the plane with **equal probability**.
3. If the product carbon has four different groups, the two attacks give the two **enantiomers**.
4. They form in **equal amounts**: a **racemic mixture**, which is **optically inactive** because the rotations
   cancel.

Quick rule: an aldehyde (except methanal) or an **unsymmetrical** ketone gives a chiral product. Methanal (H, H)
and symmetrical ketones such as propanone or pentan-3-one do not.

## Small worked reminders

**Is the product racemic?** 3-methylbutan-2-one is CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂.

- Groups on the C=O carbon: CH₃ and CH(CH₃)₂. They are different.
- After KCN then dilute acid, that carbon carries CH₃, CH(CH₃)₂, OH and CN: four different groups.
- So the product is chiral and forms as a **racemic mixture**.

**Which alcohol?** Hexanal is an aldehyde, so NaBH₄ gives the primary alcohol **hexan-1-ol**:
CH₃(CH₂)₄CHO + 2[H] → CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₂OH. Hexan-3-one is a ketone, so it gives the secondary alcohol
**hexan-3-ol**.

**Which test result?** Pentanal with Fehling's: blue to red precipitate. Pentan-2-one with Fehling's: stays
blue. Pentanal is oxidised to pentanoic acid (as pentanoate in the alkaline reagent).

## KCN hazards

- KCN is highly toxic.
- Acid releases HCN, which is highly toxic and volatile.
- Control: fume cupboard, gloves, eye protection, careful addition of acid.
- KCN rather than HCN: HCN is hard to handle safely, and as a weak acid it gives few CN⁻ ions; KCN gives plenty.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Tollens' vs Fehling's | Silver mirror vs blue → red precipitate |
| Aldehyde vs ketone reduction product | Primary alcohol vs secondary alcohol |
| [O] vs [H] equations | [O] adds O (or removes 2H); 2[H] adds two H across C=O |
| CN⁻ vs H⁻ as nucleophile | CN⁻ adds a carbon (chain grows); H⁻ does not |
| Chiral product vs racemic mixture | Each molecule is chiral; the 50 : 50 mixture is optically inactive |
| Symmetrical vs unsymmetrical ketone | Same vs different groups on the C=O carbon; only unsymmetrical gives enantiomers |

## Quick self-test

1. Name the product when 2-methylpropanal is reduced by NaBH₄.
2. Give the class of alcohol formed when a ketone is reduced by NaBH₄.
3. Write the overall equation, using [H], for the reduction of propanal.
4. What is the nucleophile in the reaction of a ketone with KCN, and which atom donates the lone pair?
5. Name CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CN.
6. Which of these gives a racemic product with KCN then dilute acid: methanal, pentan-2-one, pentan-3-one?
7. Why is the racemic product optically inactive?
8. 0.660 g of ethanal (Mr 44.0) reacts fully with Tollens' reagent. Calculate the mass of silver formed
   (Ar Ag = 107.9).
9. 1.80 g of butanal (Mr 72.0) is reduced completely. Calculate the maximum mass of butan-1-ol (Mr 74.0).
10. State the observation when hexan-2-one is warmed with Fehling's solution.
11. Give one reason KCN is used rather than HCN.

### Answers

1. 2-methylpropan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH.
2. Secondary alcohol.
3. CH₃CH₂CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH.
4. The cyanide ion, CN⁻; the lone pair is on the carbon atom.
5. 2-hydroxybutanenitrile.
6. Pentan-2-one only (CH₃ and C₃H₇ on the C=O carbon are different).
7. It contains equal amounts of the two enantiomers, which rotate plane-polarised light equally in opposite
   directions, so the effects cancel.
8. n(ethanal) = 0.660 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0150 mol; n(Ag) = 0.0300 mol; mass = 0.0300 × 107.9 = **3.24 g**.
9. n(butanal) = 1.80 ÷ 72.0 = 0.0250 mol; mass = 0.0250 × 74.0 = **1.85 g**.
10. No change: the solution stays blue (it is a ketone).
11. HCN is toxic and volatile, so it is hard to handle; or HCN is a weak acid and supplies few CN⁻ ions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- CN⁻ arrow drawn from the N atom instead of the lone pair on C.
- Missing the arrow from the C=O bond to O, leaving a five-bonded carbon.
- Showing BH₄⁻ as the attacking species when H⁻ is asked for.
- One [H] instead of 2[H] in reduction equations.
- Forgetting to protonate the O⁻ intermediate in the last step.
- Naming hydroxynitriles without counting the nitrile carbon.
- Explaining racemic mixtures without "planar" and "attack from either side equally".
- Claiming propanone or methanal gives enantiomers.
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio for aldehyde to silver instead of 1 : 2.
- "Toxic" for KCN with no mention of HCN release on adding acid.
