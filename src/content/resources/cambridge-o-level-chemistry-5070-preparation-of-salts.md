Subtopic 7.3 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 has five learning outcomes. You must describe "the preparation, separation and purification of soluble salts" by four routes, describe "the preparation of insoluble salts by precipitation", recall six solubility rules, and define hydrated, anhydrous and "water of crystallisation… including CuSO₄•5H₂O and CoCl₂•6H₂O".

Most marks here come from method descriptions, and examiners reward **reasons** for each step as well as the steps themselves. This guide explains how to choose a method, why each step is there, and how to calculate water of crystallisation. The broader [Acids, Bases and Salts guide](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/) covers all of Topic 7. For quick recall use the [salt preparation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-preparation-of-salts-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [salt preparation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-preparation-of-salts-practice/).

## Step 1: is the salt soluble?

Everything depends on the solubility rules in outcome 3:

- **Sodium, potassium and ammonium** salts are soluble.
- **Nitrates** are soluble.
- **Chlorides** are soluble, except **lead and silver**.
- **Sulfates** are soluble, except **barium, calcium and lead**.
- **Carbonates** are insoluble, except **sodium, potassium and ammonium**.
- **Hydroxides** are insoluble, except **sodium, potassium, ammonium and calcium (partially)**.

If the salt is **insoluble**, use **precipitation**. If it is **soluble**, choose one of the four routes below.

## Step 2: choosing a route for a soluble salt

| Route | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acid + alkali by titration | Both reactants are soluble: sodium, potassium and ammonium salts | Potassium nitrate from KOH and HNO₃ |
| Acid + excess metal | The metal reacts at a safe rate with dilute acid | Zinc sulfate from zinc and H₂SO₄ |
| Acid + excess insoluble base | An insoluble metal oxide or hydroxide is available | Copper(II) chloride from CuO and HCl |
| Acid + excess insoluble carbonate | An insoluble carbonate is available | Magnesium nitrate from MgCO₃ and HNO₃ |

Three restrictions catch candidates out:

- **Sodium and potassium metals are far too reactive** to add to acid safely, which is why their salts are made by titration.
- **Copper does not react with dilute acids**, so copper(II) salts are made from copper(II) oxide, hydroxide or carbonate instead.
- **An insoluble product stops the reaction.** Calcium carbonate with sulfuric acid forms calcium sulfate, which is insoluble and coats the carbonate, so the reaction soon stops. Use the solubility rules for the product as well as the reactants.

## The excess-solid method, with reasons

Making zinc sulfate crystals from zinc oxide and dilute sulfuric acid:

1. **Warm** the dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker. *Reason: the reaction is faster.*
2. **Add zinc oxide a little at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves** and some solid remains. *Reason: the excess makes sure all the acid has reacted, so no acid is left in the product.*
3. **Filter** to remove the excess zinc oxide. *Reason: it is insoluble and would contaminate the crystals.*
4. **Heat the filtrate to evaporate some of the water** until the solution is saturated: a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals. *Reason: a saturated solution will crystallise on cooling.*
5. **Leave to cool** so crystals form. *Reason: the salt is less soluble when cold.*
6. **Filter off the crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water and dry** them between filter papers or in a warm place. *Reason: washing removes soluble impurities; cold water dissolves as little of the salt as possible.*

Evaporating to dryness is avoided, because it would drive off the water of crystallisation and leave a powder instead of hydrated crystals.

With a metal or a carbonate, the signal that the acid is used up is that **fizzing stops**: no more hydrogen, or no more carbon dioxide, is produced.

## The titration method

For a soluble salt from two solutions, such as sodium sulfate from sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid:

1. Pipette a measured volume of alkali into a conical flask and add a few drops of indicator (for example methyl orange or thymolphthalein).
2. Add acid from a burette until the indicator just changes colour, and record the volume.
3. **Repeat without the indicator**, mixing the same volumes. *Reason: the indicator would contaminate the salt.*
4. Evaporate to the point of crystallisation, cool, filter and dry as above.

There is no excess to filter off: the exact volumes ensure that neither acid nor alkali is left over. The pipette and burette matter because the method depends on reproducing exactly the volumes found in the first run; a measuring cylinder is not precise enough.

## Precipitation for insoluble salts

Choose **two soluble salts**: one containing the cation and one containing the anion you need. Sodium, potassium and nitrate salts are ideal, because they are always soluble.

To make barium sulfate:

BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
ionic equation: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

1. Mix the two solutions; a white precipitate forms.
2. **Filter** to collect the precipitate.
3. **Wash** it with distilled water. *Reason: to remove the soluble sodium chloride left on it.*
4. **Dry** it in a warm oven or between filter papers.

There is no evaporation or crystallisation step: the salt is already a solid. Other examples: lead(II) chloride from lead(II) nitrate and sodium chloride; calcium carbonate from calcium chloride and sodium carbonate.

## Hydrated salts and water of crystallisation

- A **hydrated** substance is chemically combined with water.
- An **anhydrous** substance contains no water.
- **Water of crystallisation** is the water molecules present in hydrated crystals, such as CuSO₄•5H₂O and CoCl₂•6H₂O.

### Worked example: finding the water of crystallisation

2.50 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄•xH₂O, is heated until its mass stops changing. 1.60 g of white anhydrous CuSO₄ remains. Find x.

1. Mass of water lost = 2.50 − 1.60 = 0.90 g.
2. Moles of CuSO₄ = 1.60 ÷ 160 = 0.0100 mol.
3. Moles of H₂O = 0.90 ÷ 18 = 0.0500 mol.
4. Ratio CuSO₄ : H₂O = 0.0100 : 0.0500 = 1 : 5, so **x = 5**.

Heating "until the mass stops changing" (heating to constant mass) ensures all the water has been removed.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing titration when one reactant is insoluble**, or excess solid when both are soluble.
- **Leaving out the reason for excess**: it ensures all the acid reacts.
- **Evaporating to dryness** instead of to the point of crystallisation.
- **Forgetting to wash** a precipitate, or washing crystals with lots of warm water, which dissolves them.
- **Making copper(II) sulfate from copper metal**, which does not react with dilute acid.
- **Naming an insoluble reagent for precipitation**, such as silver chloride to make another silver salt. Both starting solutions must be soluble.
- **Misremembering exceptions**: lead appears in both the chloride and the sulfate exceptions.

## Command words

- **Describe** (a preparation): give the reagents and every step in order, from mixing to drying. Use names of apparatus.
- **Explain** (a step): say why it is done, as in the reasons above.
- **Suggest** (reagents): choose soluble or insoluble starting materials that fit the method, using the solubility rules.
- **Calculate** (water of crystallisation): mass of water, moles of each, then the ratio.
