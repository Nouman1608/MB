## Word order

Urdu follows **Subject–Object–Verb** order, unlike English's Subject–Verb–Object.

```
میں کتاب پڑھتا ہوں
main kitaab parhta hoon   —  "I book read am"  =  I read a book
```

**Putting the verb before the object is the most common structural error** for English-speaking learners. The verb goes last.

**Postpositions** follow the noun, where English uses prepositions before it: *mez par* (table on) = on the table.

## Gender and agreement

Every noun is **masculine or feminine**, and adjectives and verbs must agree.

```
achha larka   (good boy - masculine)
achhi larki   (good girl - feminine)
```

**Verb endings change with gender and number**, so a single mistake in gender propagates through the whole sentence. When learning a noun, learn its gender with it.

## Tenses

| Tense | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | verb stem + ta/ti/te + hoon/hai/hain | *main parhta hoon* |
| **Past** | verb stem + a/i/e + tha/thi/the | *main parhta tha* |
| **Future** | verb stem + oon-ga/ega/enge | *main parhoon-ga* |
| **Continuous** | rahaa/rahi/rahe + hai | *main parh rahaa hoon* |

**Examiners reward tense variety.** A piece written entirely in the present tense caps the language mark however accurate it is, so deliberately include past and future.

## Theme vocabulary — Identity and Culture

Build vocabulary in clusters rather than lists:

- **Family:** *khandaan* (family), *waalidain* (parents), *bhai* (brother), *behen* (sister), *rishta* (relationship).
- **Self:** *naam* (name), *umr* (age), *shakhsiyat* (personality), *shauq* (hobby).
- **Culture:** *tehzeeb* (culture), *rivaayat* (tradition), *tehwaar* (festival), *libaas* (clothing), *khaana* (food).
- **Opinions:** *mujhe pasand hai* (I like), *mere khayaal mein* (in my opinion), *kyunke* (because).

**Opinion phrases and connectives are the highest-value vocabulary**, because they let you extend and justify rather than list — which is exactly what moves an answer up the bands.

## Exam technique

**Reading:** read the questions first, then scan for the specific information. Do not translate everything — locate what is asked.

**Listening:** use the pause to read ahead and predict the kind of answer required (a number, a place, a reason). Write something for every question.

**Writing:** plan briefly, then include a **range of tenses**, **opinions with reasons**, and **connectives** (*aur*, *lekin*, *kyunke*, *is liye*). Check gender agreement and verb endings at the end.

**Speaking:** develop every answer. A one-word reply scores minimally; add a reason, an example, or an opinion to every response.

## Exam traps

- Using English word order with the verb before the object.
- Mismatching adjective or verb endings with noun gender.
- Writing entirely in one tense.
- Listing vocabulary without opinions or justification.
- Leaving listening answers blank rather than attempting them.
- Giving undeveloped one-word speaking answers.

## Self-test

1. What is Urdu's basic word order, and how does it differ from English?
2. Why does noun gender matter beyond the noun itself?
3. Give the present, past and future forms of "I read".
4. Why do examiners reward tense variety?
5. What raises a writing answer beyond simple description?

**Answers:** 1. Subject–Object–Verb, with the verb at the end, whereas English uses Subject–Verb–Object. 2. Adjectives and verb endings must agree with the noun's gender, so an error in gender propagates through the sentence. 3. *main parhta hoon*, *main parhta tha*, *main parhoon-ga*. 4. Using only one tense caps the language mark regardless of accuracy, because range is assessed separately from correctness. 5. Including opinions with reasons, connectives such as *kyunke* and *lekin*, and a range of tenses.
