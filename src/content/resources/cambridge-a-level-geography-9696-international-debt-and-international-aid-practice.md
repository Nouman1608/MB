These questions cover **13.2 International debt and international aid** from the Paper 4 option Global interdependence. All countries, organisations and data are fictional unless a question asks for your own examples. Revise first with the [13.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-international-debt-and-international-aid/) and the [13.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-international-debt-and-international-aid-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a country's external debt and its debt service. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how a rise in world interest rates can turn manageable debt into a debt crisis. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows debt data for three fictional countries (US$ billion).

| Country | External debt | GDP | Export earnings | Debt service this year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tavia | 30 | 50 | 10 | 3.5 |
| Rumeli | 90 | 300 | 60 | 6.0 |
| Kendra | 8 | 10 | 2 | 0.9 |

**(a)** Calculate the debt service ratio for Tavia and for Kendra. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, identify which country is most vulnerable to a debt crisis and explain your choice. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why a country's debt service ratio might rise even if it borrows no more money. **[4]**

**4.** Outline **two** different forms of debt relief. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why tied aid may bring fewer benefits to a receiving country than untied aid. **[4]**

**6.** Outline the difference between bilateral and multilateral aid, giving one advantage of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the problems that high levels of external debt can cause for the people of a country. **[6]**

**8.** After a poor harvest in a neighbouring region, 40 000 tonnes of donated maize arrived in the fictional district of Lumen. The local market price of maize fell from 300 to 210 currency units per tonne, and stayed low for the next planting season.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in the local maize price. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the effects of the donated maize on farmers and on consumers in Lumen. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest how the aid could have been designed to avoid harming local farmers. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why debt relief has not permanently solved the debt problems of low-income countries. **[5]**

**10.** "International aid does more harm than good to receiving countries." With reference to examples, critically evaluate this view. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** External debt is the total amount a country owes to foreign creditors [1]; debt service is the interest and principal repayments due in a particular year [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Many loans have variable interest rates, so when world rates rise the interest due increases [1]. Debt service rises while export earnings stay the same, so a larger share of foreign income goes on repayments [1]. If the country cannot pay, it must borrow more or default, as many did in the 1980s [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** Tavia: 3.5 ÷ 10 × 100 = 35% [1]; Kendra: 0.9 ÷ 2 × 100 = 45% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Kendra is most vulnerable [1]: its debt is 80% of GDP, the highest of the three [1], and 45% of its export earnings go on debt service, compared with 35% for Tavia and 10% for Rumeli [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Export earnings may fall, for example because the price of its main commodity drops [1], so the same payment is a larger share of earnings [1]. The currency may lose value [1], making debt in US dollars more expensive to service [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit rising interest rates on variable-rate loans, or the end of a grace period on older loans.

**4.** Rescheduling extends the time over which a debt is repaid [1], lowering yearly payments, usually agreed with creditor governments through the Paris Club [1]. Cancellation writes off part or all of a debt [1], as under the HIPC Initiative for qualifying poor countries [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit debt-for-nature swaps or debt-for-bond exchanges such as the Brady Plan, each with a description.

**5.** Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country [1], which may cost more than buying from the cheapest supplier [1]. Equipment may not suit local conditions, or may need spare parts only the donor supplies [1]. Much of the money returns to donor firms rather than supporting local businesses and jobs [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Bilateral aid goes directly from one government to another [1]; an advantage is that it can be agreed quickly and target the receiver's specific needs [1]. Multilateral aid is pooled from many donors through international organisations such as the World Bank or UN agencies [1]; an advantage is that it is less shaped by one donor's political or commercial interests [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Debt service takes money that could fund health and education [1], so schools and clinics are short of staff and supplies [1]. Rescue loans often require austerity, such as cutting subsidies on food or fuel [1], which raises the cost of living for the poorest [1]. A debt crisis can cause the currency to fall and inflation to rise, reducing real wages [1]. Unemployment can rise as public spending is cut and investment falls [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (210 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 [1] = −30%, a fall of 30% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Farmers received 30% less for their maize [1], so their incomes fell and some could not afford seed or fertiliser for the next season [1]. Consumers, especially poor households who buy food, gained from cheaper maize [1]. In the long term, lower local production could make the district more dependent on aid [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** The aid could have bought maize from local or regional farmers, supporting their incomes [1]. It could have been given as cash or vouchers so people bought food in local markets [1]. It could have been targeted only at households in need, and stopped before the next planting season [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Relief reduces the stock of debt but does not change the causes, such as dependence on commodity exports [1]. When commodity prices fall, countries borrow again [1]. New creditors, including commercial bondholders and new bilateral lenders, offered loans after relief [1], sometimes on harder terms [1]. Shocks such as pandemics or droughts force new borrowing [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define international aid [1] and outline its types and channels [1].

*Harm:* food aid can lower local prices and discourage farmers [1]. Tied aid returns money to donor firms and may supply unsuitable goods [1], as the Pergau Dam case showed with aid linked to donor interests [1]. Aid can create dependency and reduce pressure to raise tax revenue [1]. Corruption can divert funds from the poor [1]. Loans add to debt [1]. Moyo argued that large government-to-government aid harmed African development [1].

*Good:* relief aid saves lives after disasters [1]. Health aid has cut disease; smallpox was eradicated through a WHO-led campaign [1]. Vaccination partnerships such as Gavi have widened access to vaccines [1]. Development aid funds schools, roads and water supplies [1]. Sachs argued that well-targeted aid can break poverty traps [1].

*Critical analysis:* impact depends on type, since relief and health aid have the strongest record [1]. Untied, multilateral or community-level aid tends to suit local needs better [1]. Easterly argued for small, accountable projects with feedback from recipients [1]. Context matters: aid works better where governance is stronger [1].

*Use of examples:* named examples of both harmful and beneficial aid [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion that varies by type and context, rather than a single verdict [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* "critically evaluate" rewards answers that question the evidence and weigh viewpoints, not a list of advantages and disadvantages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using GDP instead of export earnings for the debt service ratio.
- Confusing total debt with yearly debt service.
- Describing aid types without explaining their effects.
- Food aid answers that consider farmers but not consumers.
- Essays that treat all aid as the same.
