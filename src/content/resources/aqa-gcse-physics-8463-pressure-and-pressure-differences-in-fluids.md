## 4.5.5.1.1 Pressure and p = F/A

A **fluid** is either a **liquid** or a **gas**. The particles of a fluid move about and collide with any
surface they touch. The pressure in a fluid causes a force **normal** (at right angles) to any surface.

You can see this with a plastic bottle full of water with small holes around its side: the water spurts out
at right angles to the bottle's surface at every hole.

```
pressure = force normal to a surface / area of that surface
p = F / A      p in pascals (Pa),  F in newtons (N),  A in square metres (m²)
```

You must recall this equation. 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

**Worked example 1.** A walker of weight 720 N stands in snow. Each boot has an area of 0.030 m² in contact
with the snow; snowshoes have an area of 0.12 m² each. Compare the pressures.

```
boots:     A = 2 × 0.030 = 0.060 m²     p = 720 / 0.060 = 12 000 Pa
snowshoes: A = 2 × 0.12  = 0.24 m²      p = 720 / 0.24  = 3000 Pa
```

The same force over four times the area gives a quarter of the pressure, so the walker sinks less.

### Converting areas

Areas are often given in cm² or mm². Square the conversion factor:

```
1 cm² = (0.01 m)²  = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m²
1 mm² = (0.001 m)² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m²
```

**Worked example 2.** You push a drawing pin with a force of 15 N. The head has an area of 1.0 cm²; the
point has an area of 0.10 mm².

```
head:  A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²            p = 15 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.5 × 10⁵ Pa
point: A = 0.10 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²   p = 15 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.5 × 10⁸ Pa
```

The force is the same at both ends, but the pressure at the point is a thousand times larger, so the point
pierces the board while the head does not hurt your thumb.

## 4.5.5.1.2 Pressure in a liquid (Higher tier only)

The pressure due to a column of liquid is:

```
pressure = height of the column × density of the liquid × gravitational field strength
p = h ρ g      p in Pa,  h in m,  ρ in kg/m³,  g in N/kg   (equation sheet; g is given)
```

**Why pressure increases with depth.** At a deeper point there is a taller column of liquid above it, so a
greater weight of liquid pushes on each square metre.

**Why pressure increases with density.** A denser liquid has more mass, and so more weight, in the same
height of column above each square metre.

**Worked example 3.** Find the pressure due to the water at the bottom of a 3.0 m deep pool, and the pressure
at the same depth in oil of density 800 kg/m³. Take g = 9.8 N/kg; water has density 1000 kg/m³.

```
water:  p = 3.0 × 1000 × 9.8 = 29 400 Pa
oil:    p = 3.0 × 800  × 9.8 = 23 520 Pa
```

### Pressure differences

To find the difference in pressure between two depths, use the **difference in height** in p = hρg.

```
difference in pressure between 2.0 m and 8.0 m of water:
Δp = (8.0 − 2.0) × 1000 × 9.8 = 6.0 × 1000 × 9.8 = 58 800 Pa
```

This explains why a dam is built much thicker at the bottom than the top: the pressure on it, and so the
force on each square metre, increases with depth.

### Upthrust

A partially or totally submerged object has a **greater pressure on its bottom surface than on its top
surface**, because the bottom is deeper. This creates a **resultant force upwards**, called the
**upthrust**. The pressures on the sides act horizontally and cancel.

**Worked example 4.** A cube of side 0.10 m is held under water with its top face 0.20 m below the surface.

```
area of each face = 0.10 × 0.10 = 0.010 m²
top face:     p = 0.20 × 1000 × 9.8 = 1960 Pa    force down = 1960 × 0.010 = 19.6 N
bottom face:  p = 0.30 × 1000 × 9.8 = 2940 Pa    force up   = 2940 × 0.010 = 29.4 N
upthrust = 29.4 − 19.6 = 9.8 N upwards
```

Moving the cube deeper raises both pressures by the same amount, so the upthrust on a fully submerged cube
does not change with depth.

### Floating and sinking

- An object **floats** if the upthrust can become equal to its weight before it is completely submerged.
  It sinks into the fluid until the upthrust balances its weight.
- An object **sinks** if its weight is greater than the largest upthrust it can receive, the upthrust when it
  is fully submerged.
- An object less dense than the fluid floats; an object denser than the fluid sinks.
- A steel ship floats because its hollow shape means its overall density (steel plus the air inside) is less
  than that of water.
- The **density of the fluid** matters too. In a less dense liquid, pressure increases more slowly with
  depth, so a boat has to sit lower in the water before the upthrust equals its weight. A boat floats lower in
  fresh water than in seawater.

## 4.5.5.2 Atmospheric pressure

**A simple model of the atmosphere:**

- The atmosphere is a **thin layer** of air around the Earth, thin compared with the size of the Earth.
- It gets **less dense** with increasing altitude.
- **Air molecules colliding with a surface** create atmospheric pressure.

**Why atmospheric pressure decreases with height.** The higher a surface is above ground level, the fewer
air molecules there are above it, so the **weight of air** above it is smaller. There is always less air
above a higher surface than a lower one, so atmospheric pressure decreases as height increases. The air is
also less dense higher up, so fewer molecules collide with each square metre of surface each second.

At sea level, atmospheric pressure is about 100 000 Pa. Effects you can explain with the model:

- Your ears "pop" in an aircraft or a fast lift as the outside pressure changes.
- A sealed bag of crisps carried up a mountain swells: the air inside stays at the pressure it was sealed at,
  while the pressure outside falls.

## Common mistakes

- Using cm² or mm² without converting, or dividing by 100 for cm² instead of 10 000.
- Saying pressure acts only downwards in a fluid. It acts normal to every surface, in all directions.
- Using total depth when the question asks for a **difference** in pressure.
- Explaining upthrust as "water pushes up" without the pressure difference between bottom and top surfaces.
- Saying atmospheric pressure falls because "there is less gravity" higher up. It falls because there is
  less air (fewer molecules, less weight of air) above.
- Forgetting the units: pressure in Pa, density in kg/m³.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: recall p = F/A; take p = hρg from the equation sheet. Convert areas and depths first.
- **Explain** why pressure changes with depth: "taller column of liquid above, so greater weight of liquid
  on each square metre".
- **Explain** upthrust: deeper bottom surface → greater pressure → greater force upwards than downwards →
  resultant upward force.
- **Describe** factors affecting floating: weight compared with upthrust; density of the object compared with
  the fluid.
- **Explain** a change in atmospheric pressure: number of molecules and weight of air above the surface.
