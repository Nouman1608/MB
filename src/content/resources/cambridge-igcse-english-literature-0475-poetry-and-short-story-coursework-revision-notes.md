**These notes cover Component 5 assignments based on poetry or short stories.** The syllabus states:
"If poetry or short stories are used for an assignment, candidates must cover a minimum of two poems
or stories", and that candidates "are not required to compare" them, because the assignment is
assumed to rest on "a wider selection of poems or stories broadly equivalent to a poetry or short
stories set text". They condense the
[poems or short stories coursework study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-poetry-and-short-story-coursework/);
portfolio rules in full are in the
[Component 5 coursework revision notes](/resources/igcse-english-literature-component-5-coursework-revision-notes/).

## The rules in one table

| Rule | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| Minimum of two poems or stories | One piece alone does not meet the requirement |
| Comparison not required | Discuss pieces in turn if you wish; comparison is allowed |
| Wider selection assumed | Your reading should cover far more than the pieces you write on |
| Originally written in English | No translated texts |
| Quality appropriate for IGCSE | Equivalent in scope and demand to the set texts |
| 600–1200 words | Including quotations, excluding references and bibliography |
| Title allows all four AOs | It needs a focus, scope for analysis and room for judgement |
| Different text from the other assignment | The two assignments must be on different texts |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sequential structure | Discussing one piece, then the next, with a link and a shared conclusion |
| Integrated structure | Paragraphs organised by aspects of the focus, each covering both pieces |
| Linking sentence | A sentence that connects the second half of an essay to the first |
| Shared focus | The theme, speaker type or method that unites the chosen pieces |
| Embedded quotation | A short phrase built into your own sentence |
| Line-break mark | A "/" showing where a line of poetry ends within a quotation |
| Reference | Full details of any other source: author, date, page, or web address and date accessed |

## Choosing pieces

- Pick a **shared focus** that each piece treats **differently**.
- **Two pieces** usually suit 600–1200 words best; three spreads analysis thin.
- Choose pieces with enough **method** to analyse: imagery, form, voice, endings.

## Title checklist

- A clear **focus** (theme, speaker, method).
- A **"how"** element inviting analysis of methods (AO3).
- Room for **judgement** (AO4): "how far", "how effectively", "what do you find…".
- Clear **scope**: named pieces, or "two poems you have studied".

Weak: "Write about two stories." Strong: "How do the writers of two stories you have studied make
their endings surprising, and which do you find more effective?"

## Structures

**Sequential:** intro → piece 1 (two or three paragraphs) → linking sentence → piece 2 (two or three
paragraphs) → conclusion drawing both together.

**Integrated:** intro → aspect 1 (both pieces) → aspect 2 (both) → aspect 3 (both) → conclusion.

Either is acceptable. Sequential is easier to control; integrated can produce a more developed
argument.

## Writing about short stories rather than poems

- Treat each story's **whole shape**: opening, turning point and ending, since the syllabus expects
  study of the complete text.
- Analyse **narrative voice** and **withholding**, which carry much of a story's meaning.
- Quote **less densely** than for poems, but still embed short phrases.
- In a sequential essay, a story usually needs a sentence placing each quoted moment in the plot.
- Keep plot summary to that single placing sentence; the analysis earns the marks.

## Word budget for about 1,000 words

| Part | Approximate words |
|---|---|
| Introduction | 100 |
| First piece | 375 |
| Second piece | 375 |
| Conclusion | 150 |

## Traps

- **One piece only.**
- **Two unconnected mini-essays.**
- **Forced comparison** in empty "similarly" sentences.
- **Too many pieces** treated thinly.
- **No focus** in the title.
- **A translated text.**
- **Ignoring form** in poems and structure in stories.
- **Unreferenced sources**, which risks plagiarism.

## Self-test

1. What is the minimum number of poems or stories an assignment must cover?
2. Must the pieces be compared?
3. Why does the syllabus not require comparison?
4. Can a translated poem be used?
5. What is the word guidance, and what does it include and exclude?
6. Name the two main structures for this kind of assignment.
7. What is the purpose of a linking sentence in a sequential essay?
8. Why do two pieces usually suit the word limit better than four?
9. What four things should a good title include?
10. How should a line break be shown in a quotation from a poem?
11. What details should a reference to a website include?
12. Improve this title: "Two poems about nature."

**Answers:** 1. Two. 2. No; comparison is allowed but not required. 3. Because the assignment is
assumed to rest on a wider selection of poems or stories broadly equivalent to a set text. 4. No;
texts must have been originally written in English. 5. 600–1200 words, including quotations,
excluding references and bibliography. 6. Sequential and integrated. 7. To connect the second piece
to the first, so the essay has one argument rather than two separate halves. 8. Each piece gets
enough space for close analysis of methods. 9. A focus; an invitation to analyse how effects are
created; room for judgement; clear scope. 10. With a forward slash, "/". 11. The website address and
the date it was accessed. 12. For example: "How do two poems you have studied present nature as both
beautiful and threatening, and which do you find more powerful?"
