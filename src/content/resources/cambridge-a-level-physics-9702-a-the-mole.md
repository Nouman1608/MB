This guide covers subtopic **15.1 The mole** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Ideal Gases study guide](/resources/a-physics-ideal-gases/) introduces all of Topic 15 and states the definition of the mole; this page goes further into using molar quantities: where amount of substance sits among the SI base quantities, how to move between mass, moles and number of particles without unit slips, how the mole links to the unified atomic mass unit, and what particle counts reveal about the spacing of molecules in solids, liquids and gases. For quick recall use the [The mole revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-the-mole-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [The mole practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-the-mole-practice/).

## What 15.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists two outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand that **amount of substance** is an **SI base quantity** with the base unit **mol**
- use **molar quantities** where one mole of any substance is the amount containing a number of particles of that substance equal to the **Avogadro constant N_A**

The Avogadro constant appears in the data list as N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Everything in this subtopic is a matter of using it, and the molar mass, correctly.

## Amount of substance as a base quantity

At AS Level (Topic 1), you met five SI base quantities: mass (kg), length (m), time (s), current (A) and thermodynamic temperature (K). Topic 15 adds a sixth used in this course:

| Base quantity | Base unit |
|---|---|
| Mass | kilogram, kg |
| Length | metre, m |
| Time | second, s |
| Electric current | ampere, A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin, K |
| **Amount of substance** | **mole, mol** |

Amount of substance is **not** the same as mass. A mole of hydrogen molecules and a mole of lead atoms contain the same number of particles but have very different masses. It counts particles, in units of N_A.

Because the mole is a base unit, derived units can include it: the molar gas constant R has unit J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, molar mass has unit kg mol⁻¹ (or g mol⁻¹), and N_A itself has unit **mol⁻¹** (it is a number of particles *per mole*, not a pure number).

## The three linked quantities

For a sample of mass m (in kg) of a substance with molar mass M (in kg mol⁻¹):

```
amount of substance:   n = m / M
number of particles:   N = n N_A
mass of one particle:  m₀ = M / N_A
```

The particles must be specified: atoms for a metal or a monatomic gas such as helium, molecules for water, nitrogen or oxygen.

**Molar mass and relative mass.** One unified atomic mass unit, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, and 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg × 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.00 × 10⁻³ kg mol⁻¹. So a particle of mass A u has a molar mass of A grams per mole. A nitrogen molecule (28 u) has M = 28 g mol⁻¹ = 0.028 kg mol⁻¹, and its mass is 28 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg, the same as M/N_A = 0.028 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³.

## Worked example 1: atoms in a copper block

A copper block has mass 1.00 kg. The molar mass of copper is 63.5 g mol⁻¹. Calculate the amount of copper and the number of atoms.

```
M = 63.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0635 kg mol⁻¹
n = m / M = 1.00 / 0.0635 = 15.7 mol
N = n N_A = 15.7 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 9.48 × 10²⁴ atoms
```

*Annotation.* The commonest error is dividing 1.00 kg by 63.5 without converting. Keep both in kilograms (or both in grams); the answer for n is the same either way only if the units match.

## Worked example 2: how far apart are the molecules?

Water has molar mass 18 g mol⁻¹ and density 1000 kg m⁻³. Estimate the spacing of molecules in liquid water, then compare it with the spacing in a gas where one mole occupies 0.024 m³ at room conditions.

```
liquid:  volume of 1 mol = 0.018 kg / 1000 kg m⁻³ = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ m³
         volume per molecule = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ / 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.0 × 10⁻²⁹ m³
         spacing ≈ ∛(3.0 × 10⁻²⁹) = 3.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m

gas:     volume per molecule = 0.024 / 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.0 × 10⁻²⁶ m³
         spacing ≈ ∛(4.0 × 10⁻²⁶) = 3.4 × 10⁻⁹ m
```

*Annotation.* Treating each molecule as occupying a small cube gives the typical separation. In the liquid, molecules are about 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart, roughly their own size, so they are touching. In the gas they are about ten times further apart, so the volume of the molecules themselves is a tiny fraction of the gas volume. This is the evidence behind one of the kinetic theory assumptions in the [Kinetic theory of gases guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-kinetic-theory-of-gases/).

## Number density

Many gas questions need the number of particles per unit volume, N/V (unit m⁻³). From the gas figure above, N/V = 6.02 × 10²³ ÷ 0.024 = 2.5 × 10²⁵ m⁻³. Since particle numbers are huge, give answers in standard form and check the order of magnitude: about 10²⁵ molecules per cubic metre for a gas at room conditions, about 10²⁸ to 10²⁹ for a solid or liquid.

## Worked example 3: a mixture

A container holds 0.50 g of helium (molar mass 4.0 g mol⁻¹) and 0.50 g of argon (molar mass 40 g mol⁻¹). Which contains more atoms, and by what factor?

```
helium: n = 0.50 / 4.0 = 0.125 mol → N = 0.125 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 7.5 × 10²² atoms
argon:  n = 0.50 / 40  = 0.0125 mol → N = 7.5 × 10²¹ atoms
```

Equal masses, but helium has ten times as many atoms, because each helium atom has one tenth of the mass. Questions on pressure in Topic 15 depend on N, not on mass, which is why the mole matters.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the mole as a unit of mass.** It is a unit of amount: a count of particles.
- **Giving N_A without its unit**, mol⁻¹.
- **Dividing a mass in kg by a molar mass in g mol⁻¹.**
- **Counting atoms when molecules are asked for**, or the reverse: one mole of O₂ contains N_A molecules but 2N_A atoms.
- **Missing powers of ten** in numbers of particles; check the order of magnitude.
- **Confusing n (moles) with N (number of particles)** in later equations.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (the SI base unit of amount of substance): the mole, mol.
- **Explain** or **define** (what is meant by one mole): the amount of substance containing a number of particles equal to the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 10²³.
- **Calculate**: write n = m/M and N = nN_A, convert grams to kilograms where needed, and give large numbers in standard form.
- **Estimate** (molecular spacing): volume per molecule from molar volume ÷ N_A, then a cube root.
- **Show that**: give at least one more significant figure than the value to be shown.
