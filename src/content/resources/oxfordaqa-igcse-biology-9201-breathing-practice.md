These questions cover section **3.2.5 Breathing** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. Questions 10 and 11 draw on statement d
(mechanical ventilators), which is Biology only. All data are invented or typical approximate values, for
practice. A calculator is useful for questions 8 and 9.

Learn the content first in the [Breathing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-breathing/)
and the [Breathing revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-breathing-revision-notes/).
For mixed questions on breathing and respiration, use the [Breathing and respiration practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-breathing-and-respiration-practice/).

## Section A

**1.** Name:

**(a)** the muscles found between the ribs **[1]**
**(b)** the tiny air sacs where gases enter and leave the blood. **[1]**

**2.** State what happens to the volume and to the pressure inside the thorax when you breathe out. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why breathing and respiration are not the same process. **[2]**

**4.** State the part of the body in which the lungs are found, and name the structure that protects them. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a rich supply of blood capillaries around the alveoli helps oxygen to enter the blood. **[2]**

**6.** Exhaled air contains about 4% carbon dioxide, but inhaled air contains about 0.04%. Explain why. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A teacher uses a bell-jar model to show breathing. A Y-shaped tube leads to two balloons inside a glass
jar, and a rubber sheet covers the open base of the jar.

**(a)** State what the balloons represent. **[1]**
**(b)** The teacher pushes the rubber sheet upwards. Explain what happens to the balloons. **[3]**
**(c)** Give **two** ways in which the model does not accurately represent the human breathing system. **[2]**

**8.** A student models alveoli using cubes. One large cube has sides of 4 cm. It is compared with 64 small
cubes, each with sides of 1 cm.

**(a)** Show that the large cube and the 64 small cubes have the same total volume. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total surface area of the large cube and of the 64 small cubes. **[2]**
**(c)** Use your answers to explain why the lungs contain very many tiny alveoli rather than a few large sacs.
**[1]**

**9.** A person at rest takes 14 breaths per minute, with 0.5 dm³ of air per breath. Inhaled air contains 21%
oxygen and exhaled air contains 16% oxygen.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of air breathed per minute. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of oxygen absorbed into the blood per minute. **[2]**
**(c)** During exercise the person takes 30 breaths per minute of 1.5 dm³ each, and exhaled air contains 15%
oxygen. Calculate the volume of oxygen absorbed per minute during exercise. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest why the body absorbs more oxygen during exercise. **[1]**

**10.** Compare how a negative pressure ventilator and a positive pressure ventilator help a patient to breathe
in. **[4]**

**11.** An injury to the spinal cord stops the nerve signals that make the diaphragm and intercostal muscles
contract. Explain why the patient may need a mechanical ventilator. **[3]**

**12.** Describe how oxygen in the air outside the body reaches the blood. Include the roles of the muscles and
the alveoli. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Intercostal muscles [1]
**(b)** Alveoli [1]

**2.** The volume decreases [1]; the pressure increases (above that of the surrounding air) [1]

**3.** Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs, a physical process caused by muscles [1];
respiration is a chemical reaction in cells that transfers energy from glucose [1]

**4.** The thorax (upper part of the body) [1]; the ribcage [1]

**5.** The blood carries oxygen away from the alveoli as soon as it diffuses in [1]; so the oxygen concentration
in the blood next to the alveoli stays lower than in the air, keeping the concentration gradient steep [1]

**6.** Cells produce carbon dioxide in respiration, and the blood carries it to the lungs [1]; it diffuses from the
blood into the air in the alveoli, so exhaled air contains more [1]

**7. (a)** The lungs [1]
**(b)** The volume inside the jar decreases [1]; so the pressure inside increases above atmospheric pressure [1];
air is pushed out through the tube and the balloons deflate, modelling exhalation [1]
**(c)** Any two of the following, 1 mark each [2]: the jar is rigid, so it cannot model the ribs moving or the
intercostal muscles; the rubber sheet is moved by hand, not by its own muscle contracting; balloons are not made
of alveoli, so there is no gas exchange; the real diaphragm is dome-shaped at rest

**8. (a)** Large cube: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³; small cubes: 64 × (1 × 1 × 1) = 64 cm³ [1]
**(b)** Large cube: 6 × (4 × 4) = **96 cm²** [1]; small cubes: 64 × 6 × (1 × 1) = **384 cm²** [1]
**(c)** Dividing the same volume into many small units gives a much larger surface area (four times larger here),
so more gas can diffuse at once [1]

**9. (a)** 14 × 0.5 = **7.0 dm³ per minute** [1]
**(b)** Oxygen removed = 21% − 16% = 5% [1]; 7.0 × 0.05 = **0.35 dm³ per minute** [1]
**(c)** 30 × 1.5 = 45 dm³ per minute; 21% − 15% = 6% [1]; 45 × 0.06 = **2.7 dm³ per minute** [1]
**(d)** The muscles respire faster, so they need more oxygen [1]

**10.** Negative pressure: the chest is in a sealed chamber [1]; the pressure around the chest is lowered, so the
chest expands and air is drawn into the lungs [1]. Positive pressure: air is pushed into the airway at a pressure
above atmospheric [1]; so air is forced into the lungs [1]
*Examiner insight:* A compare answer must make clear which ventilator draws air in and which forces it in.

**11.** Without contraction of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, the volume of the thorax cannot increase
[1]; so the pressure does not fall and air is not drawn into the lungs (spontaneous breathing stops) [1]; a
ventilator moves air into the lungs so that oxygen can still reach the blood [1]

**12.** Marking points, 1 mark each [6]:
- the intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up, and the diaphragm contracts and flattens
- the volume of the thorax increases, so the pressure falls below atmospheric pressure
- air moves in through the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles to the alveoli
- the alveoli give a very large surface area for diffusion
- they are richly supplied with blood capillaries, which carry oxygen away and keep the gradient steep
- oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (high concentration) into the blood (low concentration)

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the diaphragm moves up during inhalation.
- Leaving out the pressure change between "volume increases" and "air enters".
- Calling breathing "respiration".
- Forgetting that exhaled air still contains oxygen.
- Giving the ventilator types the wrong way round.
- In calculations, using 21% or 16% instead of the difference between them.
