## Questions

**1.** Define price elasticity of supply. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the price elasticity of supply when:
**(a)** price rises from 5.00 cu to 5.60 cu and quantity supplied rises from 1,500 to 1,590 units. **[3]**
**(b)** price falls from 80 cu to 68 cu and quantity supplied falls from 2,000 to 1,640 units. **[3]**

**3.** State whether the price elasticity of supply is above 1, equal to 1 or below 1 at every point on a straight-line supply curve that **(i)** passes through the origin, **(ii)** meets the price axis at a positive price, **(iii)** meets the quantity axis at a positive quantity. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the price elasticity of supply of fresh strawberries is lower in the short run than in the long run. **[4]**

**5.** When the price of a service rises from 30 cu to 36 cu, the quantity supplied falls from 500 to 470 units.
**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the sign and the size of your answer show. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by consumer surplus and why it matters when a government is judging a policy. **[4]**

**7.** In Market K demand is Qd = 800 - 8P and supply is Qs = 10P - 100. Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity, consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[5]**

**8.** Demand in Market K rises to Qd = 1,160 - 8P. Supply is unchanged.
**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why both surpluses have increased. **[2]**

**9.** The same leftward shift of supply raises price in two markets, M and N. In M the price rises from 20 cu to 26 cu and quantity falls from 500 to 470. In N the price rises from 20 cu to 22 cu and quantity falls from 500 to 400.
**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand in each market. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the fall in consumer surplus in each market. Fall in consumer surplus = rise in price x average of old and new quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how price elasticity of demand affects the extent of the fall in consumer surplus. **[2]**

**10.** A tax of 9 cu per unit is placed on producers in Market K (demand Qd = 800 - 8P, supply Qs = 10P - 100).
**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus after the tax. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the loss of total surplus. **[2]**

**11.** A severe frost destroys much of the fruit crop in Country A. Demand for the fruit is price inelastic and short-run supply is price inelastic. Analyse the effects on price, quantity and consumer surplus, and evaluate how the effects may differ in the long run. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Price elasticity of supply shows how strongly quantity supplied reacts when the good's own price changes [1]. It is measured as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both the quantity supplied and the good's own price. Saying supply is "sensitive" leaves out what is measured.

**2. (a)** Percentage change in price = 0.60 / 5.00 x 100 = +12% [1]. Percentage change in quantity = 90 / 1,500 x 100 = +6% [1]. PES = 6 / 12 = **0.5**, so supply is inelastic [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Percentage change in price = -12 / 80 x 100 = -15% [1]. Percentage change in quantity = -360 / 2,000 x 100 = -18% [1]. PES = -18 / -15 = **1.2**, so supply is elastic [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both percentage changes before dividing. Check that quantity goes on top and that the original value is the base.

**3.** **(i)** Equal to 1 at every point [1]. **(ii)** Above 1 at every point [1]. **(iii)** Below 1 at every point [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answer in terms of where the curve starts, not its steepness.

**4.** Production time [1]: strawberry plants take a season or more to grow, so in the short run output cannot be raised quickly even if price rises [1]. Fixed factors [1]: land and plants are fixed in the short run, but in the long run farmers can plant more fields and new growers can enter, so output responds more [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a reason linked to responsiveness of output. A list of factors with no explanation of how they limit supply in the short run gains little.

**5. (a)** Percentage change in price = 6 / 30 x 100 = +20% [1]. Percentage change in quantity = -30 / 500 x 100 = -6%, so PES = -6 / 20 = **-0.3** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The negative sign shows that quantity supplied moves in the opposite direction to price [1]. The size, 0.3, shows that the response is weak, as quantity changes by less than the price does [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign. Dropping it turns an unusual result into a normal one.

**6.** Consumer surplus is the gap between the highest price consumers would pay and the price they do pay [1], shown as the area beneath the demand curve and over the price line [1]. A government can compare the change in consumer surplus before and after a policy to judge whether consumers gain or lose [1]. It can also weigh this against producer surplus and tax revenue to see whether total surplus, and so efficiency, rises or falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Matters" needs a use for the idea, such as judging a policy. A definition alone answers half the question.

**7.** Equilibrium: 800 - 8P = 10P - 100, so 900 = 18P and **P = 50 cu** [1], **Q = 400** [1]. Demand meets the price axis at P = 800 / 8 = 100 cu [1]. Supply meets the price axis at P = 100 / 10 = 10 cu. Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (100 - 50) x 400 = **10,000 cu** [1]. Producer surplus = 1/2 x (50 - 10) x 400 = **8,000 cu** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Find both price-axis intercepts before calculating areas.

**8. (a)** 1,160 - 8P = 10P - 100, so 1,260 = 18P and **P = 70 cu** [1], **Q = 600** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Demand now meets the price axis at 1,160 / 8 = 145 cu. Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (145 - 70) x 600 = **22,500 cu** [1]. Producer surplus = 1/2 x (70 - 10) x 600 = **18,000 cu** [1]. The supply intercept is unchanged at 10 cu [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Consumer surplus rose by 12,500 cu even though price rose, because the demand curve has moved up and consumers are willing to pay more for each unit [1]. Producer surplus rose by 10,000 cu because producers receive a higher price on each unit and sell more units [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c), explain why consumer surplus rises despite the higher price.

**9. (a)** Market M: percentage change in price = +30% and in quantity = -6%, so PED = -6 / 30 = **-0.2** (inelastic) [1]. Market N: price +10%, quantity -20%, so PED = **-2** (elastic) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Market M: 6 x (500 + 470) / 2 = 6 x 485 = **2,910 cu** [1]. Market N: 2 x (500 + 400) / 2 = 2 x 450 = **900 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** With inelastic demand, quantity demanded hardly falls, so the supply shift raises price by more [1]. Consumers then pay far more on the units they still buy, so consumer surplus falls by more, as in M compared with N [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your figures from parts (a) and (b) in part (c).

**10. (a)** The tax raises supply by 9 cu at each quantity: Qs = 10(P - 9) - 100 = 10P - 190 [1]. 800 - 8P = 10P - 190, so 990 = 18P and **P = 55 cu** [1], **Q = 360** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Consumer surplus = 1/2 x (100 - 55) x 360 = **8,100 cu** [1]. Producers keep 55 - 9 = 46 cu per unit [1]. Producer surplus = 1/2 x (46 - 10) x 360 = **6,480 cu** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Tax revenue = 9 x 360 = **3,240 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Total surplus before the tax = 10,000 + 8,000 = 18,000 cu. After the tax it is 8,100 + 6,480 + 3,240 = 17,820 cu [1]. The loss is **180 cu** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Producers receive the price minus the tax, so use that lower figure for producer surplus. Include tax revenue in the total.

**11.** The frost reduces supply sharply, shifting the supply curve to the left [1]. Because both supply and demand are inelastic, the shortage cannot be met by extra output and price rises sharply [1]. Quantity falls and consumers cut their purchases only a little, because demand is inelastic [1]. Consumer surplus falls because consumers pay a higher price and buy fewer units, so the area under demand and above price shrinks [1]. The fall is large because inelastic demand lets price rise by a large amount [1]. Producer surplus is uneven: growers with surviving crops gain a higher price, but those who lost fruit lose [1]. In the long run farmers can replant, new growers can enter and imports can be arranged, so supply becomes more elastic [1]. The supply curve shifts back to the right, price falls and quantity recovers, so consumer surplus is restored [1]. How fast this happens depends on how long trees or plants take to regrow and on whether stocks or imports exist [1]. Overall the damage to consumers is greatest in the short run, and a judgement on its size rests on how inelastic demand is and how quickly supply recovers [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a clear judgement and say what it depends on. Listing short-run and long-run effects without weighing them is incomplete.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the new price or quantity, not the original, as the base for percentages.
- Putting price change on top of the fraction, which gives the reciprocal of PES.
- Dropping a negative sign, or describing 1.2 as inelastic.
- Treating a steeper supply curve as always less elastic.
- Listing factors affecting PES without explaining how they limit or help output.
- Shading consumer surplus below the price or producer surplus above it.
- Forgetting the half in the area of a triangle, or using zero as the base for producer surplus when supply meets the price axis higher up.
- Using the old price when working out producer surplus after a tax.
- Stating that consumer surplus must fall when price rises, even when demand has risen.
- Evaluating without saying which time period, or which elasticity, the judgement depends on.
