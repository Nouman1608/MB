## Answers

**1.** All the atoms lie in the same (flat) plane [1].

**2. (a)** 109.5° [1].

**(b)** 120° [1].

**(c)** 180° [1].

**3. (a)** Branched [1].

**(b)** Cyclic [1].

**(c)** Straight-chained [1].

**4.** One σ bond and two π bonds [1].

**5.** Any two [2]: a σ bond forms by end-on overlap, a π bond by sideways overlap of p orbitals; σ
electron density lies on the line between the nuclei, π density lies above and below it; a σ bond is
stronger than a π bond; there is free rotation about a σ bond alone but not when a π bond is present.

**6. (a)** C1 and C2 are sp² [1]; C3 and C4 are sp³ [1].

**(b)** Bonds: eight C–H, one C=C and two C–C single bonds. σ = 8 + 1 + 2 = 11 [1]; π = 1 [1].

**(c)** Not planar [1]: C3 and C4 are sp³ and tetrahedral, so some of their hydrogen atoms (and C4)
lie out of the plane of the C=C unit [1].

**7. (a)** sp² [1].

**(b)** About 120° (accept 118–122°) [1].

**(c)** In methanal the carbonyl carbon is sp² and the two H atoms and the O atom bonded to it all lie
in one trigonal planar arrangement [1]. In ethanal the carbonyl carbon is still trigonal planar, but the
CH₃ carbon is sp³ and tetrahedral [1], so at least two of its hydrogen atoms must lie out of the plane
[1].

**8. (a)** CH₃ carbon is sp³ [1]; nitrile carbon is sp [1].

**(b)** H–C–C about 109.5° [1]; C–C–N 180° [1].

**(c)** σ: three C–H, one C–C and one in C≡N = 5 [1]; π = 2 [1].

**9. (a)** π bond in C=C ≈ 610 − 350 = 260 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; second π bond in C≡C ≈ 840 − 610 =
230 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** The π bond (about 260 kJ mol⁻¹) is weaker than a C–C σ bond (350 kJ mol⁻¹), so it is broken more
easily [1]. The π electrons lie above and below the plane of the molecule, exposed and accessible, so
this region of high electron density attracts electrophiles [1]. Alkanes have only σ bonds, with electron
density held between the nuclei and C–H bonds of low polarity, so there is nothing to attract an
electrophile [1].

**10. (a)** All three carbon atoms are sp² [1].

**(b)** Bonds: four C–H (two on C1, one on C2, one on C3), one C=C, one C–C and one C=O. Each bond
contains one σ bond, so σ = 4 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7 [1]; the C=C and C=O each add one π bond, so π = 2 [1].

**(c)** Yes [1]: every carbon is sp² and trigonal planar with no sp³ atom, so the whole framework,
including both π systems, can lie in a single plane [1].

**11. (a)** Cyclohexane is cyclic; hex-1-ene is straight-chained [1].

**(b)** About 109.5° [1]. Each carbon is sp³ and tetrahedral [1]; a flat hexagon would need angles of
120°, so the ring puckers to keep angles close to 109.5° [1].

**12. (a)** Central carbon sp [1]; each end carbon sp² [1].

**(b)** 180° [1].

**(c)** Six σ bonds (four C–H and one in each C=C) and two π bonds [1].

**13.** The C–C bond in ethane is a σ bond with electron density along the internuclear axis, so
rotation does not change the overlap [1]. The π bond in ethene forms by sideways overlap of parallel p
orbitals [1]; rotation about the C=C would twist the p orbitals out of line and break the π bond, which
needs much more energy than is available at room temperature [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting σ bonds without the C–H bonds, or forgetting that a CH₂ group has two of them.
- Writing that a double bond is "two σ bonds" or that a triple bond contains three π bonds.
- Calling a molecule planar because it has a C=C, when an sp³ carbon is also present.
- Giving 109.5° for an sp² carbon, or 120° for a ring carbon in cyclohexane.
- Explaining restricted rotation without saying that rotation would break the sideways p-orbital overlap.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I count σ and π bonds quickly?**
Count every bond in the displayed formula as one σ bond, then add one π for each double bond and two π
for each triple bond. Writing the count bond type by bond type earns method marks even if one total
slips.

**Which atoms count as sp hybridised at AS Level?**
Carbon in a triple bond (C≡C or C≡N), carbon with two double bonds (as in propadiene), and the nitrogen
of a nitrile group. All have a linear 180° arrangement.
