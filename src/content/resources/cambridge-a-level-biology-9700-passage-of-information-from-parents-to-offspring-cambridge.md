This guide covers section **16.1 Passage of information from parents to offspring** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 4. The [Inheritance study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-inheritance/) introduces all of Topic 16; this page goes further into meiosis itself: what happens to chromosomes, the nuclear envelope, the cell surface membrane and the spindle at each stage, how to identify stages in images, and how to count chromosomes, chromatids and DNA through the process. ## What 16.1 asks you to do

| Outcome | In short |
|---|---|
| 16.1.1–2 | Explain haploid (n), diploid (2n) and homologous pairs of chromosomes |
| 16.1.3 | Explain the need for a reduction division in gamete production |
| 16.1.4 | Describe chromosome behaviour in plant and animal cells during meiosis, with the nuclear envelope, cell surface membrane and spindle |
| 16.1.5 | Interpret photomicrographs and diagrams and identify the main stages |
| 16.1.6 | Explain how crossing over and random orientation produce genetically different gametes |
| 16.1.7 | Explain how random fusion of gametes produces genetically different individuals |

You need the names of the eight main stages, but **not** the sub-divisions of prophase I.

## Haploid, diploid and homologous pairs

- A **haploid** cell (n) has **one** set of chromosomes. Human gametes have 23.
- A **diploid** cell (2n) has **two** sets, one from each parent. Human body cells have 46.
- A **homologous pair** is two chromosomes, one maternal and one paternal, with the **same genes at the same loci**, the same length and the same centromere position. They may carry **different alleles** of those genes.

A useful test: "Could these two chromosomes pair up in prophase I?" Only homologues can.

## Why a reduction division is needed

If gametes were diploid, a zygote formed by fertilisation would have 4n chromosomes, the next generation 8n, and so on. Meiosis **halves** the chromosome number, so the fusion of two haploid gametes restores the diploid number and the chromosome number stays **constant** from generation to generation. Meiosis also produces gametes that each carry **one chromosome from every homologous pair**, so each gamete has a complete set of genes.

## Meiosis stage by stage

Before meiosis, DNA is replicated in interphase, so each chromosome consists of **two sister chromatids** joined at the centromere.

### Meiosis I (separates homologous chromosomes)

| Stage | Chromosomes | Nuclear envelope, spindle and membrane |
|---|---|---|
| Prophase I | Condense; homologues pair to form **bivalents**; non-sister chromatids cross over at **chiasmata** | Nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms (in animal cells from centrioles at the poles; plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle) |
| Metaphase I | Bivalents line up on the **equator**; each pair orients randomly | Spindle fibres attach to centromeres |
| Anaphase I | **Homologous chromosomes** are pulled to opposite poles; centromeres do **not** divide, so each chromosome still has two chromatids | Spindle fibres shorten |
| Telophase I | Chromosomes reach the poles | Nuclear envelopes may re-form; cytokinesis gives two cells: in animals the cell surface membrane pinches in; in plants a new cell wall forms between them |

### Meiosis II (separates sister chromatids)

| Stage | Chromosomes | Nuclear envelope, spindle and membrane |
|---|---|---|
| Prophase II | Chromosomes condense again | Any re-formed nuclear envelope breaks down; new spindles form |
| Metaphase II | Individual chromosomes line up on the equator; chromatids orient randomly | Spindle fibres attach to centromeres |
| Anaphase II | **Centromeres divide**; sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles | Spindle fibres shorten |
| Telophase II | Chromatids (now chromosomes) reach the poles | Nuclear envelopes re-form; cytokinesis gives **four haploid cells** |

## Counting through meiosis

### Worked example 1: chromosomes, chromatids and DNA

A cell from an organism with 2n = 6 enters meiosis. In G1 the nucleus contains 10 arbitrary units of DNA.

| Point in the cycle (per cell) | Chromosomes | Chromatids | DNA / arbitrary units |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 before meiosis | 6 | 6 | 10 |
| After DNA replication | 6 | 12 | 20 |
| End of meiosis I (each of two cells) | 3 | 6 | 10 |
| End of meiosis II (each of four cells) | 3 | 3 | 5 |

The **chromosome number halves in meiosis I**, which is why meiosis I is the reduction division. DNA content halves at each division. Questions often ask you to sketch DNA mass against time: it doubles in interphase, then falls in two steps.

## Identifying stages in images (outcome 16.1.5)

Use these clues, in this order:

1. **Count the groups.** One cell → meiosis I (prophase I to anaphase I); two cells side by side → telophase I or meiosis II; four nuclei or four cells → end of telophase II.
2. **Look at the equator.** Pairs of chromosomes (bivalents) → metaphase I. Single chromosomes, each with two chromatids → metaphase II.
3. **Look at what is moving.** Chromosomes still made of two chromatids moving apart → anaphase I. Single chromatids moving apart → anaphase II.
4. **Check the number.** In anaphase of mitosis, each pole receives 2n chromatids; in anaphase II, each pole receives n.

### Worked example 2: an annotated identification

*A photomicrograph of an anther from a plant with 2n = 12 shows two cells side by side. In each, chromatids are moving to opposite poles, with six reaching each pole. Identify the stage, with reasons.*

- Two cells side by side: meiosis I has already finished, so this is **meiosis II**.
- Single chromatids moving apart: centromeres have divided, so this is **anaphase II**.
- Six chromatids to each pole equals n (12 ÷ 2): consistent with meiosis II, not mitosis, which would send 12 to each pole.

## How meiosis produces variation

### Crossing over (prophase I)

Non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair break and rejoin at **chiasmata**, exchanging sections. Alleles on the same chromosome are reshuffled, so chromatids that leave the cell carry **new combinations** of maternal and paternal alleles.

### Random orientation (independent assortment)

- **Metaphase I**: each bivalent lines up independently of the others, so which maternal and paternal chromosomes go to each pole is random.
- **Metaphase II**: because of crossing over, sister chromatids are no longer identical, so their random orientation adds further variety.

### Random fertilisation

Any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete, so the combination of two genetically different gametes in each zygote is random.

### Worked example 3: counting combinations

*A fruit fly has 2n = 8. Ignoring crossing over, how many genetically different gametes can one fly produce, and how many chromosome combinations are possible in the offspring of two flies?*

```
homologous pairs: n = 4
gametes per parent: 2⁴ = 16
zygote combinations: 16 × 16 = 256
```

Crossing over makes the real number of combinations far larger than this.

## Common mistakes

- Saying homologous chromosomes are "identical". They carry the same genes but may have **different alleles**.
- Putting crossing over in metaphase I. It happens in **prophase I**, between **non-sister** chromatids.
- Saying centromeres divide in anaphase I. They divide in **anaphase II**.
- Writing that meiosis II is the reduction division. The chromosome number halves in **meiosis I**.
- Describing plant cell division with a cleavage furrow, or with centrioles.
- Calling random orientation "random assortment of genes". The syllabus term is random orientation of **pairs of homologous chromosomes** (independent assortment).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (16.1.1–3, 16.1.6–7): give the consequence. "Gametes are haploid **so that** fertilisation restores the diploid number."
- **Describe** chromosome behaviour (16.1.4): for each stage, say what the chromosomes, nuclear envelope and spindle are doing; mention plant and animal differences if asked.
- **Identify** stages (16.1.5): name the stage, then give at least one visible feature as evidence.
- In counting questions, state whether you are counting **chromosomes** or **chromatids**.
