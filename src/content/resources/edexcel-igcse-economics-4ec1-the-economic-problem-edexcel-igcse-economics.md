## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | The problem of scarcity: unlimited wants and finite resources, leading to the need to make choices |
| b | Opportunity cost and its effect on economic agents (consumers, producers and government) |
| c | The use of diagrams to show the production possibility curve |
| d | The PPF used to show maximum productive potential, fully employed or unemployed resources, opportunity cost, positive or negative growth (shifts outwards and inwards), and possible and unobtainable production |
| e | Possible causes of positive or negative economic growth |

## Scarcity as a chain (point a)

The specification's wording is itself a chain, and exam answers score best when they follow it:

1. **Wants are unlimited.** Once one want is met, another appears: a family with a car wants a second one, or a better one.
2. **Resources are finite.** There is a limited amount of land, labour, capital and enterprise at any time.
3. **So not every want can be satisfied.** This gap is scarcity.
4. **So choices must be made.** Every economy, firm and household must decide which wants to meet and which to leave unmet.

Scarcity applies to time as well as goods. A student with three free hours on Saturday faces scarcity just as a government does: wants (study, sport, a part-time job, rest) exceed the hours available. The idea that choices are forced is what links scarcity to opportunity cost.

## Opportunity cost and the three economic agents (point b)

Opportunity cost is the **next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made. The specification asks about its *effect* on agents, so go beyond the definition and show how it shapes decisions.

| Agent | Typical choice | Opportunity cost | Effect on behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer | Spend income or time on one thing rather than another | The good or activity not chosen | Consumers compare what they give up, not just the price; a high opportunity cost may delay or change a purchase |
| Producer | Use a factory, land or staff for one product rather than another | The output or profit from the next best use | Firms move resources to the use with the best return, and may stop making a product whose opportunity cost is high |
| Government | Spend a budget on one service rather than another | The service or project not funded | Every new project means another is not built, or taxes or borrowing must rise |

### Worked example: a producer's choice

*Invented figures.* A farmer in Country P has 50 hectares. Planted with maize, each hectare earns a profit of 240 a year; planted with beans, 300 a year; left as grazing land, 150 a year. The farmer is deciding whether to plant maize.

```
Profit from maize   = 50 × 240 = 12 000
Profit from beans   = 50 × 300 = 15 000   (next best alternative)
Profit from grazing = 50 × 150 =  7 500
```

If the farmer plants maize, the opportunity cost is the **beans**, worth 15 000 in profit, not beans plus grazing. Because the opportunity cost (15 000) is larger than the gain from maize (12 000), opportunity cost reasoning suggests switching to beans. This shows how opportunity cost affects producers: it signals where resources earn most.

### Worked example: a government's choice

*Invented figures.* Country Q's government can spend an extra 90 million on either 600 new nurses at 150 000 each, or 1 500 new teachers at 60 000 each. Check: 600 × 150 000 = 90 million and 1 500 × 60 000 = 90 million. If it hires nurses, the opportunity cost is the 1 500 teachers. A good evaluation might note that the opportunity cost falls on a different group (pupils rather than patients), which is why such choices are political.

## Drawing the PPF (point c)

Examiners award marks for accuracy, so follow a routine:

1. Label the vertical axis with one good and the horizontal axis with the other, both with units if given.
2. Plot the points from the data, starting at each axis intercept.
3. Join them with a smooth curve bowed outwards from the origin (or a straight line if the opportunity cost is constant).
4. Label the curve PPF (or PPF1 if a second curve is to follow).
5. Mark and label any points the question names.

A straight-line PPF shows a **constant** opportunity cost; a curve bowed outwards shows an **increasing** opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods.

## Reading the PPF (point d)

*Invented data.* Country R can produce cars and wheat (thousands of units a year).

| Combination | Cars | Wheat |
|---|---|---|
| V | 0 | 50 |
| W | 5 | 45 |
| X | 10 | 36 |
| Y | 15 | 22 |
| Z | 20 | 0 |

- **Maximum productive potential:** every combination V to Z is on the frontier, so resources are fully employed.
- **Opportunity cost:** moving from X to Y gains 5 thousand cars and loses 36 − 22 = 14 thousand tonnes of wheat, so each thousand cars costs 14 ÷ 5 = **2.8** thousand tonnes of wheat. Between V and W it was only 5 ÷ 5 = 1.0. The rising cost explains the bowed shape.
- **Unemployed resources:** a combination of 10 cars and 30 wheat lies inside the curve (the frontier gives 36 wheat at 10 cars). It is possible but shows unemployed resources; 6 thousand tonnes of wheat could be added at no cost in cars.
- **Unobtainable production:** 15 cars and 30 wheat lies outside the curve (the frontier gives only 22 wheat at 15 cars), so it cannot be produced with current resources.

### Growth and decline on the diagram

- **Positive economic growth** shifts the whole PPF outwards (draw PPF2 to the right of PPF1). Points that were unobtainable may become possible.
- **Negative economic growth** shifts it inwards.
- **A change affecting one good only** pivots the curve: only that good's intercept moves.

Do not confuse a move from inside the curve to the curve with growth. That is using unemployed resources, not an increase in productive potential.

## Causes of positive or negative growth (point e)

| Cause | Direction | How it changes the PPF |
|---|---|---|
| Investment in capital goods | Positive | More machines and infrastructure raise potential output |
| Education and training | Positive | Higher-quality labour produces more per hour |
| New technology | Positive | More output from the same resources |
| Discovery of natural resources | Positive | More land (in the economic sense) available |
| Immigration of workers | Positive | More labour |
| Natural disasters or war | Negative | Capital and land destroyed, workers lost |
| Emigration of skilled workers | Negative | Less labour and lower quality |
| Depletion of resources | Negative | Less land available |

**A useful exam insight:** choosing more capital goods now has an opportunity cost in consumer goods today, but it can shift the PPF outwards in future. That ties points b, d and e together in one chain.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** *Explain one cause of negative economic growth.* (Explain means a statement of fact with two further expansion points.)

> A major earthquake can cause negative economic growth ✔ (cause). Factories, roads and power lines are destroyed, so the economy has less capital ✔ (expansion 1). With fewer resources, the maximum the economy can produce falls, so the PPF shifts inwards ✔ (expansion 2).

*Commentary:* each sentence adds a link; ending with the PPF shows the examiner the economic consequence, not just the event.

## Common mistakes

- Defining opportunity cost as "the cost of all the other options".
- Showing growth as a movement along the curve.
- Calling a point inside the PPF "unobtainable".
- Leaving axes or curves unlabelled.
- Calculating opportunity cost without dividing by the number of units gained.
- Explaining scarcity as poverty or a lack of money.

## Exam technique for the 4EC1 command words

- **Define / What is meant by:** two separate parts, for example "unlimited wants" and "finite resources".
- **Calculate:** show the subtraction and the division.
- **Draw:** label axes, curve(s) and points.
- **Explain:** statement plus two developments, in the context given.
- **Analyse / Assess / Evaluate:** develop chains and, for assess and evaluate, weigh factors and reach a judgement.
