## Plant communities and succession

**Succession** is the sequence of plant communities (seral stages) that colonise an area over time. Each stage changes the conditions, for example by adding organic matter or shade, so that it is replaced by the next.

- **Primary succession** starts on a surface with no soil, such as new lava. After the 1883 eruption of Krakatau in Indonesia sterilised the remaining islands, scientists recorded their recolonisation: ferns and grasses first, then shrubs and, over decades, forest.
- **Secondary succession** starts where vegetation is removed but soil remains, such as abandoned farmland. In the American tropics, fast-growing, light-demanding pioneer trees such as *Cecropia* quickly form a secondary forest, which is gradually replaced by slower-growing, shade-tolerant species.

**Climax.** Frederic Clements (1916) argued that each climatic region has one final, stable community, the **climatic climax** (the monoclimax view). In the humid tropics this is rainforest. Arthur Tansley later argued for a **polyclimax**, in which soils, drainage and relief can also produce stable communities.

**Subclimax.** Succession halted by a natural factor:

- *Edaphic* (soil): swamp forest on waterlogged soils, or open vegetation on very thin soils over rock.
- *Natural fire* from lightning in a seasonal climate.

**Plagioclimax.** Succession deflected or held by human activity. Examples include secondary forest left after shifting cultivation, grassland kept open by burning and grazing, and plantations.

**The savanna debate.** Savanna has been explained as all three: a climatic climax of the wet-dry climate; an edaphic subclimax on infertile or seasonally waterlogged soils; or a plagioclimax maintained by fires set by people and by grazing. Evidence from plots protected from fire, which often become woodland, supports the fire explanation in wetter savannas. In drier savannas, where rainfall is too low for closed woodland, climate is the stronger control. A good conclusion is that the balance of controls varies with rainfall.

## Vegetation characteristics and the reasons for them

| Feature | Rainforest | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Layered structure | Emergents (often over 40 m), canopy, understorey, shrub layer, sparse ground layer | Competition for light |
| Buttress roots | Large flanges at the base of tall trees | Support in shallow soils, where roots stay near the surface to catch nutrients |
| Drip-tip leaves | Pointed, waxy leaves | Shed water quickly, discouraging fungi and algae |
| Lianas and epiphytes | Climbing plants and plants growing on branches | Reach light without building a trunk |
| Thin, smooth bark | Little protection needed | No cold or dry season, few fires |
| Very high biodiversity | Many species per hectare | Stable climate over long periods, many niches |

| Feature | Savanna | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grass layer with scattered trees | Tall grasses in wetter savanna, shorter in drier | Water stress and fire limit tree cover |
| Deep tap roots | Acacias and other trees | Reach groundwater in the dry season |
| Small, waxy or thorny leaves | Reduce transpiration; thorns deter browsers | Drought and grazing |
| Deciduous trees | Leaves shed in the dry season | Saves water |
| Water storage | Baobab's swollen trunk | Survives long dry seasons |
| Thick, corky bark; underground buds | Trees and grasses survive burning (pyrophytes) | Frequent dry-season fires |

Savanna is not uniform. Tree cover usually decreases from wetter woodland savanna near the rainforest to drier grass savanna towards the semi-arid margin, a gradient worth describing in answers.

## Nutrient cycling: reading a Gersmehl diagram

Gersmehl's model (1976) shows three stores, **biomass**, **litter** and **soil**, as circles sized in proportion to the nutrients they hold, and flows as arrows sized by the amount transferred.

**Rainforest:**

- Biomass is by far the largest store.
- Litter is small: in hot, wet conditions decomposers break leaves down within weeks to months.
- Soil is small: nutrients released are quickly taken up by roots, often via fungal partners (mycorrhizae), or leached away.
- Flows of litterfall, decomposition and uptake are large and fast.

**Savanna:**

- Biomass is smaller.
- The soil store is relatively larger, because leaching is seasonal and grass roots return organic matter.
- Litter is small because fire burns it and termites remove it.
- Fire short-circuits the cycle: nutrients move straight from biomass and litter to the soil as ash, but some, such as nitrogen, are lost to the atmosphere.

## Soil fertility

Tropical rainforest soils are typically **infertile** despite supporting the most productive land vegetation. Reasons:

- **Leaching** by heavy rain removes soluble bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium).
- The dominant clay, **kaolinite**, and the iron and aluminium oxides have a low **cation exchange capacity**: they hold few nutrient ions.
- Soils are acidic, and aluminium can reach levels harmful to crops.
- Most nutrients are in the biomass, not the soil.

When forest is burned, ash gives a short burst of fertility. Within a few years, crops remove nutrients and rain leaches what remains, so yields fall. This is why shifting cultivation moved on after a few seasons, and why permanent farming on cleared rainforest needs fertiliser.

Savanna soils are often less leached and can hold more nutrients, but they suffer from seasonal water shortage, low organic matter in drier areas, and hard ironstone layers that restrict roots.

## Energy flows and trophic levels

Producers fix solar energy by photosynthesis. **Net primary productivity (NPP)** is the energy or biomass left after the plants' own respiration, available to consumers. Widely quoted estimates by Whittaker and Likens give a mean NPP of about 2 200 g of dry matter per m² per year for tropical rainforest and about 900 g per m² per year for savanna.

Energy passes through **trophic levels**: producers, primary consumers (herbivores), secondary consumers and tertiary consumers. Only a small fraction, often taken as about 10%, passes from one level to the next; the rest is lost in respiration, as heat and in waste.

**Worked example (illustrative values).** *A savanna produces NPP equivalent to 15 000 kJ per m² per year. Using a 10% transfer, estimate the energy available at each level.*

- Primary consumers (grazers such as zebra and wildebeest): 15 000 × 0.10 = **1 500 kJ**.
- Secondary consumers (such as lions): 1 500 × 0.10 = **150 kJ**.
- Tertiary consumers: 150 × 0.10 = **15 kJ**.

Each level supports far less energy, which is why large predators are few. Savanna supports large herds of grazers because much of its productivity is in grass, which is easily eaten, whereas much rainforest productivity is locked in wood and in the canopy, so large ground-dwelling herbivores are scarce.

## Soil formation

Soils form through the interaction of climate, parent material, organisms, relief and time.

**Processes:**

- **Ferrallitisation:** intense chemical weathering and leaching remove silica and bases, leaving iron and aluminium oxides (sesquioxides) that give red and yellow colours.
- **Leaching:** downward movement of soluble materials in percolating water.
- **Laterisation:** iron oxides concentrate and harden into laterite (ferricrete), especially where wetting alternates with drying or after forest removal exposes the soil.
- **Capillary rise:** in the savanna dry season, evaporation draws water and dissolved minerals upwards.

**Oxisol (latosol) profile under rainforest:** thin litter and a thin dark A horizon; a very deep red or yellow B horizon rich in sesquioxides and kaolinite, often many metres thick; gradual change to saprolite; few distinct horizons because of intense mixing by organisms.

**Tropical red and brown earths under savanna:** shallower and less leached; wet-season leaching followed by dry-season capillary rise; ironstone concretions or a laterite crust in the B horizon; red where drainage is good and iron is oxidised, browner where organic matter is higher or drainage less free.

**The catena.** On savanna slopes, soils change in a repeated sequence from hilltop to valley floor, a concept first described by Geoffrey Milne in East Africa in 1935. Well-drained red soils on upper slopes give way to browner soils on mid-slopes and to grey or black, seasonally waterlogged clays in the valley bottoms, where fine material and bases accumulate. Vegetation changes along the same slope, so relief adds variety within the savanna.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why rainforest soils are infertile even though rainforest vegetation is so productive. [6]*

> Heavy rainfall percolates through the soil all year (**cause**), leaching soluble bases such as calcium and potassium (**process**). Intense weathering leaves kaolinite and iron and aluminium oxides, which have a low cation exchange capacity, so the soil holds few nutrients (**soil chemistry**). Decomposition is so fast that nutrients are taken up by roots almost as soon as they are released (**nutrient cycle**), so the biomass, not the soil, is the main store (**Gersmehl evidence**). The vegetation is productive because it recycles nutrients rapidly in a hot, wet climate, not because the soil is rich (**link to the question**).

## Common mistakes

- Saying that rainforest has fertile soil because of its lush vegetation.
- Drawing Gersmehl diagrams with circles of equal size.
- Using plagioclimax for any disturbed vegetation, including natural fire, which produces a subclimax.
- Describing vegetation features without explaining their purpose.
- Mixing up soil horizons: the oxisol's deep red horizon is the B horizon.

## Exam technique

- **Compare** questions on nutrient cycles: use linked statements on each store and flow.
- **Draw** Gersmehl diagrams with scaled circles and arrows, labelled, and refer to them.
- **Evaluate** whether savanna is a climatic climax: weigh climate, soils and fire, and vary the judgement with rainfall.
