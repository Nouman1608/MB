These questions cover **Introduction to microeconomics** (1.1 The economic problem, 1.2 The allocation of resources, 1.3 Opportunity cost) in Component 01 of the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), for first assessment in 2021. Revise first with the [introduction to microeconomics study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-introduction-to-microeconomics/) and the [introduction to microeconomics revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-introduction-to-microeconomics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a normative statement? **[1]**

A: Raising the minimum wage by 5% would reduce employment among young workers

B: Average rents in the city rose by 8% last year

C: The government ought to spend more on public transport

D: A higher tax on fuel would lead people to drive less

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes allocative efficiency? **[1]**

A: Producing at the lowest possible average cost

B: Producing the combination of goods that best matches society's wants

C: Using all available resources so the economy is on its PPC

D: Distributing income equally between households

**3.** Distinguish between an economic good and a free good, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** State the reward earned by each of the four factors of production. **[2]**

**5.** A student could spend Saturday working a shift that pays 72 cu, revising for an exam, or playing in a football match. She chooses to revise. She would have chosen the shift as her second choice. State the opportunity cost of revising and explain why the football match is not part of it. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the difference between a movement along a production possibility curve and an outward shift of the curve. **[4]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** An economy's production possibilities for two goods are shown below (invented data).

| Point | Food (units) | Machines (units) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 0 |
| B | 90 | 10 |
| C | 70 | 20 |
| D | 40 | 30 |
| E | 0 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one machine when moving from B to C, and from D to E. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what these figures show about the shape of the PPC. **[2]**

**(c)** The economy produces 60 food and 20 machines. Explain what this shows. **[2]**

**8.** A town introduces a charge of 0.10 cu for each single-use carrier bag (invented). Bag use falls from 2.4 million to 0.6 million a year.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in bag use. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the revenue from the charge after it is introduced. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **two** reasons why this incentive was effective. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why consumers may not behave rationally when choosing an energy supplier. **[4]**

**10.** A government plans an invented programme to subsidise home insulation. Explain how the objectives of households, firms and the government might differ in relation to this programme. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that the assumption of rationality is of little use for understanding the behaviour of economic agents. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a market economy allocates resources more effectively than a planned economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. "Ought" signals a value judgement. Options A and D are testable predictions, so they are positive even if they prove wrong. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Option A is productive efficiency; C describes being on the PPC; D concerns equity. **[1]**

**3.** An economic good uses scarce resources, so producing it has an opportunity cost [1], for example a loaf of bread [1]. A free good is available without using up scarce resources, so it has no opportunity cost [1], for example air in an unpolluted place [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Land: rent; labour: wages [1]. Capital: interest; enterprise: profit [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The opportunity cost is the 72 cu wage from the shift, the next best alternative [1]. The football match is not part of it because opportunity cost is only the single next best option forgone, not every alternative [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A movement along the PPC is a reallocation of existing resources between the two goods [1], showing the opportunity cost of producing more of one in terms of the other [1]. An outward shift is an increase in the economy's productive capacity [1], caused by more or better factors of production or improved technology, so more of both goods can be produced [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** B to C: 20 food for 10 machines, so 2 food per machine [1]. D to E: 40 food for 10 machines, so 4 food per machine [1].

**(b)** The opportunity cost of machines rises as more are produced, so the PPC is concave to the origin [1], because resources are not equally suited to both goods and less suitable ones must be switched [1].

**(c)** With 20 machines, the economy could produce 70 food (point C), so 60 food lies inside the curve [1]. Resources are unemployed or used inefficiently, so 10 more food could be produced at no opportunity cost [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (2.4 − 0.6) ÷ 2.4 × 100 [1] = **75%** fall [1].

**(b)** Revenue is 0.6 million × 0.10 = **60,000 cu** a year [1].

**(c)** Reason 1: the charge was visible at the moment of purchase, so shoppers were aware of it every time [1], and the cost of avoiding it (bringing a reusable bag) was low [1]. Reason 2: close substitutes were available, such as reusable bags [1], so demand for single-use bags was price elastic and shoppers could switch easily [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Reason 1: information is complex, with many tariffs, standing charges and contract terms [1], so consumers cannot easily calculate which is cheapest and may pick a poor deal [1]. Reason 2: switching takes time and effort, so consumers stay with their current supplier out of habit or inertia [1], even when a cheaper option exists [1]. Other valid reasons: distrust of new suppliers; underestimating future energy use. **[4]**

**10.** Households aim to maximise utility: they want lower energy bills and warmer homes at the lowest net cost to themselves [1], and may be reluctant if the remaining cost or disruption is high [1]. Firms that install insulation aim to maximise profit [1]: the subsidy raises demand for their services, and they may raise prices if capacity is limited [1]. The government aims to maximise social welfare [1], for example cutting energy use and emissions and helping low-income households, while keeping the cost to taxpayers within its budget [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Define rationality: agents weigh costs and benefits to meet an objective [1].
- Useful: produces testable predictions, such as lower quantity demanded when price rises [1].
- Useful: gives a benchmark of the best possible choice [1].
- Useful: individual errors may cancel out, so market-level predictions hold [1].
- Of little use: information is often imperfect or complex [1].
- Of little use: calculation is costly, so agents use rules of thumb [1].
- Of little use: habit, emotion and concern for others shape choices [1].
- Application to an example, such as weekly grocery shopping versus choosing a pension [1].
- Depends on the type of decision: frequent and simple versus rare and complex [1].
- Judgement: useful for predicting the direction of responses, weaker for explaining individual choices, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A verdict that names the kind of decision for which rationality works reads as evaluation; a list of criticisms does not.

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- In a market economy, prices signal consumers' wants and profit gives firms an incentive to supply them [1].
- Competition pushes firms towards productive efficiency [1].
- Decisions are decentralised, so information is used without a central planner needing to gather it [1].
- But allocation follows ability to pay, so low-income households may go without essentials [1].
- Markets may under-provide some goods, such as public goods [1].
- A planned economy can direct resources to priorities, such as health care, and share output more equally [1].
- But planners lack price signals, so shortages and surpluses arise [1].
- Weak incentives reduce productive efficiency and innovation [1].
- Most real economies are mixed, so the issue is the degree of intervention in each market [1].
- Judgement: markets usually allocate more efficiently, but planning may be more effective for equity or for particular goods, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "all the alternatives" for opportunity cost.
- Calling a prediction normative because it might be wrong.
- Leaving out the condition that makes an incentive work.
- Confusing productive and allocative efficiency.
- Comparing pure systems with no reference to mixed economies.
- Ending a 10-mark answer without a judgement.
