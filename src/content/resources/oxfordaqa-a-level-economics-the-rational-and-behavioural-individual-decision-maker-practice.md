## Questions

**1.** A student's total utility from hours of video gaming in an evening is 0, 30, 52, 66, 72, 72 and 66 for 0 to 6 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal utility of each hour from 1 to 6. **[2]**
**(b)** If gaming were free, state how many hours a utility maximiser would play and explain why. **[2]**

**2.** A commuter would pay 5, 4, 3 and 2 currency units for her first, second, third and fourth pastry of the day.

**(a)** How many pastries does she buy at a price of 3.50, and at 2.50? **[2]**
**(b)** Use diminishing marginal utility to explain why her demand curve slopes downward. **[2]**

**3.** Mobile plan A charges 15 a month plus 0.05 a minute. Plan B charges 5 a month plus 0.09 a minute.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly cost of each plan for 180 minutes of calls. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of minutes at which the two plans cost the same. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how this shows that imperfect information can prevent a rational choice. **[2]**

**4.** An insurer sells health cover to 400 people, 200 low-risk with an expected claim of 50 a year and 200 high-risk with an expected claim of 350. It cannot tell them apart. Low-risk people value cover at no more than 80.

**(a)** Calculate the premium at which the insurer breaks even if all 400 buy. **[2]**
**(b)** Show what happens if the low-risk people leave at that premium. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is market failure. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between bounded rationality and bounded self-control, illustrating each with a brief case. **[4]**

**6.** A borrower takes 2,000 at 1.5% a month, compounding, to be repaid in one payment after 12 months. She believes the cost is 18% of the loan.

**(a)** Calculate the amount she repays. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how much she has underestimated the amount to repay. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the type of bias shown. **[1]**

**7.** Name the bias in each case and explain it.

**(i)** A shopper sees a watch priced at 900, then one priced at 450, and treats the second as a bargain without comparing other shops. **[2]**
**(ii)** A household has used the same broadband supplier for eight years and has never compared rivals. **[2]**
**(iii)** A student buys a reusable bottle because all her friends have one. **[2]**

**8.** A firm has 600 staff. With opt-in enrolment to a savings plan, 30% join. With opt-out enrolment, 75% stay in. Each member saves 50 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the extra members and the extra annual saving under opt-out. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the default matters, and why traditional theory would not predict it. **[3]**

**9.**
**(a)** Explain how altruism and concern for fairness challenge the assumption of self-interested behaviour. **[3]**
**(b)** A hardware shop sells 200 snow shovels a day at 15. After heavy snow it could sell 300 at 15 and still sells 300 at 24. Customers see this as unfair, and for 5 days 30% of its regular daily sales are lost. Calculate the extra revenue from the high price on the snow day and the revenue lost over the 5 days, and state what this implies. **[3]**

**10.** A council writes to 12,000 households with a message that most neighbours pay on time. On-time payment rises from 80% to 85%. Each letter costs 0.40.

**(a)** Calculate the number of extra households paying on time and the cost per extra payer. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether this nudge is better than a fine. **[6]**

**11.** Assess whether behavioural economics makes the traditional model of the rational decision maker redundant. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** MU = 30, 22, 14 [1] and 6, 0, -6 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** TU is highest (72) at 4 or 5 hours [1]. The fifth hour adds nothing (MU is zero) and the sixth lowers utility, so a utility maximiser stops at 4 or 5 hours [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the quantity to MU reaching zero, not just to the biggest number.

**2. (a)** At 3.50 she buys **2** pastries, since the third is worth only 3 [1]. At 2.50 she buys **3**, since the third (3) is at least the price and the fourth (2) is not [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Diminishing marginal utility: each extra pastry is worth less to her [1]. She takes more only if the price falls, so the quantity demanded rises as price falls, giving a downward-sloping demand curve [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect the falling value of each unit to the price needed to buy it.

**3. (a)** A: 15 + 0.05 × 180 = **24** [1]. B: 5 + 0.09 × 180 = **21.2** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Set 15 + 0.05m = 5 + 0.09m [1]. Then 10 = 0.04m, so m = **250 minutes** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A has the lower rate per minute, so a buyer who compares only that picks A, yet B is cheaper at 180 minutes [1]. Information that is incomplete or hard to use leads to a costlier choice, so the choice is not rational [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures in part (c). A general statement about information gains less than showing the wrong choice and its cost.

**4. (a)** Total expected claims 200 × 50 + 200 × 350 = 80,000 [1]. Divided by 400 gives **200** each [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Low-risk people value cover at no more than 80, so at 200 they leave [1]. Left: 200 × 200 = 40,000 premiums against 200 × 350 = 70,000 claims, a loss of **30,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The insurer cannot tell the groups apart because buyers know more about their risk (asymmetric information) [1]. Low-risk people would buy at a fair price of 50 and the insurer would sell, but this trade does not happen, so resources are misallocated [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State who knows more and who loses. Market failure is shown by trades that would benefit both sides not taking place.

**5.** Bounded rationality: people have limited information, time and ability to process it, so they cannot always make the best choice [1]. Example: choosing a phone plan by one headline price [1]. Bounded self-control: people know what is best for them but fail to act on it, giving too much weight to the present [1]. Example: planning to save but spending the money [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the two apart: ability to decide versus following through.

**6. (a)** 2,000 × 1.015¹² [1] = **2,391.24** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** She believes 18% × 2,000 = 360, so 2,360 [1]. She underestimates by 2,391.24 - 2,360 = **31.24** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A computational problem (compound interest treated as simple) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplier, then the belief it is compared with.

**7. (i)** Anchoring [1]: the first price (900) becomes the reference point that makes 450 look cheap [1]. **[2]**
**(ii)** Inertia [1]: she stays with the current supplier because changing takes effort, even if rivals cost less [1]. **[2]**
**(iii)** Social norm [1]: she copies what people around her do, following an unwritten rule [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A label alone is not enough. Say how the case's detail produces the behaviour.

**8. (a)** Opt-in: 0.30 × 600 = 180; opt-out: 0.75 × 600 = 450 [1]. Extra members 270 [1]. Extra saving 270 × 50 × 12 = **162,000** a year [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Inertia: most people stay with whatever the default is [1]. Choosing is costly, so the default carries the decision [1]. Traditional theory assumes rational people would pick the same outcome whichever way the options are presented, so take-up should not change [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Stress that the options are identical but behaviour differs.

**9. (a)** Altruism: some people give to others with no personal return, as when donating to charity [1]. Fairness: people may refuse a deal they see as unfair even when it benefits them [1]. A model of narrow self-interest cannot predict this, so behaviour must reflect others' wellbeing and fairness too [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Extra revenue on the snow day: 300 × 24 - 300 × 15 = 7,200 - 4,500 = **2,700** [1]. Lost sales: 30% of 200 = 60 a day, so 140 sold, revenue 2,100 against 3,000, a loss of 900 a day, **4,500** over 5 days [1]. The loss exceeds the gain by 1,800, so a price rise seen as unfair may reduce profit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the comparison and say what it means. Numbers without a conclusion on the firm's decision leave the point unfinished.

**10. (a)** 0.80 × 12,000 = 9,600; 0.85 × 12,000 = 10,200 [1]. Extra payers 600 [1]. Letters cost 0.40 × 12,000 = 4,800, so 4,800 ÷ 600 = **8** per extra payer [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** For: the cost per extra payer is low [1]. It keeps choice and avoids penalising anyone [1]. Against: the gain is 5 percentage points and the effect may fade [1]. A fine may be needed if the problem is large or the cost of non-payment high [1]. Weighing: judgement depends on the cost per extra payer against the cost of enforcing a fine [1]. A supported conclusion, such as trying the nudge first and adding a fine for persistent non-payers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when each point is explained and the answer ends in a judgement that follows from them.

**11.** Traditional theory gives clear predictions, such as diminishing marginal utility supporting a downward-sloping demand curve [1]. It still works well for repeated, high-stakes decisions where people learn and have good information [1]. Behavioural evidence shows systematic departures: bounded rationality, inertia, anchoring and rules of thumb [1]. It explains cases that the rational model cannot, such as take-up changing with the default [1]. Altruism and fairness can be added as sources of utility, so the model is extended and not discarded [1]. Behavioural findings sit on the rational model: biases are defined as departures from it [1]. The balance depends on context: for small, quick or complex choices behavioural insights matter more [1]. Judgement: the rational model is not redundant, but it is incomplete, so use it as a benchmark and behavioural economics as a correction [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided answer rarely reaches full credit. Use examples from both sides and state which carries more weight and why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing total utility when marginal utility is asked for.
- Calling diminishing marginal utility certain, not a hypothesis.
- Mixing up bounded rationality and bounded self-control.
- Naming a bias with no link to the case.
- Missing who knows more in an asymmetric information answer.
- Quoting figures without saying what they imply.
- Treating a nudge as a ban or a tax.
- Listing points in an evaluation with no judgement at the end.
