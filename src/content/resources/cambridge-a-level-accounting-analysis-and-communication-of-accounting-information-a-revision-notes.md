## Formulae to know exactly

The syllabus says the appendix formulae are the only ones accepted in answers and that ratios are not given in the question paper. Use year-end balances unless the question specifies average figures, and the number of decimal places the question asks for.

| Ratio | Formula | Shown as |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital cycle | Receivables days + inventory days − payables days | days |
| Receivables days | Trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 | days |
| Inventory days | Average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 | days |
| Payables days | Trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 | days |
| Net working assets to revenue | (Inventories + trade receivables − trade payables) ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Interest cover | Profit from operations ÷ interest payable | times |
| Gearing | Non-current liabilities ÷ (issued ordinary share capital + all reserves + non-current liabilities) × 100 | % |
| Earnings per share | Profit for the year ÷ number of issued ordinary shares | cents or dollars |
| Price/earnings | Market price per share ÷ earnings per share | no unit printed |
| Dividend per share | (Interim dividend paid + final dividend proposed) ÷ number of issued ordinary shares | cents or dollars |
| Dividend yield | Dividend per share ÷ market price per share | no multiplier or unit printed |
| Dividend cover | Profit for the year available to pay ordinary dividend ÷ annual ordinary dividend | times |

## Method in six steps

1. Write the formula from memory before touching the figures.
2. Pick the right inputs: credit sales for receivables, credit purchases for payables, cost of sales for inventory, profit from operations (before interest) for interest cover.
3. Keep share numbers and money in the same units (thousands with thousands).
4. Calculate, then round only at the end, to the decimal places asked.
5. Calculate the same ratio for the comparison year or business.
6. State the change, the likely cause, who is affected and your judgement.

## Worked reminder: Saltwick Tools plc

Invented figures in $000: revenue 5,000; credit sales 4,000; cost of sales 3,000; credit purchases 2,400; inventories 500; trade receivables 400; trade payables 300; profit from operations 600; interest payable 150; profit for the year 360; 1,500 thousand ordinary shares; non-current liabilities 1,250; share capital 1,500; reserves 1,900; interim dividend 45; final dividend proposed 75; market price 4.80 dollars.

| Ratio | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Receivables days | 400 ÷ 4,000 × 365 | 36.5 |
| Inventory days | 500 ÷ 3,000 × 365 | 60.8 |
| Payables days | 300 ÷ 2,400 × 365 | 45.6 |
| Working capital cycle | 36.5 + 60.8 − 45.6 | 51.7 days |
| Net working assets to revenue | (500 + 400 − 300) ÷ 5,000 × 100 | 12.0% |
| Interest cover | 600 ÷ 150 | 4.0 times |
| Gearing | 1,250 ÷ (1,500 + 1,900 + 1,250) × 100 | 26.9% |
| Earnings per share | 360 ÷ 1,500 | 24.0 cents |
| Price/earnings | 4.80 ÷ 0.24 | 20.0 |
| Dividend per share | (45 + 75) ÷ 1,500 | 8.0 cents |
| Dividend yield | 0.08 ÷ 4.80 | 0.0167 (1.67%) |
| Dividend cover | 360 ÷ 120 | 3.0 times |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Working capital cycle versus net working assets to revenue.** One is in days and measures time; the other is a percentage and measures the amount invested per dollar of sales.
- **Interest cover versus gearing.** Interest cover tests whether profit pays the interest now. Gearing tests how much of the long-term capital is borrowed. A business can have low gearing and weak cover if profit is small.
- **Earnings per share versus dividend per share.** Earnings belong to shareholders whether paid or kept. Dividend per share is what is declared, interim plus final.
- **Dividend cover versus dividend yield.** Cover looks at the company's side (how safely it can pay). Yield looks at the investor's side (return on today's price).
- **Price/earnings versus yield.** P/E compares price with profit; yield compares price with dividend. A high P/E with a low yield suggests investors expect growth.
- **Profit from operations versus profit for the year.** Interest cover uses the first; earnings per share and the dividend ratios use profit for the year.

## Reading the movement

| If this happens | The usual consequence |
|---|---|
| Customers pay more slowly | Cycle lengthens, more finance needed, possible overdraft cost |
| Inventory builds up | Cycle lengthens, more storage and obsolescence risk |
| Suppliers are paid sooner | Cycle lengthens; suppliers happier but cash lower |
| New loan notes issued | Gearing up, interest payable up, cover down |
| Profit falls but dividend is held | Dividend cover falls, so the dividend is less safe |
| Share price falls, dividend steady | Yield rises, P/E falls |

**How ratios interrelate:** a longer cycle leads to more borrowing, which raises interest and lowers interest cover and earnings per share. Higher gearing makes earnings per share more sensitive to changes in profit from operations. Dividend cover equals earnings per share divided by dividend per share when there are only ordinary shares.

## What high and low figures suggest

| Ratio | Higher figure | Lower figure |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital cycle | Cash tied up longer, more finance needed | Cash recovered faster, but check for late payment of suppliers |
| Net working assets to revenue | Heavy working investment for each dollar of sales | Lean working investment, possibly tight stock |
| Interest cover | Comfortable safety margin for lenders | Profit barely covers interest, risky |
| Gearing | More borrowing and more fixed commitments | More owner finance, safer but returns may be lower |
| Earnings per share | More profit for each share | Less profit for each share |
| Price/earnings | Market expects growth or low risk | Market doubts future earnings |
| Dividend yield | Larger return on today's price | Smaller cash return, growth may be expected |
| Dividend cover | Dividend is safe, more profit retained | Dividend may not be sustained |

The syllabus says an incorrect formula is not credited, although the figure is credited as your own when used in following calculations, so learn the formulae before practising judgement.

## Stakeholders and what each looks at

| Stakeholder | Ratios that matter most |
|---|---|
| Investor or shareholder | Earnings per share, P/E, dividend per share, yield, cover |
| Lender | Interest cover, gearing, working capital cycle |
| Supplier | Payables days, cycle, interest cover |
| Manager | Cycle, net working assets to revenue, gearing |

## Recommendation template

Name the user, state the recommendation, give one reason for and one against, quote at least two ratios, then reach a justified decision. The syllabus asks for a balance of reasons, so a one-sided answer is incomplete.

## Measures to improve

- **Shorten the cycle:** chase receivables, offer a prompt-payment discount, cut slow-moving inventory, negotiate longer credit from suppliers.
- **Improve interest cover:** lift profit from operations, repay or refinance expensive debt.
- **Lower gearing:** issue shares, retain profit, repay loan notes.
- **Support the share price and dividend:** raise earnings per share, keep a comfortable dividend cover.

## Quick self-test

1. Trade receivables 270 and credit sales 2,190. Find receivables days.
2. Inventory days 58, receivables days 42, payables days 61. Find the working capital cycle.
3. Inventories 410, trade receivables 250, trade payables 190, revenue 3,200. Find net working assets to revenue to 1 decimal place.
4. Profit from operations 840, interest payable 210. Find interest cover.
5. Non-current liabilities 600, issued ordinary share capital 1,000, reserves 1,400. Find gearing.
6. Profit for the year 450 and 1,800 thousand ordinary shares. Find earnings per share in cents.
7. Market price 3.50 dollars and earnings per share 25 cents. Find price/earnings.
8. Interim dividend 30, final dividend proposed 60, 1,800 thousand shares. Find dividend per share in cents.
9. Dividend per share 5 cents, market price 1.25 dollars. Find dividend yield as a decimal.
10. Profit available to pay ordinary dividend 450, annual ordinary dividend 90. Find dividend cover.
11. Earnings fall 10% and the dividend falls 25%. Does dividend cover rise or fall?

### Answers

1. 270 ÷ 2,190 × 365 = **45.0 days**.
2. 42 + 58 − 61 = **39 days**.
3. (410 + 250 − 190) = 470; 470 ÷ 3,200 × 100 = **14.7%**.
4. 840 ÷ 210 = **4.0 times**.
5. 600 ÷ (1,000 + 1,400 + 600) × 100 = **20.0%**.
6. 450 ÷ 1,800 = 0.25 dollars = **25.0 cents**.
7. 3.50 ÷ 0.25 = **14.0**.
8. 90 ÷ 1,800 = 0.05 dollars = **5.0 cents**.
9. 0.05 ÷ 1.25 = **0.04** (4%).
10. 450 ÷ 90 = **5.0 times**.
11. Cover rises, because the dividend fell by more than earnings. For example, from 5.0 to 405 ÷ 67.5 = **6.0**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using revenue instead of credit sales or credit purchases in the days ratios.
- Subtracting the wrong item in the working capital cycle.
- Using profit for the year, not profit from operations, for interest cover.
- Leaving non-current liabilities out of the gearing denominator.
- Using only the final dividend for dividend per share.
- Mixing thousands of shares with whole-dollar profit.
- Quoting ratios without comment, or commenting without naming a stakeholder.
- Recommending without a reason against, or ignoring what else the data shows.
