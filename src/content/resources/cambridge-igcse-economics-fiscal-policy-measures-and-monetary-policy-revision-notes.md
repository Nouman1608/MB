## Definitions to learn

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy (4.3.7) | The use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims |
| Budget deficit | Government spending is greater than government revenue |
| Budget surplus | Government revenue is greater than government spending |
| Money supply (4.4.1) | The total amount of money in an economy |
| Monetary policy (4.4.1) | The use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims |
| Interest rate | The price of borrowing and the reward for saving |
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another |
| Appreciation | The currency buys more units of another currency than before |
| Depreciation | The currency buys fewer units of another currency than before |

## 4.3.8 Budget balance

**Budget balance = revenue − spending.** A negative answer is a deficit; a positive answer is a surplus.

Method in steps:
1. Add up every revenue item.
2. Add up every spending item.
3. Subtract spending from revenue.
4. Name the result (deficit or surplus) and give the size with units.
5. If asked for a percentage of national output, divide the balance by national output and multiply by 100.

Worked reminder. Country A revenue 64, spending 70: balance = 64 − 70 = −6, a **deficit of 6**. Country G revenue 90, spending 84: balance = 90 − 84 = +6, a **surplus of 6**.

### Which measures move the balance?

| Measure | Direct effect on balance |
|---|---|
| Cut tax rates | Revenue falls, deficit rises or surplus shrinks |
| Raise tax rates | Revenue rises, deficit shrinks or surplus grows |
| Raise spending | Deficit rises or surplus shrinks |
| Cut spending | Deficit shrinks or surplus grows |

Tax yield = tax rate × taxable base. A corporation tax cut from 25% to 20% on profits of 80 lowers the yield from 20 to 16, a fall of 4, if profits stay at 80. If lower tax encourages more activity, the base can grow and the fall is smaller.

Deficits are financed by borrowing, which raises the national debt. New debt = old debt + deficit. A surplus can repay debt or fund future spending.

## 4.3.9 Fiscal policy and the aims

| Aim | Expansionary fiscal policy | Contractionary fiscal policy |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Spending and lower tax raise household and firm spending, so output rises | Slows growth |
| Employment | More orders, so firms hire | Fewer orders, fewer jobs |
| Prices | Pressure on prices if firms cannot meet extra orders | Eases price pressure |
| Balance of payments | Higher incomes raise imports; the balance may worsen | Imports fall; the balance may improve |
| Redistribution | Progressive taxes and transfers narrow the gap | Cuts to transfers can widen it |

### Chain template

Policy change → whose spending changes → firms' orders change → output and jobs change → prices or imports change → effect on the aim.

Example: a cut in income tax leaves households with more take-home pay → they spend more → shops sell more → shops hire → unemployment falls.

### Progressive tax reminder

Income 60, allowance 12, tax rate 25% on the rest. Tax = (60 − 12) × 0.25 = 12. Tax as a share of income = 12 ÷ 60 = 20%. A higher earner pays a bigger share, so the system is progressive.

### Evaluation points for fiscal policy

- Time lags between the decision and the effect.
- Borrowing means debt and later interest.
- Extra spending raises output only if there is spare capacity.
- Some extra income is spent on imports.
- Aims can conflict (jobs against prices; growth against the balance of payments).
- Tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular.

## 4.4.2 Monetary policy measures

### Interest rates

| Change | Borrowers | Savers | Spending and investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate rises | Pay more | Earn more | Fall |
| Rate falls | Pay less | Earn less | Rise |

Worked reminder. A loan of 50,000: at 4% interest is 2,000 a year; at 6% it is 3,000, which is 1,000 more.

### Money supply

| To raise it | To lower it |
|---|---|
| Central bank buys bonds, paying with new money | Central bank sells bonds |
| Lower the reserves banks must hold | Raise the reserves banks must hold |

More money makes credit easier and tends to lower interest rates. Less money does the opposite.

### Exchange rate

Higher interest rates tend to attract foreign savers, who need to buy the currency, so its value tends to rise. A central bank can also buy or sell its own currency.

Method for conversions:
- Price abroad = home price × units of foreign currency per unit of home currency.
- Home cost of an import = foreign price ÷ the same rate.

Worked reminder. 1 H-unit = 3 J-units. A good costing 30 H-units sells for 30 × 3 = 90 J-units. The H-unit appreciates to 1 H-unit = 4 J-units. The price becomes 30 × 4 = 120 J-units, a rise of 33.3%. Exports become dearer abroad; imports become cheaper at home.

Another reminder. A good priced 60 H-units, with 1 H-unit = 2 J-units, costs 120 J-units. If H depreciates to 1 H-unit = 1.5 J-units, the price falls to 90 J-units, a fall of 25%.

## 4.4.3 Monetary policy and the aims

| Aim | Cut rate / depreciate | Raise rate / appreciate |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Cheap credit and cheap exports raise spending and sales | Dear credit and dear exports reduce them |
| Employment | More output, more jobs | Fewer sales, fewer jobs |
| Prices | Risk of rising prices | Lower spending and cheaper imports ease prices |
| Balance of payments | Exports up, imports down | Exports down, imports up |
| Redistribution | Weak tool; borrowers gain, savers lose | Savers gain, borrowers lose |

Evaluation points: time lags; banks may not pass changes on; low confidence stops borrowing; a lower exchange rate raises import prices; the balance of payments only improves if demand is price elastic enough; aims can conflict.

## Must-know distinctions

- Fiscal policy uses tax and spending; monetary policy uses interest rates, money supply and the exchange rate.
- Deficit (annual shortfall) is not the same as national debt (the total owed).
- A rise in the interest rate is not a rise in the money supply.
- Appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper; depreciation does the reverse.
- Fiscal policy can directly redistribute income; monetary policy does so only weakly.

## Quick self-test

1. Define fiscal policy.
2. Revenue is 150 and spending is 138. State the balance and whether it is a deficit or surplus.
3. A tax rate falls from 25% to 20% on a base of 80. Calculate the change in revenue if the base stays the same.
4. A loan of 50,000 has an interest rate that rises from 4% to 6%. Calculate the extra interest per year.
5. 1 H-unit = 3 J-units. A good costs 30 H-units. Find its price in J-units, then after H appreciates to 1 H-unit = 4 J-units, and the percentage change.
6. Income is 60, the allowance is 12 and the tax rate is 25% on the rest. Find the tax and its percentage of income.
7. Define monetary policy.
8. Give two ways a central bank can raise the money supply.
9. Explain how a higher exchange rate may reduce inflation.
10. Give one limit of fiscal policy.

### Answers

1. The use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims.
2. 150 − 138 = **+12**, a surplus.
3. Old revenue 0.25 × 80 = 20; new 0.20 × 80 = 16. Revenue **falls by 4**.
4. 50,000 × 0.04 = 2,000; 50,000 × 0.06 = 3,000. Extra interest **1,000** a year.
5. 30 × 3 = **90** J-units; 30 × 4 = **120** J-units; (120 ÷ 90 − 1) × 100 = **33.3%** rise.
6. (60 − 12) × 0.25 = **12**; 12 ÷ 60 × 100 = **20%**.
7. The use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims.
8. Buy bonds from banks with newly created money; lower the reserves banks must hold.
9. Imported goods and materials cost less in home currency, so firms' costs and shop prices rise more slowly.
10. Any one of: time lags, debt from borrowing, imports taking extra income, little spare capacity, conflict between aims.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing the budget balance so a deficit is called a surplus.
- Forgetting units, or the word deficit or surplus after the figure.
- Giving the right measure but no chain to the aim.
- Mentioning aggregate demand when the question can be answered through spending and jobs.
- Treating a tax cut as always lowering revenue by the full amount.
- Confusing a rise in the exchange rate with a rise in the interest rate.
- Evaluating with a list of limits and no judgement about which matters most.
- Describing monetary policy as a way to redistribute income without any qualification.
