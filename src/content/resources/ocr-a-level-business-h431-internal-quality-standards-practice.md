## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between quality control and quality assurance. **[3]**

**2.** Dunmore Pies started 80,000 pies in a year and scrapped 2,800. Calculate the wastage rate. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a food manufacturer needs quality standards. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by total quality management (TQM). **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an example of quality assurance?

A: Testing a sample of finished pies at the end of the line.

B: Training every worker in food hygiene and recording each stage of production.

C: Throwing away burnt pies.

D: Refunding a customer who complains.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Dunmore Pies sets a target of cutting its wastage rate from 3.5% to 2%. Each scrapped pie costs £7.50 in ingredients, labour and energy.

**(a)** Calculate the number of pies that would be scrapped from 80,000 started at the 2% rate, and the saving compared with 2,800 scrapped. **[3]**

**(b)** Training all staff in quality methods would cost £24,000. Using the saving from part (a), calculate the payback period to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Dunmore Pies should invest in the training. **[8]**

**7.** Hartley Hotels has introduced TQM. Housekeeping staff now check each other's rooms, any employee may report a fault directly to the manager, and suppliers of linen are asked to meet agreed standards.

**(a)** Explain **two** features of TQM shown in the case. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of TQM to Hartley Hotels and its stakeholders. **[9]**

**8.** Brannock Engineering makes valves. Its wastage was 300 faulty valves from 6,000 started in March. After new procedures and training it was 180 from 6,000 in September. Analyse what these figures show about the effect of the changes and what other information the business would need. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Quality control checks products or services to find faults [1], usually after or during production, and is often done by inspectors [1]. Quality assurance is a system built into all stages of the process to prevent faults, and involves all staff [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Wastage rate = 2,800 ÷ 80,000 [1] × 100 = **3.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Customers expect food to be safe [1], and unsafe or poor-quality products can lead to illness, legal action, lost sales and damage to reputation [1]. Accept: standards reduce waste and rework, which cuts costs. **[2]**

**4.** TQM is an approach that makes quality the responsibility of every employee, not just inspectors [1], with the aim of meeting customer needs and improving continuously [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** At 2%: 80,000 × 2% = **1,600 pies** [1]. Reduction = 2,800 − 1,600 = 1,200 pies [1]. Saving = 1,200 × £7.50 = **£9,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Payback = £24,000 ÷ £9,000 [1] = **2.7 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** The payback of 2.7 years is acceptable if staff stay and the improvement lasts several years [1]. The £9,000 saving assumes that the target is met; if the rate falls only to 3%, the saving is 400 × £7.50 = £3,000 and the payback is 8 years [1].
Other financial benefits: fewer customer complaints and returns, and possibly more orders from retailers who require high standards [1]. Wastage includes only direct costs; poor quality also damages reputation [1].
Costs and limits: lost production while staff are trained, the need for refresher training and the risk that skilled staff leave [1]. Some of the improvement may come from better ingredients or machines, not training [1].
Non-financial: staff gain skills and responsibility, which may reduce turnover [1].
Judgement: Dunmore should invest because training tackles the causes of waste and brings several benefits, but it should set a target, measure the wastage rate every month and repeat training for new staff [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Everyone is responsible for quality: housekeeping staff check each other's rooms [1], so faults are found by those doing the work rather than by an inspector [1]. Empowerment or teamwork: any employee can report a fault directly to the manager [1], so problems are raised and solved quickly [1]. Supplier involvement: linen suppliers must meet agreed standards [1], so quality problems are prevented before they reach the hotel [1]. Marks for any two features, explained. **[4]**

**(b)** Guests: they receive more consistent rooms and service, and complaints are dealt with sooner [1], which builds loyalty and good reviews [1]. Owners: lower costs from fewer complaints, free room upgrades and rework [1], and a stronger reputation which allows higher room rates [1]. Employees: more responsibility and involvement can raise motivation [1], but constant checking and targets can create pressure and conflict between colleagues [1]. Suppliers: clear standards help a reliable supplier to keep the contract, but a supplier who cannot meet them is lost.
Evaluation: TQM takes time to change culture, and needs training and visible support from managers [1]. It works best in a business where service quality is central to the customer's choice, as in a hotel [1]. Judgement: TQM is likely to be very useful to Hartley because quality is its main selling point, provided managers act on the faults staff report and do not blame individuals [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Wastage rate in March = 300 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 5% [1]. In September = 180 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 3% [1]. The rate has fallen by 2 percentage points, or 120 fewer faulty valves out of 6,000 [1], which suggests that the changes have improved quality [1].
More information needed: whether the improvement is sustained over several months, because two months may be unrepresentative [1]; the cost of the changes and the cost of each faulty valve, to see if the saving is worth it [1]; whether anything else changed in the same period, such as materials or machines, and whether the types of fault have changed. Accept any one valid point; award a maximum of 2 marks for this part. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing quality control (finding faults) with quality assurance (preventing them).
- Dividing wastage by output rather than by units started.
- Comparing the number of faulty units when output differs, instead of rates.
- Writing about TQM as inspection.
- Recommending training without costs, time or evidence.
