## Questions

**1.** Explain the difference between a supply-side improvement and a supply-side policy, and illustrate each with an example of your own. **[4]**

**2.** In Country M the marginal rate of income tax falls from 28% to 22%. A worker earns an extra 600 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in the worker's net reward from the extra 600 cu. **[2]**
**(b)** Total hours worked are 1,500 million and output is 28 cu per hour. Hours respond with an elasticity of 0.5 to the net reward. Calculate the extra output. **[3]**

**3.** Country M has a labour force of 32 million. Its natural rate of unemployment falls from 7.5% to 6.25% after a retraining programme.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in the number of people unemployed at the natural rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one way in which retraining can reduce the natural rate. **[2]**

**4.** Classify each measure as free-market or interventionist, and explain how each could raise potential output: an investment allowance against corporation tax; publicly funded apprenticeships; the sale of a state-owned bus company to private owners. **[6]**

**5.** A firm pays 32 cu an hour and produces 40 units an hour.

**(a)** Calculate its unit labour cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Wages rise 4% and output rises to 44 units an hour. Calculate the new unit labour cost and its percentage change. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this matters for the rate of change of prices. **[2]**

**6.** Country M exports 250 and imports 270 (billion cu).

**(a)** Supply-side improvements raise exports by 6% and cut imports by 2%. Calculate the new balance of trade in goods and services. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why the current account balance might not improve as much as this. **[2]**

**7.** In a local market for freight handling, demand is Qd = 200 − 5P and supply is Qs = 40 + 3P. Deregulation lets new firms enter, and supply becomes Qs = 72 + 3P.

**(a)** Find the original equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** State the percentage changes in price and quantity, and name one group likely to lose. **[2]**

**8.** A firm can spend 150 million cu on a project returning 45 million cu a year for 5 years. Its discount rate is 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the net present value, using an annuity factor of 3.791. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how short-termism could lead to this project being rejected, and one policy response. **[3]**

**9.** A research project costs 12 million cu. The firm gains 9 million cu and spillovers to others are worth 6 million cu. The government offers a 40% subsidy on cost.

**(a)** Calculate the firm's net gain without the subsidy and the social net gain. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the firm's net gain with the subsidy. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost to the government. **[1]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the subsidy is good value. **[2]**

**10.** Explain, using an example, why supply-side changes often originate in the private sector and why this makes it harder to judge a policy. **[4]**

**11.** "Interventionist supply-side policies are more effective than free-market policies at raising the trend rate of growth." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

**12.** A training scheme costs 450 million cu and trains 20,000 workers. Each earns 3,500 cu more a year for 10 years.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per worker, the annual gain for the group, the payback period, and say whether the scheme is worthwhile if the present value of the gains at 4% is 567.8 million cu. **[4]**
**(b)** Policies raise trend growth from 2.0% to 2.3% for 15 years. Calculate how much higher potential output is than it would have been. **[3]**
**(c)** Assess whether cutting taxes is the best way to achieve this. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A supply-side improvement is any rise in productive capacity or efficiency, from any source [1]. A supply-side policy is a deliberate government action intended to bring such a rise about [1]. Example of improvement: a firm adopts faster machinery on its own initiative [1]. Example of policy: government funds training [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Define each term, then contrast them; examples alone rarely answer a "distinguish" command.

**2. (a)** Net reward before: 600 × 0.72 = 432 [1]. After: 600 × 0.78 = 468, a rise of 36/432 = **8.3%** [1].
**(b)** Hours rise 0.5 × 8.33% = 4.17% [1]. Extra hours: 1,500 × 0.0417 = 62.5 million [1]. Extra output: 62.5 × 28 = **1,750 million cu** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each step; a clear chain of arithmetic is easier to follow and to credit.

**3. (a)** Before: 32 × 0.075 = 2.4 million [1]. After: 32 × 0.0625 = 2.0 million, so a fall of **0.4 million** [1].
**(b)** Retraining gives workers skills that match vacancies [1], so structural unemployment falls and the equilibrium rate is lower [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that it is the natural rate, not just the actual rate, that falls and name the type of unemployment affected.

**4.** Investment allowance: free-market [1]; it raises the post-tax return on investment, so firms add capital and capacity [1]. Apprenticeships: interventionist [1]; they raise the skills and productivity of labour [1]. Sale of the bus company: free-market [1]; private owners with a profit motive have an incentive to cut costs, especially if competition increases [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each label with a mechanism. A classification alone gives a reader little to credit.

**5. (a)** 32/40 = **0.80 cu** per unit [1].
**(b)** New wage 33.28 cu; 33.28/44 = **0.756 cu** [1]. Change: 0.756/0.80 − 1 = **−5.5%** [1].
**(c)** Lower unit labour costs reduce firms' costs of production [1], so firms can hold or lower prices, reducing the rate of change of prices [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Productivity must rise faster than wages for unit costs to fall; state that comparison explicitly.

**6. (a)** Exports: 250 × 1.06 = 265 [1]. Imports: 270 × 0.98 = 264.6 [1]. Balance: 265 − 264.6 = **+0.4 billion cu** [1].
**(b)** The current account also includes income and transfers, which may move the other way [1]. Foreign demand, the exchange rate or retaliation could offset gains, and benefits take time [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep calculations to the figures given; save wider qualifications for the explanation.

**7. (a)** 200 − 5P = 40 + 3P gives 160 = 8P, so P = **20** [1] and Q = **100** [1].
**(b)** 200 − 5P = 72 + 3P gives 128 = 8P, so P = **16** [1] and Q = **120** [1].
**(c)** Price falls 20% and quantity rises 20% [1]. Existing firms protected by the old rules, or their workers, may lose [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe the diagram in words: supply shifts right, price falls, quantity rises. Naming both curves and the new equilibrium keeps the answer precise.

**8. (a)** 150/45 = **3.33 years** [1].
**(b)** Present value of returns: 45 × 3.791 = 170.6 [1]. NPV: 170.6 − 150 = **+20.6 million cu** [1].
**(c)** A firm demanding payback within, say, three years would reject the project even though NPV is positive [1], because managers or shareholders put too little weight on later returns [1]. A tax allowance or subsidy for investment could raise the private return [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link short-termism explicitly to under-investment compared with the socially desirable level, then offer a policy that targets that gap.

**9. (a)** Firm: 9 − 12 = **−3 million cu** [1]. Social: 9 + 6 − 12 = **+3 million cu** [1].
**(b)** Firm pays 12 × 0.6 = 7.2 [1]. Net gain: 9 − 7.2 = **+1.8 million cu** [1].
**(c)** 12 × 0.4 = **4.8 million cu** [1].
**(d)** Social gain of 3 million cu for a cost of 4.8 million cu looks poor value if only this project counts [1], but the spillover estimate is uncertain and the subsidy only gives value if the project would not happen without it [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A numerical judgement is strongest when you compare two figures and then state what the answer depends on.

**10.** Firms invest in machinery and train staff to cut costs or win customers [1]. For example, a bakery chain buys faster ovens and raises output per worker [1]. Such gains happen without government action [1], so it is hard to separate the effect of a policy from what the private sector would have done anyway [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a concrete example and state the consequence for judging policy.

**11.** Interventionist policies such as training and R&D subsidies address market failures like under-provision of skills and spillovers [1]. They raise human capital and innovation, which raise productivity over the long run [1]. They can reach areas the market ignores [1]. However, they are costly and slow, with long time lags [1]. Government may pick the wrong industries or waste money [1]. Free-market policies such as tax cuts, deregulation and privatisation improve incentives and competition [1] and may act faster and at lower direct cost [1]. They may do little where the problem is a skills gap or short-termism [1]. Judgement: the answer depends on the cause of weak growth in the economy concerned; a mix is likely to be best [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that picks a side and names the condition on which it depends is more convincing than a balanced list with no verdict.

**12. (a)** Cost per worker: 450m/20,000 = **22,500 cu** [1]. Annual gain: 20,000 × 3,500 = **70 million cu** [1]. Payback: 450/70 = **6.4 years** [1]. The present value of 567.8 million exceeds the 450 million cost, so it is worthwhile [1].
**(b)** 1.02^15 = 1.3459 [1]. 1.023^15 = 1.4065 [1]. Ratio: **4.5% higher** [1].
**(c)** A tax cut can raise incentives to work, save and invest [1], but the response is uncertain, with income effects possibly offsetting [1], and revenue falls [1]. Training or investment in capital may raise productivity more directly, but with a lag [1]. The best policy depends on the cause of low trend growth, so a combination is likely [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) asks you to assess: weigh alternatives against the aim of trend growth rather than describing tax cuts alone.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing a supply-side improvement with a government policy.
- Writing that demand-side policy reduces the natural rate.
- Stating a calculation answer without showing the working or units.
- Giving evaluation points with no application to the policy named in the question.
- Forgetting that the current account includes income and transfers.
- Ignoring time lags in training and R&D policies.
- Ending with no judgement.
