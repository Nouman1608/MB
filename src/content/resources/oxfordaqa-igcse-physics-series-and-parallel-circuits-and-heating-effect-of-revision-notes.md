## Key facts at a glance

| Specification | Fact |
|---|---|
| 3.5.1 p | The combined voltage of cells in series is the sum of their voltages |
| 3.5.1 q | Components join in series or in parallel; some circuits have both |
| 3.5.1 r | Series: resistances add; same current; supply pd shared |
| 3.5.1 s | Parallel: combined resistance less than any branch; current splits; same pd |
| 3.5.1 t | Charge flowing through a resistor heats it through collisions with stationary atoms; filament bulbs waste a lot of energy as heat, CFLs less |

Equation used: V = IR (pd in V, current in A, resistance in Ω).

## Series and parallel side by side

| | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Paths for charge | One | Two or more |
| Combined resistance | Sum of the resistances | Less than the smallest branch |
| Current | Same everywhere | Splits at the junction; branch currents add to the supply current |
| Potential difference | Shared; pds add to the supply pd | Same across every branch |
| One component fails (open) | Everything stops | Other branches carry on |
| Add another component | Resistance up, current down | Resistance down, supply current up |
| Larger resistance takes | The larger pd | The smaller current |

## Cells in series

- Sum the voltages: four 2.0 V cells give 8.0 V.
- Number of cells needed = required voltage / voltage of one cell.
- The cells must point the same way round the loop.

## Method boxes

**Series circuit, given supply pd and resistances**

1. R_total = R1 + R2 + ...
2. I = V / R_total.
3. Each pd = I x its own resistance.
4. Check the pds add to the supply pd.

**Parallel circuit, given supply pd and branch resistances**

1. Every branch has the supply pd.
2. Branch current = V / branch resistance.
3. Supply current = sum of branch currents.
4. Combined resistance = supply pd / supply current.

**Mixed circuit**

1. Treat the parallel block as one item in series with the rest.
2. pd across the series resistor = supply current x its resistance.
3. pd across the block = supply pd minus that.
4. Divide that pd by each branch resistance for the branch currents.
5. Check the branch currents add to the supply current.

## Small worked reminders

**Series.** A 9.0 V supply drives an 8.0 Ω and a 22 Ω resistor in series. R_total = 30 Ω. I = 9.0 / 30 = 0.30 A. pds: 0.30 x 8.0 = 2.4 V and 0.30 x 22 = 6.6 V. Total 9.0 V.

**Parallel.** A 24 V supply is across 40 Ω and 60 Ω in parallel. Currents: 24 / 40 = 0.60 A and 24 / 60 = 0.40 A. Supply current 1.0 A. Combined resistance 24 / 1.0 = 24 Ω, which is below 40 Ω.

**Equal branches.** n equal resistors in parallel: each takes the same current, so the combination has the resistance of one divided by n. Two 80 Ω resistors in parallel give 40 Ω.

## Heating effect of current

- Moving charges (electrons) collide with stationary atoms in the wire.
- Energy is transferred from the charges to the atoms, which vibrate more.
- The wire gets hotter and transfers thermal energy to the surroundings.
- A heater element has high resistance; the connecting leads have low resistance and stay cool.
- Same current through two resistors in series: the one with the bigger pd transfers more energy.

## Filament bulbs and CFLs

- A filament lamp wastes a lot of energy by heating; only a small fraction becomes light.
- A CFL (power saving lamp) wastes less energy for the same light.
- Efficiency = useful energy transferred / total energy supplied. Give it as a decimal or a percentage.
- Wasted energy = total energy supplied minus useful energy.
- When buying a new appliance you can choose a more energy efficient one.

Example: a lamp is supplied with 50 J and transfers 4.0 J as light. Efficiency = 4.0 / 50 = 0.08 = 8.0%. Wasted energy = 46 J.

## Reasoning in words

Examiners often ask for an explanation rather than a number. Use these patterns.

**Why do lamps in a house work independently?** They are in parallel, so each has the full supply pd across it and its own path for current. Switching one off or removing it leaves the others unchanged.

**Why does the combined resistance fall when a branch is added?** Charge has another route, so for the same supply pd more current flows from the supply. Since R = V/I, a bigger current for the same V means a smaller resistance.

**Why do lamps in a series string get dimmer as you add more?** The total resistance rises, so the current falls, and the same supply pd is shared between more lamps, so each gets a smaller pd.

**What happens if a lamp in a series string blows?** The loop is broken, so there is no current anywhere and all the lamps go out.

**Why use a high-resistance wire in a heater?** For the same current, a larger share of the supply energy is transferred as heat in the element, not in the leads.

## Must-know distinctions

- Current is the same in series; pd is the same in parallel.
- Resistances add in series; they do not add in parallel.
- Parallel branches share the current, not the pd.
- Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel.
- Heating comes from collisions, not from the charge being "used up".
- "Wasted" energy is transferred to the surroundings, not destroyed.

## Quick self-test

1. Five 1.5 V cells are joined in series. What is the combined voltage?
2. A 12 V supply is connected to a 6.0 Ω and an 18 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the current and each resistor pd.
3. A 12 V supply is connected across a 30 Ω and a 60 Ω resistor in parallel. Find each branch current, the supply current and the combined resistance.
4. What is the combined resistance of two 80 Ω resistors in parallel?
5. A 9.0 V supply is connected to a 10 Ω resistor in series with a parallel block. An ammeter in the supply lead reads 0.30 A. Find the pd across the block, the resistance of the block and the total resistance.
6. Explain why a resistor gets hot when there is a current in it.
7. A lamp is supplied with 50 J and gives 4.0 J of light. A replacement gives the same light from 16 J. Find both efficiencies as percentages.
8. Three identical lamps are in parallel. One filament breaks. What happens to the other two?
9. A student adds a third resistor in parallel across a supply. What happens to the supply current and the combined resistance?
10. A third resistor is added in series instead. What happens to the current?

### Answers

1. 5 x 1.5 = **7.5 V**.
2. R_total = 24 Ω; I = 12 / 24 = **0.50 A**; pds **3.0 V** (0.50 x 6.0) and **9.0 V** (0.50 x 18).
3. Each branch has 12 V: **0.40 A** (12 / 30) and **0.20 A** (12 / 60); supply current **0.60 A**; combined resistance 12 / 0.60 = **20 Ω**.
4. **40 Ω**.
5. pd across the 10 Ω resistor = 0.30 x 10 = 3.0 V, so the block has 9.0 - 3.0 = **6.0 V**; block resistance = 6.0 / 0.30 = **20 Ω**; total resistance = 10 + 20 = **30 Ω** (check: 9.0 / 0.30 = 30 Ω).
6. Moving electrons collide with stationary atoms (ions) in the resistor; energy is transferred to the atoms, which vibrate more, so the resistor's temperature rises.
7. First lamp: 4.0 / 50 = 0.08, **8.0%**. Replacement: 4.0 / 16 = 0.25, **25%**.
8. They stay lit and are unchanged, because each still has the full supply pd across it.
9. The supply current **increases** and the combined resistance **decreases**.
10. The current **decreases**, because the total resistance rises.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding the branch resistances in a parallel circuit.
- Giving a parallel combined resistance larger than the smallest branch, and not noticing.
- Forgetting that the supply current flows through the series part of a mixed circuit.
- Splitting the supply pd between parallel branches.
- Writing "electrons are used up" instead of describing energy transfer.
- Leaving out the word "stationary" or "atoms" in the explanation of heating.
- Dividing useful energy by wasted energy instead of by total energy supplied.
- Missing units, or writing a percentage efficiency as a decimal when a percentage is requested.
