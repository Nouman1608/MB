This guide covers **4.7.2 Reactions of alkenes and alcohols (chemistry only)** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.7, Organic chemistry, and is assessed on
Paper 2 in the separate chemistry qualification only. Almost all of it is for both tiers. One statement is
**Higher tier only**: explaining why carboxylic acids are weak acids, in terms of ionisation and pH.

Use the [reactions of alkenes and alcohols revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactions-of-alkenes-and-alcohols-revision-notes/)
for quick recall and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactions-of-alkenes-and-alcohols-practice/)
to test yourself. The [organic chemistry study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-organic-chemistry/)
covers the whole topic; this page goes deeper on 4.7.2.

## 4.7.2.1 Structure and formulae of alkenes

**Alkenes** are hydrocarbons with a **double carbon-carbon bond**, C=C. They form a homologous series with
the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ**. They are **unsaturated**: they have **two fewer hydrogen atoms** than the alkane with
the same number of carbons.

| Alkene | Formula | Carbon atoms | Matching alkane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | 2 | ethane, C₂H₆ |
| Propene | C₃H₆ | 3 | propane, C₃H₈ |
| Butene | C₄H₈ | 4 | butane, C₄H₁₀ |
| Pentene | C₅H₁₀ | 5 | pentane, C₅H₁₂ |

You must be able to **recognise an alkene** from its name (it ends in **-ene**) or its formula (hydrogen count is
**twice** the carbon count). Ethene drawn in full:

```
H     H
 \   /
  C=C
 /   \
H     H
```

## 4.7.2.2 Reactions of alkenes

The **C=C functional group** decides how alkenes react.

### Combustion

Alkenes burn like other hydrocarbons, but they tend to burn with **smoky flames** in air because of
**incomplete combustion** (soot, which is carbon, forms because the proportion of carbon in the molecule is higher).

### Addition reactions

In an **addition reaction**, atoms add **across the C=C bond**. The double bond becomes a **single** bond and
**only one product** forms.

| Reagent | Conditions | Product from ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | **Nickel catalyst**, about 60 °C | Ethane, C₂H₆ |
| Water (as steam) | **Phosphoric acid catalyst**, high temperature and pressure | Ethanol, C₂H₅OH |
| Halogens (chlorine, bromine, iodine) | Room temperature | Dihaloalkane, for example 1,2-dibromoethane, C₂H₄Br₂ |

The product of hydrogen addition is an **alkane**. The product of water addition is an **alcohol**. The
product of halogen addition is a compound with one halogen atom on each carbon.

### Worked example 1: bromine and propene

*Draw the product of propene with bromine and write the equation.*

1. Propene is CH₃–CH=CH₂.
2. Bromine adds across the double bond, one Br to each carbon.
3. Product: CH₃–CHBr–CH₂Br.

C₃H₆ + Br₂ → C₃H₆Br₂

```
    H   H   H            H   H   H
    |   |   |            |   |   |
H - C - C = C - H  +  Br2 -> H - C - C - C - H
    |                       |   |   |
    H                       H   Br  Br
```

### Worked example 2: butene and hydrogen

*Butene is hydrogenated. Name the product and write the equation.*

C₄H₈ + H₂ → C₄H₁₀. The product is **butane**. Check: 4 carbons, 8 + 2 = 10 hydrogens.

### The bromine water test

Alkenes decolourise bromine water, because the bromine adds across the C=C bond. **Orange → colourless**.
Alkanes do not react.

## 4.7.2.3 Alcohols

**Alcohols** contain the functional group **–OH**. The first four members are:

| Alcohol | Formula | Carbons |
|---|---|---|
| Methanol | CH₃OH | 1 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH (CH₃CH₂OH) | 2 |
| Propanol | C₃H₇OH | 3 |
| Butanol | C₄H₉OH | 4 |

Recognise alcohols from their names (**-ol**) or formulae (a carbon chain ending in -OH).

### Reactions you must be able to describe

| Reaction | What happens |
|---|---|
| With **sodium** | Sodium fizzes, giving off **hydrogen** and forming a sodium alkoxide (the reaction is slower than with water) |
| **Burning in air** | Burn with a clean flame, producing **carbon dioxide and water** |
| **Added to water** | Methanol, ethanol, propanol and butanol dissolve in water to give a **neutral** solution (pH 7) |
| With an **oxidising agent** | Oxidised to a **carboxylic acid**. With acidified potassium dichromate(VI), the colour changes from **orange to green** |

The specification only expects balanced equations for the **combustion** of alcohols:

- Ethanol: **C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O**
- Methanol: **2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O**
- Propanol: **2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O**

For ethanol, count oxygen carefully: the molecule already has one oxygen atom, so the 3O₂ gives 6 + 1 = 7 on the
left, and 4 + 3 = 7 on the right.

For oxidation, ethanol becomes ethanoic acid (the word equation is enough): ethanol + oxygen (from the oxidising
agent) → ethanoic acid + water.

### Uses

- **Methanol:** a solvent, a fuel, and a starting material for other chemicals.
- **Ethanol:** a solvent (in perfumes and cleaning products), a fuel (blended with petrol), and the alcohol in
  alcoholic drinks.
- **Propanol and butanol:** solvents.

### Fermentation

**Aqueous ethanol** is made when **sugar solutions are fermented using yeast**. The conditions are:

- **warm**, about 30 to 40 °C (too cold and the yeast works slowly, too hot and its enzymes are damaged)
- **no oxygen** (anaerobic), otherwise ethanoic acid forms instead
- **yeast**, which provides the enzymes.

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

## 4.7.2.4 Carboxylic acids

**Carboxylic acids** contain the functional group **–COOH**. The first four members are:

| Acid | Formula |
|---|---|
| Methanoic acid | HCOOH |
| Ethanoic acid | CH₃COOH |
| Propanoic acid | C₂H₅COOH |
| Butanoic acid | C₃H₇COOH |

Recognise them by the **-oic acid** ending or the –COOH group. You are not expected to write balanced equations
for their reactions, so word equations are enough.

| Reaction | What happens |
|---|---|
| With **carbonates** | Form a salt, water and **carbon dioxide** (fizzing; limewater turns milky) |
| **Dissolved in water** | Form **acidic** solutions (pH below 7, about 3 to 4 for typical solutions) |
| With **alcohols** (acid catalyst) | Form an **ester** and water |

**Esters** have a sweet, fruity smell and are used in perfumes and flavourings. The only ester you must name is
**ethyl ethanoate**:

ethanoic acid + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + water

### Higher tier only: why carboxylic acids are weak acids

Carboxylic acids are **weak** acids because they are only **partially ionised** in aqueous solution: only a
small proportion of molecules release H⁺ ions. Compared with a strong acid such as hydrochloric acid of the same
concentration, the solution has a **lower hydrogen ion concentration** and therefore a **higher pH**. For
example, 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid has a pH of about 3, while 0.1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid has a pH of about 1.

## Exam technique

- When describing an addition reaction, say the **double bond becomes a single bond** and name the **catalyst**
  and **conditions**.
- To identify the group in a formula, look for **C=C** (alkene), **–OH** (alcohol) or **–COOH** (carboxylic acid).
- For fermentation, give the conditions (**yeast, about 30 to 40 °C, no oxygen**) and the equation.
- When asked for observations, give the colour change for each oxidation or bromine test.
- For the carboxylic acid test with carbonate, write "fizzing" and name carbon dioxide.

## Common mistakes

- Writing C₂H₆ as an alkene. Alkenes have 2n hydrogens.
- Saying addition produces two products. Only one product forms.
- Saying alcohols are acidic. Their aqueous solutions are neutral.
- Confusing the colours: bromine water goes orange to colourless, but acidified dichromate goes orange to green.
- Omitting the "no oxygen" condition in fermentation.
- Saying "weak" means "dilute" when comparing acids.

## Putting it together: a model extended answer

*Ethanol can be made from ethene or by fermentation. Compare the two methods.*

> From ethene: ethene reacts with steam in an addition reaction using a phosphoric acid catalyst at high
> temperature and pressure. It is a continuous process that gives pure ethanol quickly, but it uses crude oil,
> which is finite, and a lot of energy. By fermentation: sugar solution with yeast at about 30 to 40 °C with no
> oxygen gives aqueous ethanol and carbon dioxide. It is a batch process that uses renewable sugar crops and lower
> temperatures, but it is slow, gives a dilute solution that needs distillation and uses farmland.
