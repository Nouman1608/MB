This guide covers a writing skill the Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 syllabus lists in its subject content: **"demonstrate accuracy in spelling, punctuation and grammar"**. It is assessed through writing objective **W5**, "make accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar", which applies to both sections of Paper 2: directed writing and the composition. The [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/) explains where W5 sits among the five writing objectives, and the [Reading and Writing overview](/resources/o-level-cambridge-english-language-reading-and-writing/) names the errors worth drilling in a short paragraph. This page teaches the rules behind those errors, with examples, and gives you a proofreading routine for the last minutes of the paper.

## Why accuracy matters

Accuracy is not judged in isolation from the rest of your writing. Frequent errors interrupt the reader and blur meaning, which affects how well your ideas come across (W1, W2) as well as W5 itself. A small number of slips in ambitious writing is far less damaging than errors that make sentences hard to follow. The aim is not to write timidly, but to control the most common errors so that your range of vocabulary and sentence structure (W3) can be credited.

## Sentence boundaries

Most serious accuracy problems are errors at the edges of sentences.

**Comma splice**: two complete sentences joined by a comma.

- Wrong: *The rain stopped, we went outside.*
- Right: *The rain stopped. We went outside.* / *The rain stopped, so we went outside.* / *The rain stopped; we went outside.* / *When the rain stopped, we went outside.*

**Run-on sentence**: two sentences with no punctuation at all between them.

- Wrong: *The hall was full nobody could find a seat.*
- Right: *The hall was full, and nobody could find a seat.*

**Accidental fragment**: a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a main clause.

- Wrong: *Because the bus was late. We missed the start.*
- Right: *Because the bus was late, we missed the start.*

A **deliberate fragment** can work in a composition for effect ("Silence. Then footsteps."), but only when the rest of your writing shows you control full sentences. In directed writing, avoid them.

**Test for a sentence:** does it have a subject and a main verb, and could it stand alone? If two parts of your sentence could each stand alone, you need a full stop, a semicolon or a joining word between them, not a comma.

## Commas that help

Use a comma:

- between items in a list: *bread, rice, lentils and oil*;
- after an opening subordinate clause or phrase: *Although it was late, the shop was still open.*;
- around extra information that could be removed: *My cousin, who lives in Karachi, is visiting.*;
- before a joining word linking two sentences: *It was cold, but we stayed.*;
- before or after direct speech: *"Wait," she said.*

Do not put a comma between a subject and its verb: *~~The students who arrived late, missed the talk.~~* → *The students who arrived late missed the talk.*

## Apostrophes

Apostrophes do two jobs only.

| Job | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Contraction | the apostrophe replaces missing letters | don't, it's (it is), they're (they are) |
| Possession, singular owner | add 's | the teacher's desk, James's bag |
| Possession, plural owner ending in s | add an apostrophe after the s | the teachers' room, the students' votes |
| Possession, plural owner not ending in s | add 's | the children's playground, the women's team |

**Never** use an apostrophe for an ordinary plural: *~~banana's for sale~~*, *~~the 1990's~~* → bananas, the 1990s.

**Its / it's:** *it's* always means *it is* or *it has*; *its* is possessive, like *his*. *The school changed its rules. It's been a long day.*

In formal directed writing, such as a report or a letter to an official, use few contractions. In a speech or a friendly email, contractions are natural.

## Colons and semicolons

- A **semicolon** joins two closely related complete sentences: *The first bus was full; the second never came.* It can also separate list items that already contain commas.
- A **colon** introduces something that explains or lists what came before: *There was only one solution: we had to walk.* The part before a colon should be a complete sentence.

Use each a few times, correctly, rather than many times loosely.

## Grammar checks that catch most errors

**Subject-verb agreement.**

- *The list of complaints **is** long.* (The subject is *list*, not *complaints*.)
- *Each of the students **has** a locker.*
- *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** told.* (The verb agrees with the nearer subject.)

**Consistent tense.** Narratives are usually in the past tense; do not drift into the present without a reason. *She opened the door and ~~sees~~ saw the letter.*

**Clear pronoun reference.** *When Ali met his father, he was angry.* Who was angry? Rewrite: *Ali was angry when he met his father.*

**Common confusions in grammar.**

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| could of, should of | could have, should have | "of" is a mishearing of "'ve" |
| less people | fewer people | fewer for things you can count |
| me and my friend went | my friend and I went | "I" for the subject |
| between you and I | between you and me | "me" after a preposition |
| Walking home, the rain started. | Walking home, I was caught in the rain. | the opening phrase must describe the subject that follows |

## Spelling

**Commonly confused words** cause more lost accuracy than long, difficult words.

| Pair or group | Quick guide |
|---|---|
| there / their / they're | place / belonging to them / they are |
| your / you're | belonging to you / you are |
| affect / effect | usually: affect is the verb, effect the noun |
| practice / practise | British English: practice is the noun, practise the verb |
| advice / advise | noun / verb |
| lose / loose | misplace / not tight |
| accept / except | receive / apart from |
| quiet / quite | silent / fairly |
| whether / weather | if / climate |
| principal / principle | head, main / a rule or belief |

**Patterns worth knowing**: doubling the final consonant after a short vowel (*begin → beginning*, *stop → stopped*); dropping the final *e* before a vowel suffix (*hope → hoping*), but not before a consonant (*hopeful*); *-ful* has one *l*. Choose British or American spelling and stay consistent throughout one answer.

## A proofreading routine

Leave about five minutes at the end of each section of Paper 2. Then check in passes, one error type at a time, because the eye misses errors when it reads for meaning:

1. **Sentence boundaries**: find every comma between two possible sentences.
2. **Apostrophes and its/it's**.
3. **Agreement and tense**: underline each main verb in one paragraph and check it.
4. **Your personal list**: the three errors your teacher marks most often in your work.
5. **Spelling of key words**, especially names and words copied from the question.

Correct neatly: a single line through the error and the correction above it.

## Worked example

Original paragraph with errors:

> Last week our school's football team travelled to the city final, it was the first time in ten year's
> that we had got that far. Each of the players were nervous. Because the other team had won every game.
> We should of lost, but we didnt. The effect on the whole school was amazing, there was a party the
> next day and the principle gave a speech.

Corrected:

> Last week our school's football team travelled to the city final. It was the first time in ten years
> that we had got that far. Each of the players was nervous, because the other team had won every game.
> We should have lost, but we didn't. The effect on the whole school was amazing: there was a party the
> next day, and the principal gave a speech.

**What changed:** two comma splices fixed (a full stop; a colon); a wrong apostrophe removed (*years*); agreement corrected (*each... was*); an accidental fragment joined to its main clause; *should of* corrected; a missing apostrophe added (*didn't*); the confusable *principle/principal* fixed. *School's* and *effect* were already correct.

## Common mistakes

- Using commas wherever you would pause, including between sentences.
- Adding apostrophes to every word ending in *s*.
- Leaving no time to proofread.
- Proofreading by reading for meaning once, which hides errors.
- Using semicolons and colons to look sophisticated without knowing the rules.
- Copying the question's names and key words with spelling errors.
