Content tested: "refugees, living conditions and forced labour; Allied control and the division of Germany; Denazification and the Nuremberg Trials; the expulsions of ethnic Germans; penalties imposed on the Soviet zone and sector, including reparations"; "the Marshall Plan... life for Berliners during the Berlin Blockade; the creation of the Federal Republic and GDR."

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three problems faced by people living in German cities in 1945. **[3]**

**2.** Give three features of the Nuremberg Trials of 1945-46. **[3]**

**3.** Give three ways in which the Soviet zone was treated differently from the western zones in the years 1945-49. **[3]**

**4.** Give three features of life for West Berliners during the Berlin Blockade. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which the expulsions of ethnic Germans affected Germany after 1945. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which denazification affected the lives of Germans. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the Marshall Plan and the new currency changed life in the western zones. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which Allied occupation affected the lives of Germans in the years 1945-46. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why Germany had been divided into two states by 1949. **[9]**

**10.** "Shortages of food and housing were the main problem facing Germans in the years 1945-47." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "The Berlin Blockade was the main reason for the creation of two German states in 1949." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**12.** "Denazification failed to change Germany." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation of how it affected the development [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: destroyed or damaged homes; lack of water, gas and electricity; very low food rations; a black market and worthless currency; refugees and expellees arriving; rubble to clear; few men, as many were dead or prisoners of war.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: held by the International Military Tribunal from November 1945 to October 1946; leading Nazis such as Göring were tried; charges of crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity; twelve were sentenced to death and three acquitted; Göring killed himself before execution; evidence of the Holocaust was made public; "following orders" was rejected as a defence.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: the USSR took heavy reparations, dismantling factories and railway lines; it did not receive Marshall Aid; land reform broke up big estates in 1945; the Communist and Social Democratic parties were forced to merge into the SED; denazification was used to remove old elites; special camps held suspected opponents; it became a one-party state, the GDR.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: food, coal and other supplies arrived by air; strict rationing; power cuts and little fuel in the winter of 1948-49; Berliners helped build the new airfield at Tegel; children watched for pilots who dropped sweets; the Blockade lasted from June 1948 to May 1949.

**5.** Way identified [1]: it created a severe refugee crisis in the occupied zones. Precise knowledge [2]: the Potsdam Conference approved the transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary; estimates of those who fled or were expelled commonly run to twelve million or more. Explanation [2]: these people arrived with almost nothing in a country where housing and food were already scarce, so they had to be crammed into damaged towns and villages, increasing hardship and tension with local people. Further development [1]: over time expellees became part of the workforce of the Federal Republic, though many remained bitter about their lost homes.

**6.** Way identified [1]: it affected people's jobs and status. Precise knowledge [2]: in the American zone adults filled in questionnaires about their past, and tribunals placed people in categories from major offenders to followers and the exonerated. Explanation [2]: teachers, judges and officials judged to be committed Nazis could lose their posts, so many Germans faced investigation and uncertainty about their careers. Further development [1]: as the Cold War grew, the western Allies needed experienced administrators and many were reinstated, so for many the impact was temporary.

**7.** Way identified [1]: goods returned to the shops and the black market declined. Precise knowledge [2]: Marshall Aid was announced in June 1947 and the western zones were included; in June 1948 the Deutsche Mark replaced the Reichsmark and Ludwig Erhard lifted many price controls. Explanation [2]: because people now trusted the currency, shopkeepers and farmers sold goods they had hoarded, so Germans could buy food and goods legally instead of bartering. Further development [1]: this began the recovery that became West Germany's later "economic miracle".

**8.** Way one [4]: occupation brought foreign control [1]; Germany was divided into four zones, each governed by an occupying power, with Berlin divided into four sectors [1]; so Germans lost any government of their own and had to obey the rules and priorities of the power in their zone [2]. Way two [4]: occupation brought denazification and trials [1]; denazification questionnaires and the Nuremberg Trials (November 1945-October 1946) investigated individuals and exposed Nazi crimes [1]; so many Germans had to account for their past, and the public was confronted with evidence of the Holocaust [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the Soviet zone's dismantling of factories or the land reform of 1945.

**9.** Reason one [4]: the growing Cold War [1]; the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Aid (1947) divided Europe, and the USSR refused Marshall Aid for its zone [1]; so the western and Soviet zones developed different economies and the four powers could no longer agree on Germany's future [2]. Reason two [4]: the currency reform and the Blockade [1]; the Deutsche Mark of June 1948 led Stalin to blockade West Berlin until May 1949 [1]; so the western powers decided to create a West German state, and the USSR answered with the GDR in October 1949 [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the Basic Law of May 1949 or the SED's control of the Soviet zone.

**10.** Given factor [3]: rations were very low; many lived in ruins or cellars; the black market and bartering; the hunger and cold of the winter of 1946-47. Other factors [4]: millions of refugees and expellees; the presence of displaced persons and the absence of men held as prisoners of war; foreign occupation and denazification; heavy reparations and dismantling in the Soviet zone; violence by Soviet soldiers in 1945. Comparison [3]: shortages affected almost everyone, while other problems fell on particular groups, but the arrival of expellees made the shortages worse, and in the Soviet zone dismantling slowed recovery. Judgement [2]: for example, shortages were the main problem because they were universal and threatened survival, and many other problems made them worse.

**11.** Given factor [3]: Stalin's blockade of West Berlin (June 1948-May 1949) and the airlift convinced the western powers that co-operation with the USSR over Germany was impossible; the Basic Law was adopted in May 1949, the month the Blockade ended. Other factors [4]: disagreements at Potsdam and the separate running of the zones; the merger of the British and American zones; Marshall Aid, which the USSR refused; the currency reform of June 1948; the creation of the SED and communist control in the east. Comparison [3]: the Blockade was a response to the currency reform, which itself followed the Marshall Plan, so the division was the product of a longer process, but the Blockade removed any remaining hope of agreement. Judgement [2]: for example, the Blockade was the final trigger, but the economic division created by the Marshall Plan and the new currency was the main reason.

**12.** Given factor, case for failure [3]: the process was slow and inconsistent; many people were classed as followers; as the Cold War grew many former Nazis returned to posts in the administration, courts and police. Case against [4]: the Nuremberg Trials punished leading Nazis and made the Holocaust public; Nazi organisations and symbols were banned; the Basic Law of 1949 created a democratic constitution with strong protections; in the Soviet zone old elites were removed, though replaced by another dictatorship. Comparison [3]: denazification as a process of judging individuals largely failed, but combined with democratisation and the trials it helped discredit Nazism. Judgement [2]: for example, it failed as a purge of individuals but succeeded in removing Nazism as a political force.
