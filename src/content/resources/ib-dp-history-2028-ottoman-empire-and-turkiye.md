**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 4, "The Ottoman Empire and the creation of Türkiye (circa 1790 to 1938)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The paper rewards the evaluation of historical arguments, so this guide frames each inquiry topic around arguments you can test. The six inquiry topics are: the decline of Ottoman power; the Eastern Question; the Tanzimat reforms; the Committee of Union and Progress; the impact of conflict; and the rule of Atatürk.

## 1. Decline of Ottoman power (from circa 1790)

By 1790 the empire still stretched across the Balkans, Anatolia, the Middle East and North Africa, but its armies had lost wars to Russia and Austria and provincial notables held local power. The guide asks you to consider four points.

- **Loss of influence in North Africa.** Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 showed Ottoman weakness. France took Algiers in 1830, and later Tunisia (1881). Italy took Tripolitania in 1911.
- **Impact of the Napoleonic Wars.** They brought the French into Egypt, increased Russian and British involvement, and spread ideas of nationalism and reform.
- **Rise of Muhammad Ali.** An Albanian officer sent to Egypt after the French left, he became governor in 1805, destroyed the Mamluk elite in 1811, built a modern army and administration, and fought the sultan. His son Ibrahim conquered Syria in the 1830s. European powers forced him back in 1840 but he and his family kept Egypt as hereditary rulers, so Egypt was effectively autonomous.
- **Greek War of Independence (1821).** A rising that began in the Danubian principalities and the Morea became a long war. European sympathy and the destruction of the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at Navarino (1827) led to Greek independence, recognised in 1830. Sultan Mahmud II reacted by destroying the Janissary corps in 1826 and starting army reform.

**Argument to weigh:** "Ottoman decline was caused mainly by external pressure." Support: Russia, France, Britain and Italy took territory. Challenge: internal factors mattered, including weak finances, local power of governors, and the difficulty of holding together a multi-religious empire against nationalist movements.

## 2. The "Eastern Question" and European influence

The Eastern Question meant: what would happen to Ottoman lands as the empire weakened, and who would gain? The guide names economic, religious and strategic reasons.

- **Economic:** European trade treaties gave low customs duties and legal privileges (capitulations) to foreign merchants; the Ottoman state borrowed from European banks and went into default in 1875, with European control of part of its revenue through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration from 1881.
- **Religious:** Russia claimed to protect Orthodox Christians and France claimed to protect Catholics. A dispute over holy places in Jerusalem helped cause the Crimean War.
- **Strategic:** Britain wanted to protect the route to India and keep Russia from the Straits and the Mediterranean.
- **Crimean War (1853 to 1856).** Britain and France supported the Ottomans against Russia. The Treaty of Paris (1856) confirmed Ottoman independence, but the empire was dependent on allies.
- **Balkan unrest.** Revolts in Bosnia-Herzegovina (1875) and Bulgaria (1876) were suppressed harshly and aroused outrage in Europe. Russia declared war in 1877. The Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878) created a large Bulgaria. Other powers objected, and the Congress of Berlin in July 1878, with Bismarck as host, reduced Bulgaria's size and recognised the independence of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania. Austria-Hungary occupied Bosnia-Herzegovina.

## 3. Tanzimat reforms (1839 to 1876)

*Tanzimat* means "reorganisation". The Gülhane Edict of 1839, issued under Sultan Abdulmejid I and promoted by Mustafa Reshid Pasha, promised security of life and property, regular taxation and conscription. The Reform Edict of 1856 promised equality between Muslims and non-Muslims.

- **Political factors:** a modern bureaucracy and army; ministries and councils; new law codes; Mahmud II's earlier centralisation. The 1876 constitution, written under Midhat Pasha, created a parliament, though Abdulhamid II suspended it in 1878.
- **Religious factors:** non-Muslims were promised legal equality, which pleased European powers but angered some Muslims and some religious leaders; the traditional millet system gave religious communities control of their own affairs.
- **Social factors:** secular schools and courts, new newspapers and an educated class who later demanded more change. The Land Code of 1858 affected landholding.
- **Individuals:** the sultans Abdulmejid I and Abdul Aziz (1861 to 1876). Abdul Aziz presided over a period of heavy borrowing, naval building and palace expense, and was deposed in 1876.

**Arguments:** the Tanzimat saved the empire by modernising the state (support: new institutions lasted into the republic); or it failed because it relied on foreign loans and could not satisfy either Muslim traditionalists or nationalists (support: bankruptcy in 1875 and the Balkan revolts).

## 4. Emergence and impact of the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP)

Abdulhamid II ruled autocratically after 1878, with censorship and a large secret police, though he also built schools and railways and promoted pan-Islamic ideas. Opposition formed among students, officers and exiles, known as the Young Turks. The CUP combined ideology (constitutionalism and a strong state), political organisation and military support, especially in Macedonia. In July 1908 officers forced the sultan to restore the constitution.

- **Second Constitutional Era (1908 to 1913).** Elections, a free press and political parties appeared. A counter-coup in 1909 failed, and Abdulhamid was deposed. Hopes of equality for all communities faded as Balkan losses mounted.
- **The 1913 coup.** In January 1913 Enver and others seized the Sublime Porte, and a CUP leadership (Enver, Talaat, Cemal) ruled with little opposition. Nationalism turned more Turkish and authoritarian.
- **Approach to minorities (to 1918).** Under the CUP, policies of Turkification affected Arabs, Albanians and others. During the First World War the government ordered the mass deportation of Armenians in 1915, with massacres and deaths on a huge scale. Most historians and many governments describe these events as genocide, and the Turkish state disputes this term. Present the evidence carefully, and note that the Hamidian massacres of the 1890s and the Adana massacre of 1909 came earlier.

## 5. Impact of conflict on the Ottoman Empire

- **Italo-Turkish War (1911 to 1912)** lost Tripolitania and showed weakness. **Balkan Wars (1912 to 1913)** cost almost all European territory except the area around Edirne and Istanbul, and sent many Muslim refugees into Anatolia.
- **First World War.** The government joined the Central Powers in late 1914. The empire faced fronts at the Dardanelles (where Mustafa Kemal gained prominence in 1915), the Caucasus, Mesopotamia and Palestine. The **Arab Revolt** from 1916, led by Sharif Hussein of Mecca and his sons with British support, helped to undermine control of the Arab provinces. The Armistice of Mudros followed in October 1918.
- **Turkish War of Independence (1919 to 1923).** The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) proposed to partition Anatolia. Greek forces landed at Smyrna in 1919. Mustafa Kemal organised resistance from Ankara, where the Grand National Assembly met in 1920. After victories over Greek forces in 1921 and 1922, the Sultanate was abolished in November 1922.
- **Treaty of Lausanne (1923)** replaced Sèvres, recognised the borders of the new state, and set a compulsory exchange of populations between Greece and Turkey. The Republic of Türkiye was proclaimed on 29 October 1923, with Mustafa Kemal as president.

## 6. The rule of Atatürk (1923 to 1938)

- **Political reform.** One-party rule under the People's Republican Party; abolition of the caliphate in 1924; a new constitution; centralised control of regions.
- **Social and cultural reform, including secularisation.** Religious schools were closed, Sufi orders banned (1925), the hat law (1925) replaced the fez, the Swiss-based Civil Code (1926) replaced religious law, the Latin alphabet replaced Arabic script (1928), women gained the vote in local elections in 1930 and national elections in 1934, and surnames were required in 1934 (Kemal took "Atatürk").
- **Economic reform.** State-led development ("statism") in the 1930s, with state factories, banks and five-year plans after 1934, and railway building. Results were mixed; Turkey remained largely rural.
- **Opposition.** The Sheikh Said rebellion of 1925 led to emergency laws and Independence Tribunals. Opposition parties were tried and closed (1924 to 1925 and 1930), and the Dersim operations of 1937 to 1938 involved harsh force. Supporters of the regime argue it modernised a defeated country quickly; critics stress authoritarianism and suppression of minorities.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "The Tanzimat reforms failed to save the Ottoman Empire." To what extent do you agree?

1. Define "failed to save": the empire collapsed by 1922, but the reforms may have delayed that and shaped what came next.
2. For: borrowing, Balkan losses, little equality in practice, conservative resistance.
3. Against: modern bureaucracy, army, schools and law, which the CUP and Atatürk used.
4. Perspectives: older views of constant decline; revisionist views that the empire was adapting.
5. Judgement: the reforms failed in the narrow sense that the empire fell, but succeeded in building institutions that outlasted it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating 1923 as a total break; many officials and ideas came from the late empire.
- Saying "Turkey" for the whole period; the empire was multi-ethnic.
- Omitting the Armenian events or using unsupported claims; use careful wording.
- Mixing San Stefano (1878, Russia's terms) with Berlin (1878, the powers' revision).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I use "Türkiye" or "Turkey"?** The guide uses Türkiye; either is understood, but be consistent.

**Which individuals can questions name?** Those in the guide: Muhammad Ali, Abdul Aziz, Mustafa Kemal Atatürk and events such as the Treaty of Lausanne.
