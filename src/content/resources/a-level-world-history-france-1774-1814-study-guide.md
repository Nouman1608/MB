**France, 1774-1814 is the first topic of the European option at AS Level in Cambridge International History 9489.** It runs from the accession of Louis XVI to Napoleon's first abdication and is built around four key questions: why revolution broke out in 1789, why France became a republic by 1792, how well France was governed in 1793-99, and why Napoleon rose and fell. The syllabus says you will not need to study foreign policy and military campaigns in detail, only their impact inside France.

## Where this fits in 9489

The AS European option has three topics. They rotate between Paper 1 (Historical Sources: one two-part question, 15 + 25 marks, on four sources) and Paper 2 (Outline Study: two two-part questions, 10 + 20 marks). For the 2027-2029 syllabus, France is the **Paper 1** topic in 2027 and a **Paper 2** topic in 2028 and 2029, so you need both source skills and essay knowledge. ## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL HISTORY (9489), 2027-2029 -- TOPIC 1: FRANCE, 1774-1814**

- **1.1 What were the causes and immediate consequences of the 1789 Revolution?** The Ancien Régime (absolute monarchy, the Estates, Third Estate discontent), the American War of Independence, the Enlightenment, finance under Turgot, Necker and Calonne; the crisis of 1787-89 (Assembly of Notables, Brienne and the Parlement of Paris, harvests and prices, the Estates-General and the *cahiers*); the Tennis Court Oath, the Bastille, the Great Fear, the August Decrees, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the March of the Women.
- **1.2 How and why did France become a republic by 1792?** Jacobins, Feuillants and Girondins; counter-revolution; financial, local government, justice and Church reforms; disputes over the constitution, Varennes and the Champ de Mars; the Legislative Assembly, war with Austria (April 1792), the September Massacres and the abolition of the monarchy.
- **1.3 How well was France governed in the period 1793-99?** Problems after the King's execution; the 1793 constitution and the Committee of Public Safety; Robespierre and the Terror; the White Terror and the Paris risings of 1795; the Directory's constitution, aims, problems and record.
- **1.4 What caused the rise and fall of Napoleon Bonaparte?** Reputation and ambition; the 1799 coup; the Consulate; authoritarian control; domestic reforms (Code, education, Bank of France, Concordat); his fall (conscription, the Continental System, the Peninsular War, nationalism, Russia, Leipzig, abdication in 1814).

## 1.1 Why did revolution break out in 1789?

**Long-term weaknesses.** Louis XVI (king from 1774) was an absolute monarch, but government was a patchwork of provinces, privileges and courts. The First and Second Estates -- clergy and nobility -- were largely exempt from the main direct tax, the *taille*, so the burden fell on the Third Estate, which included both peasants and a wealthy, educated bourgeoisie excluded from high office. Enlightenment writers such as Montesquieu and Rousseau supplied ideas of separated powers and popular sovereignty, and French support for the American War of Independence spread the language of rights while adding enormously to royal debt.

**Failed financial reform.** Turgot (1774-76) attempted free trade in grain and abolition of guilds and forced labour, but was dismissed after opposition from privileged groups. Necker (1777-81) financed the American war by borrowing, and his published *Compte rendu* (1781) gave a misleadingly healthy picture. Calonne found the crown close to bankruptcy and in 1787 proposed a universal land tax to an **Assembly of Notables**, which refused to approve it. His successor **Brienne** met resistance from the **Parlement of Paris**, which argued that only the Estates-General could approve new taxes. By August 1788 the crown had suspended payments, agreed to call the Estates-General, and recalled Necker.

**Short-term crisis.** The poor harvest of 1788 pushed bread prices to a peak in summer 1789 while unemployment rose in textile towns. The *cahiers de doléances* drawn up for the elections showed widespread demands for reform, though not yet for an end to monarchy.

**From Estates-General to revolution.** The Estates-General met at Versailles on 5 May 1789. Deadlock over voting by order or by head led the Third Estate to declare itself the **National Assembly** (17 June) and swear the **Tennis Court Oath** (20 June) not to separate until France had a constitution. Louis refused real reform, and his dismissal of Necker and gathering of troops near Paris prompted the **storming of the Bastille** on 14 July. In the countryside, rumours of aristocratic plots caused the **Great Fear**; the Assembly responded with the **August Decrees** abolishing feudal privileges, and on 26 August the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**. In October the **March of the Women** to Versailles forced the royal family to move to Paris.

## 1.2 How and why did France become a republic by 1792?

The Constituent Assembly rebuilt France: church lands were nationalised and **assignats** issued against them (soon a paper currency that lost value); taxes were made uniform; France was divided into departments with elected officials; and justice was reformed with elected judges and juries. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** (1790), requiring priests to swear loyalty, split the Church and drove many Catholics towards counter-revolution.

Constitutional monarchy failed because the King did not accept it. The **flight to Varennes** (June 1791) destroyed trust in Louis. When the Cordeliers and others petitioned for his removal, the National Guard fired on the crowd at the **Champ de Mars** (July 1791), and the moderate **Feuillants** split from the Jacobins. In the **Legislative Assembly** (from October 1791), the **Girondins** pressed for war, believing it would expose traitors and spread revolution; France declared war on **Austria in April 1792**. Defeats, fear of invasion and the King's vetoes led to the overthrow of the monarchy in August, the **September Massacres** of prisoners, and the new **National Convention** abolishing the monarchy in September 1792.

## 1.3 How well was France governed, 1793-99?

**1793-95.** After Louis XVI's execution in January 1793, France faced war with much of Europe, revolt in the Vendée and federalist risings, and pressure from the Paris **sans-culottes**, who wanted price controls and punishment of "enemies". The Jacobins (the Mountain) purged the Girondins in June 1793. A democratic **Constitution of 1793** was approved but suspended, and the **Committee of Public Safety**, with **Robespierre** prominent, ran a revolutionary government. The **Terror** used the Law of Suspects, revolutionary tribunals and the guillotine; price controls (the Maximum) and mass conscription supported the war. Military success by mid-1794 made the Terror seem unnecessary, and Robespierre was overthrown and executed in **July 1794 (Thermidor)**. A **White Terror** against Jacobins followed, and hunger produced the Paris risings of 1795, put down by the army.

**The Directory, 1795-99.** The **Constitution of the Year III** created a five-man Directory and two councils, with property qualifications, to prevent both dictatorship and popular rule. Its aims were stability and an end to the Terror. It faced inflation, royalist and Jacobin opposition, and a costly war. Its record is mixed: it stabilised the currency by ending the assignats and writing off much debt, and won victories in Italy, but it relied on coups against election results (such as Fructidor, 1797) and increasingly on the army -- which prepared the way for Napoleon.

## 1.4 Why did Napoleon rise and fall?

**Rise.** Napoleon's reputation came from crushing the 1795 rising and from his Italian campaign of 1796-97. With Sieyès he carried out the **coup of Brumaire (November 1799)**. As **First Consul** under a new constitution approved by plebiscite he held real power; he became Consul for life in 1802 and **Emperor in 1804**.

**Control.** He set up new ministries and a **Council of State**, appointed prefects to run the departments, reformed the police under Fouché and the courts, closed most Paris newspapers, used propaganda and managed plebiscites.

**Reforms.** The **Civil Code (Code Napoléon, 1804)** confirmed legal equality and property rights while strengthening fathers' authority; the **lycées** trained officials and officers; the **Bank of France** (1800) and efficient tax collection stabilised finances; bread prices were watched closely to keep Paris calm; and the **Concordat (1801)** reconciled the state with the Catholic Church while keeping the sale of church lands.

**Fall.** Conscription and economic hardship cost support at home. Failure to defeat Britain led to the **Continental System**, which damaged trade and drove him into the **Peninsular War (1808-14)**. Nationalism grew in Spain and the German states against French rule, though the Empire was at its greatest extent in 1812. The **Russian campaign of 1812** destroyed most of the Grande Armée, defeat at **Leipzig (1813)** lost Germany, and after the Allies took **Paris** he **abdicated in April 1814**.

## Worked example: a Paper 2 part (a)

*Explain why Louis XVI called the Estates-General.* **[10]**

Link factors: (1) bankruptcy caused by war debt and failed reforms meant new taxes were essential; (2) the Notables and the Parlement of Paris refused to approve them and insisted only the Estates-General could; (3) unrest in 1788 and the crown's suspension of payments left no alternative. The best answers show how these connected -- financial need created the crisis, and the privileged orders' resistance decided the method.

## Common mistakes

- Treating 1789 as a purely economic revolution and ignoring the political crisis of 1787-88.
- Running together the Constituent Assembly, Legislative Assembly and Convention.
- Calling the Directory a complete failure without its financial and military gains.
- Writing military narrative of Napoleon's campaigns instead of their impact inside France.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know Napoleon's battles?**
Only their impact on France. The syllabus says foreign policy and campaigns are not required in detail.

**When is France assessed on Paper 1?**
In 2027 under the 2027-2029 rotation; in 2028 and 2029 it is assessed on Paper 2.

**What is the hardest key question?**
Usually 1.3, because you must judge "how well" France was governed, comparing the Terror and the Directory with clear criteria such as stability, finance and defence.
