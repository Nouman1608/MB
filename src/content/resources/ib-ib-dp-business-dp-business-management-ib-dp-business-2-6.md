This guide goes deeper into section 2.6 (Communication) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The section applies to SL and HL and has two content points. Formal and informal methods "for an organization in a given situation" are set at AO3, so the skill is choosing and justifying, not listing. Barriers to communication are set at AO2, so apply each barrier to the case and explain its effect.

## Four ways to describe any method

A method such as "team meeting" or "email" can be described along four dimensions. Using them makes your comparison precise.

| Dimension | Options | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Formality | Formal (official channels) or informal (outside them) | Formal gives a record and authority; informal gives speed and openness |
| Medium | Written, oral, visual or digital | Written suits detail and records; oral suits feelings and questions |
| Direction | Vertical (up or down the hierarchy) or horizontal (between equals) | Upward communication lets managers hear problems |
| Flow | One-way or two-way | Two-way lets the sender check understanding |

So a weekly team huddle is formal, oral, mostly horizontal and two-way. A company newsletter is formal, written, downward and one-way. The grapevine is informal, oral or digital, in any direction and two-way.

## Choosing a method: the AO3 skill

A clear way to justify a choice is to set criteria from the case, then judge each method against them. A simple score of 1 (poor) to 3 (good) makes the comparison visible, although your conclusion must still rest on reasons, not just totals.

### Worked example: Fennimore Hotels

Fennimore Hotels is an invented group of 6 hotels with 420 staff, many working night shifts and many speaking English as a second language. It must tell all staff about a new fire evacuation procedure that starts in two weeks. Every member of staff must understand it, and the law requires proof that staff were informed.

Criteria from the case: reaches all shifts, can be understood across languages, gives a record, and allows questions.

| Method | Reaches all shifts | Understood across languages | Gives a record | Allows questions | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Email to all staff | 2 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 7 |
| Notice boards with diagrams | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 8 |
| Shift briefings with a practice drill | 3 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 11 |
| Informal word of mouth from supervisors | 2 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 7 |

The briefings score highest because they are two-way, can use demonstration rather than written language, and can be repeated for each shift. Their record is weaker, so a signed attendance sheet should be added. Visual notice boards are a useful back-up.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Recommend a method Fennimore should use to communicate the new procedure.*

> The procedure is a safety matter, so the method must make sure every employee understands it, not just receives it. **[criterion from case]** An email would be cheap and recorded, but staff with limited English may misread a written procedure, and night staff may not check email. **[method 1 judged against case]** Shift briefings with a practice drill are formal and two-way, so supervisors can check understanding and answer questions, and a drill shows the route rather than relying on language. **[method 2 judged against case]** Their weakness is cost, because 6 hotels and three shifts need many sessions. **[limitation]** I recommend briefings with a drill, plus a signed attendance list and visual notices, because understanding matters more than speed for a safety rule that starts in two weeks. If the change were minor, such as a new rota template, an email would be enough. **[judgement and condition]**

## Where informal communication fits

Informal communication is part of the content point, and answers that dismiss it lose marks. Its uses:

* **Early warning.** Managers who listen to informal talk hear about problems before they reach formal reports.
* **Reinforcing a formal message.** Supervisors chatting with staff after a briefing can check whether people have understood it.
* **Building relationships.** Informal contact between departments eases cooperation, especially in organisations organised by function.

Its risks are rumour, distortion and messages that reach some staff and not others. A balanced answer uses informal channels to support, not replace, formal ones when accuracy matters.

## Barriers: cause, effect and cure

AO2 means each barrier must be tied to the situation and its effect explained. Pairing each barrier with a cure also helps in evaluation questions.

| Barrier | Typical effect in a case | Possible cure |
|---|---|---|
| Jargon or technical language | Staff outside the specialism misunderstand instructions | Plain language; glossary; check understanding |
| Second-language receivers | Key details are missed | Visual aids, translation, demonstrations |
| Long chain of command | Messages are delayed or altered at each level | Delayering; direct briefings from senior managers |
| Information overload | Important messages are ignored among routine ones | Prioritise; label urgency; fewer, clearer messages |
| Physical distance or remote work | Fewer chances to clarify; feelings of isolation | Video meetings; scheduled check-ins |
| Time zones | Delays in replies; missed live discussion | Overlapping hours; shared written updates |
| Status and fear | Junior staff do not report problems upward | Anonymous feedback; open-door policies |
| Low trust | Messages are doubted or ignored | Consistent, honest messages over time |
| Wrong medium | Sensitive news delivered impersonally | Face-to-face for sensitive or complex messages |
| No feedback loop | Sender assumes the message was understood | Two-way methods; ask receivers to summarise |

### Applying a barrier: a short worked example

Ostler Robotics (invented) has engineers in two countries with a seven-hour time difference. Design changes are posted in a shared document, and engineers in the second country find out about them the following day. Two production runs are made to an old design.

* Barrier: time zones, combined with one-way written communication.
* Effect: rework costs and missed delivery dates, and frustration between the two teams.
* Cure: a short overlapping hour for live review of each change, with a rule that no change goes live until the other site confirms it. The cost is that some engineers must work earlier or later.

## Common mistakes

* Listing methods with no criteria from the case.
* Saying informal communication is always harmful.
* Naming a barrier without explaining its effect on the business in the case.
* Recommending a cure for a barrier that is not present in the case.
* Giving a recommendation with no condition that would change it.

## Exam technique

* **Explain** a barrier: name it, apply it to the people in the case, give the consequence.
* **Compare** or **contrast** methods: use the four dimensions above.
* **Recommend**, **evaluate** or **justify**: set criteria from the stem, weigh at least two methods, state a limitation and conclude with a condition.
