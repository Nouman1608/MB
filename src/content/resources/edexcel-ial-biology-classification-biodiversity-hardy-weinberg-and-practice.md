## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by the species concept, and give one reason why it cannot be applied to every organism. **[2]**

**2.** A research group reports that single-celled organisms from a hot spring have ribosomal RNA sequences unlike those of any known group, and proposes a new classification.

**(a)** Name the three domains in the current three-domain system. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the new grouping is not accepted immediately, and how it will be evaluated. **[3]**

**3.** The same protein of 104 amino acids was sequenced in four species, W, X, Y and Z. The numbers of amino acid differences between pairs are shown.

| | W | X | Y |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 4 | | |
| Y | 13 | 11 | |
| Z | 9 | 8 | 12 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage difference between W and Y. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the two most closely related species and justify your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why sequence data are used in classification alongside phenotype, and give one limitation of using a single protein. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** State what is meant by endemism. **[1]**
**(b)** A bird is endemic to one small island. Explain why it is at greater risk of extinction from an introduced predator than a bird found across a continent. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the two levels of biodiversity other than species diversity. **[1]**

**5.** Genetic diversity at one gene was measured in three populations of one mammal. In the island population, 27 of 90 animals were heterozygous. In a zoo group, 12 of 60 were heterozygous. In a reintroduced group, 6 of 40 were heterozygous. The island value is 0.30.

**(a)** Calculate the heterozygosity index for the zoo group and for the reintroduced group. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare the three values and suggest an explanation for the trend. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest one management step that could increase the index in the reintroduced group. **[1]**

**6.** Two grassland sites were sampled with the same effort. Site A contained 18, 14, 9, 6 and 3 individuals of five plant species. Site B contained four species with 30, 12, 6 and 2 individuals, and its index of diversity was 2.37.

**(a)** Calculate D for site A using D = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1). **[3]**
**(b)** Compare the diversity of the two sites. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two limitations of using these values for each whole site. **[2]**

**7.** Field X holds six species and 100 individuals, of which 94 belong to one species and the other five species have 2, 1, 1, 1 and 1 individuals. Field Y holds four species with 25 individuals each.

**(a)** State what is meant by species richness. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why species richness alone is a poor way to compare these fields, supporting your answer with calculations of D. **[3]**

**8.** A small desert mammal shelters in a burrow by day and feeds at night. It has large ears with many blood vessels, and it produces very concentrated urine.

**(a)** Classify each of the three features as behavioural, anatomical or physiological. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the large ears help the mammal survive. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what is meant by the niche of this mammal. **[2]**

**9.** A recessive condition affects 1 in 1600 people in a large, randomly mating population.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele, q. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the frequency of carriers. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the expected number of carriers in a population of 80 000. **[1]**
**(d)** State one assumption you made. **[1]**

**10.** A population of 300 plants has alleles A (dominant) and a (recessive) at one gene. Genotypes were counted in two generations.

| Generation | AA | Aa | aa |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 147 | 126 | 27 |
| 6 | 66 | 138 | 96 |

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of allele a in generation 1 and in generation 6. **[3]**
**(b)** If the allele frequency had stayed as in generation 1, calculate the expected number of aa plants in generation 6 and compare it with the observed number. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how mutation and natural selection could each account for the change. **[3]**

**11.** A new lake splits one population of ground beetles into two. Explain how reproductive isolation could lead to the two populations becoming separate species. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the use of zoos and seed banks in conserving endangered species and their genetic diversity. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Members of a species breed together and produce fertile offspring [1]. It cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, or to fossils, because breeding cannot be tested [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include "fertile", and give a reason with the limitation, not just the word "difficult".

**2. (a)** Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The claim rests on limited data and could contain errors [1]. Other scientists scrutinise the methods and evidence, often through peer review [1]. They repeat the work, and the grouping is accepted only if independent results support it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A three-mark "explain" needs separate points: why caution is needed, what checking happens, and what acceptance depends on.

**3. (a)** 13 ÷ 104 × 100 [1] = **12.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** W and X, with only 4 differences (3.8%) [1], the smallest number, so they share the most recent common ancestor [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Sequences show genotype differences not visible in appearance, so they give a more objective measure of relatedness [1]. One protein is a small part of the genome, so it may not reflect whole-genome relationships [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division as well as the answer, and in part (b) quote the figure and say what it means for common ancestry.

**4. (a)** A species found naturally in one geographical area and nowhere else [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The whole population lives in a small area, so one predator can reach all the birds [1]. It has no other population to recover from, and may have no defences against that predator [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Genetic diversity and habitat (ecosystem) diversity [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), compare the island bird with the continental one directly.

**5. (a)** Zoo: 12 ÷ 60 = **0.20** [1]. Reintroduced: 6 ÷ 40 = **0.15** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The index falls from 0.30 (island) to 0.20 (zoo) to 0.15 (reintroduced) [1]. Each group was founded from a few individuals and may have been inbred, so alleles were lost by genetic drift [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Introduce unrelated individuals from the wild population or another group, or plan matings between less related animals [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the values when describing a trend, and make suggestions say what adds new alleles.

**6. (a)** N = 50, so N(N − 1) = 50 × 49 = 2450 [1]. Σn(n − 1) = 306 + 182 + 72 + 30 + 6 = 596 [1]. D = 2450 ÷ 596 = **4.11** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Site A has the higher D (4.11 against 2.37), so it is more diverse [1]. It has more species and a more even spread, whereas site B is dominated by one species [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Each is a sample only, so it may not represent the whole site [1]. D counts individuals but ignores their size or biomass [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show N(N − 1) and Σn(n − 1) separately, and check that your conclusion points the right way: larger D means more diverse.

**7. (a)** The number of different species in a habitat [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Field X has more species (six against four) [1]. D for X = 9900 ÷ 8744 = 1.13 and D for Y = 9900 ÷ 2400 = 4.13 [1]. Y is far more diverse because its individuals are evenly spread; X is dominated by one species, which richness hides [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each claim with a figure, such as 94 of 100 individuals in one species.

**8. (a)** Burrowing by day and feeding at night: behavioural [1]. Large ears with many blood vessels: anatomical [1]. Concentrated urine: physiological [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Heat is lost from the blood through the large ear surface [1], keeping the body cool and reducing water loss by sweating [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The niche is the role of the species in its ecosystem: where it lives and when it is active, such as burrows by day and feeding at night [1]. It also includes its food, interactions with other species and the conditions it tolerates [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the benefit of an adaptation; "helps it adapt to the desert" only repeats the stem.

**9. (a)** q² = 1 ÷ 1600 = 0.000625 [1], so q = **0.025** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** p = 1 − 0.025 = 0.975 [1]. Carriers = 2pq = 2 × 0.975 × 0.025 = **0.049** (0.04875), about 1 in 20 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 0.04875 × 80 000 = **3900** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Any one of: random mating, no selection, no mutation, no migration, a large population [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* 1 in 1600 is q², not q. Writing p + q = 1 and 2pq explicitly makes your method clear.

**10. (a)** Generation 1: a alleles = 2 × 27 + 126 = 180 of 600, so q = **0.30** [1]. Generation 6: a alleles = 2 × 96 + 138 = 330 [1], so q = 330 ÷ 600 = **0.55** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Expected aa = q² × 300 = 0.09 × 300 = 27 [1]. Observed is 96, over three times the expected number, so the allele frequency has changed [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Mutation can produce new copies of allele a, though usually slowly [1]. If aa plants survive and reproduce better, they pass on more copies of a, so its frequency rises by natural selection [1]. Selection is the likelier cause of such a large change in six generations [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Count alleles, not individuals: divide by 600 here, not 300. Compare observed with expected using the numbers.

**11.** The lake prevents the beetles moving between the two populations, so there is no gene flow [1]. Different mutations arise in each population [1]. The environments differ, so different alleles are selected and allele frequencies diverge [1]. Eventually they can no longer breed together and have fertile offspring: separate species [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the mechanism, such as different selection pressures, rather than "they evolve differently".

**12.** Zoos: captive breeding protects animals from wild threats [1]; planned matings can keep heterozygosity high [1], but small captive populations still lose diversity through drift and inbreeding [1]. Reintroduction can restore wild populations if the cause of decline is removed and habitat exists [1]. Research and education raise knowledge and awareness [1]. Seed banks store many species and much genetic diversity cheaply in a small space [1]; however, some seeds do not survive drying and freezing, stored seeds must be tested and regrown, and the habitat is not conserved [1]. Conclusion: the methods suit different species and supplement, rather than replace, habitat protection [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a judgement based on your points, not just a list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Omitting "fertile" from the species definition.
- Describing a trend without quoting values.
- Dividing by individuals, not alleles, for allele frequency.
- Using the recessive phenotype frequency as q rather than q².
- Reading a higher D as lower diversity.
- Naming an adaptation without its benefit.
- Giving only advantages of zoos or seed banks, or saying seed banks protect the habitat.
