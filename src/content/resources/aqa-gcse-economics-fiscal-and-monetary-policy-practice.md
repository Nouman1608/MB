## Questions

**1.** Define fiscal policy. **[1]**

**2.** Define a balanced budget. **[1]**

**3.** Country G's government revenue is 640 billion units and its spending is 615 billion units.
**(a)** Calculate the budget balance and state whether it is a surplus or a deficit. **[2]**
**(b)** Next year spending rises by 8% and revenue is unchanged. Calculate the new budget balance. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how a cut in income tax could reduce unemployment. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** consequence of a government running a budget deficit for many years. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a budget surplus might slow economic growth. **[2]**

**7.** Define monetary policy and state which UK institution sets interest rates. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a rise in interest rates could reduce inflation. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a cut in interest rates could affect **(a)** a household with a large mortgage and **(b)** a pensioner living on savings. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a policy used to control inflation could conflict with the objective of full employment. **[3]**

**11.** Country H has high unemployment in one northern region and inflation close to target across the country. Evaluate whether fiscal policy or monetary policy would be more effective in reducing unemployment in the region. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy [1]. **[1]**

**2.** When government spending equals government revenue [1]. **[1]**

**3. (a)** 640 − 615 = **+25**, a **surplus** [1] [1].
**(b)** New spending = 615 × 1.08 = 664.2 [1]; balance = 640 − 664.2 = **−24.2**, a deficit [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Households keep more of their income, so disposable income rises [1]; they spend more on goods and services [1], so firms need to produce more and take on more workers [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any one developed: national debt grows [1], so more is spent on interest payments [1], leaving less for public services or requiring higher taxes later [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The government takes more out of the economy in taxes than it puts back in spending [1], so total demand is lower and firms produce less [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Using interest rates (and the money supply) to influence the economy [1]; the Bank of England [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Borrowing becomes more expensive, so consumers and firms borrow less [1]; saving becomes more attractive, so less is spent [1]; mortgage payments rise, leaving less to spend [1]; lower total spending reduces pressure on prices, so inflation falls [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Mortgage repayments fall [1], so the household has more disposable income to spend [1].
**(b)** Interest earned on savings falls [1], so the pensioner's income falls and they may cut spending [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Raising interest rates reduces spending [1], so firms sell less and need fewer workers [1], which raises unemployment and moves the economy away from full employment [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Fiscal policy:*
- Government spending can be targeted at the region, for example on new infrastructure or training [1], creating jobs where they are needed [1].
- Spending on training tackles structural unemployment if old industries have closed [1].
- But it raises borrowing and takes time to plan [1].

*Monetary policy:*
- A cut in interest rates raises spending across the whole country [1].
- With inflation close to target, a national rise in demand risks pushing inflation above target [1], and may do little for one region [1].

*Judgement:* Fiscal policy is likely to be more effective because it can be targeted at the region without raising demand everywhere, although it adds to borrowing [1] [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the context: the regional pattern and the inflation figure are both clues that point to a targeted policy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling interest-rate changes "fiscal policy".
- Forgetting to label a budget balance as a surplus or deficit.
- Stopping a chain of reasoning one step short, for example "spending falls" with no link to prices or jobs.
- Ignoring the data or context in a 9-mark question.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between fiscal and monetary policy?** Fiscal policy is the government's use of its own spending and taxation, decided by the government through the budget. Monetary policy is the use of interest rates (and the money supply) to influence the economy; in the UK the Bank of England sets the interest rate.

**How do I work out a budget balance?** Subtract government spending from government revenue. A positive answer is a budget surplus, a negative answer is a budget deficit, and zero is a balanced budget. Always state which it is.

**What is the UK's inflation target?** The government sets the Bank of England an inflation target of 2%, measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI). If inflation is above target, the Bank may raise interest rates; if it is below target and the economy is weak, it may cut them.

**How should I structure a 9-mark evaluate question?** Explain how each policy would work, using the data in the question, give at least one limitation of each, and finish with a justified judgement that answers the question set.
