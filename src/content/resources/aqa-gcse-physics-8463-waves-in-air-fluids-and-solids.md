## 4.6.1.1 Transverse and longitudinal

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Oscillations compared with direction of energy transfer | Perpendicular | Parallel |
| Features | Crests (peaks) and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Specification examples | Ripples on a water surface | Sound waves in air |

**Evidence that the wave, not the medium, travels.**

- **Ripples:** a floating cork or leaf bobs up and down as ripples pass, but it is not carried along with
  them.
- **Sound:** a candle flame or a small piece of paper in front of a loudspeaker vibrates back and forth, but
  it is not blown away. The air particles oscillate about fixed positions while the energy travels on.

## 4.6.1.2 Describing waves

- **Amplitude**: the maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its undisturbed position.
- **Wavelength (λ)**: the distance from a point on one wave to the equivalent point on the adjacent wave.
- **Frequency (f)**: the number of waves passing a point each second, in hertz (Hz).
- **Period (T)**: the time for one complete wave; **T = 1/f** (equation sheet).
- **Wave speed**: the speed at which energy is transferred (or the wave moves) through the medium.

```
wave speed = frequency × wavelength      v = f λ      (recall)
v in m/s,  f in Hz,  λ in m
```

### Two kinds of wave graph

| Graph | x-axis | What you can read from it |
|---|---|---|
| Displacement–distance | Distance along the wave | Amplitude and **wavelength** |
| Displacement–time | Time at one point | Amplitude and **period** (then f = 1/T) |

A common error is to read a "wavelength" from a displacement–time graph. On a time axis, one complete cycle
is the **period**.

**Worked example 1.** A displacement–time graph for a point on a sound wave shows one complete cycle every
0.0040 s. The sound travels at 340 m/s. Find the frequency and wavelength.

```
f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.0040 = 250 Hz
λ = v / f = 340 / 250 = 1.36 m
```

### Measuring the speed of sound in air

**Two-microphone method.** Place two microphones a measured distance apart in line with a sound source,
connected to a timer or data logger that starts when the sound reaches the first microphone and stops when it
reaches the second.

**Worked example 2.** The microphones are 1.50 m apart and the timer reads 4.4 ms.

```
v = distance / time = 1.50 / 0.0044 = 341 m/s
```

An electronic timer avoids human reaction time, which is the main problem with stopwatch methods such as
timing an echo or a distant clap. Repeating and averaging reduces the effect of random errors.

### Measuring the speed of ripples

In a ripple tank, either time how long one ripple takes to cross a measured distance and use speed =
distance ÷ time, or measure the wavelength and frequency and use v = f λ.

### Required practical 8: suitability of apparatus

The practical asks you to judge which apparatus suits each measurement.

| Measurement | Suitable approach | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ripple wavelength | Lamp above the tank to project the waves onto paper; measure across several waves with a ruler and divide | The ripples move too fast to measure one directly; several waves reduce uncertainty |
| Ripple frequency | Count waves passing a point in a measured time, or read the signal generator | A count over 10 s or more reduces timing error |
| Waves on a string (a solid) | Vibration generator and signal generator; adjust until a steady pattern forms and measure its length with a metre rule | A steady pattern can be measured at rest |

### Sound changing medium (physics only)

When sound passes from one medium to another, its **frequency stays the same**, because it is set by the
source. The **speed changes**, so the **wavelength changes in proportion**: λ = v / f.

**Worked example 3.** An 850 Hz sound passes from air (340 m/s) into water (1480 m/s).

```
λ in air   = 340 / 850  = 0.40 m
λ in water = 1480 / 850 = 1.74 m   (faster, so longer wavelength)
```

## 4.6.1.3 Reflection, absorption and transmission (physics only)

At the boundary between two materials, a wave may be:

- **reflected**: it bounces back;
- **absorbed**: its energy is transferred to the material, often warming it;
- **transmitted**: it passes through, often changing speed (and, at an angle, direction).

Usually all three happen in part. Hard, smooth walls reflect most of the sound that reaches them, giving
echoes; curtains and soft furnishings absorb more, making a room quieter. Glass transmits most visible
light but reflects some, which is why you can see a faint reflection in a window.

**Constructing a reflection ray diagram:**

1. Draw the surface and the incident ray with an arrow.
2. Draw the **normal**, a dashed line at 90° to the surface where the ray hits it.
3. Measure the angle of incidence **from the normal**.
4. Draw the reflected ray so that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence.

Required practical 9 investigates the reflection of light by different types of surface and the refraction
of light by different substances, using a ray box, protractor and a darkened room.

## 4.6.1.4 Sound waves (Higher tier only)

Sound waves can travel through solids, causing vibrations in the solid. The reverse also happens: a
vibrating solid, such as a loudspeaker cone or the wooden body of a guitar, produces sound waves in the air.

In the ear, sound waves make the **ear drum** and other parts vibrate, which causes the sensation of sound.
This conversion of sound waves into vibrations of solids **works only over a limited frequency range**,
because the parts of the ear cannot vibrate in response to frequencies that are too low or too high. That
restricts human hearing to **20 Hz to 20 kHz**.

## 4.6.1.5 Detection and exploration (Higher tier only)

Differences in the **velocity**, **absorption** and **reflection** of different types of wave in solids and
liquids let us detect and explore structures we cannot see directly.

**Ultrasound** has a frequency above 20 kHz. It is **partially reflected** at each boundary between two
different media. The time for each reflection to return to the detector gives the distance to that boundary.
It is used in medical imaging (for example scanning an unborn baby) and industrial imaging (for example
finding cracks in metal).

**Worked example 4.** Ultrasound travels at 1540 m/s in soft tissue. An echo returns 130 μs after the
pulse is sent.

```
total distance = 1540 × 130 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.200 m
depth of boundary = 0.200 / 2 = 0.100 m = 10 cm
```

**Echo sounding** uses high-frequency sound to detect objects in deep water and measure its depth. A pulse
returning after 1.2 s in water at 1500 m/s means a depth of 1500 × 1.2 / 2 = 900 m.

**Seismic waves** are produced by earthquakes.

| | P-waves | S-waves |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Longitudinal | Transverse |
| Travel through | Solids and liquids, at different speeds | Solids only: cannot travel through a liquid |

S-waves are not detected in a large region on the opposite side of the Earth from an earthquake, so part of
the core must be liquid. P-waves change speed, and so direction, at the core boundary; where they are and are
not detected shows the size of the core. The study of seismic waves gave new evidence about parts of the
Earth that cannot be observed directly.

## Common mistakes

- Reading wavelength off a time axis.
- Measuring amplitude from crest to trough.
- Saying frequency changes when sound enters a new medium.
- Forgetting to halve the distance in echo and ultrasound questions.
- Saying ultrasound is "completely" reflected at a boundary. It is **partially** reflected.
- Saying P-waves cannot travel through liquids. It is **S-waves** that cannot.

## Exam technique

- **Describe a method**: apparatus, what you measure, how you calculate the speed, and one way to improve
  accuracy.
- **Calculate**: convert ms, μs, kHz and cm first; write v = f λ or speed = distance ÷ time.
- **Explain** seismic evidence: give the observation (where waves are or are not detected), the property
  (S-waves cannot pass through liquid) and the conclusion.
