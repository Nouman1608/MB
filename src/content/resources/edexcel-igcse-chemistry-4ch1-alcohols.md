This guide teaches sub-topic 4(e) Alcohols from Topic 4, Organic chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 4.29C to 4.33C. Every point carries a C reference (printed in bold in the specification), so this whole sub-topic is assessed on **Paper 2 only**. It does not appear on Paper 1.

The [organic chemistry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-organic-chemistry/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on alcohols, with worked calculations on fermentation, hydration and combustion, and a clear comparison of the two manufacturing routes. ## The functional group and the series (4.29C and 4.30C)

An **alcohol** is an organic compound with a **hydroxyl group, -OH**, bonded to a carbon atom. The alcohols form a homologous series with the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH**.

| Name | Molecular formula | Structural formula | Mr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methanol | CH₄O | CH₃OH | 32 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₆O | CH₃CH₂OH | 46 |
| Propan-1-ol (propanol) | C₃H₈O | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH | 60 |
| Butan-1-ol (butanol) | C₄H₁₀O | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH | 74 |

The names "propanol" and "butanol" are acceptable. The "-1-" shows that the -OH is on the end carbon (carbon 1); you are only required to know the 1-ol forms. Isomers such as propan-2-ol are not required.

**Displayed formula of ethanol:**

```
    H   H
    |   |
H - C - C - O - H
    |   |
    H   H
```

The -OH group stays intact when drawn: the oxygen is bonded to one carbon and one hydrogen, and the O-H bond must be shown. A common mistake is to draw C-H-O (the hydrogen in the middle). The oxygen atom has two bonds.

**Properties.** Alcohols are colourless liquids that mix with water (the -OH group allows this), burn with a clean flame, and are used as fuels, solvents and in drinks (ethanol). The boiling point rises with chain length.

## Oxidation of ethanol (4.31C)

There are three oxidation routes you must know.

### 1. Combustion (complete oxidation)

Ethanol burns in plenty of oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water, releasing heat:

```
C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O
```

**Worked example 1.** 4.6 g of ethanol (Mr = 46) burns completely.

- Moles = 4.6 / 46 = **0.10 mol**.
- Moles of CO₂ = 2 x 0.10 = 0.20 mol; volume = 0.20 x 24 = **4.8 dm³** (at room temperature and pressure).
- Moles of H₂O = 3 x 0.10 = 0.30 mol; mass = 0.30 x 18 = **5.4 g**.

### 2. Microbial oxidation to ethanoic acid

If a solution containing ethanol (such as wine or beer) is left open to the air, bacteria in the air use oxygen to oxidise the ethanol to **ethanoic acid**, giving vinegar and a sour taste:

```
CH3CH2OH + O2 → CH3COOH + H2O     (bacteria in the air)
```

This is why an opened bottle of wine goes sour.

### 3. Heating with acidified potassium dichromate(VI)

When ethanol is heated with **potassium dichromate(VI) in dilute sulfuric acid**, it is oxidised to **ethanoic acid**. The dichromate(VI) ion is the oxidising agent. The mixture changes colour from **orange to green**, as the orange dichromate(VI) is reduced to green chromium(III) ions.

```
Ethanol  →  ethanoic acid     (heat with K2Cr2O7 / H2SO4: orange → green)
```

Heating (usually under reflux) is needed so that the ethanol does not evaporate before it reacts. The colour change can be used as a test for an alcohol, though other substances also reduce dichromate(VI) so it is not specific to alcohols.

## Manufacturing ethanol (4.32C)

### Route A: hydration of ethene

Ethene reacts with steam in the presence of a **phosphoric acid** catalyst, at about **300 °C** and **60 to 70 atm** pressure:

```
C2H4 (g) + H2O (g)  ⇌  C2H5OH (g)
```

This is a reversible reaction (see the [reversible reactions study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-reversible-reactions-and-equilibria/)). The forward reaction is exothermic and there are two moles of gas on the left and one on the right, so a high pressure favours ethanol. A compromise temperature is used so that the rate is fast enough.

**Worked example 2.** 28 g of ethene (Mr = 28) is converted. 28 / 28 = 1.0 mol of ethene could give 1.0 mol of ethanol, a mass of 1.0 x 46 = **46 g**. If only 90% is converted, the mass of ethanol = 0.90 x 46 = **41.4 g**.

### Route B: fermentation of glucose

Yeast contains enzymes that convert glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide:

```
C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2        (yeast, about 30 °C, no air)
```

**Worked example 3.** 18 g of glucose (Mr = 180) is fermented completely. Moles = 18 / 180 = 0.10 mol. Moles of ethanol = 2 x 0.10 = 0.20 mol; mass = 0.20 x 46 = **9.2 g**. If the actual mass obtained is 7.36 g, the percentage yield = 7.36 / 9.2 x 100 = **80%**.

### Why the conditions for fermentation matter (4.33C)

- **Absence of air.** If oxygen is present, the ethanol can be oxidised by the microbes to ethanoic acid, ruining the product, and the yeast would respire aerobically, forming carbon dioxide and water instead of ethanol. A water-filled airlock lets carbon dioxide out but keeps air out.
- **Optimum temperature (about 30 °C).** At lower temperatures the enzymes work slowly, so the rate is too low. At much higher temperatures the enzymes **denature** (their shape changes permanently) and the yeast dies, so the reaction stops.

### Comparing the two routes

| | Hydration of ethene | Fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material | Ethene from crude oil (non-renewable) | Glucose or sugar from plants (renewable) |
| Rate | Fast | Slow (days) |
| Type of process | Continuous | Batch |
| Product | Pure ethanol | Dilute ethanol that must be distilled |
| Conditions | 300 °C, 60 to 70 atm: high energy cost | About 30 °C, atmospheric pressure: low energy cost |
| Equipment | Expensive | Cheap |

The choice depends on the cost of the raw materials, the energy needed, the purity required and whether the oil or the farmland is plentiful.

## Trends and a comparison of the routes in numbers

**Boiling points.** The boiling point rises as the chain gets longer, because the intermolecular forces get stronger.

| Alcohol | Methanol | Ethanol | Propan-1-ol | Butan-1-ol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | 65 | 78 | 97 | 118 |
| State at 20 °C | Liquid | Liquid | Liquid | Liquid |

**Worked example 4: how efficient is each route?** The **atom economy** is the mass of the wanted product as a percentage of the total mass of the products.

- *Hydration of ethene:* C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH has a single product, so the atom economy is 46 / 46 x 100 = **100%**.
- *Fermentation:* C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂. The wanted product is 2 x 46 = 92; the total mass of products is 92 + 2 x 44 = 180. The atom economy is 92 / 180 x 100 = **51.1%**.

So hydration makes use of all the atoms in the reactants, while fermentation wastes almost half of the mass as carbon dioxide. Even so, fermentation uses a renewable raw material, a lower temperature and a cheaper plant, so a high atom economy is only one of the factors in the decision.

**Ethanol as a fuel.** Ethanol made by fermentation can be mixed with petrol or burned alone. It releases energy when it burns, and the carbon dioxide it releases was taken in by the plants that grew the sugar, so it is described as a renewable fuel. The claim needs care: growing the crop, fermenting and distilling all need energy, and farmland used for fuel is not available for food.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the alcohol group as -HO or -COH. It is -OH.
- Naming propan-2-ol or giving "propan-1-ol" for a three-carbon alcohol drawn with the OH in the middle.
- Saying fermentation needs oxygen, or that it works at a high temperature "to speed it up".
- Forgetting the catalyst (phosphoric acid), temperature or pressure for hydration.
- Describing the dichromate(VI) change as green to orange. It is **orange to green**.
- Writing the product of oxidation as ethanol. It is ethanoic acid.
- Leaving out carbon dioxide in the fermentation equation.
- Saying the yeast "dies" at 30 °C. The enzymes work best here.

## Exam technique

Learn the three conditions for each manufacturing route and the reason for each fermentation condition. For colour changes, give both colours and say what is being reduced or oxidised. Show working for moles and use the equation ratios exactly: fermentation gives 2 mol of ethanol per mole of glucose. This whole sub-topic is on Paper 2 only.
