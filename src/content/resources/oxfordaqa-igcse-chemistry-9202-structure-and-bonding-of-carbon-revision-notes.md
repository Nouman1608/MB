These notes condense section 3.2.3, Structure and bonding of carbon, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [structure and bonding of carbon study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-9202-structure-and-bonding-of-carbon/); then try the [structure and bonding of carbon practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-9202-structure-and-bonding-of-carbon-practice/).

## Statements a to e in one line each

- **a** Carbon forms four covalent bonds.
- **b** Diamond: four bonds per atom, giant covalent structure, very hard.
- **c** Graphite: three bonds per atom, layers; no covalent bonds between layers, so layers slide; soft and slippery.
- **d** Graphite: one delocalised electron per atom; conducts heat and electricity, like metals.
- **e** Fullerenes: different numbers of carbon atoms, structures based on hexagonal rings; uses in drug delivery, lubricants, catalysts and nanotubes for reinforcement.

## Carbon's four bonds

Carbon: atomic number 6, structure **2,4**. Four outer electrons → shares all four in diamond; shares three in graphite, with the fourth delocalised.

## Diamond versus graphite

| Feature | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds per atom | 4 | 3 |
| Arrangement | 3D giant lattice | flat layers of hexagons |
| Between layers | — | weak forces, no covalent bonds |
| Delocalised electrons | none | one per atom |
| Hardness | very hard | soft, slippery |
| Conducts electricity? | no | yes |
| Conducts heat? | — | yes |
| Melting point | very high | very high |
| Typical use | cutting tools, drill tips | pencils, lubricant, electrodes |

## Explanation chains

```
DIAMOND HARD
4 strong covalent bonds per atom → rigid giant lattice → much energy to break → very hard

GRAPHITE SLIPPERY
layers held by weak forces only → layers slide → soft and slippery

GRAPHITE CONDUCTS
3 bonds per atom → 1 electron per atom delocalised → moves through structure → carries charge

DIAMOND DOES NOT CONDUCT
all 4 outer electrons in bonds → no delocalised electrons → no charge carriers

BOTH HIGH MELTING POINT
melting breaks strong covalent bonds in a giant structure → much energy needed
```

## Fullerenes

- Molecules of carbon with different numbers of atoms (C₆₀ is the best known).
- Structure based on **hexagonal rings** of carbon atoms (all you need to know about structure).
- Cylindrical fullerenes = **nanotubes**.

| Use | Why it suits |
|---|---|
| Drug delivery | hollow structure can carry molecules into the body |
| Lubricants | small molecules slide and roll between surfaces |
| Catalysts | large surface area |
| Nanotubes in tennis rackets | strong, light reinforcement |

## Giant covalent structures compared

| Substance | Hard? | Conducts? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diamond | yes | no | 3D network; no free electrons |
| Silicon dioxide | yes | no | 3D network; no free electrons |
| Graphite | no | yes | layers slide; delocalised electrons |

## Uses linked to properties

| Use | Property needed | Structural reason |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond drill tips | very hard | 4 strong bonds per atom in a 3D lattice |
| Graphite in pencils | soft; layers rub off | weak forces between layers |
| Graphite electrodes | conducts; high melting point | delocalised electrons; strong bonds in layers |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | a shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Giant covalent structure | very large network of atoms all joined by covalent bonds |
| Delocalised electron | electron not tied to one atom or bond, free to move through the structure |
| Layer | flat sheet of carbon atoms in hexagonal rings, in graphite |
| Fullerene | carbon molecule with a structure based on hexagonal rings |
| Nanotube | cylindrical fullerene |
| Weak forces between layers | attractions holding graphite's layers together; much weaker than covalent bonds |

## Exam traps

- **No covalent bonds** between graphite layers: write "weak forces between layers".
- Graphite is soft **and** has a very high melting point.
- Diamond does not conduct: no **delocalised** electrons.
- Fullerenes are **molecules**, not giant structures.
- Say "one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised", not "graphite has free ions".
- A "compare" question needs both forms in each point.
- Graphite is like metals because both have delocalised electrons; it is not a metal.

## Self-test

1. How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in diamond?
2. How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in graphite?
3. Why is diamond very hard?
4. Why can graphite's layers slide over each other?
5. Explain why graphite conducts electricity.
6. Why does diamond not conduct electricity?
7. Why does graphite have a very high melting point even though it is soft?
8. In what way is graphite similar to a metal?
9. What are fullerenes based on?
10. State two uses of fullerenes.
11. What is a nanotube?
12. Give one way in which silicon dioxide is similar to diamond.

## Answers

1. Four.
2. Three.
3. Each atom is held by four strong covalent bonds in a giant three-dimensional structure; a lot of energy is needed to break them.
4. There are no covalent bonds between the layers, only weak forces.
5. One electron from each carbon atom is delocalised; these electrons can move through the structure and carry charge.
6. All four outer electrons of each atom are used in covalent bonds, so there are no delocalised electrons.
7. Melting requires breaking the strong covalent bonds within the layers, which needs a lot of energy; softness depends only on the weak forces between layers.
8. Both have delocalised electrons, so both conduct electricity (and heat).
9. Hexagonal rings of carbon atoms.
10. Any two: drug delivery into the body; lubricants; catalysts; nanotubes for reinforcing materials such as tennis rackets.
11. A cylindrical fullerene (a tube of carbon atoms).
12. It is a giant covalent structure, so it is hard / has a very high melting point / does not conduct.
