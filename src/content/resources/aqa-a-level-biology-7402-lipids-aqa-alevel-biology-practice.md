## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Name the two groups of lipid in the specification. **[1]**

**2.** Name the molecules that react to form a triglyceride and the type of bond formed. **[2]**

**3.** For each fatty acid, state whether it is saturated or unsaturated, and give the number of C=C double bonds: (a) C₁₁H₂₃COOH; (b) C₁₇H₂₉COOH. **[2]**

**4.** A student says: "An unsaturated fatty acid has fewer carbon atoms than a saturated one." Correct this statement. **[1]**

**5.** A triglyceride is made from glycerol (Mr 92) and three different fatty acids with Mr 200, 228 and 256. Calculate its Mr. Water has Mr 18. **[2]**

**6.** State how a phospholipid differs in structure from a triglyceride. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why phospholipids form a bilayer when there is water on both sides. **[3]**

**8.** Give two properties of triglycerides that make them suitable as energy stores, and explain one of them. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**9.** A student carries out the emulsion test on four samples.

| Tube | Contents | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Crushed nut + ethanol, then water | Milky white |
| 2 | Ethanol, then water | Clear |
| 3 | Crushed apple + ethanol, then water | Clear |
| 4 | Crushed seed added to water, then ethanol | Clear, with oily drops floating |

**(a)** State the purpose of tube 2. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the result in tube 1. **[2]**

**(c)** Tube 4 contains the same seed as a tube that gave a milky white result when tested correctly. Explain why tube 4 gave a clear result. **[2]**

**(d)** The student says the nut contains more lipid than the seed. Evaluate this conclusion. **[2]**

**10.** The table gives illustrative melting points for three triglycerides of the same chain length.

| Triglyceride | Total C=C double bonds | Melting point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | 0 | 65 |
| T2 | 3 | 5 |
| T3 | 6 | −12 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship shown. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the relationship. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest which triglyceride would be most suitable as an energy store in a plant seed that must stay liquid at low temperatures. Give a reason. **[1]**

**11.** Compare the structures and properties of triglycerides and phospholipids. **[6]**

**12.** Seals and other mammals living in cold water have thick layers of adipose tissue rich in triglycerides. Suggest and explain two ways this adaptation helps them survive. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Triglycerides and phospholipids [1]. **[1]**

**2.** One glycerol and three fatty acids [1]; ester bonds [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (a) Saturated, 0 double bonds (2 × 11 + 1 = 23) [1]. (b) Unsaturated, 3 double bonds (saturated would be C₁₇H₃₅; 6 H missing ÷ 2 = 3) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** An unsaturated fatty acid has one or more C=C double bonds, so it has fewer hydrogen atoms, not fewer carbons [1]. **[1]**

**5.** 92 + 200 + 228 + 256 = 776 [1]; 776 − (3 × 18) = **722** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Three ester bonds form, so subtract three water molecules whatever the fatty acids are.

**6.** In a phospholipid, one of the three fatty acids is replaced by a phosphate-containing group [1], so it has two fatty acids and a hydrophilic head [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Phospholipids have a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails [1]. The heads are attracted to water and the tails are repelled by it [1]. With water on both sides, two layers form so that the heads face the water on each side and the tails point inward, away from water [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Two properties named, for example many C–H bonds and insolubility in water [1]. Explanation of one: the many C–H bonds release a large amount of energy when oxidised [1], so a lot of energy is stored in a small mass [1]. An equally creditworthy explanation: being insoluble, they do not affect the water potential of the cell, so they can be stored in large amounts without water entering by osmosis. **[3]**

**9. (a)** A negative control, showing that ethanol and water alone do not give a milky result [1].

**(b)** Lipid in the nut dissolved in the ethanol [1]; when water was added, the lipid came out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light, forming a white emulsion [1].

**(c)** The seed was added to water first, and lipid does not dissolve in water [1], so the lipid floated as drops and never dissolved in the ethanol to form an emulsion [1].

**(d)** The conclusion is not supported [1]: the emulsion test is qualitative and the seed was not tested correctly, so no comparison of amounts can be made; masses of sample were also not controlled [1]. **[7]**

**10. (a)** As the number of double bonds increases, the melting point decreases [1].

**(b)** C=C double bonds put kinks in the fatty acid chains [1]; kinked chains cannot pack closely together [1], so the forces between molecules are weaker and less energy is needed to melt them [1].

**(c)** T3, because it has the lowest melting point and would stay liquid at low temperatures [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Structure: both have a glycerol backbone with fatty acids joined by ester bonds [1]; a triglyceride has three fatty acids, whereas a phospholipid has two and a phosphate-containing group [1]. Properties: a triglyceride is non-polar and wholly hydrophobic, whereas a phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails [1]; triglycerides form droplets or stores in cells, whereas phospholipids form bilayers in water [1]; triglycerides have more C–H bonds per molecule and are the main energy store [1], whereas phospholipids form membranes that act as a barrier to water-soluble substances [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "Compare" needs both molecules in each point. Two separate descriptions without matching words such as "whereas" can lose marks.

**12.** Thermal insulation: triglycerides conduct heat poorly, so the layer reduces heat loss to the cold water and helps keep body temperature up [1][1]. Energy store: the many C–H bonds release a lot of energy when oxidised, providing respiratory substrate when food is scarce, and lipids are less dense than water, helping buoyancy [1][1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Judging saturation by chain length.
- Subtracting the wrong number of water molecules for a triglyceride.
- Swapping hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts.
- Describing the emulsion test result as a precipitate.
- Comparing amounts of lipid from a qualitative test.
- Giving one-sided comparisons without "whereas".

**How do you find the number of double bonds from a fatty acid formula?**
Compare the hydrogens in the R group with 2n + 1 for a saturated chain of n carbons; every two missing hydrogens is one C=C.
