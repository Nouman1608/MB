This study guide teaches section **4.2.1 Principles of organisation** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018 onwards. Organisation is assessed on Paper 1, set at Foundation and Higher Tier. Nothing in 4.2.1 is marked (HT only), so everything here is needed on both tiers.

## What the specification says

Section 4.2.1 is four sentences long, and each one is examinable as written:

1. "Cells are the basic building blocks of all living organisms."
2. "A tissue is a group of cells with a similar structure and function."
3. "Organs are aggregations of tissues performing specific functions."
4. "Organs are organised into organ systems, which work together to form organisms."

Alongside these, the specification lists mathematical skill MS 1c (ratios, fractions and percentages) and says students should "develop an understanding of size and scale in relation to cells, tissues, organs and systems."

## The five levels, with the words that score

| Level | Key words for a definition | Human example | Plant example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell | Basic building block of living organisms | Muscle cell | Palisade cell |
| Tissue | Group of cells with a **similar structure and function** | Muscle tissue | Palisade mesophyll |
| Organ | **Aggregation of tissues** performing a **specific function** | Stomach | Leaf |
| Organ system | Organs **working together** | Digestive system | Roots, stem and leaves (the transport system) |
| Organism | Organ systems working together to form a living thing | A human | A sunflower |

"Aggregation" simply means a collection, so an organ is a collection of different tissues. The plural matters: one tissue on its own is never an organ.

### Following one example all the way up

The **stomach** shows each level clearly.

- **Cells**: muscle cells can contract; gland cells release digestive juices.
- **Tissues**: muscular tissue churns the contents; glandular tissue produces enzymes and acid; a lining (epithelial) tissue covers the inner and outer surfaces.
- **Organ**: the stomach, where these tissues together carry out one specific function, the digestion of food.
- **Organ system**: the stomach works with the mouth, small intestine, liver, pancreas and large intestine in the **digestive system**, which the specification describes as "an example of an organ system in which several organs work together to digest and absorb food."
- **Organism**: the digestive system works with the circulatory and respiratory systems so that every cell receives the glucose and oxygen it needs.

The **leaf** is the plant equivalent: epidermal tissue, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, xylem and phloem make up one organ, and roots, stem and leaves form an organ system for transporting substances.

## Classifying difficult examples

Exam questions often give a list and ask you to sort it. Use two tests: *is it one type of cell or many?* and *does it contain more than one tissue?*

| Example | Level | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Red blood cell | Cell | A single cell |
| Blood | Tissue | Cells (and platelets) in plasma, working together to transport substances |
| Xylem | Tissue | Many similar cells forming hollow tubes with one function |
| Heart | Organ | Contains muscle tissue plus other tissues |
| Skin | Organ | Made of several different tissues, including an epithelial covering |
| Leaf | Organ | Contains several plant tissues |
| Circulatory system | Organ system | Heart and blood vessels working together |
| Bacterium | Organism | A single-celled organism, so it has no tissues or organs |

## Why large organisms need organisation

A single-celled organism can obtain everything it needs by diffusion through its surface, because it has a large surface area compared with its volume. In a large multicellular organism, most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow. Organisation solves this in two ways:

- **Specialisation**: cells differentiate to do one job well (a red blood cell carries oxygen; a root hair cell absorbs water), and similar specialised cells group into tissues.
- **Organ systems**: exchange organs (lungs, small intestine, leaves, roots) take in substances, and transport systems (the circulatory system, xylem and phloem) carry them to every cell.

Damage at one level affects the levels above it. Fatty deposits in the coronary arteries starve heart muscle tissue of oxygen, the heart pumps less well as an organ, and the whole circulatory system and organism suffer. Being able to trace an effect up the levels is a common source of explanation marks.

## Size and scale (MS 1c)

Units you need: 1 m = 1000 mm; 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres). So 1 cm = 10 000 µm and 1 m = 1 000 000 µm.

**Worked example 1: cells in a leaf.** In this example a palisade cell is 0.1 mm long and a leaf is 6 cm long. How many palisade cells would fit end to end along the leaf?

```
Same unit:   6 cm = 60 mm
Number:      60 / 0.1 = 600 cells
```

**Worked example 2: the share of a tissue.** In the same leaf the whole leaf is 0.3 mm thick and the palisade mesophyll layer is 0.1 mm thick. What fraction and percentage of the thickness is palisade tissue?

```
Fraction:    0.1 / 0.3 = 1/3
Percentage:  1/3 x 100 = 33% (to 2 significant figures)
```

**Worked example 3: orders of magnitude.** A red blood cell is about 8 µm across, and an adult human is about 1.8 m tall. How many times taller is the human than the cell is wide?

```
Same unit:   1.8 m = 1.8 x 1 000 000 = 1 800 000 um
Ratio:       1 800 000 / 8 = 225 000, about 2 x 10^5
```

So the organism is about five orders of magnitude larger than one of its cells. Always convert to one unit before dividing; most lost marks here come from mixing millimetres with micrometres.

## Exam technique

- **Define** a level: use the specification's wording, including "similar structure and function" for a tissue.
- **Identify** or **name** a level: one word is enough, but check whether the item contains more than one tissue.
- **Describe** the levels: give them in order, each built from the one before.
- **Explain** why something is an organ: name at least two tissues it contains and the specific function they perform together.
- **Calculate**: show the unit conversion, then the division, and give a sensible number of significant figures.

## Common errors

- Calling a single tissue, such as muscle tissue, an organ.
- Calling blood an organ or an organ system; it is a tissue.
- Writing that a tissue is "a group of organs" or putting the levels in the wrong order.
- Saying a bacterium has tissues.
- Dividing millimetres by micrometres without converting.
- Describing an organ without saying it performs a specific function.
