## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by pastoral farming. **[1]**

**2.** Define sustainable food production. **[2]**

**3.** State the difference between hydroponics and aeroponics. **[2]**

**4.** Name **three** organic fertilisers listed in the syllabus. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **two** cash crops that can replace food crops. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A family grows maize, beans and pumpkins on a small plot and keeps two goats. Almost all the food is
eaten by the family. Identify **three** types of agriculture that describe this farm, giving a reason for
each. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how intercropping maize with beans can increase crop yield. **[3]**

**8.** *Invented data.* A farmer changes part of her land from flood irrigation to trickle irrigation.

| | Flood irrigation | Trickle irrigation |
|---|---|---|
| Water used per hectare (m³) | 6000 | 3600 |
| Yield per hectare (tonnes) | 3.0 | 3.3 |

(a) Calculate the percentage reduction in water used.

(b) Calculate the percentage increase in yield.

(c) Explain why trickle irrigation reduces the risk of salinisation. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how biological control can reduce crop losses to pests. **[3]**

**10.** A farmer sprays the same insecticide on a cotton crop every week for several years. Describe and
explain **two** impacts this could have. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the overuse of inorganic fertiliser on fields beside a river can lead to the death of
fish. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of genetically modified crops as a way to increase food
production. **[6]**

**13.** A crowded city imports most of its vegetables from farms several hundred kilometres away. Suggest
how urban farming could help the city's food supply. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Rearing animals [1].

**2.** The production of sufficient food for the present generation [1] using methods that ensure future
generations can grow food from the same land [1].

**3.** In hydroponics plant roots grow in a nutrient solution [1]; in aeroponics roots hang in air and are
sprayed with a nutrient mist [1].

**4.** Crop residue [1]; manure [1]; mulch [1].

**5.** Any two: coffee [1]; cotton [1]; soya beans [1]; palm oil [1].

**6.** Any three, each with a reason:

- Subsistence, because the food is eaten by the family [1].
- Mixed, because it grows crops and keeps animals [1].
- Arable (for the crop part), because maize, beans and pumpkins are grown [1].
- Not a monoculture, because several crops are grown together (mixed cropping) [1].

**7.** Any three:

- Beans add nitrogen to the soil, which the maize uses [1].
- The two crops use different nutrients, so the soil is not exhausted as quickly [1].
- Pests and diseases of one crop spread less easily through a field of two crops [1].
- The ground is better covered, reducing weeds and evaporation [1].

**8.** (a) (6000 - 3600) / 6000 x 100 = 2400 / 6000 x 100 = **40%** [2: 1 for the working, 1 for the
answer].
(b) (3.3 - 3.0) / 3.0 x 100 = **10%** [2: 1 for the working, 1 for the answer].
(c) Less water is applied and it goes straight to the roots, so less evaporates from the soil surface [1];
fewer salts are left behind to build up in the soil [1].

**9.** Any three:

- A natural predator, parasite or disease of the pest is introduced [1].
- It feeds on or infects the pest, reducing pest numbers [1].
- Fewer pests means less damage to the crop, so yield rises [1].
- No chemicals are used, so pollinators and other non-target species are not harmed [1].

**10.** Two impacts, each described [1] and explained [1]:

- **Pesticide resistance** [1]: a few insects survive each spraying and breed, so the population becomes
  resistant and the insecticide stops working [1].
- **Pest resurgence** [1]: the insecticide kills the pests' natural predators, so pest numbers rebound
  even higher [1].
- **Impacts on pollinators** [1]: bees and other pollinators are killed, so pollination of crops and wild
  plants falls [1].

**11.** Any five, in sequence:

- More fertiliser is applied than the crop can take up [1].
- Rain washes the excess through the soil (leaching) into the river [1].
- Nutrients such as nitrate build up in the water (nutrient enrichment) [1].
- Algae grow rapidly, covering the surface and blocking light [1].
- Algae and water plants die [1].
- Bacteria decompose them, using up the oxygen in the water [1].
- Fish die from lack of oxygen [1].

**12.** Benefits, any three:

- Genes can give resistance to pests, so less crop is lost [1].
- Drought-resistant varieties can grow where water is scarce [1].
- Pest resistance means fewer pesticides are needed [1].
- Higher yields from the same land, reducing the need to clear new land [1].

Limitations, any three:

- Seeds can be expensive and may need to be bought each year, which poor farmers cannot afford [1].
- Planting one GM variety widely encourages monoculture and loss of biodiversity [1].
- Pests may become resistant to the GM crop over time [1].
- Some consumers and governments do not accept GM food, limiting sales [1].

Maximum 6 marks; a judgement is expected for full marks.

**13.** Any three:

- Unused spaces such as rooftops, walls and empty plots can be used to grow food [1].
- Vertical farms or controlled environments such as hydroponics allow high yields from a small area [1].
- Food is grown close to consumers, so less is lost or spoiled in transport and it arrives fresher [1].
- Less transport means less fuel is used and supplies are less affected by disruption on the roads [1].
- Local people can grow some of their own food, reducing costs [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- In 6, naming types without a reason from the description.
- In 8, dividing by the new value instead of the original.
- Confusing resistance with resurgence in question 10.
- Saying fertiliser poisons fish, instead of explaining oxygen loss.
- One-sided answers to question 12.
