Every approach to conflict, and every method of resolution, is set at AO3, so you will be asked to weigh options and judge which is likely to work.

| Content point | Depth |
|---|---|

| Approaches to conflict by employees: collective bargaining, work-to-rule, strike action | AO3 |
| Approaches to conflict by employers: collective bargaining, threats of redundancies, changes of contract, closure, lockouts | AO3 |
| Conflict resolution: conciliation and arbitration, employee participation and industrial democracy, no-strike agreement, single-union agreement | AO3 |

Laws on strikes, lockouts and unions differ between countries. Keep answers general unless the case gives the rules.

## Why conflict arises: two sets of objectives

Conflict is easiest to analyse when you see it as a clash between legitimate objectives.

| Employers usually want | Employees usually want |
|---|---|
| Lower costs and higher productivity | Higher pay and better benefits |
| Flexibility to change hours, roles and methods | Stable hours and job security |
| Fast decisions | A voice in decisions that affect them |
| New technology to stay competitive | Protection from job losses caused by that technology |

Sources of conflict then fall into a few groups: pay and benefits, working conditions and hours, job security, the introduction of change, and management style or communication. For AO2, identify the source in the case and explain why it matters to the people involved. A pay dispute at a time of rising living costs is about protecting real income, not greed; a dispute over new technology is often about job security rather than the technology itself.

## Bargaining power: who is in the stronger position?

Which approach each side chooses, and whether it works, depends on bargaining power. These factors are standard analysis rather than wording from the guide.

**Employees are stronger when:**

* a high proportion of staff belong to the union, so action is widely supported
* their skills are scarce and hard to replace
* the product cannot be stored, so lost output is lost for good (such as transport or hospitals)
* demand is high and customers can easily switch to rivals
* public opinion supports them

**Employers are stronger when:**

* unemployment is high, so staff fear losing their jobs
* the business holds large stocks and can keep selling during action
* work can be moved to other sites or automated
* the business is making losses, so a closure threat is believable

Strong AO3 answers use these factors to predict how a dispute is likely to develop.

## The escalation ladder

Approaches range from cooperative to highly damaging. Both sides usually start low and move up only if talks fail.

| Step | Employee approach | Employer approach |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Collective bargaining | Collective bargaining |
| 2 | Work-to-rule | Changes of contract |
| 3 | Strike action | Threats of redundancies |
| 4 | | Lockouts or closure |

Each step raises the cost to both sides and makes trust harder to rebuild afterwards. This is why the guide pairs the approaches with resolution methods.

## Worked example: counting the cost of a strike

Corriebank Buses is an invented bus company with 240 drivers on an average of 36,000 a year (money in dollars). Drivers work 250 days a year. The union claims a 6% rise and the company offers 2.5%.

* Payroll: 240 × 36,000 = **8,640,000**.
* Cost of the claim: 6% × 8,640,000 = **518,400** a year.
* Cost of the offer: 2.5% × 8,640,000 = **216,000** a year.
* Gap: **302,400** a year, or 1,260 per driver.

The union proposes a 5-day strike. Daily fare revenue is 40,000.

* Company: loses 5 × 40,000 = 200,000 of revenue but does not pay striking drivers' wages. Daily pay per driver is 36,000 ÷ 250 = 144, so the wage saving is 240 × 144 × 5 = 172,800. Net short-term loss: **27,200**.
* Each driver: loses 5 × 144 = **720** of pay, compared with a possible gain of 1,260 a year.

Interpretation: the short-term figures look small for the company, but they hide longer-term costs. Passengers who switch to cars or rival transport may not return, and lost goodwill can last beyond the dispute. For drivers, five days of lost pay is recovered within the first year if the full claim is won, but not if the settlement is close to the offer. This is the kind of reasoning that turns case figures into an evaluation.

## Evaluating the approaches

### Employee approaches

* **Collective bargaining** gives staff more power than individual negotiation and costs little. It depends on the employer's willingness to negotiate.
* **Work-to-rule** keeps pay flowing while cutting the goodwill the business relies on, such as unpaid overtime. It works best where operations depend on extra effort; it has little effect where contracts already define every task.
* **Strike action** applies the strongest pressure but costs staff their pay and risks damaging the business they depend on. It works best when the product cannot be stored and staff are hard to replace.

### Employer approaches

* **Collective bargaining** keeps relations cooperative but can lead to higher costs.
* **Changes of contract** can force new terms quickly but may provoke strikes and lower morale.
* **Threats of redundancies** may win short-term agreement when unemployment is high, but damage trust and may drive away the best staff.
* **Lockouts** stop output entirely, so they only make sense if the business can survive a shutdown longer than staff can survive without pay.
* **Closure** ends the dispute permanently but destroys the business's capacity at that site, along with redundancy costs and reputational harm.

## Choosing a resolution method

| Method | Best when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Conciliation | Both sides still want a deal but talks have stalled | Depends on goodwill; no decision is imposed |
| Arbitration | A decision is needed and both sides will accept a neutral ruling | One or both sides may feel the ruling is unfair |
| Employee participation and industrial democracy | Conflict comes from staff feeling unheard | Slower decisions; managers share control |
| No-strike agreement | Continuity of output is vital to the employer | Staff give up their strongest pressure, so they need a fair alternative |
| Single-union agreement | Many unions make bargaining slow and complex | One union may not represent every group fairly |

Notice that the first two settle a dispute that has already happened, while the last three are mainly preventive: they change the relationship so that future disputes are less likely or less damaging.

### Annotated judgement

*Corriebank's talks have broken down and a strike is announced. Recommend how the dispute should be resolved.*

> Both sides still need each other: the company cannot easily replace 240 licensed drivers, and drivers lose 144 a day while on strike. **[uses bargaining power and case figures]** Conciliation suits this stage, because a neutral third party can help each side move from 6% and 2.5% towards a figure both accept, without imposing it. **[method matched to situation]** If conciliation fails, arbitration would end the dispute, though the losing side may resent the result. **[fallback with limitation]** To prevent repeat disputes, the company could offer a no-strike agreement in exchange for binding arbitration of future pay claims, which protects services while giving drivers a fair route. **[preventive method]** I recommend conciliation first, because both sides have reasons to settle. If public opinion turned strongly against the drivers, the company's position would strengthen and it might press for a settlement nearer its offer. **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

* Treating conflict as caused only by unions. Identify the clash of objectives.
* Calling a lockout an employee action, or a work-to-rule a strike.
* Confusing conciliation (helps the sides agree) with arbitration (a third party gives a ruling).
* Evaluating approaches without reference to bargaining power in the case.
* Ignoring the long-term effects of a dispute on trust and customers.

## Exam technique

* **Explain** a source of conflict (AO2): name it and show why it matters to each side.
* **Discuss** or **evaluate** an approach (AO3): give its likely effect on both sides, using case facts and numbers, then judge.
* **Recommend** a resolution: match the method to the stage of the dispute and the cause, and say what would change your view.
