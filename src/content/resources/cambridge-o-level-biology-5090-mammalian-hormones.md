## What is a hormone?

The definition has four parts, and each one can earn a mark.

**A hormone is a chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.**

- **Chemical substance**: a hormone is a molecule, not an electrical impulse.
- **Produced by a gland**: a gland is an organ that makes and releases a substance. The glands that release hormones straight into the blood are the **endocrine glands**.
- **Carried by the blood**: hormones are transported all around the body in the blood plasma.
- **Target organs**: a hormone reaches every part of the body, but only the target organs respond. A target organ has cells that can detect that particular hormone. The response is a change in the activity of the organ.

This explains why a hormone can have a very specific effect even though the blood carries it everywhere.

## The endocrine glands and their hormones

You need to identify the glands on a diagram of the body and give the hormone or hormones that each makes. The syllabus limits the list.

| Gland | Hormone(s) produced | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Adrenal glands | Adrenaline | One above each kidney |
| Pancreas | Insulin and glucagon | Below the stomach |
| Pituitary gland | Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH) | At the base of the brain |
| Testes | Testosterone | In the scrotum (male) |
| Ovaries | Oestrogen and progesterone | In the lower abdomen (female) |

On a diagram, the pituitary gland is a small structure hanging from the base of the brain. The adrenal glands are small caps on top of the kidneys, so do not confuse them with the kidneys themselves. The pancreas sits in the curve of the small intestine near the stomach. The testes and ovaries are the sex organs, and they make hormones as well as gametes.

### What the hormones do, in outline

This subtopic asks you to know which gland makes which hormone. Their detailed effects appear in other subtopics, so the outline below shows where each one connects.

- **Adrenaline** is covered below.
- **Insulin and glucagon** control blood glucose concentration (see blood glucose control).
- **FSH and LH** control the menstrual cycle together with oestrogen and progesterone (see sexual reproduction in humans).
- **Testosterone and oestrogen** control the development of secondary sexual characteristics at puberty.

## Adrenaline

Adrenaline is made by the **adrenal glands**. It is sometimes called the "fight or flight" hormone because it prepares the body for action. The syllabus asks for two effects.

1. It **increases the heart rate**, so blood is pumped faster to the muscles and more oxygen and glucose reach the cells that need them.
2. It **increases the blood glucose concentration**, by causing the liver to release glucose into the blood. More glucose is available for respiration, so muscles can release more energy.

Adrenaline also has other effects that are not required here, such as widening the pupils.

### Situations in which adrenaline is released

- Danger or fear, for example being chased by a dog.
- Excitement, for example before a race or during a sport.
- Stress, for example before an examination.
- Sudden shock, for example hearing a loud bang.

In each example, the body may need to run away or fight. More blood flow and more glucose in the blood help the muscles to respire faster and work harder.

### Worked example: explaining a sequence

*A cyclist hears a car horn very close behind. Her heart beats faster. Explain how this happens.*

1. Her brain detects the danger and signals the adrenal glands.
2. The adrenal glands release adrenaline into the blood.
3. The blood carries adrenaline to the heart, which is a target organ.
4. The heart beats faster, so more blood carries oxygen and glucose to the muscles.
5. Adrenaline also causes the liver to release glucose, increasing the blood glucose concentration, so the muscles can respire faster and release more energy.

Notice that the answer uses the language of the definition: gland, blood and target organ.

## How a hormone reaches its target

Follow the route from gland to response, because this is the pattern behind every hormone question.

1. A stimulus, such as danger or a change in blood glucose, is detected.
2. The gland receives a signal (a nerve impulse or a chemical change) and secretes the hormone into the blood.
3. The hormone is carried in the blood plasma to all parts of the body.
4. The target organ has cells that respond to that hormone.
5. The activity of the target organ changes. For example, the heart beats faster.
6. Later, the hormone is broken down and the response fades.

Because the route passes through the blood, it takes longer than the route in a reflex arc. This is the reason for the difference in speed between the two control systems.

### Worked example: interpreting data

*A student measured her pulse rate before and after being startled by a loud noise.*

| Time (minutes) | Pulse rate (beats per minute) |
|---|---|
| 0 (before the noise) | 68 |
| 1 | 96 |
| 2 | 92 |
| 4 | 84 |
| 6 | 74 |

1. The increase in the first minute is 96 - 68 = 28 beats per minute.
2. The percentage increase is 28 divided by 68 x 100 = 41.2 per cent (to one decimal place).
3. The pulse rate is still above the starting value after 4 minutes, at 84 beats per minute. This long recovery is typical of hormonal control, because adrenaline remains in the blood after the noise has stopped.

If the response were purely nervous, it would be expected to end much more quickly. The data show an effect that fades slowly, which supports adrenaline as the cause.

## Comparing nervous and hormonal control

The syllabus limits the comparison to **speed of action** and **duration of effect**.

| Feature | Nervous control | Hormonal control |
|---|---|---|
| Message carried by | Electrical impulses along neurones | Chemical hormones in the blood |
| Speed of action | Very fast | Slower |
| Duration of effect | Short-lived | Longer-lasting |
| Example | Withdrawing a hand from a hot object | Adrenaline raising the heart rate |

### Reasons for the differences

- Impulses travel along neurones in a fraction of a second. Hormones are carried by the blood, which circulates more slowly, and they must reach the target organ and act on it, so the response is slower.
- The effect of an impulse stops almost as soon as the impulse stops. Hormones stay in the blood until they are broken down or removed, so the effect lasts longer.

For this reason, nervous control suits rapid, short actions such as reflexes. Hormonal control suits changes that need to be maintained over minutes, hours or longer, such as controlling blood glucose and the changes at puberty.

### Worked example: choosing the correct system

*For each situation, state whether nervous or hormonal control is mainly responsible and give a reason.*

- A person blinks as a ball flies towards the eye. **Nervous**: the response is very fast and brief.
- The voice of a boy deepens over several months. **Hormonal**: the change is slow and lasts a long time (testosterone).
- The pupils get smaller as a light is switched on. **Nervous**: the response is rapid and automatic.
- A person's heart rate stays high for several minutes after a fright. **Hormonal**: adrenaline stays in the blood and has a longer-lasting effect.

When answering a question like this, always link the type of response to speed and duration. These are the two comparison points the syllabus names.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that hormones are carried by nerves. They are carried in the blood.
- Describing a hormone as an enzyme or a gland. It is the chemical, not the gland.
- Writing that a hormone affects every cell. Only the target organs respond.
- Mixing up the gland and its hormone, for example saying the pancreas produces adrenaline.
- Mixing up the adrenal glands with the kidneys.
- Saying that nerves are slower because the impulse is chemical. The impulse is electrical and fast; hormonal control is slower.
- Saying that adrenaline decreases blood glucose. It increases it.
- Leaving out "specific" in the definition.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the definition or the stages.
- **Identify**: name the gland on a diagram.
- **State**: give the hormone made by a gland.
- **Compare**: give similarities and differences, using both systems in the same sentence.
- **Explain**: give reasons, such as why the effect of a hormone lasts longer.
