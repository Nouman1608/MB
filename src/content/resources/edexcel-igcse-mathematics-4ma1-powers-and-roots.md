This study guide covers **section 1.4, Powers and roots**, in topic 1 (Numbers and the number system) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Statements 1.4 A to E are Foundation Tier content needed on both tiers. The Higher Tier adds surds, rationalising denominators, and fractional and negative powers, marked **(Higher tier only)** below.

Section 1.4 is also taught alongside integers in the [integers, powers and roots study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-integers-powers-and-roots/). This page works through section 1.4 on its own, with new examples and more emphasis on using prime factorisation as a tool. Afterwards, use the [section 1.4 revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-powers-and-roots-revision-notes/) and the [section 1.4 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-powers-and-roots-practice/).

## Squares, cubes and their roots (1.4 A, 1.4 B)

Know these without a calculator:

- **Squares:** 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225
- **Cubes:** 1, 8, 27, 64, 125 and 1000

1 and 64 are both square and cube numbers (64 = 8² = 4³). The square root sign √ means the **positive** root, so √49 = 7, although the equation x² = 49 has two solutions, 7 and −7. A cube root keeps the sign: ∛(−125) = −5, because (−5)³ = −125.

## Index notation and the index laws (1.4 C)

aⁿ means n factors of a. For the same base a:

- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
- aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
- (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
- a⁰ = 1, and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ

**Worked example 1.** Simplify (a) 5⁴ × 5⁻⁶ ÷ 5⁻³ and (b) (2³)⁻², and evaluate each.

```
(a) 5^(4 + (−6) − (−3)) = 5¹ = 5
(b) 2^(3 × −2) = 2⁻⁶ = 1/2⁶ = 1/64
```

Write the combined index in brackets, as above, so the subtraction of a negative is visible. The laws only work for the **same base**: 2³ × 3² cannot be combined into one power.

## Product of powers of prime factors (1.4 D)

Divide repeatedly by primes (a factor tree or a division ladder), then collect equal primes as powers, in ascending order. The specification's own example is 720 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5.

**Worked example 2.** Write 1848 as a product of powers of its prime factors.

```
1848 ÷ 2 = 924
 924 ÷ 2 = 462
 462 ÷ 2 = 231
 231 ÷ 3 = 77
  77 ÷ 7 = 11
1848 = 2³ × 3 × 7 × 11
```

Check by multiplying back: 8 × 3 × 7 × 11 = 1848.

**Prime factorisation as a tool.** A number is a perfect square when every prime power is even, and a perfect cube when every power is a multiple of 3. So 3136 = 2⁶ × 7² is a square, with √3136 = 2³ × 7 = 56, but not a cube. And 1728 = 2⁶ × 3³ is a cube, with ∛1728 = 2² × 3 = 12.

## HCF and LCM (1.4 E)

From the prime factorisations:

- **HCF:** multiply the primes common to both, each to the **lower** power.
- **LCM:** multiply every prime that appears, each to the **higher** power.

**Worked example 3.** Find the HCF and LCM of 1848 and 252.

```
1848 = 2³ × 3   × 7 × 11
 252 = 2² × 3²  × 7
HCF = 2² × 3 × 7        = 84
LCM = 2³ × 3² × 7 × 11  = 5544
```

A check: HCF × LCM = 84 × 5544 = 465 696 = 1848 × 252. This holds for any two numbers.

In word problems, "the largest possible equal size" points to the HCF; "the next time both happen together" or "the smallest number of each so the totals match" points to the LCM. For example, the largest square tile that exactly covers a 168 cm by 120 cm floor has side HCF(168, 120) = 24 cm.

## Surds (Higher tier only)

A **surd** is a root that cannot be written exactly as a fraction, such as √2 or √15. Leaving answers in surd form keeps them exact.

Rules: √a × √b = √(ab), √a ÷ √b = √(a/b), and √a × √a = a.

**Simplifying.** Find the largest square factor: √75 = √(25 × 3) = 5√3. Like surds add like terms: √48 + √27 = 4√3 + 3√3 = 7√3.

**Expanding.** Multiply every term by every term:

```
(2 + √3)² = 4 + 2√3 + 2√3 + 3 = 7 + 4√3
(√5 + 2)(√5 − 2) = 5 − 4 = 1
```

**Rationalising a denominator** means removing the surd from the bottom.

- Single surd: multiply top and bottom by that surd. 10/√5 = 10√5/5 = 2√5.
- Surd in a bracket: multiply by the bracket with the opposite sign.

**Worked example 4.** Rationalise 3/(√7 − 2).

```
3/(√7 − 2) × (√7 + 2)/(√7 + 2)
= 3(√7 + 2)/(7 − 4)
= 3(√7 + 2)/3
= √7 + 2
```

The middle terms cancel because (a − b)(a + b) = a² − b².

## Fractional and negative powers (Higher tier only)

- a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a, the nth root
- a^(m/n) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ: take the root first, then the power, to keep numbers small
- a^(−m/n) = 1/a^(m/n): a negative power means reciprocal

**Worked example 5.** Evaluate (a) 32^(3/5), (b) 81^(−1/4), (c) (9/4)^(−3/2).

```
(a) fifth root of 32 = 2;  2³ = 8
(b) 81^(1/4) = 3;  reciprocal: 1/3
(c) flip: (4/9)^(3/2);  square root: 2/3;  cube: 8/27
```

The same laws solve simple equations by matching bases: 8ˣ = 4 becomes 2^(3x) = 2², so 3x = 2 and x = 2/3.

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying the bases: 3⁴ × 3² is 3⁶, not 9⁶.
- Treating a negative power as a negative number: 2⁻³ = 1/8, not −8.
- Writing a⁰ = 0.
- Stopping a prime factorisation at a non-prime, such as 2² × 15.
- Using the higher powers for the HCF or the lower powers for the LCM.
- √9 + √16 = 7, not √25: roots do not add.
- Rationalising 3/(√7 − 2) by multiplying by √7 only.

## Exam technique

- **Write as a product of powers of its prime factors**: the answer must use index notation, such as 2³ × 3 × 7 × 11, not a list of primes.
- **Simplify** a surd: give the form k√n with n as small as possible; if the question says "in the form a + b√c", match it exactly.
- **Evaluate** (Higher): give an exact value; show the root and power steps, since a calculator answer alone does not show method.
- **Show that**: write every step, ending on the given result.
- Use a Venn diagram of prime factors if it helps you see the HCF (the overlap) and LCM (everything).
