This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.1 Cell structure** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered, so every candidate
meets the same content, and any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper.

The [Organisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation/) covers all of Topic 1 in one
place. This page stays with cells only and adds new worked examples: counting mitochondria, reading a scale bar,
and deciding what a cell does from the parts it contains. Afterwards, use the
[Organisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-organisation-revision-notes/) and the
[Cell structure practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-cell-structure-practice/).

## What 3.1.1 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | The parts of most animal cells (eukaryotic cells) and what each does |
| b | What plant cells often have in addition, including the cell wall of plant and algal cells |
| c | The structure of a bacterial cell (a prokaryotic cell), including plasmids |
| d | Cells may be specialised to carry out a particular function |

The specification adds that, when you are given suitable information, you should be able to **relate the structure
of different types of cell to their function** in a tissue, an organ or the whole organism. That is an application
skill, so you cannot rely on learning definitions alone.

## Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells

The specification uses two words you should be able to use correctly.

- **Eukaryotic cells** are the cells of animals and plants (and algae). Their genetic material is held in a
  nucleus.
- **Prokaryotic cells** are bacterial cells. Their genes are **not** in a distinct nucleus.

## The animal cell

Most animal cells have five parts. Learn the part and the job together, because questions are usually phrased as
"explain why this cell has many of..." rather than "name this part".

| Part | Job (as the specification words it) |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Controls the activities of the cell |
| Cytoplasm | Where most of the chemical reactions take place |
| Cell membrane | Controls the passage of substances into and out of the cell |
| Mitochondria | Where most energy is released in respiration |
| Ribosomes | Where protein synthesis occurs |

Two wording points. First, the membrane does not just "hold the cell together": it **controls what passes in and
out**. Second, respiration does not "make" energy in mitochondria. Energy is **released** (transferred) there.

## What plant cells add

In addition to all the parts above, plant cells **often** have:

- **chloroplasts**, which absorb light energy to make food;
- a **permanent vacuole** filled with cell sap.

**Plant and algal cells also have a cell wall made of cellulose**, which strengthens the cell.

The word "often" matters. A root cell below the soil has no light, so it has **no chloroplasts**, but it still has a
cell wall and a vacuole. Do not write that "all plant cells have chloroplasts".

A frequent mix-up: the cell wall and the cell membrane are different. The wall is made of cellulose, is strong and
lets water and dissolved substances pass through it, so it does not control what enters. The membrane does the
controlling.

## The bacterial cell

A bacterial cell consists of **cytoplasm and a membrane, surrounded by a cell wall**. Its genes are **not in a
distinct nucleus**. Some of the genes are located in **circular structures called plasmids**.

| Feature | Animal cell | Plant cell | Bacterial cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Eukaryotic | Eukaryotic | Prokaryotic |
| Nucleus | Yes | Yes | No distinct nucleus |
| Cell wall | No | Yes (cellulose) | Yes |
| Chloroplasts | No | Often | Not listed |
| Permanent vacuole | No | Yes | Not listed |
| Plasmids | No | No | Some genes are on plasmids |

For the exam, a bacterial cell is correctly described by: cytoplasm, membrane, cell wall, genes not in a distinct
nucleus, some genes in plasmids. Do not add a nucleus.

## Specialised cells: structure fits function

A cell is **specialised** when it has features that suit it to one job. The questions give you the features; you
must connect them to the job. Use the pattern: **feature, then what it allows, then why that helps the job**.

| Cell | Feature | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle cell | Many mitochondria | More respiration, so more energy is released for contraction |
| Root hair cell | A long, thin extension of the cell | Large surface area for taking in water and ions |
| Palisade cell | Many chloroplasts | Absorbs more light energy to make food |
| Sperm cell | Tail and many mitochondria | Tail for swimming, mitochondria for the energy to swim |
| Gland cell | Many ribosomes | Makes a lot of protein, such as enzymes, for release |

The sperm and gland cell lines apply the ribosome and mitochondrion statements to cells the specification does not
name. The skill is to reason from the part's job. If you know "ribosomes are where protein synthesis occurs", you
can say that a cell that makes a lot of enzyme must have many ribosomes.

### Worked example 1: comparing two cells by counting mitochondria

Invented data. A student counted mitochondria in five cells of each type on photographs.

| Cell | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heart muscle cell | 210 | 185 | 240 | 198 | 217 |
| Skin surface cell | 38 | 45 | 41 | 36 | 40 |

```
mean, heart muscle = (210 + 185 + 240 + 198 + 217) ÷ 5 = 1050 ÷ 5 = 210
mean, skin         = (38 + 45 + 41 + 36 + 40) ÷ 5 = 200 ÷ 5 = 40
ratio              = 210 ÷ 40 = 5.25, so about 5 times as many
```

Interpretation: the heart muscle cell has about five times as many mitochondria. The heart contracts continually,
so its cells need a large and steady release of energy from respiration. This links the count to the function.
The sample is only five cells of each type, so the means are indicative; counting more cells would make the
comparison more reliable.

### Worked example 2: deciding what an unfamiliar cell does

Invented stimulus. "Cell X is from the lining of a small tube. It has a nucleus, a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a
very large number of mitochondria. It has no cell wall. Its surface is folded into many tiny projections."

Step 1: no cell wall and no chloroplasts, so it is an animal cell.
Step 2: many mitochondria, so it needs a lot of energy from respiration. Cells that take in substances against a
concentration gradient (active transport, see 3.1.5) use energy, so this is a sensible suggestion.
Step 3: folded surface, so a large surface area for taking in substances.

Answer: cell X is probably an absorbing cell. Credit comes from linking each feature to a reason, not from naming
the cell exactly.

## Magnification and scale

The specification does not list a separate microscopy section in 3.1.1, but the subject content asks for
understanding of size and scale, and drawings and photographs of cells are common in questions.

```
magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object
real size = image size ÷ magnification
```

**Always convert to the same unit first.** 1 mm = 1000 micrometres (μm).

### Worked example 3: finding the magnification

A plant cell is drawn 36 mm long. Its real length is 0.06 mm.

```
magnification = 36 ÷ 0.06 = 600   (written ×600)
```

### Worked example 4: finding the real size

A photograph of a cell is 48 mm wide and the magnification is ×400.

```
real width = 48 ÷ 400 = 0.12 mm
0.12 mm × 1000 = 120 μm
```

### Worked example 5: using a scale bar

A bacterial photograph has a scale bar 20 mm long, labelled 5 μm. One bacterium measures 8 mm on the photograph.

```
scale bar:  20 mm = 20 × 1000 = 20 000 μm of image, standing for 5 μm real
magnification = 20 000 ÷ 5 = 4000   (×4000)
real length of bacterium = 8 mm ÷ 4000 = 0.002 mm = 2 μm
```

A shortcut check: 8 mm is 8/20 of the scale bar, and 8/20 × 5 μm = 2 μm. Both methods agree.

## Linking cell structure to the whole organism

The specification wants you to relate cell structure to the function of a tissue, an organ or the organism. A
useful way to practise is to write one sentence in the form: "The [tissue or organ] needs to [do job], so its cells
have [feature] to [reason]."

- Leaf: needs to absorb light for photosynthesis, so palisade cells have many chloroplasts to absorb light energy.
- Root: needs to absorb water and ions, so root hair cells have a large surface area.
- Muscle: needs to contract, so cells have many mitochondria for the energy released in respiration.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that mitochondria "produce" or "make" energy. Energy is released (transferred) in respiration.
- Saying the cell wall controls what enters the cell. That is the membrane's role.
- Saying all plant cells contain chloroplasts, or that plant cells have no mitochondria. Plant cells respire, so
  they have mitochondria.
- Giving a bacterial cell a nucleus, or forgetting that the genes may be in plasmids.
- Dividing the wrong way in magnification: it is image size divided by real size.
- Mixing mm and μm in one calculation.
- Describing a feature without the reason, such as "muscle cells have lots of mitochondria" without "for energy".

## Exam technique

- **State** or **name** questions need a single part or term. **Describe** needs features. **Explain** needs the
  feature and its reason, so link every part to its job.
- In a calculation, show the units conversion, the substitution and the answer with its unit.
- When given an unfamiliar cell, list what is present, what is absent, and what each feature suggests.
- Compare cells with paired points: "plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, whereas animal cells do not".
