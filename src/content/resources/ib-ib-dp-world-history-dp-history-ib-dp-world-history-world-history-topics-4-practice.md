Section A checks precise knowledge. Section B practises explaining, comparing and weighing change against continuity, and ends with two full essays, each to be written in about 45 minutes and marked with the five 15-mark markbands.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** ways in which printing helped spread Luther's ideas. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the main terms of the Peace of Augsburg (1555). **[2]**

**3.** Explain **two** aims of the Council of Trent. **[4]**

**4.** Identify **three** items transferred in the Columbian exchange and the direction in which each moved. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the Ming court ended Zheng He's voyages after 1433. **[3]**

**6.** Define the "price revolution" and give **one** cause. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how Akbar's religious policies strengthened Mughal rule. **[3]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why some German princes adopted Lutheranism. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the importance of silver for the Ming economy in the sixteenth century. **[4]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the responses of **two** states, each chosen from a different region, to the arrival of Christian missionaries. **[6]**

**11.** "The Scientific Revolution transformed European society before 1700." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view. **[8]**

**12.** To what extent did religious change transform **one** society between 1450 and 1700? **[15]**

**13.** Compare and contrast the effects of increased global contact on **two** societies, each chosen from a different region, between 1400 and 1700. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: pamphlets could be produced quickly and cheaply in large numbers (1); his German Bible translation spread widely (1); woodcut images reached people who could not read (1); printed sermons and hymns spread through towns and were read aloud (1). **[2]**

**2.** Each prince of the Empire could choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for his territory (1). Subjects who disagreed could emigrate, and Calvinism was not recognised (1). **[2]**

**3.** 2 marks for each developed aim: to define Catholic doctrine clearly against Protestant teaching, for example reaffirming the seven sacraments and the role of good works alongside faith (2); to reform abuses in the Church, such as poorly trained or absent clergy, by requiring bishops to reside in their dioceses and setting up seminaries (2); to strengthen papal leadership and discipline across the Catholic world (2). **[4]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each, with correct direction: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao or tobacco from the Americas to Afro-Eurasia (1); horses, cattle, pigs, wheat or sugar cane from Afro-Eurasia to the Americas (1); smallpox and measles from Afro-Eurasia to the Americas (1); enslaved Africans forcibly transported to the Americas (1). **[3]**

**5.** The voyages were very expensive and brought prestige rather than profit (1). Court officials opposed to the eunuch officials who sponsored them persuaded later emperors to stop, and the Ming faced more pressing threats on the northern frontier from the Mongols (1). Restrictions on private maritime trade reflected a view that the empire did not need foreign goods (1). **[3]**

**6.** A sustained rise in prices across Europe during the sixteenth century (1). Cause, 1 mark: population growth pushing up demand for food, or the inflow of American silver increasing the money supply (1). **[2]**

**7.** Abolishing the *jizya* on non-Muslims (1564) won the loyalty of the Hindu majority (1). Bringing Rajput princes into the nobility and marriage alliances bound powerful Hindu elites to the dynasty (1). Debates among religious scholars and the policy of *sulh-i kul* reduced conflict and placed the emperor above sectarian divisions (1). **[3]**

**8.** 2 marks for each developed reason: genuine religious conviction, as with Frederick the Wise's protection of Luther in Saxony (2); control of church property and appointments, since dissolving monasteries brought lands and revenue to the prince (2); greater independence from the Habsburg emperor and the papacy (2). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks: silver became the main medium for large-scale trade and for paying taxes (1); the Single Whip reforms converted taxes and labour services into silver payments (1); much of it came from Japan and the Americas through foreign traders and the Manila galleons, linking China to global trade (1); it stimulated commercial agriculture and crafts, such as silk and porcelain for export (1). **[4]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example Tokugawa Japan (Asia and Oceania) and the Kingdom of Kongo (Africa and the Middle East). Similarities (up to 2): rulers in both saw missionaries as connected with trade and foreign power (1); in both, conversion began among elites (1). Differences (up to 4): the Kongo king Afonso I adopted Christianity and promoted it as a state religion, while the Tokugawa banned it from 1614 and persecuted converts (1); Kongo used Portuguese links for trade and prestige, while Japan restricted European trade to the Dutch at Nagasaki (1); in Kongo Christianity became part of royal legitimacy, while in Japan it was seen as a threat to loyalty to the shogun (1); the outcome in Japan was exclusion, in Kongo a lasting local Christianity (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: Copernicus, Galileo and Newton changed educated understanding of the universe and of how knowledge should be tested (2); scientific societies such as the Royal Society (1660) spread experimental methods and new ideas among elites (2). Against: most people's lives, beliefs and work were unaffected before 1700 (2); Church authority continued to limit new ideas, as Galileo's trial in 1633 showed, and many scientists remained deeply religious (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *For example, the German lands.* Change: Luther (1517) and the end of papal authority in Protestant territories; vernacular Bible and catechisms; dissolution of monasteries; clerical marriage; princely control of churches after 1555; new schools; the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) as a long-term consequence of religious division. Continuity: social hierarchy; the defeat of the peasants in 1525; Catholicism in Bavaria and Habsburg lands; the survival of the Empire's institutions. A strong answer defines "transform", separates religious from social and political change, and judges the extent. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, the Aztec and Inca lands under Spanish rule (the Americas) and Ming China (Asia and Oceania).* The Americas: conquest, catastrophic population loss from disease, new crops and animals, forced labour, conversion. Ming China: silver imports and the commercial economy; new American crops; Jesuit contact at court; limited political change. Comparison: contact was catastrophic and transformative in the Americas, but in China it was absorbed into an existing system and mostly economic. A strong answer compares paragraph by paragraph and explains why the effects differed (power, disease, state strength). **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Mughal India in the same region as Ming China?**
Yes. Both are in Asia and Oceania, so pair one of them with a society from another region in comparative questions.

**Can I use the Columbian exchange in a religious change essay?**
Only if you link it directly to the question, for example through missions in the Americas.
