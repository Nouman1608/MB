These notes condense outcomes 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11) specification, Issue 2: the role of accounting as a need to record, analyse and communicate accounting information to users, and the purpose of accounting information as an aid to management. The full explanation is in the [role and purpose study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-role-and-purpose-of-accounting/), and exam-style questions are in the [role and purpose practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-role-and-purpose-of-accounting-practice/). Amounts are in dollars; businesses are invented.

## Core recall

- **Role (1.1.1)**: record → analyse → communicate, for users.
- **Purpose (1.1.2)**: an aid to management in planning, control and decision making.
- **Bookkeeping** = recording. **Accounting** = recording plus analysing and communicating.
- **Financial accounting**: statements of the whole business, mainly for external users, about past periods.
- **Management accounting**: detailed internal information to plan and decide.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Accounting information | Financial data recorded, analysed and presented for users |
| Internal user | Inside the business: owner, managers, employees |
| External user | Outside: lenders, suppliers, tax authority, customers, potential investors |
| Stewardship | Showing owners how their resources have been used |
| Planning | Setting targets and forecasts from past results |
| Control | Comparing actual results with plans; protecting assets |
| Decision making | Choosing between options using costs and revenues |
| Relevance | Information that helps the decision in hand |
| Reliability | Information free from material error and bias, checkable to documents |
| Comparability | Information prepared on the same basis over time and between businesses |
| Timeliness | Information available while it can still influence a decision |

## Users: question and figures

| User | Question | Figures |
|---|---|---|
| Owner | Is it worth my capital? | Profit, drawings, capital |
| Manager | Where to cut costs or grow sales? | Cost of sales, expenses, departmental profits |
| Lender | Will the loan be repaid? | Profit, cash, existing loans, assets as security |
| Supplier | Will invoices be paid? | Cash, current assets against current liabilities |
| Employee | Is my job secure? | Profit trend |
| Tax authority | How much tax? | Profit for the year |

Conflict example: an owner wants high profit for a lender but low taxable profit; concepts and IAS limit how figures can be shaped.

## Financial and management accounting compared

| | Financial | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Users | Mainly external | Internal |
| Scope | Whole business | Products, departments, projects |
| Time | Past | Past used to plan the future |
| Format | Standard, IAS terms | Flexible |

## Limits of accounting information

- Money measurement: skills, reputation and loyalty are not recorded.
- Historic: the past may not predict the future.
- Estimates: depreciation, allowances, inventory values.
- Historic cost: asset values may be out of date.
- Inflation distorts long-term comparisons.
- Totals can hide problems in one part of the business.
- Small businesses' statements may not be audited.

## Management uses with a figure

| Use | Example (invented figures) |
|---|---|
| Planning | Last year's sales of $180,000 grew 5%, so next year's target is $189,000 |
| Control | Wages for the month were $14,600 against a planned $12,000; the $2,600 overspend is investigated |
| Decision making | Hiring a van costs $7,200 a year; buying one for $24,000 with a 4-year life and $4,000 residual value costs $5,000 a year in depreciation, so buying raises profit by $2,200 a year |
| Safeguarding assets | An inventory count shows $900 less than the records, prompting a check for theft or recording errors |

## Building an evaluation

1. Make a point about the information.
2. Apply it to the user and the scenario.
3. Analyse the consequence.
4. Give a limitation or counter-point.
5. Reach a judgement in context, such as what else the user should ask for.

## Exam traps

- Treating bookkeeping as the whole of accounting.
- Listing users with no need attached.
- Claiming financial statements show what the business is worth.
- Decisions based on figures alone, with no non-financial factors.
- One-sided evaluation without a conclusion.
- Older terms (balance sheet, debtors, creditors) instead of IAS terms.

## Self-test

1. State the three parts of the role of accounting in 1.1.1.
2. Distinguish bookkeeping from accounting.
3. Give two differences between financial and management accounting.
4. Name two internal and two external users.
5. What would a trade supplier look at in the financial statements, and why?
6. State three ways accounting information helps management.
7. Explain why historic cost information may be less relevant to a lender.
8. Give two limitations of financial statements for a potential investor.
9. Explain one conflict between the interests of two users.
10. A manager finds wages were $2,600 above plan. Which management purpose does this illustrate?
11. Why should an evaluation include non-financial factors?
12. What does the command word "evaluate" require?

### Answers

1. Record, analyse and communicate accounting information to users.
2. Bookkeeping records transactions; accounting also analyses and communicates the results.
3. Any two: financial accounting is mainly for external users, management accounting for internal; financial covers the whole business, management covers parts; financial follows standard formats, management is flexible; financial looks at past periods, management uses them to plan.
4. Internal: owner, managers, employees (any two). External: lender, supplier, tax authority, customer, potential investor (any two).
5. Cash and current assets against current liabilities, to judge whether invoices will be paid on time.
6. Planning, control and decision making (also safeguarding assets).
7. Assets offered as security may be worth more or less now than their original cost, so the figures may not show what the lender could recover.
8. Any two: they report the past; they omit items that cannot be measured in money; they rely on estimates; they may be out of date by the time they are published.
9. For example, an owner wants a high profit to impress a lender but a low profit to reduce tax.
10. Control.
11. Figures do not capture factors such as staff morale, reliability of suppliers or the risk of losing a customer, which can change the decision.
12. Review the information, weigh strengths and weaknesses or alternatives, and reach a supported judgement in context.
