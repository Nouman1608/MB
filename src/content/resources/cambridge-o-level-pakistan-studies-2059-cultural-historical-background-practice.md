**What these questions cover.** Section 1 of the 2059 Paper 1 content, Key Questions 1 to 5: the religious thinkers of the 18th and 19th centuries, the decline of the Mughal Empire and British expansion, the War of Independence of 1857–58, Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, and Urdu and the regional languages. The syllabus sets Section B questions as a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation and a 14-mark evaluation, so the questions below are grouped in those three types.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the Jihad Movement led by Syed Ahmad Barelvi. **[4]**

**2.** Describe the Third Battle of Panipat of 1761. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the Battle of Plassey of 1757. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the Doctrine of Lapse. **[4]**

**5.** Describe the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College founded by Sir Syed Ahmad Khan. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why the East India Company was able to extend its control over the subcontinent between the 1750s and the 1850s. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why Sir Syed Ahmad Khan believed that Muslims needed a Western education. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why the British wanted to establish a "scientific" North-West Frontier. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why Shah Waliullah is regarded as an important religious reformer. **[7]**

**10.** Explain why there were disadvantages in choosing Urdu as the national language of Pakistan. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**11.** "The rise of the Marathas and the Sikhs was the most important reason for the decline of the Mughal Empire after 1707." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "The introduction of the greased cartridges was the most important cause of the War of Independence of 1857–58." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**13.** "Shah Waliullah did more than Syed Ahmad Barelvi or Haji Shariatullah to revive Islam in the subcontinent." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge answers earn 1 mark per relevant point or supporting detail, to a maximum of 4. The 7- and 14-mark answers are marked by levels of response (see the tables after the answers); the content listed is indicative, and other accurate, relevant points should be credited.

**1.** Syed Ahmad Barelvi wanted to restore the purity of Islam and to free the Muslims of the Punjab and the North-West Frontier from Sikh rule [1]. He set up a base among the Pathan tribes of the frontier and declared a jihad against the Sikhs in 1826 [1]. His followers, the Mujahideen, won early victories and captured Peshawar in 1830, but he lost the support of some local tribal leaders [1]. He was killed, with Shah Ismail Shaheed, at the Battle of Balakot in 1831, although the movement continued after his death [1]. **[4]**

**2.** The Marathas had expanded into the north of the subcontinent and threatened Delhi [1]. Shah Waliullah wrote to Ahmad Shah Abdali, the ruler of Afghanistan, asking him to defend the Muslims against the Marathas [1]. Abdali's army heavily defeated the Marathas at Panipat in 1761 [1]. He returned to Afghanistan afterwards rather than restoring strong Mughal rule, so the battle weakened the Marathas but left a power vacuum in the north [1]. **[4]**

**3.** The East India Company's army, led by Robert Clive, fought Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal [1]. Clive had secretly agreed with Mir Jafar, one of the Nawab's commanders, that he would not fight, and Mir Jafar was made Nawab after the battle [1]. The Company won easily [1] and gained control over the rich province of Bengal, which became the base for its later expansion [1]. **[4]**

**4.** It was a policy of Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856 [1]. If a ruler of a dependent state died without a natural male heir, the state "lapsed" to the Company [1]. Adopted heirs were not recognised, although adoption was an accepted custom in the subcontinent [1]. States such as Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur were annexed, which angered many rulers and was one of the long-term causes of the War of Independence [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Sir Syed founded it at Aligarh, opening as a school in 1875 [1]. It was modelled on the colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, with residential students [1]. It taught Western subjects such as science and English alongside Islamic studies [1]. It trained many of the future leaders of the Pakistan Movement and became Aligarh Muslim University in 1920 [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- After Aurangzeb, weak Mughal emperors could not control their provinces, so local rulers became independent and were easier to defeat or win over one at a time.
- Indian rulers were divided and fought one another; the Company made alliances with some against others.
- The Company's armies were well trained and disciplined, with modern weapons and artillery.
- Victory at Plassey (1757) and later control of the revenue of Bengal gave the Company the wealth to pay for armies.
- Subsidiary alliances made rulers pay for Company troops stationed in their states and accept British control of their foreign relations.
- The Doctrine of Lapse and annexations for "misgovernment", such as Awadh in 1856, added territory without war.
- The defeat of the Sikhs in the Anglo-Sikh wars and the annexation of the Punjab in 1849 removed the last major independent power in the north-west. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- After 1857 the British blamed the Muslims for the War, and Sir Syed believed Muslims had to rebuild trust with the British to recover their position.
- Government jobs required English and Western education; Muslims who refused it were falling behind Hindus in employment.
- Without modern education, Muslims could not take part in the political and administrative life of the subcontinent.
- He believed Western science was not in conflict with Islam, and that Muslims had to learn it to make progress.
- Through the Scientific Society and the Muhammadan Educational Conference he aimed to spread modern knowledge, including translations into Urdu.
- He wanted to overcome the opposition of some ulama to English education by showing it could be combined with Islamic teaching. **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The British feared Russian expansion into Central Asia and wanted to keep Russia away from India.
- They wanted Afghanistan to act as a buffer state between the British and Russian empires.
- The mountain passes, such as the Khyber Pass, were the traditional routes for invasions, so the British wanted to control or watch them.
- Raids by the tribes of the frontier into settled areas had to be stopped.
- A clear, defensible boundary would make the frontier cheaper and easier to defend; this led to the Durand Line agreed with the Amir of Afghanistan in 1893.
- To manage the tribes, the British combined military posts with allowances paid to tribal leaders, and created the North-West Frontier Province in 1901. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- He translated the Qur'an into Persian, the language of educated Muslims of the time, so more people could understand it directly.
- He called on Muslims to return to the teachings of the Qur'an and Hadith and to abandon practices that had been added to Islam.
- He tried to reduce divisions between the different schools of Islamic thought and promoted unity among Muslims.
- He wrote widely; his works, such as *Hujjat Allah al-Baligha*, set out his ideas for later generations.
- He sought political help for the Muslims, writing to Ahmad Shah Abdali against the Marathas.
- His sons and students, including Shah Abdul Aziz, continued his work, which influenced later reformers such as Syed Ahmad Barelvi. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Urdu was the first language of only a small proportion of Pakistan's population; most people spoke Bengali, Punjabi, Sindhi, Pushto, Balochi or other languages.
- In East Pakistan, where Bengali was the majority language, the decision caused resentment and the language movement; protesters were killed in Dhaka in February 1952.
- Speakers of regional languages felt their own cultures were being treated as less important.
- In Sindh, tension between Urdu speakers and Sindhi speakers led to language riots in 1972.
- English remained the language of much of government, the law and higher education, so Urdu did not fully replace it. **[7]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* the Marathas fought Aurangzeb for decades and later spread across central and northern India, collecting taxes from Mughal lands and threatening Delhi; the Sikhs under leaders such as Banda Bahadur rebelled in the Punjab and later, under Ranjit Singh, created a powerful Sikh kingdom in the north-west.
- *Disagree, other reasons:* Aurangzeb's long Deccan wars emptied the treasury and his religious policies alienated many subjects; his successors were weak, short-lived and fought wars of succession; the empire was too large to control and governors became independent; Nadir Shah's invasion of 1739 and the sack of Delhi, and the invasions of Ahmad Shah Abdali, weakened Mughal power and wealth; the East India Company expanded after Plassey and eventually controlled the emperor himself.
- *Judgement:* for example, the Marathas and Sikhs took advantage of Mughal weakness, but they could only rise because the emperors after 1707 had already lost control. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* in 1857 sepoys were issued cartridges for the new Enfield rifle that had to be bitten open, and it was rumoured they were greased with cow and pig fat, offending Hindus and Muslims; soldiers at Meerut who refused to use them were punished, and their comrades mutinied in May 1857 and marched on Delhi, which started the War.
- *Disagree, other reasons:* political causes such as the Doctrine of Lapse and the annexation of Awadh angered rulers and their followers; the British treatment of the Mughal emperor, and the plan to end the title after Bahadur Shah Zafar, offended Muslims; economic causes included heavy land revenue demands and the ruin of Indian crafts by British imports; social and religious causes included Christian missionary activity and reforms seen as interference with custom; Indian soldiers resented low pay and poor promotion compared with British soldiers and being sent overseas.
- *Judgement:* for example, the cartridges were the immediate trigger, but the long-term grievances explain why a mutiny at Meerut turned into a widespread war. **[14]**

**13.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Shah Waliullah:* translated the Qur'an into Persian; called for a return to the Qur'an and Hadith; worked for unity between schools of thought; his writings and his students, especially his sons, spread his ideas; his invitation to Ahmad Shah Abdali helped check the Marathas.
- *Syed Ahmad Barelvi:* combined religious revival with political action; organised the Mujahideen and led a jihad against Sikh rule; inspired followers who continued the movement after Balakot, though he failed to free the Punjab and lost tribal support.
- *Haji Shariatullah:* founded the Faraizi Movement in Bengal after returning from many years in Mecca; insisted on the obligatory duties of Islam and opposed practices borrowed from other religions; declared India Dar-ul-Harb, so Friday and Eid prayers were suspended; supported Muslim peasants against Hindu landlords and European indigo planters; his son Dudu Miyan organised the movement further.
- *Judgement:* for example, Shah Waliullah's ideas were the foundation the others built on, but Barelvi and Shariatullah took revival to ordinary Muslims in particular regions. **[14]**

## Levels of response used in these answers

These bands are Marlbridge's own summary of how Paper 1 explanation and evaluation answers are marked.

**Explanation (7) — Questions 6 to 10**

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 5–7 | Explains at least one reason with accurate supporting detail; more reasons explained, or one explained in depth, move the answer up the level |
| 2 | 2–4 | Identifies or describes relevant reasons without showing how they caused the outcome |
| 1 | 1 | Makes a general point with no specific knowledge |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing relevant |

**Extended judgement (14) — Questions 11 to 13**

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 13–14 | Explains both sides in depth and reaches a judgement supported by the evidence |
| 4 | 10–12 | Explains both sides of the argument |
| 3 | 7–9 | Explains one side only |
| 2 | 3–6 | Identifies or describes relevant factors |
| 1 | 1–2 | General comments without specific knowledge |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing relevant |

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing Syed Ahmad Barelvi (the Jihad Movement) with Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (the Aligarh Movement).
- Writing about the Faraizi Movement in a question on the Jihad Movement.
- In Question 4, saying that Awadh was annexed under the Doctrine of Lapse: it was annexed for alleged misgovernment.
- In Question 12, writing only about the cartridges: without an explained counter-argument the answer cannot go above Level 3.
- In Question 13, treating the three reformers separately without comparing their contributions.
