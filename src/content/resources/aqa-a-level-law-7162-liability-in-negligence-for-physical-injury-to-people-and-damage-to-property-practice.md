These questions test the section 3.3 row on negligence for physical injury and damage to property: duty of care, breach of the objective standard, factual causation and remoteness.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the "neighbour" principle and give its source. **[2]**

**2.** Explain when a court will apply the *Caparo* three-part test, with reference to *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* (2018). **[3]**

**3.** State the standard of care expected of a householder carrying out DIY repairs, with an authority. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the "but for" test, with reference to **one** case in which the claim failed. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between the decisions in *Hughes v Lord Advocate* (1963) and *Doughty v Turner Manufacturing* (1964). **[4]**

**6.** Explain the thin skull rule. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the courts decide the standard of care expected of a professional. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the probability of harm and the vulnerability of the claimant affect whether a defendant has breached a duty of care. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how an intervening act can break the chain of causation, using **one** case. **[4]**

**10.** Priti, aged 12, is playing football in a school playground at break. She kicks the ball hard towards a goal; it hits Gerry, a supervisor, in the eye and injures him. Advise Gerry whether Priti has breached a duty of care. **[6]**

**11.** Kamal, a driving instructor's pupil on his third lesson, loses control and mounts the pavement. He hits Lena, who suffers a broken leg, and damages the window of Mo's shop. Shards of glass from the window cause Mo's dog, which was asleep inside, to cut its paw, and Mo pays a vet. Advise Lena and Mo on Kamal's liability in negligence. **[8]**

**12.** Ffion, a roofer, leaves a ladder unsecured at a building site next to a park. A 9-year-old boy climbs it and falls, breaking his arm. When the ambulance arrives, a paramedic gives him the wrong dose of painkiller, causing a serious reaction. Advise whether Ffion is liable for the broken arm and for the reaction to the painkiller. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation [1]. It comes from Lord Atkin's speech in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) [1].

**2.** The *Caparo* test (foreseeability, proximity, fair, just and reasonable) is used in novel situations where no duty has been established [1]. *Robinson* (2018) held that where a duty already exists in precedent, such as between road users or for positive acts by police, the court simply applies it [1]. In a new situation, the courts develop the law incrementally by analogy with existing cases [1].

**3.** The standard of a reasonably competent amateur doing that kind of work [1], not that of a professional: *Wells v Cooper* (1958) [1].

**4.** The claimant must show that the harm would not have occurred but for the defendant's breach [1]. In *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC* (1969) a doctor negligently failed to examine a patient [1], but the patient would have died of arsenic poisoning even with treatment, so the breach was not a cause of death [1]. (Credit *McWilliams*.)

**5.** In *Hughes* a boy was burned when a paraffin lamp exploded in an unforeseeable way [1]; the defendants were liable because burns were a foreseeable type of harm, and the manner did not matter [1]. In *Doughty* a cover fell into molten metal and caused an unexpected eruption [1]; the defendants were not liable because the eruption was a different kind of risk from the foreseeable splash [1].

**6.** Once some harm to the claimant is foreseeable, the defendant is liable for the full extent of the harm [1], even if it is much greater because of the claimant's pre-existing condition (*Smith v Leech Brain*, 1962) [1].

**7.** A professional is judged against the standard of a reasonably competent member of that profession [1]. Under *Bolam* (1957) they are not in breach if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of professional opinion [1]. *Bolitho* (1997) adds that the body of opinion must be capable of withstanding logical analysis [1]. Knowledge is judged at the time of the act, so a risk unknown then is not a breach (*Roe v Minister of Health*, 1954) [1].

**8.** The court balances the risk against the precautions [1]. If the probability of harm is very low, a reasonable person may take no further precautions: in *Bolton v Stone* (1951) a cricket ball left the ground only rarely, so there was no breach [1]. If the defendant knows or should foresee that the claimant is vulnerable, greater care is required [1]: in *Paris v Stepney BC* (1951) goggles should have been given to a worker blind in one eye [1], and in *Haley v LEB* (1964) blind pedestrians were foreseeable, so a hammer was not an adequate guard over a hole [1].

**9.** A new intervening act by a third party may be so independent of the defendant's breach that it becomes the legal cause of the harm [1]. In *Knightley v Johns* (1982) the defendant caused a crash in a tunnel [1]. A police inspector negligently ordered an officer to ride the wrong way through the tunnel, and the officer was injured [1]. The inspector's negligence broke the chain, so the original driver was not liable for the officer's injury [1].

**10.** Priti owes a duty to people in the playground who might be hit, as injury is foreseeable [1]. As a child, she is judged against a reasonable 12-year-old, not an adult [1] (*Mullin v Richards*, 1998). In *Orchard v Lee* (2009) a child playing a game in the normal way was not in breach when he hit a supervisor [1]. Kicking a ball towards a goal during break is normal play for a child of her age [1]. Only if she kicked it in a way that a reasonable 12-year-old would see as dangerous, for example aiming at people [1], would there be breach. Conclusion: Gerry is unlikely to establish breach [1].

**11.** **Duty:** Kamal, as a road user, owes an established duty to pedestrians and owners of property near the road, so *Caparo* is unnecessary (*Robinson*) [1]. **Breach:** he is judged as a reasonably competent driver despite being a learner (*Nettleship v Weston*, 1971) [1]; a competent driver would not mount the pavement, so he is in breach [1]. **Lena:** but for the breach she would not have been hit [1]; a broken leg is a foreseeable type of harm, so it is not too remote [1]. **Mo's window:** property damage from a car mounting the pavement is foreseeable and caused by the breach [1]. **The dog:** physical damage to property inside the shop, including an animal, is within the foreseeable type of damage from smashing the window; the precise manner need not be foreseen (*Hughes*) [1], so the vet's fee is likely recoverable. Conclusion: Kamal is likely liable to both [1].

**12.** **Broken arm:** a roofer working next to a park owes a duty to people, including children, who might be injured by her equipment [1]. A reasonable roofer would secure a ladder where children are foreseeable, at little cost, so she is in breach [1]. But for the unsecured ladder he would not have fallen [1], and injury to a child meddling with equipment is a foreseeable type (*Jolley v Sutton LBC*, 2000) [1]. **The reaction:** the paramedic's negligent dose may be a new intervening act [1]; a serious independent error by a third party can break the chain (*Knightley v Johns*), so Ffion is likely liable only for the broken arm, with the paramedic's employer answerable for the reaction [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying *Caparo* to road accidents.
- Lowering the standard for learners.
- Treating any later event as breaking the chain, rather than asking whether it was independent and negligent.
- Forgetting to conclude on each claimant separately.
