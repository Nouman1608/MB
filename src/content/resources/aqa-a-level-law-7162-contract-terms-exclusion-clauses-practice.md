## Section A

**1.** Which case decided that a notice in a hotel bedroom was not incorporated because the contract had already been made at reception? A: Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel. B: Parker v South Eastern Railway. C: Chapelton v Barry UDC. D: L'Estrange v Graucob. **[1]**

**2.** Which Act controls an exclusion clause in a contract between a trader and a consumer? A: Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977. B: Misrepresentation Act 1967. C: Consumer Rights Act 2015. D: Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between an exclusion clause and a limitation clause. **[2]**

**4.** State the effect of section 2(1) of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977. **[2]**

**5.** State two factors relevant to whether an exclusion clause satisfies the requirement of reasonableness. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the rules on incorporation of an exclusion clause by notice, referring to two cases. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the rules of construction that courts apply to exclusion clauses. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how sections 31, 57 and 65 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 control exclusion clauses. **[5]**

**9.** *Greenfield Farms Ltd buys irrigation pumps from Hydra Ltd on Hydra's written standard terms. A clause limits Hydra's liability for any defect to replacing the pump. The pumps fail, and Greenfield's crop worth £80,000 is lost. Hydra is a large company that could easily have insured; Greenfield could not buy pumps elsewhere without accepting a similar clause.* Suggest whether Hydra can rely on the clause. **[5]**

**10.** *Lena parks at a multi-storey car park operated by Parkwise Ltd. A sign at the entrance barrier says "Terms apply". The ticket from the machine says "See conditions displayed inside". A notice on the third floor excludes liability for "damage to vehicles or injury to persons, however caused". An attendant negligently reverses a tow truck into Lena, injuring her and damaging her car.* Advise Lena whether Parkwise can rely on the exclusion. **[8]**

**11.** *Sam, a self-employed photographer, takes his camera to Lensworks Ltd for repair, as he has done twice before over four years, each time signing a different form. This time he is given an unsigned job card that says, on the back, "Lensworks accepts no responsibility for loss of or damage to customers' equipment". A technician drops the camera and breaks it.* Consider whether Lensworks can rely on the clause. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss how effectively the common law and statute control the use of exclusion clauses. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A: Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel. **[1]**

**2.** C: Consumer Rights Act 2015. **[1]**

**3.** An exclusion clause attempts to **remove liability completely** for a breach or loss [1]. A limitation clause **caps liability** at a stated amount or measure, such as the price paid [1].

**4.** A person **cannot exclude or restrict liability** [1] for **death or personal injury resulting from negligence**, whether by a contract term or a notice [1].

**5.** Any two of: the **relative bargaining strength** of the parties [1]; whether the customer was offered an **alternative** without the clause; whether the customer **knew or ought to have known** of the term; for limitation clauses, the **resources** of the party relying on it and whether it **could have insured** [1].

**6.** A clause in an unsigned document is incorporated only if **reasonable notice** is given **before or at the time** of contracting [1]; in **Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel (1949)** a notice in the bedroom came too late, as the contract was made at reception [1]. The notice must be in a **document a reasonable person would expect to contain terms**; in **Chapelton v Barry UDC (1940)** a deckchair ticket was a mere receipt [1]. The more **onerous or unusual** the clause, the more prominent the notice must be (Spurling v Bradshaw, 1956; Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, 1971) [1].

**7.** Under the **contra proferentem** rule, an ambiguous clause is interpreted **against the party relying on it** [1], as in **Houghton v Trafalgar Insurance (1954)**, where "load" did not include passengers [1]. **Clear words** are needed to exclude liability for **negligence** (Canada Steamship Lines v The King, 1952) [1]. Whether a clause covers even a very serious breach depends on the **construction of the whole contract**, as in Photo Production v Securicor (1980) [1].

**8.** **Section 31**: a term is not binding on the consumer to the extent that it would exclude or restrict liability under the **goods terms, including ss9, 10 and 11** [1]. **Section 57**: a term cannot exclude liability under **s49** (reasonable care and skill) [1], and a term restricting liability under s49 or s52 is not binding if it would prevent the consumer **recovering the price paid** [1]. **Section 65**: a trader cannot exclude or restrict liability for **death or personal injury resulting from negligence** by a term or a consumer notice [1], and a person is not taken to have voluntarily accepted the risk merely because they **knew of or agreed to** the term [1].

**9.** Both parties are businesses, so **UCTA 1977** applies, not the CRA [1]. Greenfield dealt on Hydra's **written standard terms**, so **s3** applies and the clause is valid only if **reasonable** [1]. Under **s11**, reasonableness is judged at the time of contracting and **Hydra must prove it** [1]. Factors against Hydra: it could **easily insure**, the loss far exceeds the remedy offered, and Greenfield had **no real alternative** [1]. As in **George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds (1983)**, the clause is likely to be unreasonable, so Hydra cannot rely on it [1].

**10.** Lena is a consumer and Parkwise a trader, so the **CRA 2015** applies [1]. Incorporation: the contract was probably made at the **entrance barrier** [1]; as in **Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking (1971)**, terms on a ticket from a machine, or a notice inside, come **too late** [1]. "Terms apply" at the barrier may not be **reasonable notice** of a wide, onerous clause, which needs prominent notice (Spurling v Bradshaw) [1]. Construction: "however caused" is likely wide enough to cover negligence (Canada Steamship) [1]. Even if incorporated, liability for Lena's **personal injury from negligence cannot be excluded** under **s65** [1]. Damage to the car falls under the **fairness test**; a clause excluding all liability for negligent damage is likely to cause a significant imbalance and not bind her [1]. Conclusion: Parkwise cannot rely on the clause for either loss [1].

**11.** Sam is acting for his business, so he is not a consumer; **UCTA 1977** applies [1]. There is no signature, so incorporation depends on **notice**; terms on the back of a job card given at the time of the contract may be enough only if a reasonable person would expect it to be contractual (Chapelton) [1]. **Course of dealing** is unlikely: two previous dealings over four years on **different forms** are not regular and consistent (**Hollier v Rambler Motors, 1972**; McCutcheon v MacBrayne, 1964) [1]. If incorporated, the clause must be construed; it does not clearly mention negligence, so it may be read **against Lensworks** [1]. Under **s2(2)**, exclusion of negligence liability for property damage is valid only if **reasonable** [1]. Conclusion: it is likely Lensworks cannot rely on the clause, though a reasonable, prominently notified clause between businesses could stand [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The **incorporation rules** protect parties who had no fair chance to see a clause, such as Olley and Thornton [1].
- But **L'Estrange v Graucob** binds signatories to unread small print, which favours certainty over fairness [1].
- The **red hand** principle and Interfoto require more notice for onerous terms, a flexible protection [1].
- **Construction** rules such as contra proferentem are useful but can be avoided by careful drafting, as in Photo Production v Securicor [1].
- **UCTA s2(1)** and **CRA s65** give absolute protection against exclusion of liability for negligent death or injury [1].
- **Reasonableness** under s11 respects freedom of contract between equal businesses (Watford v Sanderson) while protecting weaker parties (George Mitchell) [1].
- The **CRA** gives consumers stronger rules: ss31 and 57 make key terms unexcludable and unfair terms do not bind [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that control is now effective, especially for consumers, though case-by-case reasonableness creates uncertainty for businesses [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Going straight to statute without discussing incorporation.
- Using UCTA for a consumer or the CRA for a business customer.
- Assessing reasonableness with hindsight rather than at the time of contracting.
- Treating a self-employed person buying for work as a consumer.
- Failing to reach a conclusion on whether the defendant can rely on the clause.
