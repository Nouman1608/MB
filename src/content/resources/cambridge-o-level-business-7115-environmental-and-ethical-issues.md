This guide goes deeper into section **6.2 Environmental and ethical issues** of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus heading is "environmental concerns and ethical issues as both opportunities and constraints for businesses", and it lists:

- how business activity can impact on the environment, for example global warming
- the concept of externalities: possible external costs and external benefits of business decisions
- sustainable development, and how business activity can contribute to it
- how and why business might respond to environmental pressures and opportunities, for example pressure groups
- the role of legal controls over business activity affecting the environment, for example pollution controls
- ethical issues a business might face: conflicts between profits and ethics
- how business might react and respond to ethical issues, for example child labour and paying fair prices to suppliers.

The combined [environmental, ethical and international guide](/resources/o-level-business-environmental-and-ethical-issues-and-business-and-the-international-economy/) defines each idea. This page focuses on the skill examiners reward most in this section: weighing the **opportunity** against the **constraint** for a particular business. ## Opportunity or constraint?

The same issue can be both, depending on the business and how it responds.

| Issue | As a constraint | As an opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Customers worry about plastic waste | Packaging must be redesigned, which costs money | A business that switches first can attract those customers and charge a higher price |
| Stricter pollution limits | New equipment raises costs; some methods are banned | Firms that make cleaner equipment gain new orders |
| Pressure for fair prices to suppliers | Higher input costs cut profit margins | A trusted ethical brand can win loyal customers |
| Rising fuel use concerns | Transport and energy bills under pressure | Saving energy cuts costs in the long run |

A good answer says which side is stronger **for this business**, for example a small firm with little cash may find the constraint heavier, while a large firm with a strong brand may gain more from the opportunity.

## How business activity affects the environment

Business activity uses resources and produces waste. Main effects:

- **Global warming**: burning fossil fuels in factories, power stations, vehicles and aircraft releases gases that trap heat in the atmosphere.
- **Pollution** of air, water and land, including noise.
- **Using up non-renewable resources** such as oil, coal and metal ores.
- **Damage to habitats**, for example clearing forests for farmland or mining.

These effects also come back to business. Extreme weather can damage crops and disrupt supply chains, and scarce resources become more expensive.

## Externalities: tracing who bears the cost

An externality is a cost or benefit of a business decision that falls on **third parties**, people outside the business and its customers. The method for any case is to ask three questions:

1. What does the decision cost or gain the **business itself**? (private costs and benefits)
2. Who **else** is affected?
3. Are those effects harmful (external costs) or helpful (external benefits)?

**Example: Kalden Cement expands its works near a village.**

| Effect | Who bears it | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of new kilns and wages | Kalden | Private cost |
| Extra sales of cement | Kalden | Private benefit |
| Dust on homes and crops | Villagers and farmers | External cost |
| More lorries, congestion and noise | Road users and residents | External cost |
| New jobs for local people | Local community | External benefit |
| More trade for local shops and cafes | Local businesses | External benefit |

Businesses tend to ignore external costs because they do not pay them. That is why pressure groups and legal controls matter: they push external costs back onto the business.

## Sustainable development

Sustainable development means meeting the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Businesses can contribute across their activities:

- **Operations**: renewable energy, efficient machinery, less waste, recycling scrap.
- **Purchasing**: materials from managed sources, such as timber from replanted forests.
- **Product design**: products that last longer, can be repaired, or use less packaging.
- **Transport**: fuller lorries, shorter routes, sourcing locally.

Many of these cut costs as well as harm, which makes them easier to justify to owners.

## Responding to environmental pressures and opportunities

### Why a business might respond

- To avoid lost sales and damage to its reputation.
- To meet demand from customers who prefer greener products.
- To comply with the law and avoid fines.
- To cut costs through using less energy and materials.
- To attract staff and investors who care about the issue.

### Pressure groups

A pressure group is an organisation that tries to influence businesses or governments without standing for election. It may use publicity, petitions, protests, social media campaigns and calls for consumer boycotts. Whether a campaign changes a business's behaviour depends on:

- how much the business depends on its public image (a consumer brand is more exposed than a firm selling to other businesses)
- how much support the group gains and how much publicity it attracts
- how costly the change is compared with the likely loss of sales
- whether the law already requires the change.

### Why a business might not respond

Changes can be expensive, may raise prices, and a small business may not be able to afford them. If customers will not pay more, an early mover may lose sales to cheaper rivals.

## The role of legal controls

Governments use laws because voluntary action may not happen when it costs money. Legal controls over activity affecting the environment include limits on pollution, rules on safe disposal of waste, and requirements to obtain permission for activities that may harm the environment. Businesses that break them may be fined or ordered to stop. The effects on business:

- higher costs for equipment, monitoring and staff training
- the same rules for every firm, so a careful firm is not undercut by a careless one
- pressure to find cleaner methods, which can create new products.

Describe the type of control and its effect; do not invent the names of particular laws.

## Ethical issues: profit versus ethics

An ethical issue is a question of whether a business decision is morally right. Conflicts arise when the most profitable choice is not the fair one. The syllabus gives two examples:

- **Child labour.** A supplier using children may be cheapest, but it harms the children's education and health.
- **Paying fair prices to suppliers.** Pushing prices down raises margins but can leave small farmers unable to earn a decent living.

Other examples include honest advertising and safe working conditions.

### How a business might react

- Check suppliers regularly and require them to meet a written code of conduct.
- End contracts with suppliers that use child labour, or work with them to remove it and help children into school.
- Pay a guaranteed minimum price, or buy through schemes that certify fair prices.
- Tell customers what it is doing, which can support a higher price.

Each response costs money or time, so the business must weigh the cost against risks to its reputation and the benefits of customer loyalty.

## Worked example: an annotated "consider" answer

**Case.** Larkspur Clothing learns that a factory supplying 30% of its shirts employs children. Ending the contract quickly would leave it short of stock for its busiest season.

**Consider whether Larkspur should immediately stop buying from this supplier. Justify your answer.**

*Model answer, with notes.*

Stopping immediately would show customers that Larkspur does not accept child labour, protecting its reputation if a pressure group publicises the case. *(Reason for, linked to reputation.)*

However, losing 30% of its shirts before the busy season would cut sales, and the children might simply move to another factory with worse conditions. *(Reason against, using the case figure; also the effect on the children.)*

An alternative is to give the supplier a short deadline to remove child labour, check it independently, and find a second supplier in the meantime. *(A realistic middle option.)*

Overall Larkspur should not keep buying unchanged, but an immediate stop is less effective than a firm deadline backed by checks, unless the supplier refuses, in which case it should end the contract despite the lost sales. *(Judgement with a condition.)*

## Exam technique for the 7115 command words

- **Define / State**: one precise sentence (for example, an external cost is a cost borne by third parties).
- **Identify**: name the effect or stakeholder from the case.
- **Explain**: cause, who is affected, and the effect on the business.
- **Consider**: both the opportunity and the constraint, then a justified judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a cost the business pays an external cost.
- Treating every environmental issue only as a cost, ignoring opportunities.
- Saying a business "should be ethical" without saying how, or what it costs.
- Describing a pressure group without explaining why the business might give in.
- Assuming all legal controls only harm businesses.
