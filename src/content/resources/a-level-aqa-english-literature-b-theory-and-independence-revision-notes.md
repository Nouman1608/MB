Component 3 is worth 20% of the A-level and is the only coursework
element of English Literature B — it is marked by your own centre and
moderated by AQA, not sat as a timed exam. These notes condense what
the specification requires; confirm exact internal deadlines and text
choices with your teacher.

## What you actually submit

Two essays, **1,250–1,500 words each**, on **two different texts**:

- One text must be **poetry**, the other must be **prose**.
- Each text must be linked to a **different section** of the AQA
Critical anthology.
- One response is a **conventional essay**; the second can be
**re-creative** — or you can submit two conventional essays instead.

There is no exam-hall time pressure here, but there is a strict word
count and a strict requirement that both texts draw on *different*
critical lenses — a common early-planning mistake is picking two texts
that would naturally support the same critical anthology section.

## The Critical anthology sections

The anthology offers accessible extracts on six critical methods:

| Critical lens | What it foregrounds |
|---|---|
| Narrative theory | How the story is structured and told |
| Feminist theory | Gender, power and voice |
| Marxist theory | Class, economics and social structure |
| Eco-critical theory | Nature, environment and the non-human |
| Post-colonial theory | Empire, race and cultural power |
| Literary value and the canon | Why some texts are judged "great" |

Pick your two texts with the critical lens in mind *first* — a text
that offers rich material for, say, post-colonial reading is a much
safer choice than a text you simply enjoy but which resists the
critical framework you'd be applying to it.

## Conventional essay: what it looks like

A conventional response is a debate-style essay that uses the Critical
anthology to explore potential meanings in the text. The specification's
own example: using post-colonial ideas to interrogate whether *A
Passage to India* makes it "impossible to sympathise with any of the
English characters." The task format is argumentative — state a
position, then use anthology-informed ideas as evidence and counter-evidence.

## Re-creative essay: what it looks like

A re-creative response rewrites or extends part of the base text
(a new narrative voice, a missing scene, journal entries from a
marginalised character) as a way of **offering a critical reading**
of that text — not as free creative writing for its own sake. The
specification's own example: journal entries from Miss Kenton in
*The Remains of the Day*, informed by feminist and/or Marxist ideas.

Two things make a re-creative piece succeed at A-level:

1. **Purposeful narrative-gap work** — filling in an "absence" or
silence in the base text (an unheard character's perspective, an
unshown scene) to expose something the original text leaves implicit.
2. **The accompanying commentary** — this is not optional decoration.
It must explicitly connect your creative choices to the base text and
to the specific Critical anthology section you used, explaining *why*
you made each significant choice and what critical reading it produces.

Marginalised or minor characters in the base text usually make far
more interesting re-creative narrators than already-central ones,
because their absence from the original narrative is itself the
"gap" your re-creative piece can exploit.

## Text-selection rules

- One prose text, one poetry text (a single-author poetry collection
or one longer narrative poem both count).
- If using a poetry collection or short-story collection, you must
have studied the **whole** text, then select **at least two** poems
or stories to analyse in detail as representative examples — unless
your piece is re-creative, in which case a single poem or story from
the collection can be the sole focus.
- One of your two texts may be a **text in translation**, provided
it is a translation "recognised by academia" as high quality; it is
then treated as the original author's own words for assessment.
- **Exam set texts are banned outright** — you cannot use a text that
appears anywhere on the A-level exam paper set-text lists, even if
your own centre studies a different exam text and would never examine
you on it.

## The five assessment objectives, weighted differently here

Component 3 is marked against the same AO1–AO5 framework as the exam
papers, but at exactly **half the per-component weighting** of each
exam paper (Paper 1 and Paper 2 are 40% each; Component 3 is 20%):

| AO | Focus | Approx. overall weighting |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Informed, accurate, well-expressed personal response | 28% |
| AO2 | How meanings are shaped in texts | 24% |
| AO3 | Significance of context | 24% |
| AO4 | Connections across texts | 12% |
| AO5 | Different interpretations | 12% |

AO4 (connections across texts) is easy to under-serve in Component 3
because your two essays sit on different texts and different critical
lenses — but the specification still expects each essay to gesture
toward the wider network of texts and contexts the studied work sits
within, not treat the text as read in total isolation.

## Exam traps

- Choosing two texts that both naturally invite the same critical
lens, defeating the "different section of the anthology" requirement.
- Treating the re-creative commentary as an afterthought rather than
the place where the critical reading is actually demonstrated.
- Forgetting that exam set-list texts are prohibited for NEA, even
texts your own centre doesn't teach for the exam component.
- Under-studying a poetry or short-story collection — the whole text
must be studied even though only two items are analysed in detail.
- Missing the 1,250–1,500-word band on either essay, which can incur
a penalty independent of content quality.

## Self-test

1. What two structural conditions must your two chosen texts satisfy?
2. Name three of the six Critical anthology sections.
3. What is the actual purpose of a re-creative response, beyond
creative writing?
4. Why does the accompanying commentary matter as much as the
re-creative piece itself?
5. Which texts are banned from NEA regardless of your centre's own
exam text choices?

**Answers:** 1. One text must be prose and the other poetry, and each
must be linked to a different section of the Critical anthology.
2. Any three of: narrative theory, feminist theory, Marxist theory,
eco-critical theory, post-colonial theory, literary value and the
canon. 3. To offer a critical reading of the base text informed by the
Critical anthology — not creative writing for its own sake.
4. Because it is where you explicitly connect your creative choices to
the base text and the critical lens, demonstrating the critical
understanding that the re-creative piece alone cannot show on its own.
5. Any text that appears on the A-level exam paper set-text lists,
even if it is not the specific text your own centre studies for the
exam.
