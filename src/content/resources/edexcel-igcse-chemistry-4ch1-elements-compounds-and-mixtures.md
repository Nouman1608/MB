## Classifying substances (1.8)

Ask two questions in order.

1. **How many different kinds of particle are there?** If more than one, it is a mixture.
2. **If there is only one kind, does each particle contain one element or more than one?** One element: an element. More than one element chemically bonded: a compound.

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Element | A substance made of only one type of atom | Iron, Fe; oxygen, O₂; sulfur, S |
| Compound | Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions | Water, H₂O; sodium chloride, NaCl |
| Mixture | Two or more substances not chemically combined | Air, sea water, crude oil |

An element can be made of molecules: O₂ contains two atoms but only one type, so it is an element. A compound has properties different from the elements it contains and can only be separated into them by a chemical reaction. The parts of a mixture keep their own properties and can be separated by physical methods.

**Worked example 1.** Classify each sample from its description.

```
Sample 1: identical particles, each made of two linked chlorine atoms
          -> one substance, one element -> element (Cl2)
Sample 2: identical particles, each one carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms
          -> one substance, two elements bonded -> compound (CO2)
Sample 3: some separate argon atoms and some N2 molecules
          -> two substances not bonded to each other -> mixture
Sample 4: Cl2 molecules mixed with H2 molecules
          -> two elements, not combined -> mixture
```

Sample 4 is the trap: every particle is an element, but there are two different elements, so it is a mixture of elements.

## Purity and melting points (1.9)

In chemistry, a **pure substance** contains only one element or one compound. A pure substance melts and boils at fixed temperatures. A mixture usually melts or boils over a range of temperatures, because the different substances do not all change state together. An impurity generally lowers the melting point and widens the range, which is why melting point is a quick purity check.

**Worked example 2.** A pure compound K melts at 136 °C. A student makes two samples and tests them.

| Sample | Melting behaviour |
|---|---|
| 1 | Melts sharply at 136 °C |
| 2 | Melts gradually from 128 °C to 133 °C |

```
Sample 1: sharp, at the known value -> pure K
Sample 2: melts over a range, below 136 °C -> impure
```

To compare with a known value, the substance must be identified independently; a sharp melting point at the wrong temperature means a different pure substance.

This idea is used in everyday "pure" claims. Bottled water labelled "pure" is not chemically pure if it contains dissolved minerals; it would boil slightly above 100 °C at normal pressure.

## Choosing a separation method (1.10)

Match the method to the property that differs between the substances.

| Separate... | Property that differs | Method |
|---|---|---|
| An insoluble solid from a liquid | Solubility | Filtration |
| A dissolved solid from its solution, as crystals | Solvent evaporates; solid does not | Crystallisation |
| A solvent from a solution | Boiling point (solvent boils, solute does not) | Simple distillation |
| Two or more liquids that mix completely | Boiling points that are close | Fractional distillation |
| Coloured substances dissolved together | How strongly each is attracted to the paper and the solvent | Paper chromatography |

### Details that earn method marks

- **Filtration:** the solid left in the filter paper is the **residue**; the liquid that passes through is the **filtrate**.
- **Crystallisation:** heat the solution to evaporate some of the water until it is near saturated (a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals). Leave it to cool so crystals form. Filter them off, wash with a little cold water and dry between filter papers or in a warm place. Do not evaporate to dryness: some salts decompose or lose water of crystallisation, and soluble impurities would stay in the solid.
- **Simple distillation:** heat the solution in a flask; the solvent boils, the vapour passes into a water-cooled condenser and condenses, and the liquid (distillate) collects in a beaker. The thermometer bulb sits level with the side arm, so it reads the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask. Cold water enters the condenser at the bottom so that the condenser fills completely.
- **Fractional distillation:** a fractionating column is fitted between the flask and the condenser. Vapour condenses and re-boils many times on the surfaces in the column, so the liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top first. Collect each fraction while the thermometer shows a steady temperature; change the receiver when the temperature rises.

**Worked example 3.** A student has a mixture of powdered chalk (insoluble in water) and potassium chloride (soluble). Describe how to obtain dry chalk and dry crystals of potassium chloride.

```
1 Add water and stir: potassium chloride dissolves, chalk does not.
2 Filter: chalk is the residue; potassium chloride solution is the filtrate.
3 Wash the residue with distilled water and leave it to dry (chalk).
4 Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water until crystals form on a cold glass rod.
5 Leave to cool and crystallise; filter off the crystals and dry them.
```

## What a chromatogram tells you (1.11, 1.12)

Spots are placed on a pencil baseline and a solvent moves up the paper. Each dissolved substance moves at its own rate, so a mixture separates into spots.

- **Number of spots:** the minimum number of substances present. Two substances might happen to travel together.
- **One spot:** consistent with a pure substance in that solvent.
- **Same height as a reference spot (same Rf, same solvent):** probably the same substance.
- **A spot left on the baseline:** that substance is insoluble in this solvent.

```
Rf = distance moved by the spot / distance moved by the solvent front
```

Both distances are measured **from the baseline**. Rf has no units and is always less than 1. Rf values are only comparable when the solvent and paper are the same.

**Worked example 4.** The solvent front is 9.0 cm above the baseline. A food colouring F gives two spots, centred 2.7 cm and 6.3 cm above the baseline. In the same solvent, dye L has Rf 0.30, dye M 0.55 and dye N 0.70.

```
spot 1: Rf = 2.7 / 9.0 = 0.30  -> L
spot 2: Rf = 6.3 / 9.0 = 0.70  -> N
F contains L and N, and not M
```

A student who measures from the bottom edge of the paper instead of the baseline gets every Rf too large.

## The chromatography practical (1.13)

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Draw the baseline in pencil | Pencil (graphite) does not dissolve and move; ink would |
| Put small, concentrated spots on the baseline | Small spots give clear, separate spots |
| Solvent level below the baseline | Otherwise the spots dissolve into the solvent in the beaker |
| Cover the container | The air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour, so the solvent does not evaporate from the paper |
| Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top; mark the front at once | The front is needed to calculate Rf and fades as the paper dries |

If every dye stays near the baseline, the solvent is unsuitable; repeat with a different solvent, such as ethanol instead of water.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a mixture of two elements a compound.
- Saying a mixture has "no melting point". It melts over a range.
- Naming "evaporation" when crystals are asked for: the method is crystallisation.
- Using fractional distillation for a salt solution, or simple distillation for two liquids with close boiling points.
- Measuring Rf distances from the paper's edge, or giving Rf greater than 1.
- Writing that the number of spots is "the number of substances", rather than the minimum number.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a separation: give the steps in order, naming the apparatus and what is collected.
- **Explain** a step (for example, the pencil baseline or the lid): give the reason linked to the result.
- **Calculate** Rf: show both distances, divide, give 2 decimal places or as the question asks, no units.
- **Deduce** from a chromatogram: compare heights or Rf values in the same solvent and state your conclusion.
