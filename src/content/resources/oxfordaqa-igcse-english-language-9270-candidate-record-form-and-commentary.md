**This guide covers one named part of the non-exam assessment in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Language (9270).** The specification says students "will complete a Candidate record form in which
they will give details of their research resources and processes and write a commentary on their
completed assignment", and that "completion of the Candidate record form by the student is an
important feature of the assignment and will be considered in the assessment". The
[NEA study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-non-exam-assessment-study-guide/)
covers the whole project, from choosing a focus to proofreading; this page explains each requirement
of the form in detail and shows how to write a commentary that earns credit.

## Why the form matters

The NEA is assessed for reading and for writing, and both sets of marking criteria refer to what the
form shows. Every reading band includes "five sources cited on Candidate record form", and the
upper reading and writing bands (Bands 4 to 6) describe "explanation, example and commentary" that
show how well you understand the processes, while lower bands mention "explanation and commentary". At the top, the reading band refers to "sophisticated and independent
command of the reading and research processes", and the writing band to "sophisticated and
independent command of the writing process". The form is where you provide that explanation, example
and commentary. A strong assignment with a thin form leaves evidence out.

## The four requirements

The specification lists what students are required to do on the form.

**1. Give details of all their sources.** The details must be "sufficient to enable a moderator to
access them", and the form "must include details of at least five sources".

**2. Explain how and why they chose their sources and the process(es) they used for gathering
information and ideas**, "eg note making, annotation, highlighting".

**3. Include one example of their note-making method.**

**4. Write a commentary on their final assignment**, focusing on "their reasons for including or
rejecting collated information and ideas, how and why they organised their writing in a specific way
and specific presentational and/or stylistic features they adopted to achieve their purpose."

## Requirement 1: source details a moderator can follow

"Sufficient to enable a moderator to access them" is a practical test: could a stranger find exactly
what you used? For each source record:

| Type of source | Details to record |
|---|---|
| Book | Author, title, publisher, year, pages used |
| Website or online article | Author or organisation, title of page, full web address, date accessed |
| Film or documentary | Title, maker or broadcaster, year, the part used |
| Interview you conducted | Who, their role, date, how it was recorded |
| Literary text | Author, title, edition, the poem or chapter used |

The specification's examples of sources include "traditional research texts eg encyclopaedia, text
book; literary texts eg novel, poem; non-fiction texts eg review, autobiography; media texts eg film,
documentary". Using different kinds shows breadth of reading, which the top reading band describes
as "impressive knowledge and breadth of reading".

## Requirement 2: explaining choice and process

This section answers two questions: **why these sources** and **how you worked with them**.

For "why", give a reason for each source that relates to your task's purpose and audience: a source
chosen because it gives a young person's viewpoint for a speech to young people is better explained
than a source chosen because it "had lots of information".

For "how", name and describe your process: note-making in your own words, annotating printed
copies, highlighting with a colour code, a grid comparing sources. Say why the process suited the
material.

**Model extract (original):**

> I chose the documentary because my letter is addressed to conference organisers who want young
> people's views, and the interviews with students in it gave me a range of real opinions to
> represent. I rejected an older encyclopaedia entry because its information was out of date for a
> modern issue. For printed articles I used two highlighter colours, one for causes and one for
> consequences, which made it easy to see that most of my sources discussed causes but few proposed
> solutions, so I searched specifically for a source on solutions.

The extract explains a reason linked to audience, a reason for rejection, and a process that led to
a further research decision.

## Requirement 3: one note-making example

Choose an example that shows **selection and transformation**, not copying. A good example shows the
original material briefly referenced, your notes in your own words, and perhaps a note to yourself
about how you will use the point. Keep it clear and readable for a moderator.

## Requirement 4: the commentary

The commentary has three named focuses. Treat each as a short section.

**Including and rejecting information.** Explain one or two decisions about what you kept and what
you left out, and why. Rejection is as important as inclusion: it shows judgement about purpose and
audience.

**Organisation.** Explain how and why you ordered your writing: why you opened as you did, how the
sections build, why the ending works for your purpose.

**Presentational and stylistic features.** Name specific features (a sub-heading, a rhetorical
question, a statistic placed early, a formal register, a final call to action) and explain how each
helps you achieve your purpose with your audience.

**Model commentary extract (original):**

> My article for the business magazine had to inform, educate and persuade readers who run small
> companies, so I opened with a short case study of a shop whose website brought in new customers,
> because a concrete example persuades busy readers faster than a definition. I rejected detailed
> material on programming languages from two of my sources, since my audience needs to know what a
> good website does, not how it is built. I organised the article in three sections, design,
> maintenance and development, each with a sub-heading, so that readers can find the part that
> concerns them. I ended with three short questions for readers to ask about their own websites,
> turning information into action.

## Turning description into explanation

The weakest commentaries describe; the strongest explain.

| Describes | Explains |
|---|---|
| "I used sub-headings." | "I used sub-headings so that busy readers could find the section relevant to them." |
| "I found five sources." | "I chose sources from different kinds of text to balance expert facts with young people's views." |
| "I wrote in a formal style." | "I used a formal register because the organisers are professionals and I wanted my views to be taken seriously." |

The word **because** or **so that** is the simplest test of an explaining sentence.

## Common mistakes

- **Fewer than five sources**, or sources listed too vaguely for a moderator to find them.
- **Choosing sources without saying why.**
- **A note-making example that is copied text.**
- **A commentary that retells the assignment** instead of explaining choices.
- **Covering only one of the three commentary focuses.**
- **Leaving the form until the last day**, when you have forgotten why you made decisions.

## Technique tips

- Start the form when you start research, and add to it as you go.
- Keep a research log with dates, sources and decisions.
- Draft the commentary soon after finishing the assignment, while choices are fresh.
- Check every sentence of the commentary for "because" or "so that".
- Follow your school's rules on deadlines and on what help is allowed.
