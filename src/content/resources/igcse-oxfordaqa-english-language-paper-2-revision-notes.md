## Section A: reading modern-world sources

Paper 2's sources deliberately shift away from Paper 1's literary
non-fiction toward **current-affairs and issue-based writing** —
opinion pieces, reports, campaign material, and sources that may be
"solely visual." Two practical consequences for revision:

- Read outside the literary register: newspaper comment, charity
appeals, infographics, adverts. The skill being tested is analysis of
purposeful, real-world writing, not literary technique alone.
- Practise a **visual or graphic source** specifically — an infographic
or chart requires reading explicit data and implicit framing (what is
emphasised, what is omitted), which is a different skill from reading
continuous prose.

**You are not tested on prior knowledge of the topic itself.** A source
about an unfamiliar issue provides everything needed to answer well —
do not let unfamiliar subject matter feel like a disadvantage.

## AO1 reading skills (R1–R4) — what each actually asks for

- **R1** — read and understand across purpose; don't just paraphrase, show you grasp *why* the text does what it does.
- **R2** — collate and synthesise detail, often across more than one source; a strong answer draws connections rather than treating sources in isolation.
- **R3** — develop an interpretation of the writer's ideas, going beyond the literal surface meaning.
- **R4** — explain and evaluate linguistic, grammatical, structural and presentational features and their effect.

## Section B: directed writing — audience and purpose first

Before drafting, write **one sentence naming the audience** and **one
sentence naming the purpose**. Every subsequent choice — vocabulary,
tone, structure, form — should be checked against those two sentences.
AO2 (W1–W4) rewards adapting form and register to a *specified* audience
and purpose, not producing generically well-written prose.

| Element | Question to ask before writing |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who exactly is this for, and what tone will land with them? |
| Purpose | Persuade, inform, advise, entertain — which, precisely? |
| Form | What structural features does this form require (headline, greeting, sign-off, sub-headings)? |

## Reading and writing reinforce each other

Because Section A analyses how a writer achieves effect on an audience,
and Section B asks you to achieve effect for a specified audience, the
two sections train the same underlying skill. Revise them together: for
every Section A technique you analyse (rhetorical question, direct
address, the rule of three), ask whether you could deploy it
deliberately in your own Section B writing.

## Weighting to plan revision time around

Paper 2/NEA carries 20% AO1 and 20% AO2 of the full qualification —
equal to each other, and lower per-paper than Paper 1's 30%/30%, but
still worth balanced revision time across both sections rather than
favouring one.

## Planning the directed writing task under time pressure

With Paper 2 split evenly between two demanding sections, allow no more
than five minutes to plan Section B. A workable planning shape:

1. One sentence each for audience and purpose (see above).
2. Three or four content points, in the order they will appear.
3. One line noting the form's required features (opening, sign-off,
sub-headings, paragraphing style).

Skipping this in favour of starting to write immediately usually
produces a response that drifts in register partway through, because
the audience and purpose were never fixed before drafting began — a
five-minute investment protects against a much larger loss of AO2
marks.

## Exam traps

- Treating a visual or brief source as less significant than a longer
prose text.
- Bringing Paper 1's literary-analysis habits unchanged to a modern-
issue source, without adjusting for its more direct style.
- Drafting Section B without first fixing audience and purpose in
writing.
- Neglecting technical accuracy (W4) under time pressure, despite it
carrying equal weight to content and organisation within AO2.
- Assuming unfamiliarity with a source's topic is a disadvantage.

## The non-exam assessment alternative

Paper 2 is not the only route through this part of the qualification —
some centres instead enter candidates for an independent-research
non-exam assessment (NEA) project covering the same overall skills.
Confirm which route your centre has entered you for before revising
further: NEA preparation is coursework-based and follows a different
timeline and skill emphasis (independent research and sustained writing
over time) from Paper 2's timed, source-based exam format covered in
these notes. The two are genuine alternatives, not variations on the
same revision plan.

## Self-test

1. How does Paper 2's Section A reading material differ from Paper 1's?
2. Name the four AO1 reading sub-skills (R1–R4) in your own words.
3. What two sentences should be written before drafting Section B, and
why?
4. Why might a visual source carry as much assessable meaning as a
prose source?
5. What is the AO1/AO2 weighting split for Paper 2, and how does it
compare with Paper 1's?

**Answers:** 1. Paper 2 uses modern-world, current-affairs style
sources — including possibly solely visual ones — rather than Paper 1's
literary non-fiction. 2. R1: reading and understanding for purpose; R2:
collating and synthesising detail, often across sources; R3: developing
an interpretation of the writer's ideas; R4: explaining and evaluating
linguistic, grammatical, structural and presentational features. 3. One
sentence naming the audience and one naming the purpose, so every
writing choice can be checked against a fixed, specific target rather
than drifting into generic prose. 4. Because it can carry explicit
data and implicit framing (emphasis, omission) that is just as
analysable as continuous prose, even without lengthy text. 5. 20% AO1
and 20% AO2 for Paper 2, compared with 30% AO1 and 30% AO2 for Paper 1 —
together making an overall 50/50 AO1/AO2 split.
