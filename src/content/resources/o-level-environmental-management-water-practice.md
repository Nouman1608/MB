## Section A

**1.** Name the **five** oceans of the world. **[2]**

**2.** Define *potable water*. **[1]**

**3.** State the length that defines a microplastic. **[1]**

**4.** Name the parasite that causes malaria. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** (a) Name **two** stores of fresh water and **two** transfers in the water cycle. **[2]**
(b) Explain how removing forest from a river basin changes interception and surface run-off. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the four stages used to make river water potable, in order. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why access to potable water is often lower in rural areas of low-income countries than in
cities. **[2]**

**8.** A small island has no rivers and a growing tourist industry. It plans a reverse osmosis plant.
(a) Describe how reverse osmosis produces fresh water. **[2]**
(b) Discuss **one** benefit and **one** limitation of desalination for the island. **[2]**

**9.** A factory discharges mercury into an estuary. Fishermen and seabirds both eat fish from the
estuary.
(a) Explain why the seabirds have higher concentrations of mercury than the fish. **[3]**
(b) Suggest **two** strategies to improve the water quality of the estuary. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how malaria is transmitted between humans and mosquitoes. **[3]**

**11.** A village has had a cholera outbreak after floods. Describe **three** strategies to control
cholera in the village. **[3]**

**12.** (a) Define *marine aquaculture*. **[1]**
(b) Explain **two** negative impacts of a fish farm in a sheltered bay on the surrounding marine ecosystem. **[4]**

**13.** Explain how increasing the mesh size of nets and closed seasons help manage fish stocks. **[4]**

**14.** (a) State **three** causes of oil pollution in coastal waters. **[3]**
(b) Describe how oil affects seabirds and coral reefs. **[3]**

**15.** Distinguish between bioplastics and biodegradable plastics. **[2]**

## Section C

**16.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of booms, detergent sprays and controlled burning for
minimising the impacts of an oil spill. **[6]**

**17.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of strategies to control malaria. **[6]**

**18.** Discuss whether recycling is a more effective strategy than legislation for reducing plastic
pollution in the oceans. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic. **[2]** (All five for 2 marks; three or four for 1.)

**2.** Water that is safe to drink. **[1]**

**3.** Less than 5 mm. **[1]**

**4.** Plasmodium. **[1]**

**5.** (a) Stores, any two: ice sheets and glaciers, ground water, atmosphere, lakes and rivers **[1]**.
Transfers, any two: precipitation, interception, surface run-off, infiltration, through-flow, ground
water flow, transpiration, evaporation, condensation **[1]**.
(b) Fewer trees intercept rainfall **[1]**, so more water reaches the ground quickly and surface run-off
increases **[1]**.

**6.** Screening removes large objects **[1]**; sedimentation lets particles settle **[1]**; filtration removes
fine particles **[1]**; chlorination kills bacteria **[1]**.

**7.** Rural areas are often far from piped supplies and treatment plants **[1]**, and governments may lack
money to extend infrastructure to scattered populations **[1]**.

**8.** (a) Sea water is put under high pressure and passed through a membrane **[1]**, which lets water
molecules through but stops most ions **[1]**.
(b) Benefit: a reliable supply independent of rainfall for residents and tourists **[1]**. Limitation:
high energy use and cost, and brine waste can harm marine life **[1]**.

**9.** (a) Mercury builds up in each fish over time (bioaccumulation) **[1]**; seabirds eat many fish **[1]**,
so mercury becomes more concentrated at each level of the food chain (biomagnification) **[1]**.
(b) Any two: pollution control and legislation on discharges **[1]**; treatment of industrial waste before
release **[1]**; monitoring and fines **[1]**. **[max 2]**

**10.** A female Anopheles mosquito carrying Plasmodium bites a human, passing the parasite into the blood
**[1]**; an uninfected mosquito becomes infected when it feeds on the blood of an infected person **[1]**;
it can then infect another person **[1]**.

**11.** Any three: handwashing with soap **[1]**; boiling or chlorinating drinking water **[1]**; repairing
sanitation and sewage treatment **[1]**; vaccination **[1]**. **[max 3]**

**12.** (a) The farming of marine species (fish, crustaceans or seaweeds) in captivity. **[1]**
(b) Any two explained: waste and uneaten feed cause nutrient enrichment **[2]**; disease spreads to wild
fish **[2]**; escaped farmed fish compete or interbreed with wild fish **[2]**; feed made from wild-caught
fish puts pressure on other stocks **[2]**. **[max 4]**

**13.** Larger mesh lets young, small fish escape **[1]**, so they survive to breed and replace the stock
**[1]**. Closed seasons stop fishing at certain times, such as the breeding season **[1]**, allowing
populations to recover **[1]**.

**14.** (a) Any three: offshore or onshore oil extraction; pipelines; shipping accidents; cleaning tanks at
sea; refineries. **[3]**
(b) Seabirds: oil coats feathers, so they lose insulation and buoyancy and may swallow oil when preening
**[2]**. Coral reefs: oil smothers coral and blocks light **[1]**.

**15.** Bioplastics are made fully or partly from biological raw materials and may or may not be
biodegradable **[1]**; biodegradable plastics are designed to be decomposed by bacteria and fungi in water
or soil, whatever they are made from **[1]**.

**16.** **[6]** Booms: contain oil so it can be skimmed or absorbed with sorbents; limited in rough seas.
Detergent sprays: break oil into droplets that disperse; but detergents can be toxic to marine life and
the oil remains in the water. Controlled burning: removes large amounts quickly at sea; but causes air
pollution and works only on fresh, thick oil in calm conditions. Conclusion: a combination is used,
chosen for sea conditions and nearness to coasts.

**17.** **[6]** Personal protection: nets and repellent are cheap and effective at night; vaccination and
antimalarial drugs reduce severe illness; but nets must be used correctly, drugs cost money and parasites
can become resistant. Vector control: draining breeding sites removes larvae; spraying kills adults; sterile
male release and biological control reduce numbers without chemicals; but insecticides harm other species
and mosquitoes can become resistant, and draining wetlands harms habitats. Conclusion: combined strategies
work best.

**18.** **[6]** Recycling: reuses plastic and reduces new production, but many plastics are not recycled, it
needs collection systems, and plastic already in the ocean is not removed. Legislation and enforcement:
bans on single-use items or microbeads reduce plastic at source and apply to everyone, but enforcement is
difficult and plastic crosses borders. Conclusion: legislation that reduces use is likely to be more
effective, supported by recycling and safe disposal.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "chemicals" instead of naming chlorination at the end of water treatment.
- Describing biomagnification without referring to feeding levels.
- In "discuss" items, describing how a method works but giving no limitation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which oil spill strategies are prevention and which minimise impacts?**
Prevention: MARPOL, double-hulled
tankers, risk assessments and maintenance. Minimising impacts: navigation systems, booms and sorbents,
detergent sprays, skimmers and controlled burning.

**Do I need specific fish species for aquaculture?**
No. The syllabus says knowledge of specific species is
not required; refer to fish, crustaceans and seaweeds.

**How are malaria and cholera controls different?**
Malaria is controlled by avoiding bites and reducing
mosquitoes. Cholera is controlled by clean water, sanitation, hygiene and vaccination.
