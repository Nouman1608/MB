These questions cover content areas 2.2.8, duress of threats and duress of circumstances, and 2.2.9, voluntary and involuntary intoxication.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the two limbs of the test for duress set out in *R v Graham*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** offences to which duress is not a defence. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between duress of threats and duress of circumstances. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between specific intent and basic intent offences, giving **one** example of each. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by "Dutch courage" and its effect on the defence of intoxication. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Lucy is told by her violent ex-partner that he will hurt her badly next week unless she steals money from her employer. She steals £500 the next day.

Explain whether Lucy can rely on duress. **[5]**

**7.** Sam joins a gang that sells stolen phones. He knows some members carry knives. The gang leader later threatens to stab him unless he takes part in a burglary.

Explain whether Sam can rely on duress. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the courts decide which of a defendant's characteristics may be considered in the objective limb of the duress test, using **one** decided case. **[4]**

**9.** Tariq drinks heavily at a party and, in a confused state, stabs a guest, causing grievous bodily harm. He says he was too drunk to know what he was doing.

Explain how intoxication affects Tariq's liability under section 18 and section 20 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861. **[6]**

**10.** Nadia's orange juice is secretly spiked with vodka. She then damages a car in a fit of temper; she says she knew what she was doing but would never have done it sober.

Explain whether Nadia has a defence. **[4]**

**11.** Explain, using **two** decided cases, how duress of circumstances has been applied to driving offences. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the law on intoxication strikes a fair balance between protecting the public and fairness to defendants. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **Subjective**: was D compelled to act because he reasonably believed he faced death or serious injury? [1] **Objective**: would a sober person of reasonable firmness, sharing D's characteristics, have responded in the same way? [1]

**2.** Any two [1] each: **murder** (*R v Howe* [1987]), whether as principal or accessory; **attempted murder** (*R v Gotts* [1992]).

**3.** In **duress of threats** a person threatens D with death or serious injury **unless D commits a particular crime** [1]. In **duress of circumstances** the threat of death or serious injury arises from the **situation** D is in, and D commits a crime to escape it [1], as in *R v Willer* (1986) or *R v Martin* [1989]; the same *Graham* test applies to both [1].

**4.** A **specific intent** offence requires **intention** as its *mens rea* [1], for example murder, s 18 OAPA 1861 or theft [1]. A **basic intent** offence can be committed **recklessly** [1], for example manslaughter, s 20 or s 47 OAPA 1861, or criminal damage [1].

**5.** "Dutch courage" means D forms the intent while sober and then drinks to gain the courage to carry it out [1]; intoxication is then **no defence** even to a specific intent offence (*Attorney General for Northern Ireland v Gallagher* [1963]) [1].

**6.** There is a threat of **serious injury** to Lucy herself [1], directed at a **particular crime**, theft (*R v Cole* [1994]) [1]. But under *R v Hasan* [2005] D must reasonably believe the threat will be carried out **immediately or almost immediately** [1]; the threat was for **next week**, so Lucy had time to go to the **police** or take other evasive action [1]. Duress is therefore likely to **fail**; *Hudson and Taylor* [1971], which was more generous, was disapproved in *Hasan* [1].

**7.** Sam faced a threat of **death or serious injury** to commit a nominated crime [1]. However, he **voluntarily associated** with a criminal gang [1] and knew members carried knives, so he **knew or ought reasonably to have known** he might be subjected to violent pressure (*R v Hasan* [2005]; compare *R v Sharp* [1987]) [1]. Duress is therefore **not available** [1].

**8.** The objective limb asks whether a sober person of reasonable firmness **sharing D's relevant characteristics** would have acted the same way [1]. In *R v Bowen* [1996] the Court of Appeal held that relevant characteristics include **age, sex, pregnancy, serious physical disability and recognised psychiatric illness** [1], because they may affect a person's ability to resist threats [1]. Characteristics such as **low IQ** short of mental impairment, or being unusually timid, are **not** relevant [1].

**9.** **Section 18** is a **specific intent** offence [1]: if Tariq was so drunk that he did **not form the intent** to cause grievous bodily harm, he is not guilty (*R v Sheehan and Moore* [1975]) [1]; but if he formed the intent while drunk, a drunken intent is still an intent and he is guilty [1]. **Section 20** is a **basic intent** offence [1]: under *DPP v Majewski* [1977] voluntary intoxication is **no defence**, because becoming drunk is a reckless course of conduct [1]. So even if he escapes s 18, he will be **guilty under s 20** [1].

**10.** Nadia's intoxication is **involuntary** because her drink was spiked [1]. Involuntary intoxication is a defence only if D **did not form the mens rea** [1]. Nadia says she knew what she was doing, so, as in *R v Kingston* [1994], "a drugged intent is still an intent" [1]; she has **no defence**, although her involuntary intoxication may be relevant to **sentence** [1].

**11.** Duress of circumstances applies the *Graham* test where the threat comes from the situation [1]. In *R v Willer* (1986) a driver who drove on the pavement to escape a gang threatening him should have had the defence put to the jury [1]. In *DPP v Bell* [1992] a drunk driver who drove off to escape an attack had the defence, because he **drove only as far as necessary** to escape [1]. (Or *R v Martin* [1989]: driving while disqualified because his wife threatened suicide; or *R v Conway* [1988].) The defence ends once the danger has passed (*R v Pommell* [1995]) [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: the specific and basic intent distinction; *Majewski*; *Sheehan and Moore*; *Lipman*; *Gallagher*; involuntary intoxication (*Kingston*, *Hardie*, *Allen*); drunken mistakes (*O'Grady*, *Hatton*; *Jaggard* exception).
- **Protecting the public**: alcohol is linked to violent crime; *Majewski* stops intoxicated offenders escaping liability; Dutch courage cannot be used; drunken mistakes do not excuse violence.
- **Fairness to defendants**: specific intent defendants who did not form intent are acquitted of the more serious offence; involuntary intoxication is recognised where no intent was formed; *Hardie* protects those who take non-dangerous drugs.
- **Criticisms**: *Majewski* treats the decision to drink as substituting for *mens rea* at the time of the offence, which conflicts with the principle that *actus reus* and *mens rea* coincide; the specific and basic intent labels are not always logical (*Heard*); *Kingston* may be harsh on a blameless defendant; *Jaggard* produces an inconsistency between criminal damage and violent offences.
- **Evaluation**: conclude, for example, that the law leans towards public protection, which is defensible as policy but sits uneasily with subjective *mens rea*.

Top-level answers use cases accurately and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Allowing duress where the threat was not immediate after *Hasan*.
- Ignoring voluntary association.
- Using characteristics excluded by *Bowen*.
- Treating intoxication as a defence to a basic intent offence.
- Assuming spiked drinks always provide a defence.
