These notes condense **3.2.1.1 Operations management** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS, Unit 2). For explanations and full worked examples, use the [operations management study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725/); to test yourself, go to the [operations management practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Recall in one minute

- **Objectives you must calculate:** total costs, unit costs, waiting times, delivery times, proportion of returned items, proportion of defects, labour productivity, capacity, capacity utilisation.
- **Other objectives:** flexibility, environmental objectives.
- **Trade-offs:** for example costs versus speed of response; objectives vary with the operational context.
- **Processes:** job, batch, flow, mass customisation; labour-intensive versus capital-intensive.

## Formulas (9725 appendix)

| Measure | Formula | Unit of answer |
|---|---|---|
| Total costs | fixed costs + variable costs | $ |
| Unit cost | total costs ÷ output | $ per unit |
| Waiting times | total waiting time ÷ number of customers | minutes per customer |
| Delivery times | total delivery time ÷ number of deliveries | minutes, hours or days per delivery |
| Returned items (%) | returned items ÷ items sold × 100 | % |
| Defects (%) | defective items ÷ items produced × 100 | % |
| Labour productivity | output in a period ÷ average employees in the period | units per employee |
| Capacity | maximum potential output | units |
| Capacity utilisation (%) | actual output ÷ maximum potential output × 100 | % |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operations | The function that turns inputs into goods and services |
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run, such as rent |
| Variable costs | Costs that change directly with output, such as materials |
| Unit cost | The average cost of producing one unit |
| Labour productivity | Output per employee in a period |
| Capacity | The maximum a business can produce with its current resources |
| Capacity utilisation | Actual output as a percentage of capacity |
| Flexibility | Ability to change the volume or variety of output quickly |
| Environmental objective | A target to reduce harm, such as waste, energy use or carbon footprint |
| Job production | One-off products made to each customer's order |
| Batch production | A group of identical items made together before switching to another product |
| Flow production | Continuous production of a standard product along a line |
| Mass customisation | High-volume production systems that tailor each item to the customer |
| Labour-intensive | Relies mainly on people; lower fixed costs, higher variable costs |
| Capital-intensive | Relies mainly on machinery; higher fixed costs, lower variable costs |

## Interpretation reminders

- **Unit cost falls as output rises** towards capacity, because fixed costs are spread over more units.
- **High utilisation** = low unit cost but little room for extra orders or maintenance.
- **Productivity** comparisons only make sense between similar operations; a hand-finishing workshop will always make fewer items per worker than an automated line.
- **Quality measures** need a comparison (last month, a rival, a target) before you can call them good or bad.
- **Returns** may reflect customer choice (fit, change of mind) as well as faults.

## Trade-offs to quote

- Low cost **vs** speed of response (spare capacity and inventory cost money).
- Low cost **vs** flexibility (long runs of one product are cheapest).
- Quality **vs** speed (inspection and care slow output).
- Environmental targets **vs** short-run costs (new equipment, dearer materials).

Context decides priority: budget airline (cost), emergency department (waiting time), bespoke tailor (quality and customisation).

## Processes at a glance

| | Volume | Variety | Unit cost | Flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job | Very low | Very high | Highest | High |
| Batch production | Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Flow | Very high | Very low | Lowest | Low |
| Mass customisation | High | High | Low to medium | Medium |

**Labour versus capital:** capital-intensive becomes cheaper per unit once volume is high enough to spread its higher fixed costs. Find the output where total costs are equal; above it, capital-intensive wins.

## Exam traps

- Divide total costs by **actual output**, not capacity.
- Returns use items **sold**; defects use items **produced**.
- Use the **average** number of employees for productivity.
- Utilisation cannot exceed 100%: check your figures if it does.
- Do not call a site "more efficient" on one measure alone.
- When asked about a process, link it to the volume and variety in the case, not to the product in general.

## Self-test

1. Fixed costs are $12,000 and variable cost is $3 per unit. Output is 6,000 units. Calculate total costs and unit cost.
2. Capacity is 8,000 units and output is 6,000 units. Calculate capacity utilisation.
3. 12 employees (average) produce 6,000 units. Calculate labour productivity.
4. 90 of 6,000 units produced are defective. Calculate the proportion of defects.
5. A shop sold 1,500 items and 45 were returned. Calculate the proportion returned.
6. A call centre records 1,890 minutes of total waiting time for 90 callers. Calculate the average waiting time.
7. Give one reason why unit cost falls when capacity utilisation rises.
8. State one disadvantage of operating at 100% capacity utilisation.
9. Explain the trade-off between cost and speed of response.
10. Which process suits a high-volume standard product?
11. Give one disadvantage of mass customisation.
12. Why might a labour-intensive process be cheaper at low output?

### Answers

1. 12,000 + (3 × 6,000) = **$30,000**; 30,000 ÷ 6,000 = **$5.00** per unit.
2. 6,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = **75%**.
3. 6,000 ÷ 12 = **500 units per employee**.
4. 90 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **1.5%**.
5. 45 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **3%**.
6. 1,890 ÷ 90 = **21 minutes**.
7. Fixed costs are spread over more units.
8. No spare capacity for extra orders or maintenance (or staff overstretched).
9. Fast response needs spare capacity or extra inventory, both of which raise costs.
10. **Flow** production.
11. Complex, costly ordering and production systems to set up.
12. Its fixed costs are lower, so at low output there is less fixed cost to spread over each unit.
