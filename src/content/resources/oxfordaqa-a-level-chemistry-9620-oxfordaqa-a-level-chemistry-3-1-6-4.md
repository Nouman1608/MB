This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.6.4 Effect of concentration and pressure** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification asks for "the qualitative effect of changes in concentration on collision frequency" and "the qualitative effect of a change in the pressure of a gas on collision frequency", and students should be able to "explain how a change in concentration or a change in pressure influences the rate of a reaction". It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1), and Unit 5 can draw on it.

The [kinetics study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) introduces all five kinetics points. This page focuses on one: how to build a full-mark explanation, read rate graphs, and avoid the traps examiners set. Condense it with the [3.1.6.4 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-4-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [3.1.6.4 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-4-practice/).

## What "qualitative" means here

At International AS you explain the direction of the effect and the reason for it. You do not predict by how much the rate changes. Whether doubling a concentration doubles the rate, quadruples it or leaves it unchanged depends on the order of reaction, which is International A2 content in [rate equations](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-rate-equations/). A Unit 2 answer that says "doubling the concentration doubles the rate" makes a claim the course has not yet given you the tools to justify, so avoid it.

## Two things decide the rate

The rate of a reaction depends on how many **successful collisions** happen **per unit time**. That number is the product of two factors:

1. the **collision frequency**: how many collisions between reactant particles occur each second in a given volume;
2. the **proportion** of those collisions with energy at or above the activation energy, Ea (and a suitable orientation).

Concentration and pressure act **only on the first factor**. They do not change the energy of the particles, so the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution keeps the same shape, and the proportion of particles with E ≥ Ea is unchanged.

| Change | Collision frequency | Proportion with E ≥ Ea | Effect on rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher concentration of a solute | Increases | Unchanged | Increases |
| Higher pressure of a gas (smaller volume, same temperature) | Increases | Unchanged | Increases |
| Higher temperature (3.1.6.3, for contrast) | Increases slightly | Increases greatly | Increases |
| Catalyst (3.1.6.5, for contrast) | Unchanged | Increases (lower Ea route) | Increases |

## Concentration in solution

Concentration in mol dm⁻³ counts particles per unit volume. A 0.10 mol dm⁻³ solution holds 0.10 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² solute particles in every dm³. Doubling the concentration puts twice as many reactant particles in the same space, so a given particle meets reactant particles more often.

A full explanation has three linked steps:

1. **More particles in the same volume** (higher concentration).
2. **More frequent collisions** between reactant particles.
3. **More successful collisions per unit time** (more collisions with E ≥ Ea each second), so the rate increases.

The words "per unit time" or "per second" matter. A higher concentration does not make each collision more likely to succeed; it makes collisions, and therefore successful collisions, happen more often.

### A note on Maxwell–Boltzmann curves and concentration

If a Maxwell–Boltzmann curve is drawn for the particles in a **fixed volume**, a more concentrated sample gives a curve that is taller at every energy by the same factor. The peak stays at the same energy, the shape is the same, and the **fraction** beyond Ea is unchanged; only the **number** beyond Ea in that volume rises. Do not shift the peak sideways: that would mean the particles had more energy, which only a temperature change causes.

## Pressure of a gas

For a gas, pressure and concentration are linked. Rearranging pV = nRT gives

```
n/V = p/(RT)
```

At constant temperature, the amount of gas per unit volume is proportional to the pressure. For example, at 400 K a gas at 250 kPa has n/V = 250 000 ÷ (8.31 × 400) = 75.2 mol m⁻³, or 0.0752 mol dm⁻³. At 125 kPa and the same temperature the value halves to 0.0376 mol dm⁻³.

So raising the pressure of a gas by compressing it:

- leaves the number of molecules the same but squeezes them into a smaller volume;
- increases the number of molecules per unit volume;
- increases the collision frequency, so more successful collisions occur per second and the rate rises.

Three points examiners test:

- **Pressure only matters for gases.** Liquids and solids hardly compress, so changing the pressure over a reaction in solution has no significant effect on its rate.
- **Every gas in a mixture is affected.** Compressing a mixture of two reacting gases to one-third of its volume at constant temperature triples the concentration of each gas.
- **How the pressure rises matters.** If extra unreactive gas is pumped into a sealed vessel of fixed volume, the total pressure rises but the amount of each reactant per unit volume does not change, so the collision frequency between reactant molecules, and the rate, stay the same. Raising pressure speeds up a gas reaction because it raises the reactant concentrations.

## Why the rate falls as a reaction proceeds

As reactants are used up, their concentration falls, collisions between reactant particles become less frequent and the rate drops. This is why a graph of product formed against time is steepest at the start and gradually levels off. The curve becomes horizontal when one reactant (the limiting reagent) is used up.

### Worked example 1: comparing two experiments

All figures in this example are invented for practice. A student adds 0.0486 g of magnesium ribbon (Ar = 24.3) to 50.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid and collects the hydrogen in a gas syringe:

```
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
```

Experiment A uses 1.00 mol dm⁻³ acid; experiment B uses 0.50 mol dm⁻³ acid. Everything else is the same.

```
n(Mg) = 0.0486 ÷ 24.3 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol
n(HCl) needed = 2 × 2.00 × 10⁻³ = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol
n(HCl) in A = 1.00 × 0.0500 = 0.0500 mol (excess)
n(HCl) in B = 0.50 × 0.0500 = 0.025 mol (excess)
n(H₂) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol, so volume at room conditions = 2.00 × 10⁻³ × 24.0 = 0.0480 dm³ = 48.0 cm³
```

How the two curves compare:

- **Initial gradient**: B's curve starts less steeply, because the lower acid concentration gives less frequent collisions between H⁺ ions and the magnesium surface.
- **Final volume**: both level off at 48.0 cm³, because magnesium is the limiting reagent in both.
- **Time to finish**: B takes longer to reach 48.0 cm³.

If instead the acid were limiting (for example, 10.0 cm³ of 0.20 mol dm⁻³ acid, only 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol HCl), the final volume would fall to half, 24.0 cm³, as well as the start being slower. Always check which reagent is limiting before predicting the final volume.

### Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

**Question.** Nitrogen monoxide reacts with oxygen: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). A mixture in a gas syringe is compressed from 120 cm³ to 40 cm³ at constant temperature. Explain the effect on the rate of reaction. **[3]**

**Model answer.**

- The same number of molecules now occupies one-third of the volume, so the pressure and the concentration of each gas are three times greater. *(cause: more molecules per unit volume)*
- The NO and O₂ molecules collide more frequently. *(collision frequency)*
- More collisions with energy at or above Ea occur per second, so the rate increases. *(link to successful collisions per unit time)*

**What would lose marks:** "the molecules have more energy" (wrong: temperature is constant), or "the rate increases because there are more collisions" with no time reference and no mention of Ea.

## Common mistakes

- Saying higher concentration gives particles **more energy** or increases the **proportion** of successful collisions.
- Writing "more collisions" without "per second" or "more frequent".
- Applying a pressure argument to a reaction in solution.
- Predicting a numerical change in rate (for example "rate doubles") without data.
- Assuming a lower concentration always reduces the final amount of product; only a change in the **limiting** reagent does that.
- Moving the Maxwell–Boltzmann peak to explain a concentration change.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State**: give the effect only ("the rate increases").
- **Explain**: give the chain cause → collision frequency → successful collisions per unit time. Each link is usually worth a mark.
- **Sketch** or **draw** a graph for a second experiment: start from the origin, show a different initial gradient and decide the final level from the limiting reagent.
- **Compare**: refer to both experiments in each sentence, for example "B's initial gradient is less steep than A's because...".
