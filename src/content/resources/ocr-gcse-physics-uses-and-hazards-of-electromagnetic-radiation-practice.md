These questions cover statements P5.2g to P5.2j of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Gateway Science Physics A specification (J249, Version 5.0, July 2026). Questions 8 to 12 test P5.2i and are marked (Higher Tier only). The other questions are for both tiers. Where a question needs the speed of ultrasound it is given in the question.

## Questions

**1.** Name the type of electromagnetic wave best suited to each job: (i) sending a signal to a television satellite, (ii) checking a banknote for a hidden marking, (iii) treating a tumour deep in the body, (iv) a thermal imaging camera looking for heat escaping from a house. **[4]**

**2.** Explain why a microwave oven heats a bowl of soup but a plastic bowl stays much cooler. **[3]**

**3.** A tanning salon uses lamps that give out ultraviolet waves.

**(a)** Describe two hazards to a customer's body from ultraviolet. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two ways a customer can reduce the risk when outdoors in strong sunshine. **[2]**

**4.** A radiographer takes X-ray images of many patients each day and stands behind a screen. Explain why the screen is needed for the radiographer but a patient has one image without a screen between the machine and them. **[3]**

**5.** Gamma rays are used in radiotherapy. The beam is directed at a tumour from several different directions.

**(a)** Explain why gamma rays can be used to treat cancer. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the advantage of using several directions. **[2]**
**(c)** State one reason why staff operate the machine from another room. **[1]**

**6.** Describe how a radio programme is sent from a transmitter and detected by a portable radio. **[4]**

**7.** Gamma rays are used to sterilise packaged surgical gloves.

**(a)** Give two reasons why gamma rays suit this job. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the packs are treated in a room with thick shielding and no staff present. **[2]**

**8.** (Higher Tier only) An X-ray image of a child's wrist shows a thin dark line across a pale bone. Explain how the image is formed and what the dark line shows. **[4]**

**9.** (Higher Tier only) Ultrasound is used to scan an unborn baby.

**(a)** Explain how echoes from the baby are used to build up an image. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why gel is spread on the skin. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why ultrasound is used instead of X-rays for this scan. **[2]**

**10.** (Higher Tier only) A pulse of ultrasound travels through soft tissue at 1500 m/s. Echoes come back from two boundaries, the first after 48 microseconds and the second after 132 microseconds. (1 microsecond = 10⁻⁶ s.)

**(a)** Calculate the depth of the first boundary. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the depth of the second boundary. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the distance between the boundaries. **[1]**

**11.** (Higher Tier only) A doctor wants to check a patient's inflamed ankle.

**(a)** Explain why a thermal imaging camera can show the inflammation on the skin. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why infrared cannot be used to find a fracture in the ankle bone. **[2]**

**12.** (Higher Tier only) For each patient, name a suitable imaging method and explain your choice. (a) A pregnant woman needs her baby's position checked. **[3]** (b) A cyclist may have a broken bone in the lower leg. **[3]** (c) A patient swallows a tracer so that the way a deep organ is working can be seen. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) Microwaves [1]. (ii) Ultraviolet [1]. (iii) Gamma rays [1]. (iv) Infrared [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers naming the wave group, rather than "rays" or "radiation", are the ones that can be matched to the job without guessing.

**2.** Microwaves are absorbed by water molecules in the soup [1]. The absorbed energy makes the water particles move faster, which raises the temperature [1]. The plastic contains little water and absorbs far less, so it stays cooler (it may warm a little because the soup heats it) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the absorber (water) to the heating is what turns a statement into an explanation.

**3. (a)** Sunburn or skin damage [1]; skin cancer (or premature skin ageing, or eye damage) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Using sunscreen or covering the skin [1]; wearing sunglasses, or staying in shade, or limiting time at midday [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct hazards or measures are needed when two are asked for, because a repeated idea in different words is the same point.

**4.** X-rays are ionising and can damage cells and DNA, which can cause cancer [1]. The radiographer is exposed many times a day, so small doses add up to a large total [1]. The patient has one short exposure and gains a benefit (a diagnosis), so the risk is judged acceptable [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers compare total exposure with benefit, rather than simply saying "X-rays are dangerous".

**5. (a)** Gamma rays are ionising and damage the cells they pass through [1]. A high dose can kill the cancer cells [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each beam passes through different healthy tissue [1], but the beams all meet at the tumour, which gets a high dose while healthy tissue gets a lower dose [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Staff would be exposed repeatedly, so their total dose would build up [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer to (b) gains credit when it says where the beams overlap and where they do not.

**6.** Alternating current in the transmitter circuit makes electrons in the transmitting aerial oscillate [1]. The oscillating electrons give out radio waves of the same frequency [1]. The radio waves make electrons in the receiving aerial oscillate [1], producing an alternating current of the same frequency, which the radio circuit turns into sound [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Describing both ends of the link, with the aerial and the circuit named, is what the question asks for.

**7. (a)** Gamma rays penetrate the packaging to reach the gloves [1]. They kill microorganisms without heating the gloves [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Gamma rays are ionising and could harm staff [1]. Thick shielding absorbs them and no staff are present, so no one receives a dose [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Explaining the hazard before the protection tends to earn the marks in order.

**8.** X-rays pass through the wrist onto a detector [1]. Bone absorbs more X-rays than soft tissue, so fewer reach the detector behind bone and it shows as a pale area [1]. A break lets more X-rays through than solid bone [1], which gives a dark line across the pale bone [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the amounts absorbed in bone, soft tissue and the gap is the core of the explanation.

**9. (a)** The probe sends pulses of ultrasound into the body [1]. Part of each pulse is reflected at boundaries between different tissues [1]. The time for each echo to return shows how deep the boundary is, and the echoes are combined into an image [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The gel removes air between probe and skin, which would reflect most of the ultrasound at the skin [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Ultrasound is non-ionising, so it does not damage the cells or DNA of the baby [1], but X-rays are ionising, so they carry a risk of cancer [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming ultrasound as non-ionising and X-rays as ionising is the contrast that completes part (c).

**10. (a)** Round trip distance = speed × time = 1500 × 48 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.072 m [1]. Depth = 0.072 ÷ 2 = 0.036 m, which is **3.6 cm** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1500 × 132 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.198 m [1]. Depth = 0.198 ÷ 2 = 0.099 m, which is **9.9 cm** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 9.9 − 3.6 = **6.3 cm** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Halving the distance for the there-and-back trip, and converting microseconds to seconds, are the two places most solutions go wrong.

**11. (a)** The skin emits infrared [1], and inflamed skin is warmer, so it emits more infrared than its surroundings and shows as a different area on the image [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Skin and tissue absorb infrared, so it does not penetrate to the bone [1]. The camera therefore only detects the surface and shows nothing about structures inside [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Separating "emitted from the surface" from "passes through the body" is what makes the contrast clear.

**12. (a)** Ultrasound [1]. Echoes from the baby show its position [1], and it is non-ionising so it is safe for the baby [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** X-ray [1]. Bone absorbs more X-rays than soft tissue, so the bone shows up and a break is visible [1]. A short exposure keeps the dose low [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** A gamma tracer [1]. The tracer emits gamma rays that pass out of the body and are detected outside, showing where it collects and how the organ works [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A method alone earns little here. The reason has to come from absorption, reflection or emission in that particular case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "radiation" or "rays" instead of naming the wave group.
- Giving uses with no reason when the question asks you to explain.
- Writing "burn" for X-rays and gamma rays without mentioning cell or DNA damage and cancer.
- Saying bones "stop" X-rays, when they absorb more than soft tissue does.
- Saying ultrasound is an electromagnetic wave.
- Using the there-and-back distance as the depth.
- Leaving microseconds unconverted in the calculation, or forgetting the unit on the answer.
- Mixing up the roles of transmitting and receiving aerials.
