These notes condense **4.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification
(for teaching from September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. For full explanations, see the
[giant covalent structures study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-giant-covalent-structures/); then
practise with the
[giant covalent structures practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-giant-covalent-structures-practice/).

## Core facts

- Giant covalent structures are **solids with very high melting points**.
- **All of the atoms** are linked to other atoms by **strong covalent bonds**.
- These **bonds must be overcome** to melt or boil the substance.
- Examples: **diamond** and **graphite** (forms of carbon) and **silicon dioxide** (silica).
- You must be able to **recognise** giant covalent structures from diagrams of their bonding and structure.

## Silicon dioxide in brief

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Formula | SiO₂ |
| Bonding | Each Si bonded to 4 O; each O bonded to 2 Si |
| Why SiO₂ | 4 ÷ 2 = 2 oxygen atoms for each silicon atom |
| Found in | Sand and quartz |
| Melting point | 1710 °C |
| Conducts? | No |

```
  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
     |    |
     O    O
     |    |
  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
```

A 2D diagram shows only a small part. It cannot show all four bonds around silicon in three dimensions.

## Why the melting point is very high

1. Giant covalent structure: a network of atoms.
2. Strong covalent bonds link every atom to its neighbours, in all directions.
3. The covalent bonds themselves must be broken to melt it.
4. There are very many strong bonds, so a very large amount of energy is needed.

## Comparison with other structures

| | Small molecules | Polymers | Giant covalent | Ionic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | Molecules | Very large molecules | Atoms | Ions |
| Overcome on melting | Weak intermolecular forces | Relatively strong intermolecular forces | Covalent bonds | Ionic (electrostatic) bonds |
| Melting point | Low | Moderate; solid at room temperature | Very high | High |
| State at room temperature | Mostly gas or liquid | Solid | Solid | Solid |

## Recognising a giant covalent structure

| Look for | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A network of bonded atoms continuing off the edge | Giant covalent |
| Every atom joined to neighbours, no separate groups | Giant covalent |
| Brackets and *n* | Polymer |
| Ions with charges | Ionic |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Giant covalent structure | A network of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds in all directions |
| Covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Silica | Silicon dioxide, SiO₂ |
| Network | A continuous arrangement of bonded atoms |
| Ratio formula | A formula that gives the ratio of atoms, not the atoms in a molecule |

## Worked examples in brief

**Example 1.** "Explain why silicon dioxide has a very high melting point." Giant covalent structure; all atoms
linked by strong covalent bonds; the bonds must be broken; many bonds, so a lot of energy.

**Example 2.** "Explain why carbon dioxide is a gas but silicon dioxide is a solid." CO₂ is made of small molecules
with weak intermolecular forces. SiO₂ is a giant covalent structure and its covalent bonds must be broken.

**Example 3.** "A substance has a melting point of 2000 °C and does not conduct when solid or molten. What
structure does it have?" Giant covalent. It has a very high melting point, and it is not ionic because it does
not conduct when molten.

## Two oxides side by side

| | Carbon dioxide, CO₂ | Silicon dioxide, SiO₂ |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Small molecules | Giant covalent |
| Forces or bonds overcome on melting | Weak intermolecular forces | Strong covalent bonds |
| State at room temperature | Gas | Solid |
| Melting point | Very low | 1710 °C |
| Conducts? | No | No |

The formulae look similar, but the structures are very different. This is a popular exam comparison.

## Building a full-mark answer

1. Name the structure (giant covalent).
2. Say what links the atoms (strong covalent bonds, in all directions).
3. Say what must happen to melt it (the covalent bonds must be broken).
4. Say what that needs (a very large amount of energy).
5. State the result (a very high melting point).

## Exam traps

- **"Intermolecular forces" in an answer about silicon dioxide.** There are no separate molecules.
- **"Strong bonds" without "covalent bonds must be broken".** Say what is broken.
- **"SiO₂ molecules".** It is a giant structure with a ratio formula.
- **Forgetting that the diagram is partial.** Real structures extend in three dimensions.
- **Assuming all giant covalent structures behave like diamond.** Graphite conducts, which is covered in its own
  subsection.
- **Mixing up ionic and giant covalent.** Ionic compounds conduct when molten. Silicon dioxide does not.

## Quick self-test

1. State the state of a giant covalent structure at room temperature.
2. What type of bond holds all the atoms together?
3. Name two forms of carbon that are giant covalent structures.
4. Name one compound that has a giant covalent structure.
5. How many oxygen atoms is each silicon atom bonded to in silicon dioxide?
6. How many silicon atoms is each oxygen atom bonded to?
7. Explain why the formula of silicon dioxide is SiO₂.
8. What must be overcome to melt silicon dioxide?
9. Why does silicon dioxide have a much higher melting point than carbon dioxide?
10. State one limitation of a 2D diagram of silicon dioxide.

### Answers

1. Solid.
2. Strong covalent bonds.
3. Diamond and graphite.
4. Silicon dioxide (silica).
5. Four.
6. Two.
7. Each Si has four O neighbours and each O is shared between two Si, so there are 4 ÷ 2 = 2 O for each Si.
8. Strong covalent bonds, in all directions.
9. Silicon dioxide has covalent bonds to break; carbon dioxide has only weak intermolecular forces to overcome.
10. It cannot show all the bonds in three dimensions, or it shows only part of the structure.
