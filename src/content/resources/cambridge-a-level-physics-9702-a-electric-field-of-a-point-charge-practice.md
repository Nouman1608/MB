Take 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ m F⁻¹ and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

---

## Section A

**1.** Starting from Coulomb's law, show that the electric field strength at distance r from a point charge Q is E = Q/(4πε₀r²). **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the electric field strength 0.050 m from a point charge of −3.0 nC, and state its direction. **[3]**

**3.** The field strength at a distance r from a point charge is E. State the field strength at a distance 3r. **[1]**

**4.** Calculate the distance from a point charge of +5.0 μC at which the field strength is 2.0 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹. **[2]**

**5.** A conducting sphere of radius 0.10 m carries a charge of +2.0 × 10⁻⁸ C.

**(a)** Calculate the field strength at its surface.

**(b)** Calculate the field strength 0.30 m from its centre. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why electric field strengths due to two charges must be added as vectors, and describe how this is done for two fields at right angles. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A charge of +9.0 nC is at x = 0 and a charge of +4.0 nC is at x = 0.25 m.

**(a)** Determine the position between the charges where the electric field strength is zero. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the resultant field strength at x = 0.10 m and state its direction. **[3]**

**8.** A charge of +8.0 nC is at x = 0 and a charge of −2.0 nC is at x = 0.30 m.

**(a)** Determine the position on the x-axis where the electric field strength is zero. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why there is no such point between the two charges. **[1]**

**9.** The dome of an electrostatic generator is a sphere of radius 0.20 m. The surrounding air breaks down when the field strength reaches 3.0 × 10⁶ V m⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum charge the dome can hold. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the field strength 0.50 m from the centre of the dome when it carries this charge. **[2]**

**10.** A charge of +6.0 nC is at the origin and a charge of −6.0 nC is at (0.080 m, 0). Point P is at (0, 0.060 m).

**(a)** Calculate the magnitude of the field at P due to each charge. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the magnitude of the resultant field at P and its angle to the positive y-direction. **[3]**

**11.** Measurements of the field strength near a small charged sphere give 3.6 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹ at 0.10 m from its centre.

**(a)** Predict the field strengths at 0.20 m and 0.30 m, assuming the sphere behaves as a point charge. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the charge on the sphere. **[1]**

**12.** In a simple model of the hydrogen atom, the electron is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m from the proton.

**(a)** Calculate the field strength due to the proton at the electron's position. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the force on the electron, and state its direction. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Force on a test charge q: F = Qq/(4πε₀r²) [1]. E = F/q, so E = Q/(4πε₀r²) [1].

**2.** E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.050² [1] = **1.1 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1], directed **towards the charge** (it is negative) [1].

**3.** **E/9** [1].

**4.** r = √(8.99 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁵) [1] = **0.47 m** [1].

**5. (a)** E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 0.10² [1] = **1.8 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Three times the radius, so one ninth: **2.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹** [1].

**6.** Field strength has **direction** as well as magnitude, so the fields add as vectors [1]. For perpendicular fields the resultant is √(E₁² + E₂²), at an angle tan⁻¹(E₂/E₁) to E₁ [1].

**7. (a)** √9.0/x = √4.0/(0.25 − x) [1], so 3(0.25 − x) = 2x [1]; x = **0.15 m** [1].

**(b)** From +9.0 nC: 8.99 × 10⁹ × 9.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.10² = 8090 N C⁻¹ in +x [1]. From +4.0 nC: 8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.15² = 1600 N C⁻¹ in −x [1]. Resultant = **6.5 × 10³ N C⁻¹ in the +x direction** [1].

**8. (a)** The point is beyond the smaller charge, at x > 0.30 m: 8.0/x² = 2.0/(x − 0.30)² [1], so √8.0 (x − 0.30) = √2.0 x, giving 2(x − 0.30) = x [1]; x = **0.60 m** [1].

**(b)** Between the charges both fields point in the **same direction** (away from + and towards −), so they cannot cancel [1].

**9. (a)** Q = E r² ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ = 3.0 × 10⁶ × 0.20² ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ [1] = **1.3 × 10⁻⁵ C** [1].

**(b)** E = 3.0 × 10⁶ × (0.20 ÷ 0.50)² [1] = **4.8 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹** [1].

**10. (a)** From +6.0 nC (0.060 m): 8.99 × 10⁹ × 6.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.060² = **1.50 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1]. From −6.0 nC (distance √(0.080² + 0.060²) = 0.10 m): 8.99 × 10⁹ × 6.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.10² = **5.39 × 10³ N C⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Field from + charge: 1.50 × 10⁴ in +y. Field from − charge points from P towards (0.080, 0): components (+0.8 × 5390, −0.6 × 5390) = (4310, −3240) [1]. Resultant components: x = 4310, y = 15 000 − 3240 = 11 750; magnitude = √(4310² + 11 750²) = **1.25 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1]; angle = tan⁻¹(4310 ÷ 11 750) = **20°** from the +y direction towards +x [1].

**11. (a)** At 0.20 m: 3.6 × 10⁵ ÷ 4 = **9.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1]. At 0.30 m: 3.6 × 10⁵ ÷ 9 = **4.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Q = 3.6 × 10⁵ × 0.10² ÷ 8.99 × 10⁹ = **4.0 × 10⁻⁷ C** [1].

**12. (a)** E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰)² [1] = **1.4 × 10¹¹ N C⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** F = eE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.44 × 10¹¹ = **2.3 × 10⁻⁸ N**, **towards the proton** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using r instead of r², or using the potential formula by mistake.
- Measuring distances from the surface of a sphere instead of from its centre.
- Adding the fields of two charges without considering direction.
- Placing a zero-field point between unlike charges, or nearer the larger charge.
- Giving a magnitude without a direction when a direction is asked for.
- Not taking square roots before solving a zero-point equation, leading to algebra errors.
