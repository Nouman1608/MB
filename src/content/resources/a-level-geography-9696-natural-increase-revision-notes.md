Condensed recall for **4.1 Natural increase as a component of population change**. For full explanations, read the [Core Human Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-human-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Birth rate (BR) | Live births per 1000 population per year |
| Death rate (DR) | Deaths per 1000 population per year |
| Natural increase rate | BR minus DR (per 1000, or as a percentage after dividing by 10) |
| Total fertility rate | Average number of children per woman |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths under one year per 1000 live births |
| Life expectancy | Average years a person is expected to live |
| Dependency ratio | Dependents (young and old) per 100 people of working age |
| Age/sex structure | Population pyramid showing age bands by sex |
| Natural decrease | Deaths exceed births, so the natural increase rate is negative |
| Replacement-level fertility | Fertility at which each generation just replaces itself: roughly 2.1 children per woman where child mortality is low |
| Cohort | A group of people born in the same period |

## Worked calculations

**Natural increase rate.** A country has a birth rate of 32 per 1000 and a death rate of 8 per 1000.

- Natural increase rate = 32 - 8 = **24 per 1000**, which is **2.4 per cent** a year.
- A quick guide to doubling time is 70 divided by the percentage growth rate: 70 / 2.4 is roughly **29 years**.

**Dependency ratio.** Using the common bands, young dependents are aged 0 to 14 and old dependents 65 and over.

- Dependency ratio = (aged 0-14 + aged 65 and over) / (aged 15-64) x 100.
- If 4 million are aged 0-14, 1 million are 65 and over and 5 million are 15-64: (4 + 1) / 5 x 100 = **100**. Every working-age person supports, on average, one dependent.
- Split it into **youth** (4 / 5 x 100 = 80) and **old-age** (1 / 5 x 100 = 20) ratios to show which group drives dependency.

**Why rate per 1000 matters:** a crude birth rate depends on age structure. An ageing country can have a low birth rate even if each woman has the same number of children as in a younger country, because a smaller share of its population is of childbearing age. This is why the total fertility rate is a better measure of family size.

## Factors affecting fertility

- **Social:** female education, status and age at marriage; culture and religion.
- **Economic:** cost of children, children as labour or support in old age, female employment.
- **Environmental and health:** infant mortality (high loss encourages high fertility), access to health care.
- **Political:** family planning, pro- or anti-natalist policy.
- Contraception availability.

**Female education** is the factor most often linked to falling fertility. Educated women tend to marry later, know more about and can access contraception, have paid work that raises the cost of time out of work, and have fewer infant deaths, so they do not need "replacement" births. In rural areas where children work on farms and support parents in old age, children are an economic asset and fertility stays higher.

## Factors affecting mortality

Health care, clean water and sanitation, nutrition, conflict, disease (including HIV/AIDS and epidemics), age structure and disasters.

- **Infant mortality** is very sensitive to clean water, sanitation, vaccination and the mother's education, so it is widely used as an indicator of development.
- **Age structure** explains a paradox: some HICs have a higher crude death rate than some LICs, because a larger share of their people are elderly, not because health care is worse.
- **HIV/AIDS** lowered life expectancy sharply in several countries of southern Africa from the 1990s, before antiretroviral treatment became widespread.

## Reading population pyramids

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wide base | High birth rate, youthful population |
| Narrow base | Falling birth rate |
| Steep sides, tall | High life expectancy, low death rate |
| Concave sides | High death rate at younger ages |
| Bulge | Baby boom or migration |
| Indent | Low births in a past period, or deaths from war or disease |
| More males in working ages | In-migration of male workers, as in some Gulf states |
| More females at older ages | Women's higher life expectancy |

Youthful populations have high **youth dependency**; ageing populations have high **old-age dependency**.

## Population structure and its consequences

| Structure | Opportunities | Challenges |
|---|---|---|
| Youthful | Future workforce; a possible "demographic dividend" if jobs are created as children reach working age | Demand for schools, health care and jobs; high youth dependency |
| Ageing | Experienced workforce; older people provide care and volunteer work | Pension and health costs; fewer workers and taxpayers; high old-age dependency |

Note that many people over 65 still work, and many aged 15 to 18 are still in education, so the standard bands are only a guide.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain why fertility rates are often high in LICs."*

> Where infant mortality is high, parents have more children to ensure some survive (**health factor**). Children work on farms and support parents in old age (**economic factor**). Low female education and early marriage lengthen the childbearing period (**social factor**). Limited access to contraception adds to this (**political factor**).

## Exam traps

- Birth rate is per 1000 total population, fertility rate per woman.
- Natural increase excludes migration.
- Dependency ratio uses a working-age band set by the source, so check its definition.
- When asked to interpret a pyramid, quote the age bands and compare, do not just describe the shape.
- A high crude death rate in an HIC usually reflects an older population, not poor health care.
- Show the working in calculations and state the units (per 1000, per cent, or per 100 of working age).

## Self-test

1. Define natural increase rate.
2. Difference between birth rate and total fertility rate?
3. Give two social factors lowering fertility.
4. What does a wide base on a pyramid show?
5. What does the dependency ratio measure?
6. Why might high infant mortality raise fertility?
7. Birth rate 18 per 1000, death rate 11 per 1000: what is the natural increase rate as a percentage?
8. Calculate the dependency ratio for 2 million aged 0-14, 3 million aged 65+ and 10 million aged 15-64.
9. Why can an HIC have a higher crude death rate than an LIC?
10. What would an excess of males aged 20 to 40 on a pyramid suggest?
11. What is a demographic dividend?

**Answers:** 1. Birth rate minus death rate. 2. Birth rate is births per 1000 population; fertility is average births per woman. 3. Female education, later marriage, contraception use, changed culture. 4. High birth rate and a youthful population. 5. Number of dependents per 100 people of working age. 6. Parents have more children to ensure some survive. 7. 7 per 1000, which is 0.7 per cent. 8. (2 + 3) / 10 x 100 = 50. 9. It has a larger share of elderly people. 10. In-migration of male workers. 11. Faster economic growth possible when a large cohort reaches working age and dependency falls.
