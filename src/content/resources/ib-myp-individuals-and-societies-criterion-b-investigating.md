This study guide teaches Criterion B: Investigating in IB MYP Individuals and Societies. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Individuals and societies guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. Criterion B is about doing research properly: asking a question that can be answered, planning how to answer it, collecting the right information and then looking back at how well the investigation worked. The guide says these are systematic research skills and processes from the humanities and social sciences.

Use it with the [Criterion B revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-b-investigating-revision-notes/) and the [Criterion B practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-b-investigating-practice/). The wider course is on the [IB MYP Individuals and Societies course hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/).

## What the guide says about Criterion B

The objective is "Investigating". Students develop systematic research skills and develop successful strategies for investigating independently and in collaboration with others. At the end of Year 5 the four strands are:

1. **Formulate** a clear and focused research question and **justify** its relevance.
2. **Formulate and follow** an action plan to investigate a research question.
3. **Use** research methods to collect and record appropriate, varied and relevant information.
4. **Evaluate** the process and results of the investigation.

Criterion B has a maximum of 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Band | i Question | ii Action plan | iii Methods and information | iv Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | clear or focused; describes relevance | limited plan, or plan not followed | limited information, not always consistent with the question | limited evaluation |
| 3-4 | clear and focused; describes relevance in detail | partial plan, somewhat followed | mostly relevant information | evaluates some aspects |
| 5-6 | clear and focused; explains relevance | substantial plan, followed | appropriate, relevant information | evaluates the process and results |
| 7-8 | clear and focused; thoroughly justifies relevance with appropriate evidence | comprehensive plan, effectively followed | appropriate, varied and relevant information | thoroughly evaluates process and results |

In Year 3 the evaluation is done "with guidance" and the question may be formulated or chosen. By Year 5 you are expected to work independently.

## Key words from the guide's glossary

- **Research question**: the methodological point of departure of an investigation, guiding its scope and nature. Clear and focused questions consider relevance, manageability, originality, ability to be assessed, availability of resources, student interest and connection with the discipline. Questions can be general statements or lines of inquiry.
- **Action plan**: the steps and information the student defines in order to complete the investigation. It might include identifying sub-questions, defining methods and sources of information, and the main stages. There is flexibility, and students can revise their plans depending on their findings.
- **Research methods**: the series of systematic steps needed to search for relevant information. They include selection of sources (type and range), questionnaires, surveys, interviews, observation, experiments, measurement, analysis of quantitative data and the formulation of questions.
- **Reflect**: think about deeply; consider.
- **Bibliography**: a list of the important sources used.

## Strand i: a clear and focused research question

Test a question against the guide's list.

| Test | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Relevance | does it matter to real people or a real issue? |
| Manageability | can I answer it in the time and with the resources I have? |
| Originality | does it ask something specific, not just repeat a textbook title? |
| Ability to be assessed | will the evidence let me reach a conclusion? |
| Resources | can I reach the sources I need? |
| Interest | do I care enough to stay with it? |
| Connection | does it belong to history, geography or economics, and the unit studied? |

**Worked example.** The topic is tourism and a coastal town. The town of Seabridge is fictional.

- Too broad: "Tourism."
- Not focused: "Is tourism good or bad?"
- Better: "How has the growth of tourism since 2010 changed daily life for residents of Seabridge?"
- Stronger: "To what extent has the growth of tourism since 2010 improved or worsened daily life for residents of Seabridge, in terms of jobs, housing costs and crowding?"

The strong version names a place, a time period and three aspects. It is focused enough to plan and to answer.

**Justifying relevance.** Give reasons, supported by evidence. For the top band the evidence should be appropriate, for example: "Tourism is the largest employer in the region according to the regional statistics office. Newspaper reports suggest that residents are concerned about housing costs. The question links to the unit on the impacts and management of tourism." Describe why the question matters, to whom, and what the evidence shows.

## Strand ii: an action plan you follow

A comprehensive action plan covers **what, how, where, when and why**.

| Part | Content for the Seabridge investigation |
|---|---|
| Sub-questions | What changed in visitor numbers? Which jobs were created? How did housing costs change? How do residents feel about crowding? |
| Methods | questionnaire to residents; interviews with two business owners and a council tourism officer; secondary data on visitor numbers and house prices; observation of crowding on the beach at set times |
| Sources | regional statistics, council reports, local newspaper archive, residents |
| Sample | 40 residents across different parts of town and age groups |
| Timeline | week 1: write questions and request interviews; week 2: gather secondary data; week 3: questionnaires and observation; week 4: analyse; week 5: write up |
| Ethics and safety | explain the purpose, keep answers anonymous, ask permission, do not observe in unsafe places |
| Presentation | report with graphs, a map and quotations |

**Following the plan.** The guide says plans can be revised depending on findings. Keep a log: what you did, what changed and why. If only 25 of 40 questionnaires were returned, say so and explain what you did next.

## Strand iii: collecting and recording information

Use methods that match the sub-questions, and **vary** them so that findings can be compared.

| Method | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire or survey | opinions and experiences of many people | leading questions, small or biased samples |
| Interview | detailed views of key people | one person's bias, time |
| Observation | behaviour in a real place | observer effect, a single day may not be typical |
| Secondary data (statistics, maps, reports) | trends over time | whether the source is reliable and up to date |
| Measurement or fieldwork | numerical data | accuracy, sampling method |

**Recording.** Keep a log of each source: who, what, when, where and why you used it. Take notes in a table with columns such as source, key information, origin and purpose, and link to a sub-question. Record quotations exactly and mark page numbers or URLs. Collect only information that helps answer the question; irrelevant material should not be recorded.

**Questionnaire tip.** Write neutral questions: "How has the number of visitors affected your daily life?" is better than "Don't you think too many visitors cause problems?". Use some closed questions that can be counted and some open ones that gather reasons.

## Strand iv: evaluating the process and results

Evaluate both **how you investigated** and **what you found**.

**Process.**

- Which methods worked well and which did not?
- Did the sample represent the town? How might that affect the results?
- Did you keep to the timeline?
- What would you change?

**Results.**

- Did you answer the research question? How fully?
- How reliable and how complete is the evidence? Do different sources agree?
- What could be investigated next?

**Worked example.** "The questionnaire reached 25 residents, mostly in the town centre, so the views of people who live on the edges of town may be missing. Interviews gave detail about business owners' views but not about residents who work elsewhere. Secondary data showed house prices rising faster than in nearby towns, which supports the residents' concerns about housing. The conclusion that tourism has raised housing costs is therefore supported, but the effect on jobs is less certain because only two business owners were interviewed. In future I would send the questionnaire online and increase the sample, and I would interview a local worker."

What makes it top band: it names specific methods, links limits to effects on the results, and suggests improvements.

## How Criterion B is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for individuals and societies, the Investigating task has 26 marks: 20 for Criterion B and 6 for Criterion A. The guide says it is difficult to replicate a whole investigation in an examination, so the task tests discrete skills such as formulating and justifying research questions, formulating action plans or sections of an action plan (identification of media, stakeholders, research methods, sources of information and presentations), and evaluating the process or results of an investigation. Students are also asked to show knowledge and understanding from their course or from source material. Criterion B is worth 20 of the 80 marks in the whole examination.

## Common errors

- A question that is a topic ("Tourism") or a yes/no question that cannot be investigated.
- Relevance described in a general statement, with no evidence.
- A plan that lists methods but gives no sources, sample, timeline or sub-questions.
- Methods that do not match the question, for example a questionnaire to answer a question about long-term change.
- Only one type of source.
- Recording material that is not relevant, or no record of where information came from.
- An evaluation that says "it went well" and no more.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion B in IB MYP Individuals and Societies?**
Criterion B is Investigating. It assesses how well you formulate a research question and justify its relevance, plan and follow an action plan, collect and record information with research methods, and evaluate the process and results.

**What makes a research question focused?**
It names what is being studied, where and when, and what aspect. It is manageable in the time available and can be answered with the evidence you can reach.

**Do I have to carry out the research in the examination?**
No. The guide says the examination tests discrete skills, such as formulating questions, plans and evaluations, rather than a full investigation.

**How many marks is Criterion B worth in the on-screen examination?**
Twenty of the 80 marks, all in the Investigating task. The guide says the distribution may vary by no more than three marks.
