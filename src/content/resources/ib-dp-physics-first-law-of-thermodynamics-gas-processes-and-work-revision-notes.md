## Key ideas

- The first law is conservation of energy for a closed system: Q = ΔU + W.
- Closed system: no mass transfer, but energy can cross as heat or work.
- Isolated system: neither mass nor energy crosses, so Q = 0, W = 0 and ΔU = 0.
- Quantitative problems use monatomic ideal gases only.

## Sign convention (Clausius)

| Quantity | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Q | heat supplied to the gas | heat removed from the gas |
| W | the gas pushes the piston out (expansion) | the piston pushes the gas in (compression) |
| ΔU | temperature rises | temperature falls |

## Formulas

| Result | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| First law | Q = ΔU + W | Rearrange to ΔU = Q − W |
| Work at constant pressure | W = PΔV | P in Pa, V in m³ |
| Work, varying pressure | area under line on P-V graph | right = positive, left = negative |
| Internal energy change | ΔU = 3/2 N kB ΔT = 3/2 nRΔT | monatomic ideal gas, ΔT in K |
| Using PV | ΔU = 3/2 (P₂V₂ − P₁V₁) | from PV = nRT |
| Adiabatic | PV^(5/3) = constant | monatomic ideal gas |
| Link | N kB = nR | N = n × Avogadro constant |

## Processes at a glance

| Process | Fixed | W | ΔU | Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Isovolumetric | V | 0 | 3/2 nRΔT | = ΔU |
| Isobaric | P | PΔV | 3/2 nRΔT | ΔU + PΔV |
| Isothermal | T | area | 0 | = W |
| Adiabatic | Q = 0 | −ΔU | 3/2 nRΔT | 0 |

Particle reasoning to quote:

- Isothermal expansion: same mean kinetic energy, but particles hit a larger wall area less often per unit time, so pressure falls.
- Adiabatic compression: the piston does work on the gas, nothing escapes as heat, random kinetic energy rises, so temperature rises.
- Adiabatic expansion: the gas does work at the expense of its own internal energy, so it cools.

## Method boxes

**First-law problem**
1. Draw or list: is the gas expanding or compressing?
2. Assign signs: W positive if expanding, Q positive if heat enters.
3. Find W (PΔV or area), then ΔU (from ΔT, or from PV values).
4. Use Q = ΔU + W for whatever is left.

**Adiabatic problem**
1. Write Q = 0, so ΔU = −W.
2. Find the missing pressure or volume from P₁V₁^(5/3) = P₂V₂^(5/3).
3. Find ΔU from 3/2 (P₂V₂ − P₁V₁), or from ΔT if n is known.
4. State W = −ΔU with its sign and meaning.

**P-V graph problem**
1. Identify each leg: vertical (W = 0), horizontal (W = PΔV), or sloping.
2. Split areas into rectangles and triangles.
3. Get ΔU between end points from P and V alone.
4. Remember ΔU depends only on end states, whereas Q and W depend on the path.

## Worked reminders

**Reminder 1 (isobaric).** 3.0 mol at 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa expand by 0.012 m³. W = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.012 = 2 400 J. Since PΔV = nRΔT here, ΔU = 3/2 × 2 400 = 3 600 J, and Q = 6 000 J. For an isobaric change of monatomic gas, Q is five halves of PΔV.

**Reminder 2 (isothermal).** A gas absorbs 330 J during an isothermal expansion. ΔU = 0, so W = 330 J.

**Reminder 3 (adiabatic).** A monatomic gas is expanded adiabatically to twice its volume. The pressure falls by a factor 2^(5/3) = 3.17, which is more than the factor 2 for an isothermal change at the same volume ratio.

**Reminder 4 (compression).** 90 J of work is done on a gas that loses 40 J of heat. W = −90 J, Q = −40 J, so ΔU = −40 + 90 = +50 J.

## Must-know distinctions

- Adiabatic is not isothermal. Adiabatic: Q = 0 and T changes. Isothermal: ΔU = 0 and heat flows.
- Closed is not isolated. A closed system can exchange energy.
- W by the gas is not W on the gas. The guide's convention is work done by the system.
- ΔU is a state change; Q and W depend on the process.

## Definitions to quote

- **Internal energy** of an ideal gas: the total random kinetic energy of its particles. There is no potential energy term because there are no intermolecular forces.
- **Closed system**: matter cannot cross the boundary, but energy can cross it in either direction, as heat or as work.
- **Isolated system**: the boundary lets through neither matter nor energy.
- **Isovolumetric, isobaric, isothermal**: volume, pressure or temperature is kept fixed.
- **Adiabatic**: no thermal energy is transferred to or from the gas.

## Reading a P-V diagram

- Moving right on the diagram means expansion and positive work. Moving left means compression and negative work.
- A horizontal line is isobaric. A vertical line is isovolumetric. A hyperbola-like curve through points with equal PV is isothermal.
- Two points with the same value of PV are at the same temperature, so ΔU between them is zero, whatever path joins them.
- A higher value of PV means a higher temperature. If PV rises, ΔU is positive; if it falls, ΔU is negative.
- An adiabatic curve falls more steeply than an isothermal curve through the same starting point.

**Reminder 5 (sloping line).** A gas goes from 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa at 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ to 6.0 × 10⁵ Pa at 3.0 × 10⁻³ m³ along a straight line. The area under the line is average pressure times ΔV: 4.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 800 J. P₁V₁ = 200 J and P₂V₂ = 1 800 J, so ΔU = 3/2 × 1 600 = 2 400 J. Then Q = 2 400 + 800 = 3 200 J.

## Command words and what they ask for

- **State** a sign or a value: give it with a unit, no explanation.
- **Calculate**: show the substitution, then the answer with a unit and sensible significant figures.
- **Explain**: link cause to effect, for example no heat transfer, so the work done changes the internal energy, so the temperature changes.
- **Sketch** a process on a P-V graph: label axes and mark the direction with an arrow.

## Quick self-test

1. A gas takes in 500 J of heat and does 320 J of work. Find ΔU.
2. 75 J of work is done on a gas with no heat transfer. Find ΔU and say whether T rises or falls.
3. A gas at 2.5 × 10⁵ Pa expands from 0.80 × 10⁻³ m³ to 3.4 × 10⁻³ m³. Find W.
4. Why is W zero in an isovolumetric process?
5. Find ΔU for 0.40 mol of monatomic gas warmed by 60 K (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).
6. State ΔU for an isothermal change and what that implies for Q and W.
7. A gas at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa is compressed adiabatically to one eighth of its volume. Find the final pressure.
8. On a P-V graph, a gas moves from (1.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa) to (4.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa). Find W and ΔU.
9. Give the Q, W and ΔU of an isolated system.
10. Explain, using particles, why a gas cools when it expands adiabatically.

### Answers

1. ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 320 = 180 J.
2. W = −75 J, Q = 0, so ΔU = +75 J. Temperature rises.
3. W = 2.5 × 10⁵ × 2.6 × 10⁻³ = 650 J.
4. The boundary does not move, so ΔV = 0 and W = PΔV = 0.
5. ΔU = 3/2 × 0.40 × 8.31 × 60 = 299 J.
6. ΔU = 0 because T is constant, so Q = W.
7. P₂ = 1.0 × 10⁵ × 8^(5/3) = 1.0 × 10⁵ × 32 = 3.2 × 10⁶ Pa.
8. W = 3.0 × 10⁵ × 3.0 × 10⁻³ = 900 J. P₁V₁ = 300 J, P₂V₂ = 1 200 J, ΔU = 3/2 × 900 = 1 350 J.
9. Q = 0, W = 0, ΔU = 0.
10. The gas does work on its surroundings while no heat enters, so the particles lose random kinetic energy and the mean kinetic energy, hence temperature, falls.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Wrong sign on W for compression, then a wrong ΔU.
- Using Celsius temperatures where T itself is needed.
- Leaving volume in cm³ or litres when pressure is in Pa.
- Writing ΔU = 0 for an adiabatic process.
- Forgetting that a vertical line on a P-V graph has no work, while ΔU still changes.
- Using PV^(5/3) for a process that is not adiabatic.
- Giving an answer to the wrong number of significant figures, or without a unit.
- Explaining temperature changes without referring to work done and heat transferred.
