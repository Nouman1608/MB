## Section A

**1.** Name **five** approaches in psychology and state the core assumption of each. **[5]**

**2.** Define the nature–nurture debate. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain the behaviourist approach.

**(a)** Distinguish between classical and operant conditioning, giving an example of each. **[6]**
**(b)** Give **two** strengths and **two** limitations of the approach. **[8]**

**4.** Explain the difference between hard determinism, soft determinism and free will, and explain why determinism creates a problem for the legal system. **[7]**

**5.** Explain the difference between idiographic and nomothetic approaches, giving an example of a method associated with each. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **four** ethical issues in psychological research and how each can be dealt with. **[8]**

## Section C

**7.** Distinguish between reductionism and holism, giving **one** strength and **one** limitation of a reductionist explanation. **[5]**

**8.** Distinguish between alpha bias and beta bias, and explain how Ainsworth's Strange Situation illustrates an **imposed etic**. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why research on the genetic basis of criminality can be scientifically defensible yet still raise ethical concerns beyond participant protection. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. Behaviourist** — all behaviour is **learned from the environment through conditioning**, and only observable behaviour should be studied [1]. **Cognitive** — behaviour is explained by **internal mental processes** that can be studied scientifically through inference [1]. **Biological** — behaviour has a **physical basis in genes, neurochemistry and brain structure** [1]. **Psychodynamic** — behaviour is driven by **unconscious conflicts and early childhood experience** [1]. **Humanistic** — people have **free will and an innate drive towards self-actualisation**, and should be studied as whole individuals [1].

**2.** The debate over the extent to which behaviour is determined by **inherited biological factors (nature)** [1] versus **experience and the environment (nurture)** [1].

**3. (a) Classical conditioning** — learning through **association**, in which a neutral stimulus repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus eventually produces the same response alone [1] [1]; e.g. Pavlov's dogs salivating to a bell [1]. **Operant conditioning** — learning through **consequences**, in which behaviour followed by reinforcement is more likely to be repeated and behaviour followed by punishment less likely [1] [1]; e.g. a rat pressing a lever for food in a Skinner box [1].
**(b) Strengths:** it is **highly scientific**, using controlled laboratory experiments with objective, measurable variables, so cause and effect can be established [1] [1]. It has produced **effective real-world applications**, such as token economies and systematic desensitisation for phobias [1] [1].
**Limitations:** it is **environmentally deterministic**, ignoring free will and conscious choice, which many find an unsatisfying account of human behaviour [1] [1]. Much of the evidence comes from **animal studies**, and humans have cognitive processes and language that rats and pigeons do not, so extrapolation is questionable [1] [1]. (Also accept: reductionist, ignores biological and cognitive factors.)

**4. Hard determinism** — all behaviour is **entirely caused by internal or external factors beyond our control**, so free will is an illusion [1]. **Soft determinism** — behaviour is **caused, but people can still exercise conscious choice within those constraints** [1]. **Free will** — people are **self-determining and can choose their own behaviour** [1].
Determinism is a problem for the legal system because the law **assumes offenders are responsible for their actions and could have chosen otherwise** [1]. If behaviour is fully determined by genes or upbringing, **holding someone morally responsible and punishing them is difficult to justify** [1]. Defences based on genetic or neurological causes have been argued in court [1], though the courts generally maintain responsibility on the grounds that a determinist defence would make all punishment unjustifiable [1].

**5. Idiographic** — focuses on the **individual as a unique case**, producing rich, detailed data rather than general laws [1]; associated with **case studies and unstructured interviews** [1]. **Nomothetic** — seeks to establish **general laws applying to everyone**, using large samples and statistical analysis [1]; associated with **experiments and questionnaires** [1].

**6.** Any four, 2 marks each: **Informed consent** — participants must know what the study involves before agreeing; dealt with by a **consent form, or presumptive/retrospective consent where full disclosure would invalidate the study** [1] [1]. **Deception** — misleading participants; dealt with by **full debriefing afterwards and approval from an ethics committee that the deception is necessary and proportionate** [1] [1]. **Protection from harm** — participants must not face greater risk than in everyday life; dealt with by **stopping the study if distress occurs and offering counselling** [1] [1]. **Right to withdraw** — participants must be told they can leave at any time and have their data removed [1] [1]. **Confidentiality** — data must be **anonymised, using numbers or initials rather than names**, and stored securely [1] [1].

**7. Reductionism** breaks a complex phenomenon down into **simpler, more basic components**, such as genes or neurochemistry (biological reductionism) or stimulus-response links (environmental reductionism) [1]. **Holism** argues that behaviour can only be understood by studying the **whole system**, not its isolated parts, since properties emerge from the interaction of components [1]. Strength of reductionism: it produces **testable hypotheses** and has led to **effective treatments**, such as drug therapies based on the biological approach [1]. Limitation: it can **oversimplify** behaviour and risks **explaining away meaning**, losing sight of the complex, real-world context in which behaviour actually occurs [1] [1].

**8. Alpha bias** **exaggerates** differences between groups (e.g. genders or cultures); **beta bias** **minimises or ignores** them [1] [1]. Ainsworth's Strange Situation was developed in the **US** and then applied elsewhere **as though it were a culturally universal measure** — an **imposed etic** [1]. Applying this US-derived measure to German infants classified a **higher proportion as insecure-avoidant**, when the behaviour actually reflected a culture that **values early independence**, not genuine attachment insecurity — the measure was biased because it assumed one culture's norm applied everywhere [2].

**9.** Such research can be conducted using **sound, rigorous scientific methods** and therefore be methodologically defensible [1]. However, ethical concern extends beyond how participants in the study itself are treated, to **how the findings will be used, who funds the research, how it is reported, and what policies it might invite** — for example, findings could be misused to justify discrimination against a group even if the original study was conducted properly [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment.
- Giving evaluation points without explaining why they matter.
- Describing free will and determinism as a simple binary.
- Naming ethical issues without saying how each is addressed.
