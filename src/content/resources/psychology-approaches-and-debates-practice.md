## Section A — short answer questions (38 marks)

**1.** Outline **one** main assumption of the learning approach. **[2]**

**2.** Saavedra and Silverman studied a child with a phobia of buttons.

**(a)** Identify the research method used in this study. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain **one** weakness of this research method, using this study as an example. **[2]**

**3.** Dement and Kleitman investigated the relationship between rapid eye movements (REM) and dreaming.

**(a)** Describe how the researchers found out whether a participant had been dreaming. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the findings of this study may lack ecological validity. **[2]**

**4.** Milgram investigated obedience.

**(a)** Explain why Milgram's main study is described as having a dependent variable but no independent variable. **[2]**
**(b)** Outline **one** ethical issue raised by this study. **[2]**

**5.** Piliavin et al. carried out a field experiment on a New York subway train.

**(a)** Outline what is meant by *diffusion of responsibility*. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe **one** difference in helping behaviour between the conditions of this study. **[2]**

**6.** Andrade investigated whether doodling helps concentration and memory.

**(a)** Describe what participants in the doodling condition were asked to do. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the findings of this study could be applied to everyday life. **[2]**

**7.** Fagen et al. trained working elephants to take part in a trunk wash. Using this study, explain the difference between a **primary** reinforcer and a **secondary** reinforcer. **[4]**

**8.** Bandura et al. observed young children after they had watched an adult model. Explain **one** ethical issue raised by the use of children in this study. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how the study by Hassett et al. relates to the nature versus nurture debate. **[3]**

**10.** Outline **one** strength of using MRI scans to measure the effects of mindfulness in the study by Hölzel et al. **[2]**

**11.** In the study by Perry et al., some participants were given oxytocin and some a placebo. Explain why a placebo condition was used. **[2]**

**12.** Pozzulo et al. tested children and adults using line-ups of cartoon and human faces. Explain what is meant by a *false positive response* in a target-absent line-up. **[2]**

**13.** Baron-Cohen et al. revised the 'Reading the Mind in the Eyes' Test. Outline what the test measures. **[2]**

## Section B — extended response questions (22 marks)

**14. (a)** Evaluate the study by Piliavin et al. in terms of **two** strengths and **two** weaknesses. At least **one** of your evaluation points must be about the use of a field experiment. **[10]**

**(b)** "Psychological research should not use animals." With reference to the studies by Hassett et al. **and** Fagen et al., evaluate this statement. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any one, fully outlined: we all begin life as a **blank slate**, and **experiences and interactions with the environment shape our behaviour**, so changes in behaviour are **directly observable** [1] [1]; **or** we learn through **operant conditioning, classical conditioning and social learning** [1], which can be understood using the **stimulus–response model** [1].

**2. (a)** A **case study** [1].
**(b)** One weakness, explained and linked to the study: a case study examines **one individual**, so findings may **not generalise** [1] — the treatment that worked for one child's button phobia, which had a particular origin, may not work for other children with the same or a different phobia [1]. *(Also creditable: data from interviews and the child's own ratings may be affected by social desirability or by the researchers' interpretation, linked to this child.)*

**3. (a)** Participants' eye movements and brain activity were recorded with **EEG** while they slept [1]; they were **woken** at intervals (for example by a doorbell) during REM or non-REM sleep and **asked to describe any dream** into a recording device, without speaking to the experimenter first [1].
**(b)** The study took place in a **sleep laboratory** with electrodes attached to the face and scalp [1], so participants' sleep and dreaming may differ from sleep at home, and being woken repeatedly is not how people normally sleep [1].

**4. (a)** A **dependent variable was measured** — the level of obedience, as the maximum voltage each participant was prepared to give [1] — but **no variable was manipulated** between conditions in the main study, because every participant experienced the same procedure [1].
**(b)** One ethical issue outlined and linked: participants were **deceived** about the purpose of the study and about the shocks being real [1], **or** they experienced **psychological harm** such as extreme stress, sweating and nervous laughter [1]; **or** the prods made the **right to withdraw** difficult to exercise [1]. Second mark for linking the issue to what happened in the study [1].

**5. (a)** When several people witness an emergency, each **feels less personal responsibility** to act [1] because the responsibility is **shared among all the bystanders**, so each is less likely to help [1].
**(b)** One difference described: the victim carrying a **cane** (apparently ill) received **spontaneous help far more often** than the victim who appeared **drunk** [1]; for example, the cane victim was helped on almost every trial, while the drunk victim was helped on about half of trials [1]. *(Also creditable: the drunk victim was more likely to be helped by someone of the same race than the cane victim was.)*

**6. (a)** Participants in the doodling condition **shaded printed shapes** on a sheet of paper [1] **while listening to a monotonous recorded telephone message** and noting the names of people coming to a party [1].
**(b)** One application, explained: doodling may help people **maintain attention during dull tasks** [1], so people in long meetings or lectures, or students in class, could be allowed to doodle rather than being discouraged from it [1].

**7.** A **primary reinforcer** satisfies a **biological need** and is rewarding in itself [1], for example the **food rewards** given to the elephants [1]. A **secondary reinforcer** has **no value in itself** but becomes rewarding by being **associated with a primary reinforcer** [1], for example the **signal used to mark the correct behaviour**, which was paired with food so that it told the elephant immediately that a reward would follow [1].

**8.** One issue explained and linked: children **cannot give informed consent** themselves [1], so consent had to come from adults, and the children could not understand that they would watch aggressive behaviour; **or** exposing young children to an adult hitting a doll may cause **distress or leave them more aggressive** afterwards, which is psychological harm [1]. Second mark for the link to the study [1].

**9.** Rhesus monkeys are assumed to be **less affected by socialisation** than children [1]. Male monkeys showed a stronger preference for **stereotypically boys' (wheeled) toys** over **stereotypically girls' (plush) toys**, similar to children [1]. This suggests sex differences in toy preference may be partly due to **nature** (for example hormones) rather than only **nurture**, since the monkeys had not been taught gender stereotypes [1].

**10.** One strength outlined: MRI gives an **objective, quantitative measure** of brain structure [1], so changes in grey matter could be compared before and after the mindfulness programme without relying only on self-report [1]. *(Also creditable: it is non-invasive; scans can be repeated to allow the longitudinal before-and-after design.)*

**11.** A placebo was used to **control for expectations** [1]: participants in both conditions received a nasal spray, so any difference in preferred interpersonal distance could be attributed to the **oxytocin itself** rather than to the experience of being given a substance [1].

**12.** In a target-absent line-up the **culprit is not present** [1]; a false positive response is when the witness **identifies someone from the line-up anyway**, choosing an innocent person rather than saying the culprit is not there [1].

**13.** It measures **theory of mind** — the ability to **attribute mental states** to other people [1] — by asking participants to choose which word best describes what a person is **thinking or feeling from a photograph of their eyes** [1].

**14. (a)** Mark as a levels-based evaluation: up to 2 marks for each of four points, plus 2 for quality of evaluation (clear, well-developed, each point applied to the study rather than to field experiments in general).
*Strengths (examples):* **high ecological validity** — real passengers on a real subway journey, so behaviour is likely to reflect real helping [2]; **large sample** of passengers across many trials, making the results more reliable [2]; **standardised procedure** — the victim collapsed at the same point in each journey and behaved the same way [2].
*Weaknesses (examples):* as a field experiment there was **less control** over extraneous variables, such as how crowded the carriage was [2]; **ethical issues** — passengers did not consent, were deceived and may have been distressed, and could not be debriefed [2]; **observational data** may be affected by observer bias or by observers missing behaviour in a busy carriage [2].
At least one point must address the field experiment; if none does, maximum 8.

**(b)** Mark by levels:
- **10–12:** a well-organised, balanced evaluation using **both** studies in detail; considers arguments for and against animal research (ethics, generalisation to humans, welfare benefits); reaches a reasoned conclusion.
- **7–9:** evaluation using both studies with some detail; some balance; a conclusion is given.
- **4–6:** some relevant points, possibly limited to one study or one side; limited evaluation.
- **1–3:** basic, mainly descriptive points with little reference to the studies.
- **0:** no creditable response.
*Indicative content:* **against** animal use — ethical cost to animals kept for research; findings from rhesus monkeys (Hassett et al.) **may not generalise** to children, whose toy preferences are strongly shaped by socialisation. **For** animal use — Hassett et al. used animals precisely because monkeys are **less affected by socialisation**, allowing a test of biological influence that could not be done with children; Fagen et al. used positive reinforcement, which **benefited the elephants' welfare** and provided an alternative to harsh traditional training, so the research improved the animals' treatment. A strong answer distinguishes research that harms animals from research that benefits them and judges the statement accordingly.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a core study under the wrong approach, or describing a study from another board's specification.
- Answering Section A questions in general terms without linking the point to the named core study.
- In Section B, listing strengths and weaknesses of a research method without applying each one to the study.
- Confusing a primary reinforcer with a secondary reinforcer, or negative reinforcement with punishment.
- Saying Milgram's main study had an independent variable.
