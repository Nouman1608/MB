These questions cover section 5.3, Irrecoverable debts, in Topic 5 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. Several start from a trial balance and notes, the Paper 2 setting for Topic 5. Amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign, and every business is invented. Teaching is in the [irrecoverable debts study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-irrecoverable-debts-4ac1-eop/) and the [irrecoverable debts revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-irrecoverable-debts-4ac1-eop-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.**
**(a)** Define an irrecoverable debt. **[1]**
**(b)** State the double entry to write off a debt of 620 owed by T. Brandt. **[2]**

**2.** Explain one reason why a business makes a provision for irrecoverable debts. **[2]**

**3.** Trade receivables are 36,500. This includes a debt of 500 that must be written off. The provision is to be 3% of trade receivables. Calculate the provision required. **[2]**

**4.** The opening provision for irrecoverable debts is 1,440. The provision required at the year end is 1,180.
**(a)** Calculate the change in the provision. **[1]**
**(b)** State the effect of the change on profit for the year. **[1]**

**5.** Distinguish between an irrecoverable debt and a provision for irrecoverable debts. **[2]**

**6.** State where each item appears in the financial statements.
**(a)** An increase in the provision for irrecoverable debts. **[1]**
**(b)** The closing balance of the provision for irrecoverable debts. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Thornbury Tools has a year end of 31 December. On 1 January 2027 the provision for irrecoverable debts was 1,650. During 2027 these debts were written off: A. Rao 540 on 14 May and D. Pike 310 on 2 November. At 31 December 2027 trade receivables, after the write-offs, were 42,000. At 31 December 2028 they were 37,500, with no write-offs in 2028. The provision is kept at 4% of trade receivables.
**(a)** Prepare the irrecoverable debts account for 2027. **[3]**
**(b)** Prepare the provision for irrecoverable debts account for 2027 and 2028. **[6]**
**(c)** Show trade receivables in the statement of financial position at 31 December 2028. **[2]**

**8.** Penrose Garden Supplies has a year end of 30 June 2028. Its trial balance includes trade receivables 74,600, irrecoverable debts 1,320 and provision for irrecoverable debts 2,900. Gross profit is 96,000 and all other expenses total 61,500. Notes: a debt of 1,100 owed by H. Lam is irrecoverable; the provision is to be 4% of trade receivables.
**(a)** Calculate irrecoverable debts for the year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the provision required and the change in the provision. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit for the year. **[2]**
**(d)** Show trade receivables in the statement of financial position. **[2]**

**9.** Ellery Fabrics has a draft profit of 27,800. These items were not dealt with: a debt of 900 is irrecoverable; trade receivables before this write-off are 45,900; the provision is to be 5% of trade receivables; the existing provision is 2,600.
**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Show that the change in net trade receivables equals the change in profit. **[2]**

**10.** The draft profit of Moorgate Cycles is 41,600. Two errors are found. First, the new provision for irrecoverable debts of 3,200 was charged in full, although the opening provision was 2,750. Second, a debt of 400 written off during the year was never transferred from the irrecoverable debts account to the income statement.
**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why charging the whole new provision overstates expenses. **[2]**

**11.** The owner of Ellery Fabrics says: "We should not make a provision, because none of our customers has told us they will not pay." Explain why the owner should make a provision, and advise the owner. **[5]**

**12.** Moorgate Cycles has trade receivables of 50,000 and an opening provision of 1,500. Past experience suggests about 3% of receivables are not collected. The owner wants to use 10% "to be safe".
**(a)** Calculate the effect on profit of using 3% and of using 10%. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether the 10% rate is consistent with the prudence concept. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A specific amount owed by a named customer that the business decides will not be received [1].
**(b)** Debit irrecoverable debts 620 [1]; credit T. Brandt (trade receivable) 620 [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Some credit customers are likely not to pay [1], so under prudence trade receivables (and profit) should not be shown at more than the business expects to collect [1]. Accruals is also accepted: the expected loss is matched with the year of the sales. **[2]**

**3.** Receivables after write-off 36,500 − 500 = 36,000 [1]; 3% × 36,000 = **1,080** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Deduct the write-off first; 3% of 36,500 (1,095) loses the second mark.

**4. (a)** 1,180 − 1,440 = a **decrease of 260** [1].
**(b)** Profit increases by 260 [1]. **[2]**

**5.** An irrecoverable debt is a known loss on a named customer, whose account is written off [1]; a provision is an estimate of possible losses on trade receivables in general, with no customer named and no customer account changed [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** In the income statement, as an expense [1].
**(b)** In the statement of financial position, deducted from trade receivables in current assets [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)**

**Irrecoverable debts**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 14 May A. Rao | 540 | 31 Dec Income statement | 850 |
| 2 Nov D. Pike | 310 | | |
| | 850 | | 850 |

A. Rao [1]; D. Pike [1]; transfer of 850 [1].
**(b)** 2027: required 4% × 42,000 = 1,680; increase 30. 2028: required 4% × 37,500 = 1,500; decrease 180.

**Provision for irrecoverable debts**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 2027 31 Dec Balance c/d | 1,680 | 2027 1 Jan Balance b/d | 1,650 |
| | | 2027 31 Dec Income statement | 30 |
| | 1,680 | | 1,680 |
| 2028 31 Dec Income statement | 180 | 2028 1 Jan Balance b/d | 1,680 |
| 2028 31 Dec Balance c/d | 1,500 | | |
| | 1,680 | | 1,680 |

1,680 calculated [1]; increase 30 on the credit side [1]; balance c/d 1,680 [1]; 1,500 calculated [1]; decrease 180 on the debit side [1]; balance c/d 1,500 [1].
**(c)** Trade receivables 37,500 less provision 1,500 [1] = **36,000** [1]. **[11]**
*Examiner insight:* Last year's closing provision is this year's opening balance; never start the second year from zero.

**8. (a)** 1,320 + 1,100 = **2,420** [1].
**(b)** Trade receivables 74,600 − 1,100 = 73,500 [1]; provision 4% × 73,500 = **2,940** [1]; increase 2,940 − 2,900 = **40** [1].
**(c)** 96,000 − 61,500 − 2,420 − 40 [1] = **32,040** [1].
**(d)** Trade receivables 73,500 less provision 2,940 [1] = **70,560** [1]. **[8]**

**9. (a)** Receivables 45,900 − 900 = 45,000 [1]; provision 5% × 45,000 = 2,250 [1]; decrease 2,600 − 2,250 = 350 [1]; profit 27,800 − 900 + 350 = **27,250** [1].
**(b)** Net receivables before: 45,900 − 2,600 = 43,300; after: 45,000 − 2,250 = 42,750 [1]. Both fall by 550 (27,800 − 27,250 = 550) [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Only the increase of 3,200 − 2,750 = 450 should be charged, so 2,750 was overcharged [1] and profit rises by 2,750 [1]. The 400 irrecoverable debt is an expense of the year, so profit falls by 400 [1]. Corrected profit 41,600 + 2,750 − 400 = **43,950** [1].
**(b)** The opening provision of 2,750 was charged as an expense in earlier years [1]; charging it again counts the same expected loss twice, so only the change in the provision is a new expense this year [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Some customers are likely not to pay even though none has said so yet; experience shows a share of receivables is not collected [1]. Under prudence, trade receivables and profit should not be overstated [1], so a provision reduces receivables to the amount expected to be collected [1]. Under accruals, the loss belongs to the year of the sales that created the debts [1]. Advice: make a provision at a rate based on experience, so that the owner and any lender see realistic figures [1]. **[5]**

**12. (a)** 3%: provision 1,500, the same as the opening balance, so no effect on profit [1]. 10%: provision 5,000, an increase of 3,500, so profit falls by 3,500 [1].
**(b)** Prudence asks for caution, so assets and profit are not overstated [1]. It does not allow deliberate understatement: 10% is far above the 3% experience suggests [1], so profit and receivables would be understated and users misled; the 3% rate is the prudent one [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Prudence uses reasonable estimates; an extreme figure is not "more prudent".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the provision before deducting a write-off.
- Charging the whole provision when there is an opening balance.
- Forgetting write-offs already in the trial balance.
- Recording a decrease in the provision on the wrong side.
- Showing the provision as a current liability.
- Treating prudence as permission to understate profit.
