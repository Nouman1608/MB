## Section A

**1.** Define the term "rain shadow". **[2]**

**2.** State two characteristics of rainfall in hot arid environments, other than its low total. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by a "subsidence inversion". **[2]**

**4.** Explain why desert surfaces cool rapidly at night. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why hot deserts on the west coasts of continents extend closer to the equator than those inland. **[3]**

**6.** Outline two reasons why hot deserts are high wind energy environments. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows data for three fictional weather stations.

| Station | Annual precipitation (mm) | Annual PET (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| Arvan | 80 | 2,400 |
| Bekka | 300 | 1,800 |
| Coroz | 520 | 1,650 |

**(a)** Calculate the aridity index (P/PET) for each station to two decimal places. **[3]**

**(b)** Using the UNEP classes, classify each station. **[3]**

**8.** Annual rainfall (mm) at the fictional desert station Tazrit over ten years was: 42, 110, 8, 65, 0, 23, 180, 51, 12, 79.

**(a)** Calculate the mean annual rainfall. **[1]**

**(b)** The standard deviation is 52.5 mm. Calculate the coefficient of variation. **[1]**

**(c)** A semi-arid station nearby has a mean of 590 mm and a coefficient of variation of 6%. Explain what the comparison shows, and suggest one consequence for people at Tazrit. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the seasonal movement of the ITCZ produces a short wet season in semi-arid areas. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how pressure and wind systems cause aridity in subtropical deserts. **[5]**

**11.** Describe and explain the global distribution of hot arid and semi-arid environments. **[6]**

**12.** "Aridity in hot deserts is caused mainly by global pressure systems rather than local factors." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** An area on the lee side of mountains that receives little rainfall [1] because air descending the mountains warms and dries after losing moisture on the windward side [1]. **[2]**

**2.** It is highly variable from year to year [1]. It falls in short, intense storms, so much runs off [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A layer of warm air aloft formed where air sinks and warms by compression [1], which caps rising air and prevents tall rain clouds forming [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Clear skies have little cloud to absorb and re-radiate longwave radiation [1]. Dry air has little water vapour to absorb outgoing radiation [1], so heat escapes freely to space and the surface temperature falls quickly [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Cold ocean currents and upwelling cool the air on west coasts [1]. Cool lower air beneath warmer air is stable, so convection is suppressed [1]. This adds to the effect of the subtropical high, so aridity continues closer to the equator, as in the Atacama-Peruvian desert [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Intense daytime heating creates steep temperature and pressure differences [1], producing strong, gusty convective winds [1]. Sparse vegetation offers little friction or shelter [1], so winds move fast across the surface and lift loose, dry sediment [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Arvan: 80 / 2,400 = 0.03 [1]. Bekka: 300 / 1,800 = 0.17 [1]. Coroz: 520 / 1,650 = 0.32 [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Arvan is hyper-arid (below 0.05) [1]. Bekka is arid (0.05-0.20) [1]. Coroz is semi-arid (0.20-0.50) [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 570 / 10 = 57 mm [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 52.5 / 57 x 100 = 92% [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Tazrit's rainfall varies far more from year to year than the semi-arid station's (92% against 6%) [1], so its mean of 57 mm is a poor guide to any one year [1]. Very dry stations have rainfall dominated by a few storms [1]. People cannot rely on rain-fed farming, so they need stored water, groundwater or flexible herding [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The ITCZ is a zone of converging trade winds and rising air [1]. It follows the overhead sun, moving poleward in the summer of each hemisphere [1]. When it moves over a semi-arid area, rising moist air produces convectional rain [1]. As it retreats towards the equator, the subtropical high returns with sinking dry air [1]. Areas at the poleward limit of its movement are under it only briefly, so they have a short wet season and a long dry season [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Air rises at the ITCZ and loses most of its moisture as rain [1]. It moves poleward at high level and sinks at about 20-30° [1]. Sinking air warms by compression, lowering its relative humidity [1]. A subsidence inversion caps convection, so rain clouds cannot form [1]. Dry trade winds blow outward from the subtropical highs over land, bringing little moisture [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Hot deserts form a broken belt between about 15° and 30° north and south [1], such as the Sahara and Kalahari, under the descending air of the subtropical highs [1]. They extend towards the equator along west coasts, as in the Namib, where cold currents add stability [1]. Some lie in rain shadows, such as the Patagonian desert east of the Andes [1]. Semi-arid belts such as the Sahel fringe the deserts, where the ITCZ brings a short wet season [1]. East coasts at similar latitudes are wetter because onshore winds bring moist air [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define aridity as a long-term water deficit (P/PET) [1]; global factors are the Hadley cell and subtropical highs, local factors include ocean currents, relief and continentality [1].

*Global pressure systems dominant:* the main desert belt lies under the subtropical highs [1]. Sinking air warms and dries, producing a subsidence inversion [1]. The Sahara and Arabian deserts are largely explained this way [1]. Semi-arid margins depend on the seasonal ITCZ [1]. Dry trade winds blowing out of the highs carry little moisture over land [1].

*Local factors important:* cold currents and upwelling explain the Atacama and Namib extending towards the equator [1]. These are among the driest places, showing local factors can intensify aridity [1]. Rain shadows create deserts outside the subtropical belt, such as Patagonia [1]. Continentality dries interior regions [1]. Coastal fog without rain shows that stability, not a lack of moisture, limits rainfall there [1].

*Interaction:* the Atacama combines subtropical high, cold current and Andean rain shadow [1]. Cold currents are part of the global ocean circulation driven by the same wind belts [1]. Local factors usually intensify rather than create aridity [1].

*Variation by scale:* at the global scale the belt pattern reflects pressure [1]. At the regional scale the location and severity of individual deserts reflect local factors [1].

*Judgement:* pressure systems are the main cause of the overall pattern [1]; local factors explain the most extreme and the exceptional deserts [1]; a reasoned final view on "how far" with named deserts [1]. **[20]**
