These notes condense **Economies and diseconomies of scale** for the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, for first assessment in 2016, Component 01, Microeconomics. In the current specification these ideas are named in the A Level (H460) costs topic, and the AS content links to them through specialisation, the division of labour and productive efficiency, so confirm the scope with your teacher. Full explanations and the Northfield Bottling example are in the [economies of scale study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-economies-and-diseconomies-of-scale-ocr-aslevel-economics/); practise with the [economies of scale practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-economies-and-diseconomies-of-scale-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scale | Size of the whole operation; changes only in the long run |
| Economies of scale | Falls in long-run average cost as scale increases |
| Diseconomies of scale | Rises in long-run average cost as scale increases beyond a point |
| Internal economies | Cost savings from the growth of the firm itself |
| External economies | Cost savings from the growth of the industry or local area |
| External diseconomies | Cost increases from the growth of the industry or area (congestion, higher rents and wages) |
| LRAC | Long-run average cost: lowest cost per unit at each output when all factors can vary |
| Minimum efficient scale (MES) | Lowest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum |
| Indivisibility | Equipment that cannot be bought in small pieces, so only large outputs use it fully |

## The quick test

| Output change | Total cost change | Result |
|---|---|---|
| +100% | +60% | LRAC falls: economies |
| +100% | +100% | LRAC constant |
| +100% | +136% | LRAC rises: diseconomies |

## Internal economies: the mnemonic "Really Fun Mums Try Making Pies"

| Letter | Economy | One-line mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| R | Risk-bearing | Many products or markets spread risk |
| F | Financial | Lower interest rates, easier finance |
| M | Marketing | Advertising spread over more units |
| T | Technical | Bigger machines, specialisation, volume and surface area |
| M | Managerial | Specialist managers |
| P | Purchasing | Bulk-buying discounts |

## Internal diseconomies

- **Communication:** more layers, slower and distorted messages.
- **Co-ordination and control:** harder to monitor many sites; waste goes unnoticed.
- **Motivation:** workers feel remote, so effort falls and absence rises.
- **Bureaucracy:** paperwork and meetings add cost without output.

## External economies and diseconomies

- Economies: skilled labour pool, nearby suppliers, shared research, local training, better infrastructure.
- Diseconomies: congestion, rising land rents, competition for skilled workers.
- Diagram: external economies shift the **whole LRAC curve down**; internal economies are a movement **along** LRAC.

## Worked reminders

**Volume and surface.** A cube with 3 m sides has surface area 54 m² and capacity 27 m³: 2 m² of material per m³, compared with 6 m² per m³ for a 1 m cube.

**Bulk buying.** A firm buying 20,000 kg at £5 a kilogram receives an 8% discount: saving = 20,000 × 5 × 0.08 = **£8,000**.

**LRAC.** £60,000 for 5,000 units (£12 each) and £90,000 for 10,000 units (£9 each): LRAC falls by 25%.

## Significance: evaluation chains

- Lower costs → lower prices for consumers **if** competition forces firms to pass them on.
- Lower costs → higher profit → more investment, but also possible market power and higher prices.
- Large MES relative to market size → few firms (cars, aircraft); small MES → many firms (hairdressers).
- Small firms survive through local markets, personal service, flexibility and niche products.
- Growth can bring diseconomies, so bigger is not always cheaper.

## Short run versus long run

| | Short-run AC | Long-run AC |
|---|---|---|
| Why it falls | Fixed costs spread, better use of the fixed factor | Economies of scale |
| Why it rises | Diminishing returns to the fixed factor | Diseconomies of scale |
| Factors | At least one fixed | All variable |

## Exam traps

- An economy of scale reduces **average** cost, not necessarily total cost.
- Cause must be a change in scale; a fall in raw material prices for everyone is not an economy of scale.
- Internal: growth of the firm. External: growth of the industry.
- Do not use diseconomies to explain rising short-run costs.
- Do not assume large firms always charge less.

## Self-test

1. Define economies of scale.
2. Output rises by 50% and total cost by 30%. What is happening to LRAC?
3. Give two internal economies of scale and explain one.
4. Give one external economy of scale.
5. Why do diseconomies of scale arise?
6. Calculate LRAC at each output: £45,000 for 3,000 units and £120,000 for 10,000 units, and say what it shows.
7. A firm buys 12,000 litres at £2.50 a litre with a 6% bulk discount. Calculate the saving.
8. Define minimum efficient scale.
9. Why might an industry with a large MES have only a few firms?
10. How is an external economy shown on an LRAC diagram?
11. Give two reasons why small firms survive despite economies of scale.
12. Why might economies of scale not lead to lower prices?

### Answers

1. Falls in long-run average cost that result from increasing the scale of production.
2. Cost rises by a smaller percentage than output, so LRAC **falls**: economies of scale.
3. Any two, for example purchasing (bulk discounts lower input prices per unit) and technical (larger machines produce more per unit of input).
4. Any one of: skilled labour pool, local suppliers, shared research, local training, infrastructure.
5. As firms grow, communication, co-ordination and motivation become harder, so waste and bureaucracy raise cost per unit.
6. **£15** at 3,000 units and **£12** at 10,000 units: LRAC falls, so the firm gains economies of scale.
7. 12,000 × 2.50 × 0.06 = **£1,800**.
8. The lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
9. Each firm must produce a large share of market output to reach lowest cost, so the market supports only a few such firms.
10. As a downward shift of the whole LRAC curve.
11. Any two of: small or local markets, personal service, flexibility, niche products, low MES in the industry.
12. A firm with few rivals may keep prices high and take the savings as profit.
