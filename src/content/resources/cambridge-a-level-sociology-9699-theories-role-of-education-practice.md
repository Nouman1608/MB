These questions cover section 5.1, *Theories about the role of education*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed on Paper 3 – Education. Section A practises short answers and explanations; Section B practises the "two arguments against" question and the essay. All schools, places and policies in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [theories about the role of education study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-theories-role-of-education/) and the [theories about the role of education revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-theories-role-of-education-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by the term "role allocation"? **[2]**

**2.** Identify the three functions Marxists claim education performs for capitalism. **[3]**

**3.** What is meant by "symbolic violence" in Bourdieu's theory of education? **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between social democratic and New Right views of who should provide education. **[4]**

**5.** Explain two criticisms of the functionalist claim that education promotes social solidarity. **[6]**

**6.** Read the item.

> In Valmora, a fictional country, every secondary pupil takes the same national citizenship course, sings the national anthem each morning, and is ranked against classmates in monthly tests. The top 10% receive prizes at a public ceremony.

Using the item, explain one way it supports functionalist views of education and one way it could be used to criticise them. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which education may legitimate class inequality, according to Marxists. **[6]**

**8.** Read the item.

> The government of Corran, a fictional state, replaces its school-leaving examination for lower-attaining pupils with a two-year "Work Ready" scheme. Trainees spend four days a week on placements in local warehouses and shops, paid a small allowance, and one day in college.

Explain two reasons why Marxist sociologists might criticise the scheme. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Education today mainly serves the needs of the economy."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Schools are effective instruments of ideological control."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate Marxist explanations of the role of education. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that market competition between schools benefits society as a whole. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** Sorting people into the occupations that best suit their talents and abilities [1], mainly through examinations and qualifications that sift pupils by merit (Davis and Moore, 1945) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Reproducing labour power, producing workers with the skills and attitudes capitalism needs [1]; legitimating inequality, making it seem fair through the myth of meritocracy [1]; transmitting ruling-class ideology such as individualism and acceptance of hierarchy [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The imposition of the dominant class's culture [1] as if it were natural good taste or intelligence (Bourdieu and Passeron) [1], so working-class pupils come to feel academic culture is not for them and may withdraw by choice rather than by force [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Social democrats argue the state should provide and fund education [1] to guarantee equal opportunity and invest in human capital for the whole population (Schultz, 1961) [1]. The New Right argues state monopoly lowers standards [1], so parents should choose between competing schools, for example through vouchers (Friedman, 1955) or open enrolment (Chubb and Moe, 1990) [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Criticism 1: Hargreaves (1982) argued that schools teach individual competition rather than solidarity [1]; constant ranking sets pupils against each other [1], and the academic focus denies many working-class pupils dignity, so they may feel excluded rather than bound in [1].
Criticism 2: Marxists argue the "shared values" are ruling-class ideology [1] passed on through the ideological state apparatus (Althusser) [1], so what looks like consensus is imposed agreement that serves capitalism [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Support: the shared citizenship course and daily anthem pass on common values [1], which Durkheim saw as building attachment to society beyond the family [1]; ranking by test results reflects Parsons's view that pupils learn status is achieved through effort [1].
Criticism: public ranking and prizes for only the top 10% encourage competition [1], which Hargreaves saw as undermining solidarity [1]; those never rewarded may feel failures, so the same practices could divide pupils rather than unite them [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Way 1: the myth of meritocracy [1]. Schools claim to reward ability, so pupils who fail see it as their own fault [1], and the class inequality behind results is hidden, as Bowles and Gintis argued [1].
Way 2: cultural reproduction [1]. Bourdieu argues schools reward middle-class cultural capital as if it were ability [1], so class advantage is converted into qualifications that look earned [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Reason 1: the scheme channels lower-attaining pupils, who are likely to be disproportionately working class, into low-paid work [1], reproducing labour power for routine jobs [1]; it removes their chance to gain the examination that could open other routes [1].
Reason 2: Finn (1987) argued British youth training schemes provided cheap labour and reduced unemployment figures [1]. Trainees on a small allowance give employers cheap workers [1], while four days of placements teach work discipline and obedience more than transferable skills, fitting the correspondence principle [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: functionalists argue that education's primary role is moral and social [1]. Durkheim saw schools as teaching discipline and attachment to the group [1]; school assemblies, citizenship lessons and shared rules build solidarity rather than output [1]. So education meets social needs the view ignores, although Marxists may call these values ideology [1].
Argument 2: pupils do not simply become the workers the economy wants [1]. Willis's lads rejected school values [1], and Giroux (1983) argues schools are sites of struggle where dominant ideas are contested [1]. Widespread disengagement suggests schooling is not efficiently geared to economic need [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: pupils resist [1]. Willis (1977) showed lads forming a counter-school culture that mocked teachers' authority [1]; Giroux argues schools are contested spaces [1]. If many pupils reject school messages, control is far from effective [1].
Argument 2: functionalists deny that the values passed on are ideological [1]. Parsons argued the school teaches achievement and equal opportunity, values that benefit everyone [1]. On this view what Marxists call ideology is genuine consensus [1], and wide public support for state schooling suggests it is not simply imposed [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content, not a points list.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** the three functions — reproducing labour power, legitimation, ideology; Althusser's ideological state apparatus; Bowles and Gintis's correspondence principle and "long shadow of work", including their argument that IQ does not explain the link between background and income; Bourdieu's cultural reproduction and symbolic violence.
- **Application (AO2):** examples of hidden curriculum practices that mirror work (punctuality, hierarchy, extrinsic rewards); vocational schemes for lower attainers; class gaps in attainment in a named country.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** neo-Marxist resistance (Willis, Giroux) — reproduction is contested; functionalist alternative — shared values benefit all; postmodern and modern workplaces may value creativity, weakening correspondence; Marxism can be hard to falsify, since both success and failure are read as reproduction; national systems differ.
- **Judgement:** for example, Marxism explains persistent class inequality well, but works best in its neo-Marxist form, which recognises pupils' agency.

**Level guidance:** top answers build a sustained argument using Marxist concepts accurately and weigh them against at least two alternative perspectives; middle answers describe the theory with limited evaluation; low answers rely on assertion.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view (AO1/AO2):** New Right reasoning — competition raises standards because schools must attract pupils (Chubb and Moe, 1990); Friedman's voucher argument (1955); published results give parents information; diversity of provision may meet varied needs; links to human capital and national economic competitiveness.
- **Against the view:** Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe (1995) — privileged/skilled choosers are mainly middle class; Brown (1990) — parentocracy replaces meritocracy; schools may select or "cream-skim" pupils likely to boost results; social democrats argue state provision is needed for equal opportunity; Marxists argue markets disguise class reproduction as consumer choice.
- **Evaluation (AO3):** distinguish average standards from the distribution of benefits; consider whether any gains reach all groups; note evidence varies between countries and types of market reform.
- **Judgement:** for example, competition may benefit some parents and schools, but "society as a whole" is harder to defend if gaps widen.

**Level guidance:** top answers apply theory to specific market mechanisms and reach a reasoned judgement on "as a whole"; middle answers list arguments for and against; low answers give unsupported opinion.
