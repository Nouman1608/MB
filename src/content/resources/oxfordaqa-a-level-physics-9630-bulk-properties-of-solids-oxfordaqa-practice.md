These questions cover **3.2.9 Bulk properties of solids** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Material data are chosen for these questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [bulk properties of solids study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-bulk-properties-of-solids-oxfordaqa/) or the [bulk properties of solids revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-bulk-properties-of-solids-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) first. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Define the elastic limit. **[1]**

**2.** What is the unit of tensile strain?

A: Pa

B: N m⁻¹

C: m

D: it has no unit

**[1]**

**3.** A cube of copper has sides of 2.0 cm and a mass of 71.7 g. Calculate the density of copper in kg m⁻³. **[2]**

**4.** A spring of spring constant 35 N m⁻¹ supports a load of 2.8 N within its limit of proportionality. Calculate the extension and the energy stored. **[2]**

**5.** A wire of diameter 0.30 mm carries a tension of 45 N. Calculate the tensile stress in the wire. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how the force–extension graph of a brittle material differs from that of a ductile material. **[2]**

**7.** On a force–extension graph, what does the area under the line up to the limit of proportionality represent?

A: the stiffness

B: the stress

C: the elastic strain energy

D: the breaking stress

**[1]**

### Section B

**8.** A student loads a spring and records these results:

| Force / N | 0 | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 | 8.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extension / mm | 0 | 16 | 32 | 48 | 70 |

**(a)** Identify the reading taken beyond the limit of proportionality, and explain how you know. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the spring constant. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the energy stored in the spring at a load of 6.0 N. **[1]**

**(d)** When the 8.0 N load is removed, the spring returns to its original length. State what this shows about the elastic limit. **[1]**

**9.** A steel wire of length 1.80 m and diameter 0.40 mm hangs vertically. A load of 50 N extends it by 3.6 mm, within the limit of proportionality.

**(a)** Calculate the tensile stress. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the tensile strain. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the elastic strain energy stored. **[1]**

**(d)** The breaking stress of this steel is 1.2 × 10⁹ Pa. Calculate the largest load the wire can support. **[1]**

**10.** A copper wire is loaded until the tension is 30 N and the extension is 6.0 mm. The area under the loading curve is 0.12 J. When the load is removed, the unloading line is straight and parallel to the initial part of the loading curve, and the wire has a permanent extension of 2.0 mm.

**(a)** Explain why the wire does not return to its original length. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the elastic strain energy recovered during unloading. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the energy used to deform the wire permanently and state what happens to it. **[2]**

**11.** A spring of spring constant 1200 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 0.040 m and used to launch a 0.060 kg ball along a smooth horizontal track, which then curves up a smooth ramp.

**(a)** Calculate the elastic potential energy stored in the spring. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the speed of the ball as it leaves the spring. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the maximum vertical height the ball reaches up the ramp. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the height reached on a real ramp is smaller. **[1]**

**12.** The stress–strain graphs of two materials are described below.

- Material X: a straight line from the origin to a stress of 900 MPa at a strain of 0.0045, where it fractures.
- Material Y: a straight line from the origin to 250 MPa at a strain of 0.0025, then a curve rising to a maximum of 400 MPa at a strain of 0.20; it fractures at 350 MPa at a strain of 0.25.

**(a)** State which material is brittle and explain your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the straight-line sections to determine which material is stiffer. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest which material is better for the crumple zone of a car, and explain why. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The maximum force (or stress) that can be applied before the object no longer returns to its original length when the force is removed [1] **[1]**

**2.** **D** [1] **[1]**

**3.** V = 2.0³ = 8.0 cm³ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³ [1]; ρ = 0.0717 ÷ 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ = **8.96 × 10³ kg m⁻³** [1] **[2]**

**4.** ΔL = 2.8 ÷ 35 = **0.080 m** [1]; energy = ½ × 2.8 × 0.080 = **0.11 J** [1] **[2]**

**5.** A = π(0.15 × 10⁻³)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]; σ = 45 ÷ 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ = **6.4 × 10⁸ Pa** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Using r = 0.30 mm gives a stress four times too small. Halve the diameter, convert to metres, then square.

**6.** A brittle material's graph is a straight line (or nearly so) up to the point of fracture [1]; a ductile material's graph shows a large plastic region, with large extension for little extra force, before it breaks [1] **[2]**

**7.** **C** [1] **[1]**

### Section B

**8.**

**(a)** **8.0 N** [1]; if extension were still proportional it would be 64 mm, but it is 70 mm [1]

**(b)** k = 2.0 ÷ 0.016 = **125 N m⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** ½ × 6.0 × 0.048 = **0.14 J** [1]

**(d)** The elastic limit had not been reached at 8.0 N, so it lies beyond the limit of proportionality [1] **[5]**

**9.**

**(a)** A = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; σ = 50 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = **4.0 × 10⁸ Pa** [1]

**(b)** ε = 3.6 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.80 = **2.0 × 10⁻³** [1]

**(c)** ½ × 50 × 3.6 × 10⁻³ = **0.090 J** [1]

**(d)** F = 1.2 × 10⁹ × 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = **150 N** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Stress ÷ strain here is 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, the Young modulus you will meet in 3.2.10. Checking that a steel wire gives about this value is a useful sense-check.

**10.**

**(a)** It was loaded beyond its elastic limit, so it was deformed plastically [1]

**(b)** Elastic extension recovered = 6.0 − 2.0 = 4.0 mm [1]; energy = ½ × 30 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ = **0.060 J** [1]

**(c)** 0.12 − 0.060 = **0.060 J** [1]; transferred to internal energy of the wire (it warms slightly) [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The unloading triangle uses the elastic part of the extension only, not the full 6.0 mm.

**11.**

**(a)** ½ × 1200 × 0.040² = **0.96 J** [1]

**(b)** ½ × 0.060 × v² = 0.96 [1]; v = √32 = **5.7 m s⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** h = 0.96 ÷ (0.060 × 9.81) = **1.6 m** [1]

**(d)** Friction and air resistance do work against the ball, transferring some energy to internal energy, so less is available as gravitational potential energy [1] **[5]**

**12.**

**(a)** **X** [1]; its graph is a straight line up to fracture, with no plastic region [1]

**(b)** Gradient of X = 900 × 10⁶ ÷ 0.0045 = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa; gradient of Y = 250 × 10⁶ ÷ 0.0025 = 1.0 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]; **X is stiffer** (steeper gradient) [1]

**(c)** **Y** [1]; it has a large plastic region, so it can absorb a large amount of energy in permanent deformation before breaking, reducing the energy transferred to the occupants [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* X has the larger breaking stress, so it is stronger, but strength is not what a crumple zone needs. Answer the property the question is about.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using diameter as radius, or forgetting that mm² to m² is 10⁻⁶.
- Unloading energy found from the whole extension instead of the elastic part.
- Confusing stiffness (gradient) with strength (breaking stress).
- Saying the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are always the same point.
