## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Identify the **audience**, **purpose** and **form** in this task: *"Write an article for a local newspaper in which you argue for or against building a skate park on the town's playing field."* **[3]**

**2.** Rewrite this sentence in a formal register suitable for a letter to a council officer: *"Loads of people round here reckon the bins are a total joke."* **[2]**

**3.** Add a suitable discourse marker to link each pair of sentences, and name the relationship it shows: (a) *The trip was expensive. Most families paid in full.* (b) *The bridge was closed. Traffic built up across the town.* **[4]**

**4.** Identify and correct the consistency error in this narrative extract: *"I walked into the hall and the noise hit me. Everyone is shouting and nobody notices me. I found a seat at the back."* **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between **coherence** and **cohesion** in writing. **[2]**

**6.** Give **three** conventions of a speech that would not normally appear in a newspaper article. **[3]**

**7.** Explain what is ambiguous about the pronoun in this sentence and rewrite it clearly: *"When the head teacher met the new caretaker, he said he was worried about the boiler."* **[2]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** Put these sentences into a coherent order for one paragraph of an argument, and explain your order. **[4]**

> *A. As a result, many pupils arrive at their first lesson already tired.*
> *B. Starting the school day later would improve learning.*
> *C. Teenagers' sleep patterns tend to shift later during adolescence.*
> *D. A later start would let lessons begin when pupils are more alert.*

**9.** Read this original draft paragraph from a letter to a head teacher.

> *Our school should have more clubs. Also clubs are fun. Also my friend likes chess. Also some people can't do
> sport. Also it would be good.*

Rewrite the paragraph so that it is clear and coherent, with a topic sentence, developed reasons and varied cohesive devices. **[6]**

**10.** You are given this information by a charity:

> *Notes: volunteers needed Saturday mornings; community garden on Elm Road; no experience needed; tools provided;
> wear old clothes; sign up at the library.*

Use the information to write the opening of a **leaflet** aimed at teenagers. Do not copy the notes as a list. **[6]**

**11.** Plan a five-paragraph structure for this task: *"Write a speech for a school assembly arguing that every student should learn to cook."* Give a one-line focus for each paragraph and explain how the ending will link to the opening. **[6]**

**12.** Read this original opening of a story written for a Paper 1-style task.

> *The station was empty. I stand on the platform and wait. You could hear the wind. Suddenly a train arrived and I get
> on it and it was full of people and they all look at me.*

Identify **three** problems of clarity or consistency, and rewrite the opening to fix them. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Audience: readers of a local newspaper, including residents of all ages [1]. Purpose: to argue a viewpoint for or against the skate park [1]. Form: a newspaper article [1].

**2.** A formal rewrite, for example: "Many local residents feel that the current waste collection service is unsatisfactory" [1], with slang and exaggeration ("loads", "reckon", "a total joke") removed [1].

**3.** (a) "However" or "Nevertheless" [1], showing contrast [1]. (b) "As a result" or "Consequently" [1], showing cause and effect [1].

**4.** The tense shifts from past ("walked", "hit") to present ("is shouting", "notices") and back [1]. Corrected: "Everyone was shouting and nobody noticed me" [1].

**5.** Coherence is whether the text makes logical sense as a whole, with ideas in a sensible order [1]; cohesion is the set of linguistic devices, such as discourse markers and pronoun reference, that link sentences and paragraphs [1].

**6.** Any three of the following, one mark each (3 × [1]): direct address to listeners, such as "Good morning, everyone"; spoken signposting, such as "My second point is..."; repetition or a refrain to help listeners remember; a closing appeal to the audience present. Headlines and subheadings belong to written forms and are not credited.

**7.** "He" could refer to either the head teacher or the caretaker [1]. Clear rewrite, for example: "When the head teacher met the new caretaker, the caretaker said he was worried about the boiler" [1].

**8.** Order: B, C, A, D [1]. B is the topic sentence stating the claim [1]; C gives the cause [1]; A follows from C, signalled by "As a result", and D returns to the claim with the solution, closing the paragraph [1].

**9.** Award up to 6 marks:
- a clear topic sentence stating the request, for example "I am writing to ask you to consider expanding the range of lunchtime clubs" [1];
- at least two reasons developed rather than listed, for example inclusion of students who do not enjoy sport [1] and the skills clubs build [1];
- the anecdote about the friend either removed or used purposefully as an example [1];
- varied cohesive devices, such as "in addition", "for example" and "consequently", replacing repeated "also" [1];
- formal register suitable for a head teacher, with vague phrases such as "it would be good" made precise [1].

**10.** Award up to 6 marks:
- an engaging title or opening suited to teenagers, for example "Got a spare Saturday morning? Grow something." [1];
- register appropriate to the audience: direct, friendly and accessible [1];
- information integrated into sentences rather than copied as notes [1];
- all key details included accurately: Saturday mornings, Elm Road, no experience, tools provided, old clothes, sign up at the library [1];
- leaflet conventions such as subheadings or a clear call to action [1];
- accurate spelling and punctuation [1].

**11.** One mark for each of five paragraph focuses in a logical sequence, and one for the link between ending and opening (6 × [1]). For example: (1) opening anecdote of a student unable to boil an egg, then the claim; (2) health: cooking from fresh ingredients; (3) money: cooking saves on takeaways; (4) counter-argument that the timetable is full, rebutted by the life-long value of the skill; (5) conclusion returning to the egg, now cooked confidently, with a call to the head teacher and students. The ending echoes the opening anecdote to give the speech a circular shape.

**12.** Three problems, one mark each: tense shifts between past and present ("was", "stand", "arrived", "get") [1]; inconsistent point of view, moving from "I" to "You could hear" [1]; an over-long run-on sentence linked by "and", with "Suddenly" announcing rather than creating surprise [1]. A rewrite that keeps one tense [1], keeps the first-person viewpoint [1] and controls sentence boundaries for effect [1]. For example: "The station was empty. I stood on the platform and waited, listening to the wind. When the train finally arrived, I stepped on board. Every seat was taken, and every face turned towards me."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing the audience or form the task specifies.
- Listing points with repeated "also" instead of developing them.
- Slipping between tenses or narrative persons.
- Copying given information instead of reshaping it.
- Pronouns that could refer to more than one person.
