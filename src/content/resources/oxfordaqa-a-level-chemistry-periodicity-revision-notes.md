## Words you must use precisely

- **Proton number (Z):** sets an element's place in the Periodic Table, and so its block.
- **Block:** named after the sub-shell (s, p, d or f) holding the highest-energy electron.
- **Period:** a row. In Period 3, outer electrons occupy the third shell.
- **First ionisation energy:** the energy for a mole of gaseous atoms to each lose one electron, making a
  mole of gaseous 1+ ions. Pattern: M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻
- **Nuclear charge:** the positive charge of the nucleus; it goes up by one with each proton added.
- **Shielding:** the reduction in attraction an outer electron feels because of the electrons in inner
  shells.
- **Metallic bonding:** attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions in a lattice.
- **Macromolecular (giant covalent):** atoms linked by covalent bonds in a continuous network.
- **Van der Waals forces:** induced dipole–dipole forces, the only forces between non-polar molecules or
  noble gas atoms.

## 3.2.1.1 Blocks

| Block | Where in the table | Sub-shell being filled | Period 3 members |
|---|---|---|---|
| s | Groups 1 and 2, plus H and He | ns | Na, Mg |
| p | Groups 3 to 0 (13 to 18), except He | np | Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar |
| d | between Groups 2 and 3 | (n − 1)d | none |
| f | lanthanide and actinide rows | (n − 2)f | none |

### Method: classifying any element

1. Use the proton number (in brackets in the table below) to place the element, or build its configuration in the usual sequence (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p).
2. Identify the sub-shell that receives the last electron in that order.
3. That sub-shell's letter is the block.

**Watch for:** helium (s block although it sits in Group 0); elements Z = 31 to 36 (p block although 3d¹⁰
appears in the configuration); chromium and copper (d block despite ending 4s¹); any lanthanide or actinide
(f block from its position alone).

## Atomic radius: the three-link chain

**Trend:** decreases from Na to Cl.

| Link | What to write |
|---|---|
| 1 | Proton number, and so nuclear charge, increases across the period |
| 2 | Outer electrons stay in the third shell, behind the same inner electrons, so shielding is similar |
| 3 | So the nucleus pulls the outer electrons in nearer |

A comparison of two atoms needs all three links, with the proton numbers quoted.

## First ionisation energy

**Trend:** general increase, Na lowest (496 kJ mol⁻¹), Ar highest (1521 kJ mol⁻¹).

**General rise:** the radius chain again, then one more link. The outer electron is gripped more tightly, so
more energy is needed to remove it.

**The two exceptions:**

| Drop | Reason in one line |
|---|---|
| Mg (738) → Al (578) | Al loses a 3p electron, higher in energy than Mg's 3s electron and partly shielded by 3s² |
| P (1012) → S (1000) | In S, one 3p orbital is doubly occupied; repulsion within that pair makes one electron easier to take |

### Worked reminder

Why is the first ionisation energy of phosphorus higher than that of silicon?
P has 15 protons and Si 14; both lose a 3p electron from the third shell with similar shielding; P is
smaller. So P's outer electron is held more strongly. Note this is a normal step in the trend, not one of
the exceptions.

## Melting point

**Order, highest to lowest (data above):** Si > Al > Mg > S > Na > P > Cl > Ar.

| Elements | Structure | Melting overcomes | Why the values fall where they do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | giant metallic | attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons | ion charge 1+, 2+, 3+; delocalised electrons per atom 1, 2, 3; ions smaller; so bonding strengthens Na → Al |
| Si | macromolecular | many strong covalent bonds | highest in the period |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | molecular | van der Waals forces between molecules (covalent bonds stay intact) | more electrons per molecule, stronger forces: S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ |
| Ar | single atoms | van der Waals forces between atoms | fewest electrons per particle, lowest in the period |

### Method: any two-element melting point comparison

1. Name each element's structure.
2. State what has to be overcome or broken in each.
3. Compare the strength of those forces or bonds, with a reason (charge, electrons, number of bonds).
4. Finish with energy: stronger attraction means more energy needed, so a higher melting point.

### Worked reminder

Why does magnesium melt at a much higher temperature than argon?
Magnesium is giant metallic: melting must overcome strong attraction between Mg²⁺ ions and delocalised
electrons. Argon is made of single atoms: only weak van der Waals forces between atoms are overcome. Much
less energy is needed for argon (84 K) than for magnesium (923 K).

## Must-know distinctions

| Keep apart | Because |
|---|---|
| Block and group | Block is a sub-shell type; group is a column (outer electron count for s and p blocks) |
| Aluminium's block and its bonding | Al is p block but metallic; block does not tell you the structure |
| Covalent bonds in Si | Broken on melting |
| Covalent bonds in P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | Not broken on melting; only forces between molecules are overcome |
| Trend in radius across a period | Falls (same shell, more protons) |
| Trend in radius down a group | Rises (more shells), covered in 3.2.2 for Group 2 |
| Ionisation energy peak | Argon |
| Melting point peak | Silicon |

## Quick self-test

1. State the block of the element with Z = 13.
2. Copper (Z = 29) has the configuration [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. State its block.
3. Krypton (Z = 36) is a noble gas. State its block.
4. Give the first ionisation equation for chlorine, including state symbols.
5. Which atom is larger, aluminium or sulfur? Give the main reason.
6. Name the only p-block element in Period 3 with metallic bonding.
7. Explain why the first ionisation energy falls from phosphorus to sulfur.
8. What is overcome when argon melts?
9. Arrange P₄, S₈ and Cl₂ in order of increasing melting point.
10. Why is silicon's melting point higher than magnesium's?
11. Using the data table, state by how much the melting point rises from sodium to magnesium.

### Answers

1. p block (aluminium, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹: the last electron enters 3p).
2. d block. It sits in the d block, where 3d is being filled; the 4s¹ ending does not change that.
3. p block (4p⁶).
4. Cl(g) → Cl⁺(g) + e⁻
5. Aluminium. Sulfur has 16 protons against 13, with outer electrons in the same shell and similar
   shielding, so its outer electrons are pulled closer.
6. Aluminium.
7. In sulfur one 3p orbital holds a pair of electrons. They repel each other, so one is easier to remove.
8. Van der Waals forces between argon atoms.
9. Cl₂ < P₄ < S₈ (34, 60 and 128 electrons per molecule).
10. Silicon is macromolecular, so melting breaks many strong covalent bonds. Magnesium only needs the
    metallic bonding between Mg²⁺ ions and delocalised electrons to be overcome, which takes less energy.
11. 923 − 371 = 552 K.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying atoms gain shells, or gain shielding, as you move from sodium to chlorine.
- Quoting "more protons" for a radius or ionisation energy comparison with no mention of the same shell.
- Calling the Mg → Al and P → S drops "anomalies" without giving the sub-shell or orbital reason.
- Saying the covalent bonds in P₄, S₈ or Cl₂ break on melting.
- Describing silicon as having intermolecular forces at all.
- Linking van der Waals strength to "bigger atoms" with no reference to the number of electrons.
- Writing metallic bonding as "atoms held together by electrons" without positive ions.
- Assuming aluminium is non-metallic because it is in the p block.
- Writing an ionisation equation with a solid on the left, or with two electrons removed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which block is helium in?**
Helium is in the s block, even though it sits in Group 0, because its electrons are in the 1s sub-shell. Chromium and copper are d block despite their configurations ending in 4s¹.

**What is the melting point order of the Period 3 elements?**
Using the rounded data on this page, the order from highest to lowest is Si > Al > Mg > S > Na > P > Cl > Ar. Silicon is giant covalent, while argon has only weak van der Waals forces between single atoms.

**Why do P₄, S₈ and Cl₂ have low melting points?**
They are molecular, so melting only overcomes weak van der Waals forces between molecules; the covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact. More electrons per molecule give stronger van der Waals forces, which is why S₈ melts higher than P₄.

**What do you need in a two-atom radius comparison?**
Give all three links: quote the proton numbers to show the nuclear charge difference, say the outer electrons are in the same shell with similar shielding, and conclude that the outer electrons are attracted more strongly and held closer.
