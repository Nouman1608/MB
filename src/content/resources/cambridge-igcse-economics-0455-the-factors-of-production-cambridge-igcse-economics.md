## 1.2.1 The four factors in more depth

### Land

Land is **all natural resources**: the earth's surface, and what lies under it, grows on it, lives in the sea and comes from the sky, such as minerals, forests, fish, rivers, sunlight and wind. Its reward is **rent**.

**Nature of land.**

- It is a **gift of nature**: no one produced it.
- Some land resources are **renewable** (fish, forests, wind) if they are not used faster than they regenerate.
- Others are **non-renewable** (oil, coal, metal ores): once used, they are gone.
- The total area of land is largely fixed, but its quality can be changed by people.

### Labour

Labour is **human effort, physical and mental, used in production**. Its reward is **wages** (or salaries).

**Nature of labour.**

- It can be **skilled** (a surgeon, an electrician) or **unskilled** (a fruit picker with no training needed).
- It cannot be separated from the worker: a worker must be present, and has preferences about where to live and what to do.
- Its productivity depends on education, training, health and experience.
- The quantity of labour depends on the size of the population of working age and how many of them choose to work.

### Capital

Capital is **goods made by people that are used to produce other goods and services**: machines, tools, factories, offices, vehicles, computers and also infrastructure such as roads and ports. Its reward is **interest**.

**Nature of capital.**

- It is **man-made**, so producing it uses other resources, which means giving up consumer goods today for more output later.
- It **wears out** (depreciates) and must be maintained or replaced.
- Some capital is **specific** to one use (a printing press); some is **general** (a delivery van).
- **Money is not capital** in the economic sense. Money buys capital; the machines it buys are the factor.

### Enterprise

Enterprise is **the organising of the other factors and the taking of the risks of production**. The person who supplies it is the **entrepreneur**. Its reward is **profit**.

**Nature of enterprise.** The entrepreneur:

- decides what to produce and how;
- brings together land, labour and capital;
- bears the risk of losses if the business fails;
- often introduces new products or methods.

Profit is uncertain: it is what remains after the other factors are paid, so it may be negative.

## The same item, different factor

Classification depends on **use**, not on the item itself.

| Item | In this use, it is... | Because |
|---|---|---|
| A car used by a family for holidays | A consumer good, not a factor | It satisfies a want directly |
| The same model used as a taxi | Capital | It is used to produce a service |
| A river in its natural state | Land | A natural resource |
| A canal dug for barges | Capital | It is man-made and used in production |
| A shop owner who runs the business and takes the risk | Enterprise | She organises and bears the risk |
| The same person serving customers in her shop | Also labour | She supplies effort too; small-business owners often supply both |

**Worked example: a beach hotel.** Identify each factor and its reward.

- The plot of land by the sea and the fresh water from its well: **land**, rewarded by rent (if rented) or an equivalent return to the owner.
- Receptionists, cooks and cleaners: **labour**, paid wages.
- The building, kitchen equipment, minibus and booking system: **capital**, rewarded by interest on the money borrowed to buy them.
- The owner who opened the hotel, chose its location and risked her savings: **enterprise**, rewarded by profit.

## 1.2.2 Mobility of the factors

**Mobility** is the ease with which a factor can move. There are two kinds:

- **Geographical mobility**: moving from one place to another.
- **Occupational mobility**: moving from one use or job to another.

### Influences on mobility, factor by factor

**Land** cannot move geographically at all. Its occupational mobility depends on its nature and on rules: a field can switch from wheat to maize easily, but turning it into a car park may need planning permission, and a mountainside cannot become farmland.

**Labour.**

- Geographical: cost of moving and of housing in the new area; family and social ties; lack of information about jobs elsewhere; language and cultural differences; immigration rules between countries.
- Occupational: skills and qualifications needed for the new job; cost and time of retraining; age, since older workers may find retraining harder or have fewer years to benefit; licensing rules, such as for doctors.

**Capital.** General capital such as vans, computers and office furniture can be moved and reused easily. Specific capital such as a steel furnace or an oil rig is hard to move or to convert. Large fixed structures cannot move at all.

**Enterprise** is usually the most mobile factor: entrepreneurial skills of organising and risk-taking can be applied in many industries and places. Limits include lack of knowledge of a new market, the finance needed and different rules in other countries.

### Why mobility matters

When demand changes, resources need to move from shrinking industries to growing ones. If a coal mine closes, miners who cannot retrain or move remain unemployed even if there are vacancies in a growing industry elsewhere. Low mobility therefore wastes resources. Higher mobility helps an economy adapt and use its resources fully.

## 1.2.3 Changes in quantity and quality

**Quantity** is how much of a factor is available. **Quality** is how productive each unit is.

### Causes of changes, with fresh examples

**Land.**

- Quantity rises: discovery of new mineral deposits; land reclaimed from the sea; new farmland from irrigation of desert.
- Quantity falls: overfishing reduces fish stocks; oil wells run dry; rising sea levels flood coastal land.
- Quality changes: irrigation and fertiliser raise yields; soil erosion and pollution lower them.

**Labour.**

- Quantity rises: a larger population of working age; immigration; more people choosing to work (for example, more women joining the labour force, or later retirement).
- Quantity falls: emigration of workers; an ageing population with more people retiring; longer time spent in education.
- Quality rises: better education and training; better healthcare and nutrition; experience.
- Quality falls: poor health; skills becoming out of date when technology changes.

**Capital.**

- Quantity rises: firms invest in new machines and buildings faster than old ones wear out.
- Quantity falls: equipment wears out and is not replaced; destruction by war or natural disaster.
- Quality rises: new technology, such as faster computers or automated machines.

**Enterprise.**

- Quantity rises: low taxes on profit; easy access to loans; simple rules for starting a business; a culture that rewards risk.
- Quantity falls: high taxes on profit; heavy regulation; difficulty borrowing.
- Quality rises: business education, mentoring, experience.

### Worked example: a fishing town

Port Selwyn's fishing fleet has 40 boats. Over five years, overfishing cuts the fish stock, so the average catch per boat falls from 120 tonnes to 90 tonnes a year. At the same time, the town replaces 10 old boats with new boats fitted with better equipment that each catch 30% more than an old boat.

- Before: 40 × 120 = 4,800 tonnes.
- After: old boats 30 × 90 = 2,700 tonnes; new boats 10 × (90 × 1.3) = 10 × 117 = 1,170 tonnes.
- Total after: 2,700 + 1,170 = 3,870 tonnes, a fall of 930 tonnes.

The **quantity of land** (fish stock) fell, while the **quality of capital** (better boats) rose. The improvement in capital was not enough to offset the fall in the natural resource. A strong answer would add that more efficient boats could make overfishing worse, reducing the quantity of land further.

## Common mistakes

- Calling money capital.
- Forgetting that the same item can be capital or a consumer good, depending on use.
- Describing mobility without stating whether it is geographical or occupational.
- Confusing quantity (more workers) with quality (better-trained workers).
- Giving "profit" as the reward of every factor.

## Exam technique

| Command word | What it needs in 1.2 |
|---|---|
| Define | The precise meaning of a factor, with its reward if asked |
| Identify or State | Name the factor shown in an example |
| Describe | The features of a factor, such as its nature |
| Explain | A cause and an effect: why a factor is immobile, or how a change raises output |
| Analyse | A chain linking a change in a factor to output and the economy |
| Discuss | Both sides, for example whether more labour will raise output, with a conclusion |
