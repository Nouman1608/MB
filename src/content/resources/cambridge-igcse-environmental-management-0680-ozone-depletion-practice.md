## Section A

**1.** State the full name of CFCs. **[1]**

**2.** State the **two** uses of CFCs given in the syllabus as the cause of ozone depletion. **[2]**

**3.** State what a cataract is. **[1]**

**4.** Name the international agreement of 1987 that phased out CFCs. **[1]**

**5.** Give **one** alternative to CFCs used in aerosol products. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why CFCs, which are unreactive at ground level, can still damage the ozone layer. **[3]**

**7.** Describe how ozone depletion can lead to reduced crop yields. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows the number of new cases of skin cancer per 100,000 people in one country.

| Year | Cases per 100,000 |
|---|---|
| 1980 | 12 |
| 2000 | 21 |

(a) Calculate the percentage increase.
(b) Explain how ozone depletion could contribute to this increase.
(c) Suggest **one** other reason for the increase. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why an international agreement was needed to tackle ozone depletion, rather than action by individual countries. **[2]**

**10.** A town is replacing thousands of old refrigerators. Describe how the CFCs in them should be disposed of safely, and explain why this matters. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why replacing CFCs with HFCs solved one environmental problem but contributed to another. **[3]**

**12.** The table shows ozone measured above a research station each October (units of ozone thickness).

| Year | Ozone |
|---|---|
| 1975 | 280 |
| 1995 | 120 |
| 2015 | 135 |

(a) Calculate the percentage fall between 1975 and 1995.
(b) Explain why ozone recovered only slightly between 1995 and 2015, even though CFC production had largely stopped. **[4]**

**13.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of a CFC ban and the safe disposal of CFCs as strategies to manage ozone depletion. **[6]**

**14.** Under the international agreement on ozone, developing countries were given more time to phase out CFCs and received financial help to switch. Suggest **three** reasons why this was done. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Chlorofluorocarbons [1].

**2.** Aerosols [1]; refrigerants [1].

**3.** A clouding of the lens of the eye that impairs sight [1].

**4.** The Montreal Protocol [1].

**5.** A pump-action spray, a roll-on product or a propellant gas that contains no chlorine [1].

**6.** Because CFCs are unreactive, they last a long time in the atmosphere and drift up into the stratosphere [1]; there, ultraviolet radiation breaks them apart and releases chlorine [1]; chlorine destroys ozone, and each chlorine atom can break down many ozone molecules [1].

**7.** Less ozone means more ultraviolet radiation reaches the Earth's surface [1]; ultraviolet radiation damages plant leaves and cells [1]; this reduces photosynthesis and growth, so crops produce lower yields [1].

**8. (a)** Increase = 21 − 12 = 9 [1]; 9 / 12 × 100 = 75% [1].
**(b)** Ozone depletion lets more ultraviolet radiation reach the surface [1]; ultraviolet radiation damages skin cells, which increases the risk of skin cancer [1].
**(c)** Any one: people spend more time sunbathing or on holiday in sunny places; better diagnosis and reporting; an ageing population [1].

**9.** CFCs released in any country spread through the whole atmosphere and damage the ozone layer everywhere [1]; one country acting alone would gain little if others kept producing CFCs, so all producers had to agree to phase out together [1].

**10.** The refrigerators should be collected rather than dumped [1]; the CFC refrigerant and CFC-containing foam should be removed before scrapping and destroyed at high temperature or stored securely [1]; otherwise the CFCs leak into the air and continue to deplete the ozone layer [1].

**11.** HFCs do not contain chlorine, so they do not deplete ozone [1]; however, HFCs are powerful greenhouse gases [1], so their increasing use adds to global warming and climate change [1].

**12. (a)** Fall = 280 − 120 = 160 [1]; 160 / 280 × 100 = 57.1% [1].
**(b)** CFCs already released remain in the atmosphere for decades [1], so they continued to release chlorine and destroy ozone after production stopped, and recovery is slow [1].

**13.** CFC ban: benefit, stops new CFCs entering the atmosphere, so the cause of the damage is removed [1]; benefit, practical alternatives were available, so the ban could be followed [1]; limitation, illegal production and smuggling can continue where monitoring is weak [1]; limitation, some substitutes such as HFCs are greenhouse gases [1]. Safe disposal: benefit, captures CFCs still held in old fridges and air-conditioners [1]; limitation, it needs collection systems and specialist plants that are costly, so some appliances are still dumped [1].

**14.** Any three, 1 mark each: developing countries needed affordable refrigeration for food and medicines, and alternatives were more expensive at first [1]; developed countries had released most of the CFCs, so it was seen as fair for them to act first and pay [1]; financial help and extra time persuaded more countries to join, which was essential because CFCs from any country damage the ozone layer everywhere [1]. Also accept: developing countries needed time to train technicians and build new factories for substitutes.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Linking ozone depletion to carbon dioxide or to global warming.
- Writing "radiation" without making clear it is ultraviolet radiation reaching the surface.
- Forgetting crop yields as one of the three impacts.
- Saying HFCs damage the ozone layer; they are a climate problem, not an ozone one.
- Ignoring the long lifetime of CFCs when explaining slow recovery.
- Treating every country as able to act at the same speed; agreements succeed partly by giving poorer countries time and support.
