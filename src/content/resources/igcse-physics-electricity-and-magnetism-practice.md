This set covers current, potential difference and resistance, series and parallel circuits, electrical energy, mains safety, magnetism and electromagnetic induction. Work in order of difficulty: questions 1 to 8 are short, questions 9 and 10 are calculations, and questions 11 and 12 are longer. Write every formula before you substitute, and include units in each answer. Mark your own work strictly. Questions 11 and 12 give one mark per point so you can self-mark; the real exam marks long answers as a whole.

## Questions

**1.** A resistor has a potential difference of 12 V across it and a current of 0.40 A in it. Calculate its resistance. **[2]**

**2.** A 4.0 Ω resistor and a 6.0 Ω resistor are connected in series to a 5.0 V battery.

**(a)** Calculate the combined resistance. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the current in the circuit. **[1]**

**3.** Compare how the current behaves at different points in a series circuit with how it behaves in a parallel circuit. **[2]**

**4.** A 2.0 kW kettle is switched on for 3.0 minutes. Calculate the energy it transfers, in joules. **[2]**

**5.** *(Extended)* Describe how to make a simple electromagnet stronger. Give **two** ways. **[2]**

**6.** A student pushes the north pole of a bar magnet into a coil of wire. The coil is connected to a sensitive centre-zero voltmeter.

**(a)** State what the student sees on the voltmeter while the magnet moves into the coil. **[1]**

**(b)** The magnet is then held still inside the coil. State the reading on the voltmeter. **[1]**

**(c)** The magnet is now pulled out of the coil. State what happens to the reading. **[1]**

**7.** A student has three metal bars that look the same. One is a permanent magnet, one is unmagnetised iron and one is copper. Describe how the student can identify each bar using a bar magnet with labelled poles. **[3]**

**8.** **(a)** State what is meant by a magnetic field. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how a small compass is used to find the direction of the magnetic field at a point near a bar magnet. **[1]**
**(c)** A scrap-yard crane uses an electromagnet with a soft-iron core. Explain why the core is made of soft iron rather than steel. **[2]**

**9.** *(Extended)* A 12 V battery is connected across two resistors in parallel. Resistor X has a resistance of 20 Ω and resistor Y has a resistance of 30 Ω.

**(a)** State the potential difference across each resistor. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the current in X and the current in Y. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the current in the battery. **[1]**

**(d)** Use your answer to (c) to calculate the resistance of the whole circuit. **[1]**

**(e)** Explain why the resistance of the whole circuit is smaller than the resistance of X. **[1]**

**10.** *(Extended)* A lamp is marked 6.0 V. It is connected to a 6.0 V battery and the current in it is 0.25 A. The lamp stays on for 2.0 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the charge that passes through the lamp. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the electrical energy transferred in the lamp. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the power of the lamp. **[1]**

**11.** A student investigates how the current in a filament lamp depends on the potential difference across it.

**(a)** Describe how the student can collect a set of readings. Include the apparatus and the measurements. **[4]**

**(b)** At 2.0 V the current is 0.20 A. At 6.0 V the current is 0.40 A. Calculate the resistance of the lamp at each potential difference. **[2]**

**(c)** *(Extended)* Explain why the two resistances are different. **[2]**

**12.** A heater is rated 2300 W and works from 230 V mains. It has a metal case and a three-pin plug. The plug can be fitted with a 3 A, 5 A or 13 A fuse.

**(a)** Calculate the normal current in the heater. **[2]**

**(b)** State which fuse should be fitted and give a reason. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the fuse is connected in the live wire. **[2]**

**(d)** The live wire becomes loose and touches the metal case. Explain how the earth wire and the fuse make the heater safe. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** *R* = *V* ÷ *I* [1] = 12 ÷ 0.40 = **30 Ω** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the mark for the equation is given only if you write it or substitute correctly, so a bare "30" with no unit loses the second mark.

**2. (a)** 4.0 + 6.0 = **10 Ω** [1].
**(b)** *I* = *V* ÷ *R* = 5.0 ÷ 10 = **0.50 A** [1].

*Examiner insight:* candidates often divide 5.0 by one resistor only. In series the resistances add first, and the same current then passes through both.

**3.** In a series circuit the current is the **same at every point** [1]. In a parallel circuit the current from the source **divides between the branches**, so the current from the source is larger than the current in each branch [1].

*Examiner insight:* "current is used up" in a series circuit is never creditworthy. Say that the current is the same everywhere.

**4.** *E* = *Pt* = 2000 W × 180 s [1] = **360 000 J** (3.6 × 10⁵ J) [1].

*Examiner insight:* the usual loss is leaving the time as 3.0 and the power as 2.0, which gives 6. Convert kW to W and minutes to seconds before you multiply.

**5.** *(Extended)* Any two of the following, one mark each: **more turns** on the coil; a **larger current**; a **soft-iron core** in the coil [2].

*Examiner insight:* "a bigger battery" is accepted only if you link it to a larger current. "A stronger core" gains nothing, so name soft iron.

**6. (a)** The pointer **deflects** (an e.m.f. is induced) [1].
**(b)** **Zero** [1].
**(c)** The pointer deflects in the **opposite direction** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the reading depends on the magnet moving relative to the coil, not on the magnet being present. A still magnet, however strong, induces nothing.

**7.** Bring one pole of the bar magnet up to each end of each bar in turn. The bar that is **repelled** by one end is the permanent magnet (only a magnet can repel another magnet) [1]. The bar that is **attracted** at both ends by either pole, but never repelled, is the iron: magnetism is induced in it [1]. The bar that is **neither attracted nor repelled** is the copper, which is non-magnetic [1].

*Examiner insight:* using attraction to identify the magnet fails, because the iron bar is attracted too. Only repulsion proves a bar is a magnet.

**8. (a)** A region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force [1].
**(b)** Place the compass at the point: the field direction is the direction in which the compass needle's **north pole** points [1].
**(c)** Soft iron is magnetised easily but **loses its magnetism** when the current is switched off, so the crane can drop the scrap [1]. Steel would **stay magnetised** (it forms a permanent magnet), so the scrap would not be released [1].

*Examiner insight:* saying soft iron is "a stronger magnet" gains no credit. The reason is that it is a temporary magnet.

**9. (a)** **12 V** across each, because the branches of a parallel circuit have the same potential difference as the battery [1].
**(b)** X: *I* = *V* ÷ *R* = 12 ÷ 20 = **0.60 A** [1]. Y: 12 ÷ 30 = **0.40 A** [1].
**(c)** 0.60 + 0.40 = **1.0 A** [1].
**(d)** *R* = *V* ÷ *I* = 12 ÷ 1.0 = **12 Ω** [1].
**(e)** The branches give the charge **more than one path**, so the total current is larger than the current in X alone for the same battery [1].

*Examiner insight:* in (d) do not average the two resistors or add them. Use the battery voltage and the battery current, which belong to the whole circuit.

**10. (a)** *t* = 2.0 × 60 = 120 s. *Q* = *It* [1] = 0.25 × 120 = **30 C** [1].
**(b)** *E* = *IVt* = 0.25 × 6.0 × 120 [1] = **180 J** [1].
**(c)** *P* = *IV* = 0.25 × 6.0 = **1.5 W** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the power is the energy per second, so 180 ÷ 120 gives the same 1.5 W. Use that to check your answers to (b) and (c) agree.

**11. (a)** Connect the lamp in series with a power supply, an ammeter and a variable resistor (or use a variable power supply) [1]. Connect a voltmeter in parallel across the lamp only [1]. Change the potential difference in steps and record the voltmeter and ammeter readings each time [1]. Collect several pairs of readings, then plot a graph of current against potential difference (or calculate *V* ÷ *I* for each pair) [1].
**(b)** At 2.0 V: *R* = 2.0 ÷ 0.20 = **10 Ω** [1]. At 6.0 V: *R* = 6.0 ÷ 0.40 = **15 Ω** [1].
**(c)** The larger current makes the filament **hotter** [1], and the resistance of a metal filament **increases as its temperature rises** [1].

*Examiner insight:* a voltmeter in series, or an ammeter across the lamp, loses the circuit marks at once. Say "in series" and "in parallel" for each meter.

**12. (a)** *I* = *P* ÷ *V* [1] = 2300 ÷ 230 = **10 A** [1].
**(b)** The **13 A** fuse [1], because its rating is just above the normal current of 10 A, so it will not melt in normal use but will protect against a larger fault current. The 3 A and 5 A fuses are below 10 A and would melt as soon as the heater is switched on [1].
**(c)** If the fuse were in the neutral wire, the appliance would be **disconnected from the neutral but still live** [1], so parts connected to it could still be at a dangerous potential [1].
**(d)** The earth wire gives the current a **low-resistance path** from the case to earth, so a large current flows [1]. This large current **melts the fuse**, which disconnects the live supply so the case is no longer live [1].

*Examiner insight:* for (b) the reason must be a comparison with 10 A. Writing that a 13 A fuse "is the biggest" or "is the safest" scores nothing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving time in minutes or power in kW when using *E* = *Pt*.
- Saying current is "used up" around a series circuit.
- Using one resistor's value, or an average, for the whole circuit's resistance.
- Putting a voltmeter in series, or an ammeter in parallel, in a described circuit.
- Writing the equation only in words or only as numbers, with no unit on the final answer.
- Choosing a fuse without comparing its rating with the normal current.
- Saying a bar is a magnet because it is attracted, instead of because it is repelled.
- Saying a soft-iron core is used because it makes a "stronger" magnet, instead of because it demagnetises.
- Saying a magnet inside a coil induces a reading, when the reading needs relative movement.
