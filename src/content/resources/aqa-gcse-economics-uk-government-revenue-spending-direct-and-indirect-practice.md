## Questions

**1.** State two main sources of UK government revenue. **[2]**

**2.** State two main areas of UK government spending. **[2]**

**3.** **(a)** Explain what is meant by an indirect tax. **[2]**
**(b)** From income tax, VAT, corporation tax and fuel duty, identify the two indirect taxes. **[2]**

**4.** Country B charges income tax at 0% on the first 9,000 of income, 25% on income from 9,000 to 30,000, and 45% on income above 30,000. Each rate applies only to the income in that band.
**(a)** Work out the tax due on an income of 24,000. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the tax due on an income of 50,000. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the average tax rate at an income of 50,000. **[1]**
**(d)** Use your answers to show that this income tax is progressive. **[2]**

**5.** A product is sold for 84 including a sales tax of 20%.
**(a)** Calculate the price before tax. **[2]**
**(b)** State the amount of tax in the price. **[1]**
**(c)** The tax rate rises to 25% and the price before tax is unchanged. Calculate the new selling price. **[2]**

**6.** Country E charges every household a fixed licence fee of 180 a year. Household P has an income of 15,000. Household Q has an income of 72,000.
**(a)** Calculate the fee as a percentage of income for each household. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the fee is regressive. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows the government accounts of Country C (invented figures, billions).

| Revenue | Amount | Spending | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 64 | Social protection | 70 |
| National Insurance | 41 | Health | 52 |
| VAT | 47 | Education | 30 |
| Corporation tax | 18 | Defence | 14 |
| Excise duties | 15 | Transport | 11 |
| Other | 15 | Debt interest | 13 |
| | | Other | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate total revenue and total spending. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether there is a deficit or surplus, and its size. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate health spending as a percentage of total spending, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the share of total revenue from direct taxes, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**8.** Country A charges a fuel duty of 0.40 per litre. Household R has an income of 20,000 and buys 1,000 litres in a year. Household S has an income of 80,000 and buys 1,600 litres.
**(a)** Calculate the duty paid by each household as a percentage of income. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this duty is regressive, and why it might be criticised. **[3]**

**9.** Using the income tax bands in question 4, a worker's pay rises from 29,000 to 31,000. The worker thinks the extra tax will leave them worse off.
**(a)** Calculate the extra tax paid. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the rise in take-home pay and explain why the worker is mistaken. **[2]**

**10.** "The government should raise more of its revenue from indirect taxes than from direct taxes." Evaluate this statement. **[9]**

**11.** Country D plans to raise health spending from 80 to 88 (billions).
**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain two ways in which the government could pay for the extra spending, and give one opportunity cost of the plan. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: income tax, National Insurance contributions, VAT, corporation tax, excise duties (fuel, alcohol, tobacco), council tax or business rates **[1] [1]**. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A named tax earns credit; "taxes" or "tax on people" is too vague to separate from the question, so name the tax.

**2.** Any two from: social protection (pensions and benefits), health, education, defence, public order and safety, transport, debt interest **[1] [1]**. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Public services" is a general label, so give the specific area. Naming two different areas matters, since a repeat of the same area is only one point.

**3. (a)** A tax on spending [1], collected by the seller and passed to the government, so the burden is usually passed on to the consumer through the price [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **VAT [1] and fuel duty [1].** **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Paid by the consumer" alone is incomplete. Include who collects it or how it is passed on. Do not choose corporation tax, which is a direct tax on profit.

**4. (a)** Taxable slice = 24,000 - 9,000 = 15,000 [1]; tax = 15,000 x 0.25 = **3,750 [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** 25% band: 21,000 x 0.25 = 5,250 [1]; 45% band: 20,000 x 0.45 = 9,000 [1]; total = **14,250 [1]**. **[3]**
**(c)** 14,250 / 50,000 x 100 = **28.5% [1]**. **[1]**
**(d)** At 24,000 the average rate is 3,750 / 24,000 = 15.6% [1]. It rises to 28.5% at 50,000, so a higher income pays a larger percentage of income, which is progressive [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Set out the working band by band. Multiplying all of 50,000 by 0.45 is a frequent slip, and showing each band separately makes your method clear and easy to check.

**5. (a)** Price before tax = 84 / 1.20 [1] = **70 [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** 84 - 70 = **14 [1]**. **[1]**
**(c)** 70 x 1.25 [1] = **87.50 [1]**. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Taking 20% of 84 (16.80) is the usual error and does not give the tax, because the 20% was added to the pre-tax price, not to the final one.

**6. (a)** P: 180 / 15,000 x 100 = **1.2% [1]**. Q: 180 / 72,000 x 100 = **0.25% [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** The percentage of income paid falls as income rises (1.2% to 0.25%) [1], so the fee takes a larger share of the lower income, which is the meaning of regressive [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer needs the percentages, not only that both pay 180. Equal amounts are the reason the share falls, but the share is what defines the term.

**7. (a)** Revenue = 64 + 41 + 47 + 18 + 15 + 15 = **200 [1]**. Spending = 70 + 52 + 30 + 14 + 11 + 13 + 20 = **210 [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** **Deficit of 10 [1].** **[1]**
**(c)** 52 / 210 x 100 [1] = **24.8% [1]**. **[2]**
**(d)** Direct taxes = 64 + 41 + 18 = 123 [1]; 123 / 200 x 100 = **61.5% [1]**. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Pick the right total as the denominator, since spending shares use total spending and revenue shares use total revenue. Say which taxes you treated as direct.

**8. (a)** R: 1,000 x 0.40 = 400, and 400 / 20,000 x 100 = **2.0% [1]**. S: 1,600 x 0.40 = 640, and 640 / 80,000 x 100 = **0.8% [1]**. **[2]**
**(b)** The duty is an indirect tax charged per litre and usually passed on in the price [1]. The share of income paid falls from 2.0% to 0.8% as income rises, so it is regressive [1]. It may be criticised because it takes a bigger share from lower-income households and so can widen inequality (or because households with no alternative to a car cannot avoid it) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your figures from (a) in the explanation. A link back to inequality or fairness turns a description into a point about consequences.

**9. (a)** Tax at 29,000 = 20,000 x 0.25 = 5,000 [1]. Tax at 31,000 = 5,250 + 1,000 x 0.45 = 5,700 [1]. Extra tax = **700 [1]**. **[3]**
**(b)** Take-home pay rises by 2,000 - 700 = **1,300 [1]**. Only the income above 30,000 is taxed at 45%, so the higher rate applies to part of the rise, not to all earnings, and the worker is better off [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish the rate on the extra income (marginal) from the average rate. Explaining only that "45% is paid on the extra" does not show why total pay does not fall.

**10.** Indirect taxes, such as VAT and fuel duty, are charged on spending and collected by sellers [1]; direct taxes, such as income tax, are charged on income and profits [1]. For: indirect taxes are cheap and easy to collect and hard to avoid [1], they let people choose how much to pay by choosing what to buy [1], and duties on goods like tobacco can discourage harmful consumption as well as raise revenue [1]. Against: they are often regressive, because a poorer household spends most of its income on taxed goods [1], so inequality could widen [1]. Raising prices may also reduce sales, so revenue may rise less than expected [1]. Judgement: a mix is best, because direct progressive taxes support fairness while indirect taxes add stable revenue. The balance depends on whether the government's priority is raising revenue or reducing inequality [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the two sides against each other and finish with a decision that your own points support. Setting out pros and cons without choosing between them, or arguing only one side, leaves the evaluation unfinished.

**11. (a)** (88 - 80) / 80 x 100 = **10% [1]**. **[1]**
**(b)** Way 1: raise taxes, such as income tax [1], which leaves households with less disposable income and may reduce their spending [1]. Way 2: borrow more [1], which adds to the national debt and means higher debt interest in future years [1]. Opportunity cost: the money could have been spent on another area, such as education, or used to cut taxes [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a consequence to be developed. State a way and then say what it does to households or the debt. The opportunity cost must name what is given up.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Judging a tax by the pounds paid rather than by the percentage of income.
- Charging the top band rate on all earnings instead of the slice above the threshold.
- Taking a percentage off a tax-inclusive price instead of dividing by 1 + the rate.
- Using the wrong total when working out a share of revenue or spending.
- Naming no specific tax or spending area when asked to state one.
- Mixing up direct/indirect with progressive/regressive.
- Describing a deficit and the national debt as the same thing.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an evaluate question, or one with no judgement.
