## Energy is transferred, not used up

Before the definitions, fix one idea. In a reaction, energy is **transferred** between the chemicals and their surroundings. It is not created, lost or used up. By "surroundings" chemists mean the solvent, the container, the thermometer and the air: anything other than the substances actually reacting.

- In an **exothermic** reaction, energy is transferred **from** the reacting chemicals **to** the surroundings. The surroundings warm up, so the temperature reading **rises**.
- In an **endothermic** reaction, energy is transferred **from** the surroundings **to** the reacting chemicals. The surroundings cool down, so the temperature reading **falls**.

The thermometer measures the surroundings, so a fall means energy has gone *into* the chemicals.

Exothermic examples: combustion, neutralisation, and many metal–acid reactions. Endothermic examples: thermal decomposition of a metal carbonate, and hydrogencarbonates reacting with citric or ethanoic acid.

### Worked example: classifying from temperature data (C3.2a)

A student mixes reagents in a polystyrene cup and records the temperature before mixing and the highest or lowest temperature reached.

| Reaction | Start (°C) | Final (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| A: magnesium ribbon + dilute sulfuric acid | 18.5 | 31.0 |
| B: potassium hydrogencarbonate + ethanoic acid | 20.0 | 14.2 |
| C: potassium hydroxide + nitric acid | 19.0 | 25.5 |

Step 1: temperature change = final − start.

- A: 31.0 − 18.5 = **+12.5 °C**
- B: 14.2 − 20.0 = **−5.8 °C**
- C: 25.5 − 19.0 = **+6.5 °C**

Step 2: interpret the sign. A rise means the surroundings gained energy, so A and C are **exothermic**. A fall means the surroundings lost energy to the chemicals, so B is **endothermic**.

## Measuring temperature change in practice (PAG C8)

The specification's practical suggestion for C3.2a is measuring the temperature change in reactions, which falls within PAG C8. A reliable method looks like this:

1. Measure a fixed volume of the first solution with a measuring cylinder and pour it into a polystyrene cup standing in a beaker (the beaker stops it tipping).
2. Record the starting temperature with a thermometer or temperature probe.
3. Add the second reagent (a measured volume of solution, or a weighed mass of solid), put a lid on and stir.
4. Record the highest temperature (or lowest, for an endothermic reaction).
5. Repeat the whole experiment and work out a mean temperature change.

**What the cup and cover are for.** Expanded polystyrene traps air, so heat passes through it slowly, and covering the cup stops warm or cool air escaping from the surface. Without them, energy leaks to or from the room, and the measured change is smaller than it should be every time. That is a **systematic error**: it pushes all results the same way. Misreading the thermometer scale, or catching the peak a few seconds late, gives **random errors** that scatter results above and below the true value.

**Handling repeat data.** Suppose four repeats give temperature rises of 6.4, 6.9, 6.8 and 8.7 °C. The 8.7 °C result is far from the others, so treat it as **anomalous** and leave it out. Mean = (6.4 + 6.9 + 6.8) ÷ 3 = 20.1 ÷ 3 = **6.7 °C**. The range of the three is 6.9 − 6.4 = 0.5 °C, so one simple estimate of the uncertainty is half the range, **±0.25 °C**. Results that sit close together are **precise** and show good **repeatability**. If another group using the same method gets similar values, the results are **reproducible**.

## Reaction profiles (C3.2b, CM3.2i)

A **reaction profile** is a graph of energy, plotted up the page, against how far the reaction has gone, plotted across. It shows four things you must label:

- **reactants**: a horizontal line at the start
- **products**: a horizontal line at the end
- **activation energy**: an arrow from the reactants line up to the top of the curve (the peak)
- **energy change**: an arrow from the reactants line to the products line

Here is a sketch of an **exothermic** profile:

    energy
      |     ^      .---.
      |     |     /     \
      |  Ea |    /       \
      |     |   /         \
      | ----+--'           \
      | reactants |         \
      |           | energy   \
      |           v change    '-------
      |                       products
      +----------------------------------
                 progress of reaction

On an exothermic profile the **products line sits below** the reactants line, so the energy change arrow points **down**. 

For an **endothermic** reaction, draw the products line **higher** than the reactants. The energy change arrow points **up**, from reactants to products. The peak is still above both lines, and the activation energy arrow still starts at the reactants.

### Worked example: reading a profile

A profile shows the reactants at 250 kJ/mol, the top of the curve at 330 kJ/mol and the products at 60 kJ/mol.

- Activation energy = peak − reactants = 330 − 250 = **80 kJ/mol**
- Energy change = products − reactants = 60 − 250 = **−190 kJ/mol**
- Products sit below reactants, which makes this reaction **exothermic**. The negative sign says the same thing: the chemicals end up with less energy, and 190 kJ/mol has gone to the surroundings.

Measure both arrows from the **reactants** line. A common slip is measuring activation energy from the products line or from the axis.

## Activation energy (C3.2c)

**Activation energy** is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur. Particles must collide with at least this much energy for bonds in the reactants to start breaking.

This explains why many exothermic reactions need a "start". A candle, a gas cooker or a firework needs a flame or spark first. That initial energy supplies the activation energy. After that, the energy released keeps it going. So a burning candle is **exothermic**, even though you had to light it. The specification lists this exact confusion as a common misconception: needing heat to start a reaction does **not** make it endothermic. Whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic depends only on the **overall** energy change, from reactants to products.

On a profile, a large activation energy shows as a tall peak above the reactants.

## Bond energies (C3.2d, Higher Tier only)

The bond model explains where the energy goes:

- To **break** any bond, energy must be **supplied**, so bond breaking is an endothermic process.
- When any bond **forms**, energy is **given out**, so bond making is an exothermic process.

A frequent mistake is to say energy is released when bonds break. It is the other way round. Energy is released only when new bonds form.

Each bond has a **bond energy**: the energy needed to break one mole of that bond, in kJ/mol. The same amount is released when one mole of that bond forms. Every calculation here supplies the bond energies to use.

**Energy change = total energy to break bonds in the reactants − total energy released making bonds in the products**

- A **negative** answer means bond making gave out more than bond breaking took in: **exothermic**.
- A **positive** answer means bond breaking took in more than bond making gave out: **endothermic**.

### Method in four steps

1. Draw (or picture) the displayed formula of every molecule, so you can see every bond.
2. List and add up the bonds broken, multiplying by the number in the balanced equation.
3. List and add up the bonds made in the same way.
4. Subtract: broken − made. Give the sign and the unit, kJ/mol.

### Worked example 1: ethene and bromine

CH₂=CH₂ + Br–Br → CH₂Br–CH₂Br

Use these values (kJ/mol): C=C 612, Br–Br 193, C–C 348, C–Br 276. The four C–H bonds are unchanged, so you can leave them out of both totals.

- Bonds broken: C=C + Br–Br = 612 + 193 = **805 kJ**
- Bonds made: C–C + 2 × C–Br = 348 + (2 × 276) = 348 + 552 = **900 kJ**
- Energy change = 805 − 900 = **−95 kJ/mol**, exothermic

### Worked example 2: nitrogen and oxygen

N≡N + O=O → 2 N=O

Use these values (kJ/mol): N≡N 945, O=O 498, N=O 630.

- Bonds broken: 945 + 498 = **1443 kJ**
- Bonds made: 2 × 630 = **1260 kJ**
- Energy change = 1443 − 1260 = **+183 kJ/mol**, endothermic

Breaking the very strong triple bond in nitrogen takes in more energy than forming the two new bonds gives out. That strong bond also means a high activation energy, which is one reason the nitrogen and oxygen in air do not react at room temperature. They combine only at very high temperatures, such as inside a car engine or in a lightning strike.

### Worked example 3: burning propane

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Use these values (kJ/mol): O–H 464, C=O 805, C–C 348, O=O 498, C–H 413.

Propane has 2 C–C bonds and 8 C–H bonds. Each CO₂ has 2 C=O bonds and each H₂O has 2 O–H bonds.

| Bonds broken | Energy (kJ) | Bonds made | Energy (kJ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 × C–C | 696 | 6 × C=O | 4830 |
| 8 × C–H | 3304 | 8 × O–H | 3712 |
| 5 × O=O | 2490 | | |
| **Total** | **6490** | **Total** | **8542** |

Energy change = 6490 − 8542 = **−2052 kJ/mol**, exothermic. The equation is per mole of propane, so this is the energy change when one mole of propane burns.

The value from bond energies is an estimate. Bond energies are averages taken over many different molecules, so a calculated answer is usually a little different from a measured one.

## Maths skills (CM3.2i and CM3.2ii)

Reading a profile (M4a) means taking levels off the energy axis and subtracting from the reactants level. Energy change arithmetic (M1a) means multiplying each bond energy by its number of bonds before adding, then keeping the sign.

## Common errors

- Saying a temperature fall means the reaction "lost heat". In an endothermic reaction the **chemicals gain** energy; the surroundings lose it.
- Drawing the activation energy arrow from the axis or from the products.
- Drawing an endothermic profile with the products below the reactants.
- Forgetting the multiplier: 5O₂ means five O=O bonds, not one.
- Missing a bond: counting one C=O for CO₂ instead of two.
- Writing "products − reactants" for bond energies. For bond energies it is **broken − made**.
- Dropping the sign on the final answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between exothermic and endothermic reactions?**
In an exothermic reaction energy is transferred from the reacting chemicals to the surroundings, so the temperature rises. In an endothermic reaction energy is transferred from the surroundings to the chemicals, so the temperature falls.

**What must be labelled on a reaction profile?**
Label the reactants, the products, the activation energy and the energy change. Both arrows are measured from the reactants line: activation energy goes up to the peak, and the energy change goes to the products line.

**What is activation energy?**
Activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur. Particles must collide with at least this much energy for bonds in the reactants to start breaking.

**Why is a polystyrene cup with a lid used to measure temperature changes?**
Expanded polystyrene traps air, so heat passes through it slowly, and a lid stops warm or cool air escaping. This reduces heat transfer to or from the room, which would otherwise make every measured temperature change too small.
