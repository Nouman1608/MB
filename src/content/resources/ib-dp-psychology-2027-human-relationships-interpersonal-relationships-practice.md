> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the interpersonal relationships strand of the human relationships context (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios and survey in Question 6 are invented. Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

## Section A

**1.** Explain the role of one chemical messenger in interpersonal relationships. **[4]**

**2.** Outline one cognitive explanation for interpersonal relationships. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how self-disclosure can affect a friendship. **[3]**

**4.** Describe one strategy for improving interpersonal relationships. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Two housemates, Jon and Leila, argue about shared costs. Jon often says "you never listen". Leila replies "you are always criticising me". Leila believes Jon's sharp tone shows he does not respect her. Jon believes Leila's silence shows she does not care. Last week they agreed to a weekly ten-minute meeting in which each person states a concern and the other summarises it before replying.

**(a)** Apply a cognitive explanation to explain why the conflict continues. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the weekly meeting may improve the relationship. **[3]**

**6.** A class practical used a survey to investigate what students want in a friend. Students answered 12 closed questions, each rated from 1 (not important) to 5 (very important), and 3 open questions. The survey was handed out in one lesson with the teacher in the room.

**(a)** Identify one advantage of using closed questions. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one way the teacher's presence may have affected the data. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest one change that would improve the survey, and explain why. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one limitation of using brain imaging to study romantic love. **[3]**

**8.** Compare two explanations of why relationships continue or end. **[6]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate research into interpersonal relationships with regard to the usefulness of its strategies for improving relationships. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any one messenger, for example oxytocin. It is a hormone and neuromodulator associated with bonding **[1]**. In an experiment, participants given oxytocin by nasal spray invested more in an unknown partner than those given a placebo **[1]**, suggesting that oxytocin increases trust **[1]**. Evaluation point such as: the sample was male students and the task was artificial, so the effect in real relationships is unclear **[1]**. Alternatives: dopamine and reward in early romantic love, testosterone and aggression.

**2.** Any one explanation, for example social exchange theory. Relationships are maintained when perceived rewards outweigh costs **[1]**. People compare the outcome with what they expect (comparison level) **[1]** and with alternatives **[1]**. Example or evaluation, such as a friendship ending when one person feels they give much more than they receive, or the criticism that people do not always calculate **[1]**. Equally creditable: the investment model, attribution, similarity-attraction, hostile attribution bias.

**3.** Self-disclosure is sharing personal information **[1]**. When it is gradual and reciprocal, it builds trust and a sense of being known **[1]**, as in social penetration theory. Example or evidence, such as strangers asked escalating personal questions reporting greater closeness than pairs asked small-talk questions **[1]**.

**4.** Any one strategy. For example communication training for couples **[1]**: partners practise listening and summarising what the other said **[1]**, soften complaints and take a break when overwhelmed **[1]**, which reduces defensiveness and escalation **[1]**. Equally creditable: attribution retraining, structured self-disclosure, shared goals, anger management.

**5.** (a) Cognitive explanation: attributions **[1]**. Each person explains the other's behaviour negatively (Leila reads sharp tone as disrespect, Jon reads silence as not caring) **[1]**. These distress-maintaining attributions keep the conflict going, because each person expects hostility and responds defensively, which confirms the other's belief **[1]**. Credit hostile attribution bias with the same development.

(b) Summarising makes each person check their interpretation instead of assuming **[1]**, which is a communication strategy that reduces defensiveness **[1]**. A set time limits arguments at random moments and allows calmer discussion, and might change attributions over time **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Closed questions give quantitative data that are easy to compare and analyse **[1]**, and they are quick to answer so more questions can be asked **[1]**.

(b) Social desirability bias: students may give answers they think the teacher approves of **[1]**, so the importance of "kindness" may be overstated **[1]**.

(c) Any one sensible change **[1]**, for example anonymous completion with the teacher out of the room, a pilot test, or a larger and more varied sample. A reason it improves the survey **[1]**, for example less social desirability bias, clearer wording or better generalisability. A link to the details of this survey **[1]**, for example the 1 to 5 importance ratings being open to different interpretations without labelled anchors.

**7.** Any one limitation, for example correlational evidence: scans show activity while a person views a photograph, not that a brain region causes love **[1]**, so direction of cause cannot be established **[1]**. Or: small samples and artificial scanner conditions limit generalisation **[1]**. Or: it is reductionist and neglects the cognitive and social aspects of relationships **[1]**. Award up to **[3]** for explanation.

**8.** Example: social exchange theory and the investment model. Similarities, any two (up to 2): both use rewards and costs; both are cognitive and testable; both are supported by self-report evidence. Differences, any two (up to 2): the investment model adds investment and quality of alternatives and so explains why people stay in unhappy relationships; social exchange is simpler. Judgement or evaluation (up to 2): the investment model has stronger predictive evidence but relies on self-report; both assume rational calculation. Credit explicit comparison words **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for critical analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Communication training and Gottman-style research: observable behaviours can be trained, but outcomes rely on partners' commitment and on long-term data **[2]**.
- Attribution retraining: links to cognitive explanations, with the problem of bidirectional ambiguity (do thoughts cause unhappiness or the reverse?) **[2]**.
- Structured self-disclosure: short-term closeness in controlled studies, with limited evidence of lasting change **[2]**.
- Biological strategies are weaker: chemical messengers show associations, and treatment with oxytocin is not an established strategy **[2]**.
- Change: whether improvements last and who benefits; barriers such as unwillingness to change **[2]**.
- Causality and measurement: self-reports and observation coding are subjective, and experiments often use strangers rather than long-term couples **[2]**.
- Perspective and bias: most research is Western, so strategies such as direct communication may not suit all cultures **[2]**.
- Responsibility: advice may assume a single ideal relationship, and researchers must avoid harm and protect privacy **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the strategies, for example that those targeting thinking and communication are best supported but need more long-term evidence **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** saying a chemical "causes" love without any study or evaluation.
- **Question 5:** retelling the quarrel rather than applying an explanation.
- **Question 6:** identifying a bias without saying how it affects the data.
- **Question 9:** listing strategies without evaluating the evidence or using more than one concept.
