This guide covers subtopic **10.3 Anaerobic respiration** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe anaerobic respiration as the release of a relatively small amount of energy by the breakdown of glucose without using oxygen, to state the word equations for anaerobic respiration in humans and in yeast, to explain why lactic acid builds up in muscles and blood during vigorous exercise, causing Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption (EPOC), also called an oxygen debt, and to outline how the oxygen debt is removed after exercise.

The [Respiration guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-respiration/) covers the whole topic. This page treats each part in depth, adds the uses of yeast in industry, and works through data on lactic acid and recovery. Use the [10.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-anaerobic-respiration-revision-notes/) for recall and the [10.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-anaerobic-respiration-practice/) to test yourself.

## The definition

**Anaerobic respiration is the release of a relatively small amount of energy by the breakdown of glucose without using oxygen.**

| Phrase | What it means |
|---|---|
| Release of energy | Energy is released, not made |
| Relatively small amount | Much less energy is released from each glucose molecule than in aerobic respiration |
| Breakdown of glucose | The glucose is only partly broken down, so much of its energy remains in the products |
| Without using oxygen | "Anaerobic" means "without air" |

Anaerobic respiration is useful because it releases energy quickly and does not depend on oxygen. It lets a cell carry on for a short time when oxygen is short.

## The word equations

**In humans (and other animals)**

glucose → lactic acid

**In yeast (and plants)**

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide

Both equations release energy. The syllabus asks for the word equations only. Notice three points.

- No oxygen appears on the left of either equation.
- The products are different. Human cells make **lactic acid**. Yeast makes **ethanol** (alcohol) and **carbon dioxide**.
- Neither equation produces water.

### Comparing the two kinds of respiration

| Feature | Aerobic | Anaerobic (human) | Anaerobic (yeast) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen needed | Yes | No | No |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Lactic acid | Ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Energy released | Relatively large amount | Relatively small amount | Relatively small amount |
| Breakdown of glucose | Complete | Partial | Partial |
| Site | Mitochondria (and cytoplasm) | Cytoplasm | Cytoplasm |

## Anaerobic respiration in humans

### Why lactic acid builds up

During vigorous exercise, such as a sprint, muscle cells need energy very quickly. The breathing system and the heart cannot supply oxygen fast enough to meet all of this demand, so aerobic respiration alone is not enough. The muscles also respire anaerobically to release extra energy.

The anaerobic respiration produces **lactic acid**. It is made faster than it can be removed, so it **builds up in the muscles and then in the blood**. A high level of lactic acid contributes to muscle fatigue and cramp.

### Oxygen debt (EPOC)

After the exercise stops, the person keeps breathing hard for several minutes. The extra oxygen taken in after exercise is called **Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption (EPOC)**, or the **oxygen debt**. The oxygen is needed to break down the lactic acid that built up.

### How the oxygen debt is removed

The syllabus limits the outline to two points.

1. **The fast heart rate continues.** The heart keeps beating fast, which transports the lactic acid in the blood from the muscles to the **liver**.
2. **Deeper and faster breathing continues.** This supplies the oxygen needed for the **breakdown of lactic acid in the liver**.

Once the lactic acid is gone, the oxygen debt has been repaid, and the heart rate and breathing rate return to their resting values.

## Anaerobic respiration in yeast

Yeast can respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not. Anaerobic respiration in yeast is called **fermentation**.

- In **bread-making**, the carbon dioxide forms bubbles in the dough, which makes the bread rise. The ethanol evaporates during baking.
- In **brewing**, the ethanol is the useful product, for example in making alcoholic drinks and biofuel.
- Yeast has been used for thousands of years, and in modern industry fermenters are sealed to keep out oxygen so that the yeast uses anaerobic respiration.

### A simple demonstration

Place yeast in boiled, cooled glucose solution. Boiling removes dissolved oxygen, and cooling stops the yeast being killed. Add a layer of oil on top, to keep out air. Connect the tube to limewater. After some time the limewater turns cloudy, showing that carbon dioxide is produced without oxygen.

## Worked example: lactic acid and recovery

*A sprinter's blood lactic acid concentration was measured at rest, straight after a race, and during recovery (illustrative data).*

| Time | Lactic acid in blood (mmol per dm cubed) |
|---|---|
| At rest | 1.0 |
| End of the sprint | 12.0 |
| 10 minutes after | 8.0 |
| 30 minutes after | 3.0 |
| 60 minutes after | 1.2 |

1. **Describe**: the lactic acid concentration rose from 1.0 to 12.0 during the sprint, then fell gradually, almost to the resting value after 60 minutes.
2. **Percentage fall in the first 30 minutes**: (12.0 - 3.0) divided by 12.0 x 100 = **75 per cent**.
3. **Mean rate of fall in the first 30 minutes**: 9.0 divided by 30 = **0.3 mmol per dm cubed per minute**.
4. **Explain the rise**: the muscles respired anaerobically to meet the demand for energy, producing lactic acid faster than it was removed.
5. **Explain the fall**: the lactic acid was carried in the blood to the liver and broken down using oxygen, which was supplied by deeper and faster breathing.
6. **Compare the rates**: from 0 to 10 minutes the fall was 4.0, in the next 20 minutes it was 5.0. So the rate was faster early on: 0.4 per minute against 0.25 per minute. The concentration gradient and the supply of oxygen explain why the rate slows.

## Sprints and long-distance running

The way an athlete uses the two kinds of respiration depends on the event.

| Event | Energy demand | Main type of respiration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m sprint | Very high for a short time | Mostly anaerobic, with some aerobic | Lactic acid builds up; large oxygen debt afterwards |
| Marathon | Moderate for a long time | Mostly aerobic | Little lactic acid; steady heart and breathing rates |
| Walking | Low | Aerobic | No oxygen debt |

A sprinter can run for only a short time at full effort, because the lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire. A marathon runner paces the race so that aerobic respiration can supply most of the energy, because it releases much more energy from each glucose molecule and does not produce a harmful build-up of lactic acid.

Regular training improves the delivery of oxygen to the muscles, for example by strengthening the heart and increasing the number of capillaries. The muscles can then rely on aerobic respiration for longer, and less lactic acid is made at the same effort.

## Three model answers

**1. Explain why a person breathes heavily after running a race.** The muscles respired anaerobically during the race and made lactic acid. After the race, extra oxygen is needed to break down the lactic acid in the liver. Deep, fast breathing supplies this oxygen, so the oxygen debt is repaid.

**2. Explain why the pulse rate stays high for a while after exercise.** The heart carries the lactic acid in the blood from the muscles to the liver, where it is broken down. The faster heart rate moves the lactic acid more quickly. Once the lactic acid has been removed, the pulse rate falls back to the resting value.

**3. Compare the energy released by aerobic and anaerobic respiration.** Aerobic respiration releases a relatively large amount of energy, because the glucose is completely broken down to carbon dioxide and water. Anaerobic respiration releases a relatively small amount, because the glucose is only partly broken down and much of the energy remains in the lactic acid or ethanol.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that lactic acid is made in aerobic respiration, or that it contains oxygen as a reactant.
- Saying that anaerobic respiration "makes no energy". It releases a small amount.
- Writing water as a product of anaerobic respiration.
- Saying that humans make ethanol, or that yeast makes lactic acid.
- Describing the oxygen debt as a shortage of oxygen *after* exercise. It is the extra oxygen needed after exercise to remove lactic acid.
- Forgetting the liver in the removal of lactic acid.
- Saying that lactic acid is broken down in the muscles by the oxygen debt. It is carried to the liver.
- Confusing the two types of respiration by naming them "respiration with energy" and "respiration without energy".

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the definition of anaerobic respiration.
- **State**: write the word equation for humans or yeast.
- **Explain**: give the cause of the build-up of lactic acid and of the oxygen debt.
- **Outline**: set out the two steps by which the oxygen debt is removed.
