## What 6.1 asks of you

Section 6.1 has two parts.

**6.1.1 The Earth** asks you to know five approximate facts and to define, recall and use one equation:

- the Earth orbits the Sun once in about 365 days;
- that orbit is an ellipse which is approximately circular;
- the Earth rotates on its tilted axis once in about 24 hours;
- the Moon takes about one month to orbit the Earth;
- light from the Sun takes about 500 s to reach the Earth;
- average orbital speed v = 2πr / T, where r is the average radius of the orbit and T is the orbital period.

**6.1.2 The Solar System** asks you to describe what the Solar System contains, to analyse and interpret planetary data, and to know how gravitational field strength depends on mass and distance, what force keeps objects in orbit, and how orbital speed changes with distance from the Sun.

Notice the verbs. "Know" items are recall marks. "Recall and use" means the equation is not given to you. "Analyse and interpret" means the examiners will hand you an unfamiliar table and expect reasoning from it.

## Reading the five Earth facts as physics

Each fact is a period or a time, and each feeds a calculation.

- **365 days** is the Earth's **orbital period** T around the Sun.
- **24 hours** is the period of **rotation** about its own axis. Do not use 24 hours as an orbital period for the Earth around the Sun; that is the most common mix-up.
- **One month** is the Moon's orbital period around the Earth.
- **500 s** is a light travel time. With the speed of light in a vacuum, 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s (from section 3.3), it converts to a distance using distance = speed × time.
- **Approximately circular ellipse** is the reason the equation uses the *average* radius. The Earth–Sun distance changes slightly through the year, so a single "radius" is an average value.

## Using v = 2πr / T without unit errors

The equation is distance over time: 2πr is the circumference of one orbit and T is the time taken for it. Most lost marks come from units, not from the physics. Choose a consistent set before substituting:

| r in | T in | v comes out in |
|---|---|---|
| m | s | m/s |
| km | s | km/s |
| km | hours | km/h |

Convert days to seconds with × 24 × 3600 (so 1 day = 86 400 s).

**Worked example 1: the Moon from a light-time measurement.** Light reflected from the Moon takes 1.28 s to reach the Earth. The Moon's orbital period is 27.3 days. Calculate the Moon's average orbital speed.

1. Radius: r = speed × time = 3.0 × 10⁸ × 1.28 = 3.84 × 10⁸ m.
2. Period: T = 27.3 × 24 × 3600 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s.
3. Speed: v = 2π × 3.84 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.36 × 10⁶ = 1.02 × 10³ m/s, about 1.0 km/s.

The two-stage structure (first a distance from light time, then a speed) is a favourite way to join 6.1.1 to the wave topic.

**Worked example 2: an object orbiting the Earth once a day.** A satellite orbits at an average radius of 4.2 × 10⁷ m with a period of 24 hours.

T = 24 × 3600 = 86 400 s, so v = 2π × 4.2 × 10⁷ ÷ 86 400 = 3.1 × 10³ m/s.

**Worked example 3: rearranging for the period.** Mars has an average orbital radius of 2.28 × 10¹¹ m and an average orbital speed of 2.41 × 10⁴ m/s. Find its orbital period in Earth days.

T = 2πr ÷ v = 2π × 2.28 × 10¹¹ ÷ 2.41 × 10⁴ = 5.94 × 10⁷ s. Dividing by 86 400 gives about 688 days, so a Martian year is nearly twice an Earth year. As a check, light from the Sun takes 2.28 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁸ = 760 s to reach Mars, longer than the 500 s it takes to reach the Earth, which is consistent with Mars being further out.

## What is in the Solar System: classifying bodies

The syllabus list is short, but questions often ask you to classify a described object. Ask two questions: *what does it orbit?* and *which named group does it belong to?*

| Body | Orbits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The Sun | (centre of the system) | The only star; contains most of the mass |
| Eight planets | The Sun | Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, in order from the Sun |
| Minor planets | The Sun | Include dwarf planets such as Pluto, and the asteroids in the asteroid belt |
| Moons | A planet | For example, the Moon orbits the Earth |
| Smaller Solar System bodies | Various | Include comets and natural satellites |

So an object described as "orbiting Jupiter" is a moon, whatever its size, and Pluto is a dwarf planet, not one of the eight planets. The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter.

## Gravitational field strength: two separate ideas

Keep these apart in your answers:

1. **Surface field strength depends on the planet's mass.** A more massive planet has a stronger field at its surface. The Sun has most of the Solar System's mass, so the field at its surface is stronger than at the surface of any planet.
2. **Field strength around a body decreases with distance from it.** Move away from a planet and its field weakens; move away from the Sun and the Sun's field weakens.

Field strength links to weight through W = mg from section 1.3, Mass and weight. A 60 kg astronaut has a weight of 60 × 9.8 = 588 N on the Earth, but only 60 × 3.7 = 222 N on a planet whose surface field strength is 3.7 N/kg. The mass stays 60 kg everywhere; only the weight changes.

## Why planets orbit, and why outer planets are slower

The force that keeps a planet in orbit is the **gravitational attraction of the Sun**, directed towards the Sun. From section 1.5.4, a force perpendicular to the motion makes an object move in a circular path; without it, the planet would continue in a straight line. The force changes the direction of the planet's velocity continuously.

The syllabus then states two linked trends as distance from the Sun increases: the Sun's field strength decreases, and the planets' orbital speeds decrease. An outer planet therefore has both a longer path (larger 2πr) and a lower speed, so its period is much longer. Doubling the radius more than doubles the period.

## Analysing and interpreting planetary data

The data below are **invented for practice**; they do not describe real planets.

| Planet | Orbital distance (10⁶ km) | Orbital period (days) | Density (kg/m³) | Surface temperature (°C) | Surface field strength (N/kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W | 40 | 50 | 5600 | 380 | 4.1 |
| X | 95 | 183 | 5100 | 150 | 9.1 |
| Y | 210 | 601 | 3900 | −40 | 3.5 |
| Z | 700 | 3660 | 1200 | −170 | 19 |

**Annotated model answer.** *Question: Using the data, describe and explain the trends shown by these planets.*

- "Orbital period increases with orbital distance, from 50 days for W at 40 × 10⁶ km to 3660 days for Z at 700 × 10⁶ km." (A trend stated with quoted figures and units.)
- "Average orbital speed decreases with distance: W moves at 2π × 40 × 10⁶ ÷ 4.32 × 10⁶ s = 58 km/s, Z at 2π × 700 × 10⁶ ÷ 3.16 × 10⁸ s = 14 km/s." (Calculated evidence, not just assertion.)
- "This fits the star's gravitational field becoming weaker further away." (Explanation linked to the syllabus statement.)
- "Surface temperature falls with distance, from 380 °C to −170 °C." (Second trend.)
- "Surface field strength shows no pattern with orbital distance, because it depends on each planet's own mass, not on how far the planet is from the star. Z has the strongest surface field, so it is likely to be the most massive." (The discriminating point.)

A good data answer states the direction of a trend, quotes two pairs of values with units, and says when a quantity does not follow a trend.

## Common mistakes

- Using 24 hours, not 365 days, as the Earth's orbital period.
- Using r instead of 2πr as the distance travelled in one orbit.
- Mixing km with seconds and then writing the answer in m/s.
- Saying a planet's surface field strength depends on its distance from the Sun.
- Calling Pluto a planet, or calling a moon a planet because it is large.
- Saying gravity "pushes" planets round; it pulls them towards the Sun.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **Give**: one fact, no explanation needed ("about 500 s").
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units converted, give the answer with a unit and sensible significant figures (two or three, matching the data).
- **Describe** (a trend): direction plus quoted data.
- **Explain**: give the reason, using the syllabus wording ("the gravitational attraction of the Sun").
- **Deduce** or **Determine**: use the data provided, and show which values you used.
- **Suggest**: a reasoned proposal for an unfamiliar situation; more than one answer may be credited.
