## Key terms

- **Basic oxide**: reacts with acids to form a salt and water (Na₂O, MgO).
- **Acidic oxide**: reacts with bases to form a salt and water (SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃).
- **Amphoteric oxide**: reacts with both acids and bases (Al₂O₃).
- **Highest oxide**: the oxide with the element in its maximum oxidation state (Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂,
  P₄O₁₀, SO₃).

## Sodium and magnesium with water

| Reaction | Equation | What you see / pH |
|---|---|---|
| Na + cold water | `2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂` | vigorous; floats, melts, fizzes; pH 13 to 14 |
| Mg + cold water | `Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂` | very slow, few bubbles; pH 9 to 10 |
| Mg + steam | `Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂` | burns with bright white flame; white solid |

Why Na is faster: it loses only one electron per atom. Mg must lose two, and the total energy needed is
much larger.

## Elements with oxygen

| Element | Equation | Flame / product |
|---|---|---|
| Na | `4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O` | yellow-orange; white solid |
| Mg | `2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO` | bright white; white solid |
| Al | `4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃` | powder burns brightly; white solid |
| Si | `Si + O₂ → SiO₂` | strong heating needed; white solid |
| P | `P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀` | bright flame; white fumes |
| S | `S + O₂ → SO₂` | blue flame; colourless choking gas |
| SO₂ → SO₃ | `2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃` | needs V₂O₅ catalyst and heat |

Maximum oxidation state: Na +1, Mg +2, Al +3, Si +4, P +5, S +6. It equals the number of outer
electrons. Bulk aluminium is protected by a thin Al₂O₃ layer.

## Melting points of the highest oxides

Order: **MgO > Al₂O₃ > SiO₂ > Na₂O > P₄O₁₀ > SO₃** (SO₂ is lower still).

| Oxide | Structure | Why that melting point |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | giant ionic | strong attraction between Na⁺ and O²⁻ |
| MgO | giant ionic | Mg²⁺ has higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺, so stronger attraction than in Na₂O |
| Al₂O₃ | giant ionic, some covalent character | Al³⁺ polarises O²⁻; high, but below MgO |
| SiO₂ | macromolecular | many strong covalent bonds to break |
| P₄O₁₀ | simple molecular | van der Waals only; bigger molecule (more electrons) than SO₃ |
| SO₃ | simple molecular | weak van der Waals; low |

**Method in steps: explaining a melting point**

1. Name the structure (giant ionic, macromolecular or simple molecular).
2. Name what is overcome on melting (ionic attraction, covalent bonds, or intermolecular forces).
3. Say how strong it is and so how much energy is needed.
4. For a comparison, give the reason one is stronger (ion charge and size; number of electrons).

## Oxides with water

| Oxide | Equation | pH | Bonding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | `Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH` | 13 to 14 | ionic |
| MgO | `MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂` | 9 to 10 | ionic |
| Al₂O₃ | insoluble | 7 | ionic with covalent character |
| SiO₂ | insoluble | 7 | macromolecular covalent |
| P₄O₁₀ | `P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄` | 0 to 1 | simple molecular covalent |
| SO₂ | `SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃` | 2 to 3 | simple molecular covalent |
| SO₃ | `SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄` | 0 to 1 | simple molecular covalent |

- Ionic oxides: `O²⁻ + H₂O → 2OH⁻`, so alkaline. MgO gives a lower pH because Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly
  soluble.
- Giant structures Al₂O₃ and SiO₂: too strongly bonded to dissolve; pH stays 7.
- Simple covalent oxides: form acids that release H⁺. H₂SO₃ is weak (`H₂SO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HSO₃⁻`), so pH is
  higher than for H₂SO₄.

## Acid and anion structures

| Acid | Fully deprotonated anion | Other anions | Shape at P or S |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₃PO₄: P=O plus three P–OH | PO₄³⁻: P=O plus three P–O⁻ | H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻ | tetrahedral |
| H₂SO₃: S=O plus two S–OH, lone pair on S | SO₃²⁻: S=O plus two S–O⁻, lone pair | HSO₃⁻ | pyramidal |
| H₂SO₄: two S=O plus two S–OH | SO₄²⁻: two S=O plus two S–O⁻ | HSO₄⁻ | tetrahedral |

Quick sketch of H₂SO₃ to remember the lone pair:

```
         O
         ‖
  H–O – S – O–H      with one lone pair on S
```

## Acid and base equations

| Oxide | With acid | With base |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | `Na₂O + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O` | no reaction |
| MgO | `MgO + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂O` | no reaction |
| Al₂O₃ | `Al₂O₃ + 6H⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3H₂O` | `Al₂O₃ + 2OH⁻ + 3H₂O → 2Al(OH)₄⁻` |
| SiO₂ | no reaction | `SiO₂ + 2OH⁻ → SiO₃²⁻ + H₂O` (hot, concentrated) |
| P₄O₁₀ | no reaction | `P₄O₁₀ + 6Na₂O → 4Na₃PO₄` |
| SO₂ | no reaction | `SO₂ + 2OH⁻ → SO₃²⁻ + H₂O`; with less base, `SO₂ + NaOH → NaHSO₃` |
| SO₃ | no reaction | `SO₃ + CaO → CaSO₄` |

**Method in steps: balancing an oxide + base equation**

1. Write the salt: the anion keeps the oxidation state of P or S (S +4 → SO₃²⁻; S +6 → SO₄²⁻; P +5 →
   PO₄³⁻).
2. Balance the central atom first (P₄O₁₀ gives 4 PO₄³⁻).
3. Balance the metal, then hydrogen with water.
4. Check that charges and oxygen atoms balance.

## Practical link

There is no required practical in section 3.2.4, but the specification suggests burning elements in oxygen
and testing the pH of the oxides (AT a, c and k; PS 2.2).

- Burn the element on a combustion spoon in a gas jar of oxygen, add distilled water and shake.
- Measure pH with universal indicator and a pH chart, or with a pH meter or probe.
- Burn phosphorus and sulfur in a fume cupboard: their oxides are toxic and irritant.
- Present results in a table: element, observation, oxide, pH, and whether the oxide is acidic or basic.
- Expected pattern: alkaline for Na and Mg, neutral for Al and Si (insoluble oxides), acidic for P and S.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mg + cold water** gives Mg(OH)₂; **Mg + steam** gives MgO.
- **SO₂ → H₂SO₃** (S +4, weak acid); **SO₃ → H₂SO₄** (S +6).
- **Insoluble** (Al₂O₃, SiO₂: pH 7) is not the same as **neutral oxide**: Al₂O₃ is amphoteric and SiO₂ is
  acidic.
- **Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ melting** both need lots of energy, but for different reasons: ionic attraction against
  covalent bonds.
- **Simple molecular melting** overcomes forces between molecules, never the bonds inside them.

## Quick self-test

1. Write an equation for the reaction of magnesium with steam.
2. Give the approximate pH of the solution formed when Na₂O is added to water.
3. Name the oxide of Period 3 with the highest melting point.
4. State the type of structure in SiO₂.
5. Write an equation for the reaction of SO₃ with water.
6. Why does aluminium oxide not change the pH of water?
7. State the shape around sulfur in the SO₃²⁻ ion.
8. Write an ionic equation for Al₂O₃ reacting with hydroxide ions.
9. How many moles of hydrogen form when 0.46 g of sodium reacts with water? (Ar Na = 23.0)
10. How many moles of NaOH react with 0.010 mol of Al₂O₃ to form NaAl(OH)₄?
11. How many moles of H₃PO₄ form from 0.25 mol of P₄O₁₀ with excess water?
12. Why does MgO give a lower pH than Na₂O in water?

### Answers

1. `Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂`
2. About 13 to 14.
3. MgO.
4. Macromolecular (giant covalent).
5. `SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄`
6. It is insoluble, because of its strong bonding in a giant lattice.
7. Pyramidal (three bonding regions and one lone pair).
8. `Al₂O₃ + 2OH⁻ + 3H₂O → 2Al(OH)₄⁻`
9. 0.46 / 23.0 = 0.020 mol Na; 2Na → H₂, so **0.010 mol H₂**.
10. Al₂O₃ : NaOH = 1 : 2, so **0.020 mol**.
11. P₄O₁₀ : H₃PO₄ = 1 : 4, so **1.0 mol**.
12. Mg(OH)₂ is only slightly soluble, so fewer OH⁻ ions are in solution.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "MgO" as the product of magnesium and cold water.
- Writing H₂SO₄ for the product of SO₂ and water.
- Describing Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ as "neutral oxides" because the pH is 7.
- Explaining the low melting point of P₄O₁₀ by "weak covalent bonds".
- Comparing MgO and Na₂O without mentioning both ion charge and ion size.
- Leaving out water in `Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄`.
- Drawing H₂SO₃ or SO₃²⁻ without the lone pair on sulfur.
- Giving the wrong anion charge: sulfate(IV) is SO₃²⁻ and phosphate is PO₄³⁻.
- Forgetting that SiO₂ needs hot, concentrated NaOH and does not react with water.
