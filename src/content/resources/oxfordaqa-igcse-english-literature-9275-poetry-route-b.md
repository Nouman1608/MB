This guide covers **3.3.1 Poetry** in Route B of OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature
(9275). The specification says students "will study the OxfordAQA Exams Poetry Anthology *People
and Places*", whose 20 poems "are thematically linked in a variety of ways, allowing students to
study a range of connected and linked ideas and themes from poets and poetry from different times
and contexts". On Route B this is assessed in **Section A of Paper 2b: Poetry**.

It concentrates on what is specific to Route B and on skills the general anthology pages treat
briefly: building a theme grid, writing precisely about form, and using context. For the whole of
Route B, see the [Route B poetry and non-exam assessment study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-route-b-poetry-and-non-exam-assessment-study-guide/);
for the anthology question on both routes, see the [People and Places poetry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-poetry/).
This page has its own [Route B poetry revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-poetry-route-b-revision-notes/)
and [Route B poetry practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-poetry-route-b-practice/).

## Paper 2b at a glance

| Feature | Paper 2b: Poetry (Route B) |
|---|---|
| Length | 1 hour 30 minutes |
| Format | Open book |
| Marks | 60, which is 40% of the qualification |
| Section A: Poetry | 30 marks: one question from a choice of two on *People and Places* |
| Section B: Unseen poetry | 30 marks: one question on a previously unseen poem |

On Route B the non-exam assessment replaces Route A's unseen prose, so Paper 2b is your only exam on
poetry and it carries the same weight as Paper 1. The anthology and the 30-mark question are the
same as on Route A; the difference is that you have two sections, not three, in 90 minutes, so
about 45 minutes for each.

The anthology and the set-text lists can change between specification versions, so check with your
teacher which edition of *People and Places* applies to your exam series.

## What the marks reward

The specification's assessment objective weightings give Paper 2b 16% of the qualification for AO1,
16% for AO2 and 8% for AO3. Within the paper, that is roughly 40% AO1, 40% AO2 and 20% AO3:

- **AO1**: understanding of, and engagement with, themes, ideas and contexts;
- **AO2**: analysis of how writers create meanings and effects;
- **AO3**: informed, personal responses, using appropriate terminology, and coherent, accurate
  writing.

Two practical consequences follow. First, ideas and methods are equally weighted, so a paragraph
that only analyses technique, or only discusses theme, is doing half the job. Second, context sits
inside AO1, alongside themes and ideas, so it earns credit only when it deepens what you say about
the poem's ideas.

## Building a theme grid

With 20 poems, revising one poem at a time leaves you unready for a question that cuts across them.
Build a grid instead: themes down the side, poems across the top, and in each cell a few words on
**how** that poem treats that theme.

| Theme | Poem 1 | Poem 2 | Poem 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Belonging | Speaker feels outside; first-person, hesitant tone | — | Collective "we"; celebratory |
| Memory | Present tense makes the past vivid | Fragmented stanzas mirror forgetting | — |
| Power of a place | — | Landscape personified as a threat | Place as refuge; gentle imagery |

Use real anthology titles in your own grid. Three habits make it work:

1. Every poem should appear under at least two themes, so you have flexible choices on the day.
2. Each cell names a **method**, not just a feeling.
3. Mark the pairs that **contrast** within a theme. Two poems that treat belonging differently give
   you more to compare than two that agree.

## Writing precisely about form

Form comments are where many answers stay general ("the poem has four stanzas"). A form comment
earns AO2 credit when it links the shape of the poem to its meaning. Useful forms to recognise:

- **Sonnet**: fourteen lines, often with a turn (volta). Ask what changes at the turn and why the
  poet compresses the idea into such a tight space.
- **Dramatic monologue**: a single speaker, not the poet, addressing a listener. Ask what the speaker
  reveals without meaning to.
- **Free verse**: no regular metre or rhyme. Ask whether the freedom suits a speech-like voice, a
  restless mind or a break from tradition.
- **Regular stanzas and rhyme**: ask whether the order supports the meaning, such as stability or
  ritual, or works against it, for example a cheerful pattern carrying a painful subject.
- **Line length and enjambment**: ask where the poem speeds up, slows down or pauses, and what the
  reader feels at that point.

## Using context well

Context includes when and where a poem was written, the situation it describes and the experiences
that shape its ideas. It works best as a single sentence that explains a choice in the poem: "Writing
as someone who has migrated, the poet presents the old home through present-tense memories, so it
stays alive rather than lost." It works badly as a paragraph of biography before any analysis.

## Worked example: comparing voice and form

The two extracts below are original lines written for this guide, not anthology poems. Imagine they
come from two poems about how people see the land.

> **Extract X:** *You think this hill is nothing. Walk it, then, / at five, with forty kilos on your
> back.*
>
> **Extract Y:** *The surveyor looks at the hill / through a lens, and writes it down as a number.*

A comparative paragraph:

> Both poets present a gap between how outsiders and insiders understand a landscape, but they use
> voice to place the reader on opposite sides of that gap. Extract X is a dramatic monologue that
> addresses "you" directly, so the reader becomes the outsider being challenged; the imperative
> "Walk it, then" and the blunt time "at five" make the hill a daily burden, measured in the body's
> effort and "forty kilos". Extract Y, by contrast, uses a detached third-person observer. The lens
> and the "number" turn the hill into data, and the plain, unemotional syntax mirrors the surveyor's
> distance. Whereas X insists the land is known through labour, Y quietly suggests what is lost when
> it is known only through measurement, so both poems criticise the outsider's view, one with anger
> and one with irony.

Notice how the paragraph names a shared idea (AO1), compares a method in each poem with its effect
(AO2), and builds a personal argument with comparative connectives (AO3).

## A model opening and ending

**Opening:** "Both poets explore how a place shapes the people who live in it, but while one
presents that influence as a burden the speaker resents, the other presents it as an inheritance
the speaker chooses to keep."

**Ending:** "Ultimately, both poems suggest that a place is never simply scenery. The difference
lies in whether the speaker is held by it or holds on to it, and each poet's form, one tense and
one spacious, makes the reader feel that difference."

## Planning your 45 minutes

1. **Choose (2 minutes).** Read both questions and pick the one where your grid gives a strong
   contrasting pair.
2. **Annotate (5 minutes).** Mark four or five short quotations in each poem, including one about
   form or structure.
3. **Argue (3 minutes).** Write a one-sentence argument covering both poems.
4. **Write (30 minutes).** Three or four comparative paragraphs.
5. **Check (5 minutes).** Make sure every paragraph mentions both poems.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the two poems you like best rather than the pair that best fits the question.
- Describing form ("it is a sonnet") without explaining what it does.
- Adding context that does not connect to the poem's ideas.
- Spending too long on Section A and rushing the unseen poem in Section B.
- Calling the speaker "the poet" in a dramatic monologue.

## Command words

- **Compare** asks for similarities and differences throughout, not only at the end.
- **How do the poets present...?** asks for methods and effects in relation to the named idea.
- **Explore** invites more than one interpretation, so use tentative phrasing such as "this could
  suggest".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the anthology question the same on Route A and Route B?**
The specification lists the same anthology and the same 30-mark section on both routes; what differs
is the rest of the paper.

**Can I prepare quotations in advance if the exam is open book?**
Yes: know where your key quotations are, because finding them quickly saves planning time.
