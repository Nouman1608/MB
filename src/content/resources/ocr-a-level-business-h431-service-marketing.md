## What makes services different

A **service** is a product that is mainly an activity or experience, such as haircuts, banking, teaching, travel or healthcare. **Service marketing** is the marketing of such products. Because services differ from goods, the four Ps are not enough. Four features explain why:

- **Intangibility:** a service cannot be seen, touched or tried before purchase. Customers rely on reputation, reviews and appearance to judge it.
- **Inseparability:** the service is produced and consumed at the same time, usually with the customer present. The person delivering it is part of the product.
- **Variability:** quality differs between staff, days and customers. It is hard to standardise.
- **Perishability:** a service cannot be stored. An empty seat on a flight or an unfilled appointment is lost revenue for ever.

These features lead to three extra elements, giving the **seven Ps**: product, price, place and promotion, plus **process, people and physical evidence**.

## The additional three Ps

| Element | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Process** | The procedures, systems and flow by which the service is delivered | Online booking, check-in, waiting-time rules, complaint handling |
| **People** | The staff and customers involved, their skills, attitude and appearance | Trained, friendly dental nurses |
| **Physical evidence** | The tangible clues that show the quality of the service | The look of a clinic, uniforms, website design, receipts, reviews |

### Worked example 1: the cost of perishability

Linden Day Spa offers 36 treatment slots a day. The average price is **£48** and 75% of slots are used.

1. Slots used = 36 × 0.75 = **27**.
2. Daily revenue = 27 × £48 = **£1,296**.
3. Unused slots = 36 − 27 = **9**, worth 9 × £48 = **£432** of lost revenue each day.

On weekdays, 8 of the empty slots are mid-afternoon. Linden offers a **25% off-peak discount** (price £36) and fills 6 of them. The variable cost of a treatment (products and laundry) is £9.

4. Revenue from the extra 6 = 6 × £36 = **£216**.
5. Extra contribution = 6 × (£36 − £9) = **£162**.

**Evaluation.** The discount turns lost revenue into profit because the treatment room and therapists are already paid for. A price-sensitive off-peak customer is not likely to pay £48 anyway. Risks: regular customers may switch to off-peak times and pay less (so the gain is smaller), and a discount may weaken the premium image. The spa can control this by limiting offers to certain days, to new customers or to certain treatments. The example shows why services use price, promotion and booking systems to manage demand.

## Process

Process affects how fast, reliably and smoothly a service is delivered. Poor processes cause queues, mistakes and complaints. Good processes lower cost and raise satisfaction, but they can feel impersonal if too automated.

### Worked example 2: process and capacity

A hotel has **2** receptionists. Each check-in takes **9 minutes**. At the busiest hour, **30** guests arrive.

1. Check-ins per receptionist per hour = 60 ÷ 9 = 6.67.
2. Capacity = 2 × 6.67 = **13.3** check-ins per hour.
3. Guests waiting at the end of the hour = 30 − 13.3 = **16.7**.

The queue grows by about 17 guests each hour. Introducing online pre-check-in cuts the desk time to **3 minutes**:

4. Capacity = 2 × (60 ÷ 3) = **40** check-ins per hour, above 30 arrivals.

The queue disappears without hiring staff. The change in process improves service, reduces stress on staff and can be advertised ("no queues"). **Limits:** the system costs money, some guests prefer personal contact, and failures such as a broken app create new problems. The importance of process is highest where waiting is the main source of dissatisfaction.

## People

In services, staff are part of the product because the customer experiences the service through them. Their skills, attitude, appearance and knowledge decide the quality. Marketing therefore includes **recruiting, training, rewarding and motivating** staff (sometimes called internal marketing). Customers are also "people" in the mix, since their behaviour affects the experience of others, for example in a restaurant.

### Worked example 3: the return from training

A dental group trains **25** staff in customer care at **£800** each, a total cost of **£20,000**.

- Staff turnover falls from 40% to 28%. Leavers fall from 10 (25 × 0.40) to 7 (25 × 0.28), saving 3 replacements at £3,000 each = **£9,000**.
- Better service raises customer retention by 3 percentage points among 8,000 customers: 8,000 × 0.03 = **240** more customers. At £70 average contribution each, this is **£16,800**.
- Total benefit = £9,000 + £16,800 = £25,800.
- Net gain = £25,800 − £20,000 = **£5,800**.

**Evaluation.** The training pays back in the first year only because of retained customers. The 3 percentage point rise is an estimate. If it were 1 point (80 customers, £5,600), the benefit would be £14,600 and the training would make a loss of £5,400. The result depends on whether better service really changes customer behaviour. Staff motivation and pay matter too: training alone will not keep staff who are poorly paid.

## Physical evidence

Because a service is intangible, customers look for tangible clues to judge it in advance. Physical evidence includes the building and its cleanliness, furniture, uniforms, brochures, the website, packaging of any products, branded vehicles, certificates, and even online reviews. A law firm with a tidy, professional office and clear paperwork signals competence. A hospital with a dirty waiting room signals poor care, however good the doctors. Physical evidence builds trust before purchase and reminds the customer of the service afterwards (a receipt or photo).

Its importance is greatest when the service is high risk or costly, or when customers cannot assess quality themselves. Its costs are fit-out and maintenance. It can also mislead: an impressive environment cannot cover for poor service for long.

## A mix for a service

To **recommend and justify** a seven Ps mix for a service:

1. Begin with the target customer and the objective.
2. Define the **product**, including the level of service, and its guarantee to reduce risk.
3. Set a **price** that signals quality and manages demand (peak and off-peak).
4. Choose **place**: location, online booking, or home visits.
5. Use **promotion** that makes the intangible visible: reviews, case studies, demonstrations, trials.
6. Design the **process** so that it is quick and consistent.
7. Invest in **people**: selection, training and reward.
8. Provide **physical evidence** that fits the price and image.
9. Check that all seven are consistent and state the main risk.

## Expectation and experience

Because services are intangible and variable, satisfaction depends on whether the **experience** matches the customer's **expectation**. Promotion creates the expectation; process, people and physical evidence create the experience. If an advertisement promises "no waiting" and the patient waits 25 minutes, the gap produces dissatisfaction even though the treatment was good. This is why the seven elements must be consistent, and why over-promising is a risk. A business can measure the gap with short surveys after each visit, for example a score out of 10, and track the average over time. A fall from 8.6 to 7.9 would prompt an investigation of which element changed.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the seven Ps without applying them to the service in the case.
- Treating the three extra Ps as optional extras. For many services they decide the customer's experience.
- Forgetting perishability, which explains peak and off-peak pricing.
- Claiming training or process changes will pay for themselves without any figures.
- Describing physical evidence as buildings only.

## Exam technique

Name the element, say what it means in the context given, explain how it affects the customer's experience or the business's costs, and weigh the importance against the other elements. For a recommendation, cover all seven elements briefly, then justify the two or three that matter most.
