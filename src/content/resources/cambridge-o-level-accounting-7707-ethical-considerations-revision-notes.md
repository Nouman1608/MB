These notes condense section **7.2 Ethical considerations** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The section covers "the need for an ethical framework in accounting", the fundamental principles of "integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour", and "the significance to stakeholders and society of applying an ethical framework". The worked case, with its effect on the figures, is in the [ethical considerations study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-ethical-considerations/); test yourself with the [ethical considerations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-ethical-considerations-practice/). People and figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethics | Principles of right and wrong conduct |
| Ethical framework | A set of standards that guides accountants' behaviour, set in advance |
| Integrity | Being honest and straightforward |
| Objectivity | Not letting bias, conflicts of interest or pressure override judgement |
| Professional competence and due care | Having and maintaining the knowledge and skill needed, and working carefully |
| Confidentiality | Not disclosing or using work information unless there is a legal or professional duty |
| Professional behaviour | Obeying laws and regulations; avoiding conduct that discredits the profession |
| Conflict of interest | A situation where personal interests could affect professional judgement |
| Stakeholder | Anyone affected by, or relying on, the business and its accounts |

## Why a framework is needed

1. Users rely on figures they cannot check.
2. Accountants face pressure from owners and managers.
3. Many figures depend on estimates and judgement.
4. Accountants handle private information.
5. Standards set in advance apply equally whoever is asking.

## Spotting the principle

| Ask | Principle |
|---|---|
| Is a figure or record knowingly false? | Integrity |
| Is judgement swayed by a relationship, bias or pressure? | Objectivity |
| Was the work done without the right knowledge or proper care? | Professional competence and due care |
| Was private information passed on or misused? | Confidentiality |
| Was the law broken, or the profession brought into disrepute? | Professional behaviour |

One case can breach several principles. Give a separate reason for each one you name.

## Significance to stakeholders and society

| Stakeholder | Why ethical accounting matters to them |
|---|---|
| Owners and investors | Decisions to invest or expand rest on true results |
| Banks and lenders | Loans are made only to businesses able to repay |
| Suppliers | Credit is given on a true picture of liquidity |
| Employees | Problems are seen early, protecting jobs |
| Tax authorities | Tax is charged on the true profit |
| Society | Trust in accounts keeps finance available and supports public services |

## Typical responses to pressure

- Explain the correct treatment and why it is required.
- Refuse to record or present figures known to be false.
- Record the transaction correctly.
- Seek advice, for example from a professional body, if pressure continues.

## Measuring an unethical request

When a question gives figures, show the effect. Draft profit is $40,000 and current assets are $30,000, with current liabilities of $20,000. The owner asks for a $2,000 irrecoverable debt not to be written off before a loan application.

- Reported profit stays at $40,000 instead of the true $38,000.
- The current ratio shows 30,000 / 20,000 = 1.50 : 1 instead of 28,000 / 20,000 = 1.40 : 1.
- The bank would lend on a better picture than the truth: integrity and objectivity are at stake.

## Exam traps

- "Explain" needs the principle, what it requires and how the case breaches it.
- Objectivity is about bias and pressure; integrity is about honesty.
- Confidentiality is not absolute: a legal duty can require disclosure.
- Society answers must go beyond the business owner: trust, finance, tax for public services.
- If figures are given, calculate the effect of the unethical treatment.
- Use the principle names exactly as the syllabus gives them.
- An ethical response is a realistic action, such as refusing or seeking advice, not simply "report them".
- Stakeholder answers should say what decision the false figures would distort.

## Self-test

1. Give three reasons why accounting needs an ethical framework.
2. List the five fundamental principles.
3. A book-keeper tells his friends the salary of the managing director. Which principle is breached?
4. An accountant approves payments to a firm owned by her brother without independent checks. Which principle is at risk?
5. A clerk records a sale that never took place to meet a sales target. Which principle is breached?
6. An assistant prepares a bank reconciliation without understanding how, and does not check it. Which principle is breached?
7. An accountant helps a client hide income from the tax authority. Which principle is most clearly breached?
8. State the difference between integrity and objectivity.
9. When may an accountant disclose confidential information?
10. Explain the significance to society of accountants applying an ethical framework.
11. A business's true profit is $40,000. The owner asks for a $3,000 expense to be left out. By what percentage would profit be overstated?
12. Why does ethical accounting matter to a supplier?

### Answers

1. Any three: users rely on figures they cannot check; accountants face pressure; figures involve judgement; accountants handle private information; standards set in advance apply whoever is asking.
2. Integrity; objectivity; professional competence and due care; confidentiality; professional behaviour.
3. Confidentiality.
4. Objectivity.
5. Integrity.
6. Professional competence and due care.
7. Professional behaviour (also integrity, as the records would be false).
8. Integrity is about being honest and straightforward; objectivity is about not letting bias, a conflict of interest or pressure override judgement.
9. When there is a legal or professional duty to disclose it, for example when required by law.
10. Trustworthy accounts let lenders and investors provide finance with confidence, help tax to be collected fairly for public services, and protect jobs and suppliers from hidden failures.
11. Reported profit would be $43,000; 3,000 / 40,000 x 100 = **7.5%** overstated.
12. Suppliers decide whether to give credit from the accounts; false figures could lead them to give credit that is never paid.
