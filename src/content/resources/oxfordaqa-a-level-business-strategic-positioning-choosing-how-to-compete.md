## 1. Market mapping: purpose and value

A **market map** (also called a perceptual map or positioning map) plots the brands or businesses in a market against two features that matter to customers, such as price and quality. Positions come from customer research or managers' judgement.

**Purpose:**

- show where each competitor is positioned and which ones are closest rivals
- reveal **gaps**: combinations of features that no business offers
- check whether customers see the firm the way the firm intends
- support decisions on launching, repositioning or targeting a segment

### Worked example 1: reading a market map

Glimmerbay has five swimming schools for children. A local survey asked parents to score each school's overall benefits (teaching quality, class size, facilities) out of 10.

| School | Price per 30-minute lesson | Benefits score |
|---|---|---|
| Splashbright | £14 | 8.6 |
| Finwise Academy | £16 | 7.0 |
| Ripple Kids | £11 | 7.4 |
| Aquanest | £9 | 6.1 |
| Council pool | £6 | 5.2 |

Plot price on one axis and benefits score on the other. Then calculate price per benefit point to compare value:

```
Splashbright     14 ÷ 8.6 = £1.63 per point
Finwise Academy  16 ÷ 7.0 = £2.29 per point
Ripple Kids      11 ÷ 7.4 = £1.49 per point
Aquanest          9 ÷ 6.1 = £1.48 per point
Council pool      6 ÷ 5.2 = £1.15 per point
```

**What the map shows.**

- Finwise charges the most but scores below Splashbright and Ripple Kids. It looks poor value, so it is exposed if parents compare.
- No school offers a score above 8 for less than £12. A new entrant, or Ripple Kids upgrading, could aim at that gap.
- The council pool is the low price option; Aquanest sits close to it at a higher price.

**Judgement.** The gap is worth filling only if enough parents want it and a school can serve it profitably; the map alone cannot show that.

**Value and limitations of market mapping.**

| Value | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Quick, visual picture of the competition | Only two variables; real choices involve many |
| Highlights gaps and close rivals | A gap may exist because there is no demand, or no profitable way to serve it |
| Based on customer views, if built from research | Perceptions vary by segment and change over time |
| Helps plan positioning and repositioning | If based on managers' opinion, it may reflect bias, not customers |

## 2. Choosing a strategic position: benefits and price

A business can choose how to compete by deciding **what benefits** it offers (quality, features, design, service, convenience, brand) and **what price** it charges compared with rivals. Common positions:

| Position | Benefits vs rivals | Price vs rivals | Likely outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low price, standard benefits | Similar | Lower | Wins price-sensitive buyers; needs low costs to stay profitable |
| Budget | Fewer | Lower | Basic product for buyers who want the cheapest option |
| Better value | More | Similar | Attracts customers from rivals; costs may rise |
| Premium (differentiated) | More | Higher | Higher margin per unit; needs a real reason to pay more |
| More for less | More | Lower | Strong appeal but hard to fund for long |
| Overpriced | Same or fewer | Higher | Customers leave unless they have no alternative |

A business can only charge more than rivals if customers believe they receive more.

## 3. Low cost versus differentiation

These are the two broad ways to gain an edge.

**Low cost strategy.** The business aims to have lower costs than rivals for a similar product. It can then either charge a lower price and win volume, or charge the same price as rivals and earn a higher margin. Low cost does not have to mean low price.

**Differentiation strategy.** The business makes its product different from and better than rivals' in ways customers value, so it can charge a higher price or win loyalty at a similar price.

| | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Can survive price wars; appeals to the many price-sensitive buyers; scale may create a barrier to entry; works well when incomes fall | Higher price and margin per unit; loyal customers are less price-sensitive; harder for rivals to copy a strong brand |
| **Disadvantages** | Thin margins need high volume; a rival with even lower costs can undercut you; cost cutting may damage quality or staff morale; a cheap image is hard to change later | Costly design, marketing and quality control; customers may not value the extras enough; rivals can copy features; demand can fall in a downturn |

### Worked example 2: comparing the two strategies

Larchpoint Bikes is launching an e-bike range and must choose a position.

| | Low cost option | Differentiated option |
|---|---|---|
| Price | £900 | £1,450 |
| Variable cost per bike | £640 | £960 |
| Expected annual sales | 6,000 | 3,000 |
| Fixed costs | £1,100,000 | £1,050,000 |

```
Low cost:        (900 − 640) × 6,000 − 1,100,000   = £460,000
                 margin = 460,000 ÷ 5,400,000 × 100 = 8.5%
Differentiated:  (1,450 − 960) × 3,000 − 1,050,000 = £420,000
                 margin = 420,000 ÷ 4,350,000 × 100 = 9.7%
```

Now test each against a threat. Suppose a rival starts a price war:

```
Low cost option must cut price 5% to £855:
  (855 − 640) × 6,000 − 1,100,000 = £190,000  (profit falls 58.7%)
Differentiated option keeps its price but loses 5% of sales:
  (1,450 − 960) × 2,850 − 1,050,000 = £346,500  (profit falls 17.5%)
```

**Judgement.** The low cost option makes more profit in the base case, but its low contribution per bike (£260 against £490) leaves it badly exposed to price cuts. The better choice depends on whether Larchpoint really can produce more cheaply than rivals and how price-sensitive e-bike buyers are.

## 4. Influences on the choice of positioning strategy

- **The firm's strengths and resources.** Large scale and efficient operations suit low cost; design skills, patents or a strong brand suit differentiation. A SWOT analysis helps here.
- **Customers.** If buyers are price-sensitive (price elastic demand) or incomes are falling, low cost appeals. If they value quality, image or service, differentiation fits.
- **Competitors' positions.** A market map shows which positions are crowded. Copying the market leader's position rarely works without a cost or quality edge.
- **Industry forces.** Powerful buyers and many substitutes push margins down, making cost control vital; high entry barriers can protect a differentiated position.
- **Objectives.** A growth or market share objective points towards low cost and volume; a profit margin objective may favour differentiation.
- **Finance.** Building scale and building a brand both need investment; what the firm can fund limits the choice.
- **External environment.** The economy, technology and regulation shift what customers will pay for.

## 5. Implications for functional areas

A position only works if every function supports it.

| Function | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Price-led promotion; wide distribution; limited product range | Brand building; promotion stresses quality or features; selective distribution |
| Operations | High capacity use, standardised processes, lean production, bulk buying | Quality assurance, innovation, flexible or customised production, research and development |
| Finance | Investment in capacity and automation; tight budgets and cost monitoring | Investment in research, design and promotion; needs higher margins to cover them |
| Human resources | Efficient staffing, possibly lower skill roles, multi-skilling | Skilled, well-trained staff; recruitment and retention matter more; may need higher pay |

## 6. Competitive advantage

A **competitive advantage** is an advantage over rivals that lets a business earn higher profits or win more customers, because it offers better value through lower costs or greater benefits.

**Benefits of a competitive advantage:**

- higher margins (from a price premium or lower costs) and so more profit
- customer loyalty, which steadies sales and lowers marketing cost per sale
- more bargaining power with suppliers and distributors
- retained profit to reinvest, which can extend the advantage
- a stronger position from which to grow into new markets or products

**Difficulties of maintaining a competitive advantage:**

- **Imitation.** Rivals copy features, processes or prices; patents expire.
- **Changing customer tastes.** What customers valued may stop mattering.
- **New technology** can remove a cost advantage or make a feature standard.
- **New entrants and substitutes** offer customers other ways to meet the need.
- **Rising costs** or loss of a key supplier erode a cost advantage.
- **Complacency** inside the business, and loss of the staff whose skills created the edge.

### Worked example 3: an advantage that fades

Sablewick makes chilli sauce from chillies grown on its own farm, giving a flavour customers rate highly. It charges £1.20 a bottle more than rival sauces and sells 85,000 bottles a year. Two rivals then source similar chillies and copy the recipe style. Sablewick's premium falls to £0.45 and sales drop to 78,000 bottles.

```
Extra revenue from premium before = 1.20 × 85,000 = £102,000
Extra revenue from premium after  = 0.45 × 78,000 = £35,100
Fall = 102,000 − 35,100 = £66,900
     = 66,900 ÷ 102,000 × 100 = 65.6%
```

**Analysis.** Most of the advantage's value vanished once its source was copied. Advantages rivals cannot easily copy (a protected brand, unique know-how, exclusive supply) last longer. Sablewick could build a brand around its own farm or launch new flavours regularly, each with a cost to weigh up.

## Common errors

- Treating "low cost" as the same as "low price". A low cost firm can match rivals' prices and keep a higher margin.
- Saying a market map "proves" a gap should be filled. It shows a gap, not demand or profit.
- Ending a positioning answer without a judgement that weighs risk, not just base-case profit.
