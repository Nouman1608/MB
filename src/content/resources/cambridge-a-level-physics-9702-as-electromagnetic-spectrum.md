## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, 7.4 Electromagnetic spectrum**

Candidates should be able to:

- state that all electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that travel with
the same speed c in free space
- recall the approximate range of wavelengths in free space of the principal
regions of the electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves to γ-rays
- recall that wavelengths in the range 400–700 nm in free space are visible to
the human eye

An understanding of colour from IGCSE/O Level is assumed for Topic 7.

## What every electromagnetic wave has in common

An electromagnetic wave is a travelling disturbance of linked **electric and
magnetic fields**. The fields oscillate at right angles to each other and at
right angles to the direction in which the wave travels, so every
electromagnetic wave is **transverse**. This is why electromagnetic waves,
including light and microwaves, can be polarised (subtopic 7.5).

Because the disturbance is in the fields themselves, an electromagnetic wave
does not need a material medium. In a vacuum ("free space") all
electromagnetic waves, whatever their frequency, travel at the same speed:

```
c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1      (in free space)
c = f lambda
```

The two statements the syllabus asks you to *state* go together: **all
electromagnetic waves are transverse** and **all travel at the same speed c in
free space**. An answer giving only one of them is incomplete.

## Why "in free space" matters

When an electromagnetic wave enters a material such as glass or water, it
slows down. The frequency is fixed by the source and does not change, so the
wavelength becomes shorter (λ = v/f with v < c). A wavelength quoted for a
region of the spectrum, or the 400–700 nm visible band, is therefore always
the **free-space** value. In air the speed is so close to c that the
difference can be ignored in AS calculations.

## The principal regions and their approximate wavelengths

The syllabus asks for **approximate** ranges. The boundaries are not sharp,
and different textbooks quote slightly different values, so learn orders of
magnitude rather than exact limits. Typical values are:

| Region | Approximate wavelength in free space | Approximate frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Radio waves | longer than about 10⁻¹ m (up to kilometres) | below about 3 × 10⁹ Hz |
| Microwaves | about 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m | about 3 × 10⁹ Hz to 3 × 10¹¹ Hz |
| Infrared | about 7 × 10⁻⁷ m to 10⁻³ m | about 3 × 10¹¹ Hz to 4 × 10¹⁴ Hz |
| Visible | 4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m (400–700 nm) | about 4 × 10¹⁴ Hz to 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz |
| Ultraviolet | about 10⁻⁸ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m | about 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz to 3 × 10¹⁶ Hz |
| X-rays | about 10⁻¹³ m to 10⁻⁸ m | about 3 × 10¹⁶ Hz to 3 × 10²¹ Hz |
| γ-rays | shorter than about 10⁻¹⁰ m | above about 3 × 10¹⁸ Hz |

Two features need comment:

- **X-rays and γ-rays overlap.** In the overlapping range they are the same
kind of radiation; the name normally reflects how the radiation was produced
(γ-rays come from nuclei), not a sharp wavelength boundary.
- **Visible light is a tiny slice.** The whole visible band covers less than a
factor of two in wavelength, while the spectrum as a whole covers more than
fifteen powers of ten.

**Memory aid for the order.** From longest wavelength to shortest: radio,
microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, γ-ray. Frequency runs the
other way. Within the visible band, red is at the long-wavelength end (about
700 nm) and violet at the short end (about 400 nm), which is why the
neighbouring regions are called *infra*-red and *ultra*-violet.

## Moving between wavelength, frequency and region

Every calculation in this subtopic uses c = fλ with c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

**Worked example 1.** A microwave oven operates at 2.45 GHz. Find the
wavelength.

```
lambda = c / f = 3.00 x 10^8 / 2.45 x 10^9 = 0.122 m  (12.2 cm)
```

This lies inside the microwave range of about 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m, close to its
long-wavelength end.

**Worked example 2.** Find the frequency of green light of wavelength 550 nm.

```
lambda = 550 nm = 550 x 10^-9 m = 5.50 x 10^-7 m
f = c / lambda = 3.00 x 10^8 / 5.50 x 10^-7 = 5.45 x 10^14 Hz
```

**Worked example 3.** Radiation has a frequency of 1.5 × 10¹⁵ Hz. Identify the
region.

```
lambda = 3.00 x 10^8 / 1.5 x 10^15 = 2.0 x 10^-7 m = 200 nm
```

200 nm is shorter than 400 nm but longer than about 10 nm, so the radiation
is **ultraviolet**.

**Worked example 4.** A source emits radiation of frequency 3.0 × 10¹² Hz.
λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 3.0 × 10¹² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m, which is in the **infrared**.

## Prefixes you must convert confidently

| Prefix | Factor | Example |
|---|---|---|
| mm | 10⁻³ m | microwaves around 1 mm at the short end |
| μm | 10⁻⁶ m | infrared around 10 μm |
| nm | 10⁻⁹ m | visible 400–700 nm |
| pm | 10⁻¹² m | X-rays and γ-rays around 1 pm |
| MHz, GHz | 10⁶ Hz, 10⁹ Hz | radio and microwave frequencies |

## Exam technique

- **State** the properties of electromagnetic waves: give both "transverse"
and "same speed c in free space".
- **Recall** an approximate wavelength: give a power of ten with a unit, such
as "about 10⁻² m" for microwaves. A value in the right order of magnitude
and inside the accepted range earns the mark.
- **Identify** a region: always convert to a wavelength in metres first, then
compare with the ranges.
- **Calculate**: show the conversion of nm or GHz into SI units on its own
line.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the order, especially placing ultraviolet before visible on the
long-wavelength side.
- Quoting the visible range as 400–700 μm or 400–700 mm.
- Saying that electromagnetic waves travel at c in all materials.
- Claiming that γ-rays always have shorter wavelengths than X-rays.
- Forgetting that frequency, not wavelength, stays constant when the wave
enters a new medium.

## Quick revision checklist

- All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
in free space
- Order: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, γ-ray
(decreasing wavelength)
- Approximate wavelength range of each region, to an order of magnitude
- Visible: 400–700 nm in free space; red longest, violet shortest
- c = fλ to move between frequency, wavelength and region

## Frequently asked questions

**Do all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed?**
In free space, yes: all travel at c. In a material they travel more slowly,
and the speed can depend on frequency.

**How precise do the wavelength ranges need to be?**
The syllabus asks for approximate ranges. Learn the order of magnitude for
each region and the exact visible band of 400–700 nm.

**Why are X-rays and γ-rays hard to separate by wavelength?**
Their ranges overlap. The names usually describe the origin of the radiation
rather than a fixed wavelength boundary.
