(7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards. They cover 3.2.1.1

## Key terms

- **Periodicity:** a repeating pattern of properties across each period of the Periodic Table.
- **Proton number (Z):** the number of protons in the nucleus. It sets an element's position in the
  Periodic Table.
- **s, p, d, f block:** the block is named after the sub-shell that holds the element's highest-energy
  electron.
- **First ionisation energy:** the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous
  atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions. Equation pattern: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻
- **Metallic bonding:** attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions.
- **Macromolecular (giant covalent):** a lattice of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout.
- **Van der Waals forces:** induced dipole–dipole forces between molecules or atoms.

For the full ionisation energy material (successive ionisation energies and the evidence for sub-shells),
see the [atomic structure revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-atomic-structure-revision-notes/).

## 3.2.1.1 Blocks in one table

| Block | Highest-energy electron | Where it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| s | s sub-shell | Groups 1 and 2, plus H and He | Na, Mg, K, Ca, He |
| p | p sub-shell | Group 3 to Group 0 (13 to 18), except He | Al to Ar, Ga to Kr |
| d | d sub-shell | between Group 2 and Group 3 | Sc to Zn in Period 4 |
| f | f sub-shell | lanthanides and actinides | the two rows below the main table |

### Method in steps: classifying an element

1. Write the electron configuration from Z (or read Z from the Periodic Table).
2. Find the sub-shell filled **last in energy order** (remember 4s fills before 3d).
3. Name the block after that sub-shell type.

**Traps:** helium (1s²) is s block, not p block. Gallium to krypton contain 3d¹⁰ but are p block, because
their last electron goes into 4p. Chromium and copper are d block even though they end 4s¹.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

(vdW = van der Waals forces.)

## Atomic radius

**Trend:** decreases from Na to Cl.

**Method in steps (three marks' worth of ideas):**

1. Nuclear charge increases (one more proton each time).
2. Electrons are added to the same shell, so shielding is similar.
3. Stronger attraction pulls the outer electrons closer.

## First ionisation energy

**Trend:** general increase from Na to Ar. Na is lowest, Ar is highest. Two small drops: Al (below Mg) and
S (below P).

**Explaining the general rise:** greater nuclear charge, similar shielding (same shell), smaller radius, so
the outer electron is held more strongly and needs more energy to remove.

**Explaining the drops:** see the [atomic structure study guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-chemistry-atomic-structure/).
In one line each: Al loses a 3p electron that is higher in energy than 3s; S loses one of a pair of
electrons sharing a 3p orbital.

## Melting point

**Pattern:** rises Na → Mg → Al, peaks at Si, drops sharply to P, rises to S, then falls through Cl to Ar
(the lowest).

### Method in steps: any melting point comparison

1. Name the structure of each element.
2. State what must be overcome or broken to melt it.
3. Say which is stronger and why.
4. Link to energy: stronger forces or bonds need more energy, so the melting point is higher.

### Small reminders

**Metals, Na to Al.** Ion charge goes +1, +2, +3. Delocalised electrons per atom go 1, 2, 3. The ions get
smaller. So the metallic bonding gets stronger and the melting point rises.

**Silicon.** Many strong covalent bonds must be broken, so it has the highest melting point in the period.

**P₄, S₈, Cl₂.** Only van der Waals forces between molecules are overcome; the covalent bonds inside each
molecule stay intact. More electrons per molecule means stronger van der Waals forces:

```
S₈ 128 electrons  >  P₄ 60 electrons  >  Cl₂ 34 electrons
```

So the order of melting points for these three is S > P > Cl.

**Argon.** Single atoms with 18 electrons each: the weakest van der Waals forces, so the lowest melting
point in Period 3.

## Sketch shapes to remember

- **Atomic radius:** a steady fall from Na to Cl.
- **First ionisation energy:** a zig-zag rise from Na to Ar, with drops at Al and S.
- **Melting point:** up to a sharp peak at Si, a big drop to P, a small rise to S, then down to Ar.

If you are asked to identify an element from a graph, use the most distinctive point: Si is the highest
melting point, Ar the lowest, and Ar also has the highest first ionisation energy in the period.

## Must-know distinctions

| Don't confuse | With |
|---|---|
| Breaking covalent bonds (Si) | Overcoming van der Waals forces between molecules (P₄, S₈, Cl₂) |
| Metallic bonding (ions and delocalised electrons) | Ionic bonding (oppositely charged ions in a lattice) |
| Atomic radius falling across a period | Atomic radius rising down a group (more shells) |
| Ionisation energy trend (rises to Ar) | Melting point trend (peaks at Si) |
| Block (sub-shell of the highest-energy electron) | Group (number of outer electrons, or column) |
| Nuclear charge (number of protons) | Shielding (repulsion from inner electrons) |

## Quick self-test

1. What property of an element sets its position in the Periodic Table?
2. State the block of the element with Z = 20.
3. State the block of the element with Z = 34.
4. An element has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s². Name its block.
5. Why is helium an s-block element?
6. Which Period 3 element has the lowest first ionisation energy, and which the highest?
7. Name the two Period 3 elements whose first ionisation energy is lower than that of the element before.
8. Give two reasons why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.
9. What is overcome when sulfur melts?
10. Why does chlorine have a lower melting point than phosphorus?
11. Why does silicon have the highest melting point in Period 3?
12. Explain why a chlorine atom is smaller than a sodium atom.

### Answers

1. Its proton number (atomic number).
2. s block (calcium, 4s²).
3. p block (selenium; the last electron is added to 4p).
4. d block (iron; the last electron is added to 3d).
5. Its configuration is 1s², so its highest-energy electron is in an s sub-shell.
6. Lowest: sodium. Highest: argon.
7. Aluminium (lower than magnesium) and sulfur (lower than phosphorus).
8. Mg²⁺ has a greater charge than Na⁺ (and is smaller); magnesium has two delocalised electrons per atom
   compared with one. So the attraction between ions and delocalised electrons is stronger.
9. Van der Waals forces between S₈ molecules (no covalent bonds break).
10. Both are simple molecular. Cl₂ has 34 electrons and P₄ has 60, so the van der Waals forces between Cl₂
    molecules are weaker and less energy is needed to overcome them.
11. It is macromolecular, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy.
12. Chlorine has a greater nuclear charge (17 protons against 11), its outer electrons are in the same shell
    with similar shielding, so they are attracted more strongly and held closer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "more shells" or "more shielding" to explain a trend across Period 3. Every element has its
  outer electrons in the third shell.
- Giving only "more protons" for the radius or ionisation energy trend, with no mention of similar shielding.
- Saying covalent bonds break when phosphorus, sulfur or chlorine melts.
- Calling silicon simple molecular, or describing it as having "strong intermolecular forces".
- Describing metallic bonding without the words "positive ions" and "delocalised electrons".
- Comparing van der Waals forces by Mr alone, without linking it to the number of electrons.
- Classifying helium as p block, or gallium as d block.
- Leaving out the (g) state symbols in a first ionisation energy equation.
- Describing the ionisation energy trend as a smooth rise and forgetting the drops at Al and S.
