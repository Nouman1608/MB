These questions cover **Macroeconomic equilibrium**, part of topic 1.4 The interaction of aggregate demand and supply in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026): equilibrium in the macroeconomy and the effects of changes in AD and AS on macroeconomic indicators. All countries and data are invented. Revise first with the [macroeconomic equilibrium study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-macroeconomic-equilibrium/) and the [macroeconomic equilibrium revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-macroeconomic-equilibrium-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** At the current price level, real output supplied exceeds real output demanded. Which **one** of the following is most likely to happen? **[1]**

A: Stocks fall and the price level rises

B: Stocks rise and the price level falls

C: Stocks fall and output rises

D: Stocks rise and output rises

**2.** Which **one** of the following describes long-run macroeconomic equilibrium? **[1]**

A: AD equals SRAS at any level of output

B: Injections equal withdrawals

C: AD, SRAS and LRAS intersect at the same point

D: The price level is stable

**3.** Define macroeconomic equilibrium. **[2]**

**4.** Equilibrium real output in Country G rises from 1,500 to 1,527 billion and the price level index from 100.0 to 101.5. Calculate the rate of economic growth and the rate of inflation. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why short-run macroeconomic equilibrium may occur with high unemployment. **[2]**

**6.** SRAS shifts to the left. State the likely effect on **two** macroeconomic indicators. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows invented schedules for Country H (real output in billions). Potential output is 800.

| Price level | AD1 | SRAS | AD2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90 | 830 | 750 | 750 |
| 100 | 800 | 800 | 720 |
| 110 | 770 | 850 | 690 |
| 120 | 740 | 900 | 660 |

**(a)** State the initial equilibrium using AD1 and SRAS, and explain whether it is a long-run equilibrium. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what would happen if the price level were 110 with AD1. **[2]**

**(c)** AD falls to AD2. State the new equilibrium and calculate the shortfall of output below potential as a percentage of potential. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain how, in the neo-classical view, the economy would return to potential output. **[2]**

**8.** A cut in interest rates in Country J moves equilibrium from P = 100, Y = 1,200 to P = 104, Y = 1,236.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of growth and the rate of inflation. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the likely effects on unemployment and on the current account. **[4]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the effects of a large fall in world commodity prices on economic growth and inflation. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a rise in aggregate demand may cause much more inflation in one economy than in another. **[4]**

**11.** Explain the difference between the Keynesian and neo-classical views of whether an economy below full capacity will return to it without government action. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that an increase in aggregate demand will always improve a country's macroeconomic performance. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Unsold output builds up as stocks, so firms cut prices and output. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Long-run equilibrium needs AD = SRAS = LRAS. **[1]**

**3.** The price level and level of real output at which aggregate demand equals aggregate supply [1], so there is no pressure for either to change [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Growth = 27 / 1,500 x 100 = **1.8%** [1]. Inflation = **1.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** AD may meet SRAS at an output level below potential [1]; firms produce only what is demanded, so they employ fewer workers than are available, leaving unemployment [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two from: inflation rises [1]; growth falls (real output falls) [1]; unemployment rises; the current account may worsen as competitiveness falls. **[2]**

**7. (a)** P = **100**, Y = **800** [1]. Output equals potential output of 800, so AD = SRAS = LRAS and this is a long-run equilibrium [1].

**(b)** Output supplied (850) exceeds output demanded (770): excess supply of 80 [1]. Unsold stocks build up, so firms cut prices and output until the price level falls back to 100 [1].

**(c)** AD2 = SRAS at **P = 90, Y = 750** [1]. Shortfall = 50 / 800 = **6.25%** of potential [1].

**(d)** High unemployment pushes money wages down [1], lowering firms' costs so SRAS shifts right until output returns to 800 at a lower price level [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), name the mechanism (falling wages shift SRAS), not just "the economy self-corrects".

**8. (a)** Growth = 36 / 1,200 = **3.0%** [1]. Inflation = **4.0%** [1].

**(b)** Unemployment: firms need more workers to produce 3% more output [1], so unemployment is likely to fall, especially if output was below potential [1]. Current account: higher incomes raise spending on imports [1], and a 4% rise in prices makes exports less competitive, so the current account is likely to worsen [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Cheaper commodities lower firms' costs of production [1], so SRAS shifts right from SRAS1 to SRAS2 [1]. The diagram shows a new equilibrium at a lower price level and higher real output [1], so growth rises while inflation falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A supply-side improvement gives the rare combination of higher growth and lower inflation; say so explicitly.

**10.** The effect depends on the amount of spare capacity [1]. In an economy with high unemployment and idle capital, the AS curve is flat, so higher AD mainly raises output [1]. In an economy close to full capacity, AS is steep, so firms respond by raising prices [1]. Expectations also matter: if people expect inflation, wages rise faster and more of the AD increase becomes inflation [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Neo-classical economists argue that wages and prices are flexible [1], so unemployment pushes wages down, SRAS shifts right and output returns to potential on its own [1]. Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards and falling prices may delay spending [1], so the economy can stay in equilibrium below full capacity for a long time, which justifies action to raise AD [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- A rise in AD increases real output, so growth rises in the short run [1].
- Firms need more workers, so unemployment falls [1].
- But the price level rises, so inflation increases [1].
- Higher incomes raise imports and higher prices hurt competitiveness, so the current account may worsen [1].
- With spare capacity, the gain in output and jobs is large and the inflation cost small [1].
- Near full capacity, most of the rise in AD becomes inflation, with little extra output [1].
- In the neo-classical long run, AD affects only the price level, so the growth gain is temporary [1].
- If the rise in AD comes from investment, it may also raise LRAS, giving lasting benefits [1].
- Judgement: an increase in AD improves performance when there is spare capacity and inflation is low [1], but can worsen it when the economy is near capacity or the current account is already weak [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "Always" is the word to challenge: show the conditions under which it is true and when it is not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating equilibrium as full employment.
- Stating P and Y without the indicators asked for.
- Mixing up excess supply (stocks build up) with excess demand (stocks run down).
- Giving the current account effect as certain.
- Writing "the economy self-corrects" without the wage mechanism.
- Saying a rise in AD always raises output in the long run.
