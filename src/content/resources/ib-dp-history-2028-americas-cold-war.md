**HL only.** This is the ninth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The Cold War reached the Americas in three ways: through US policy towards Latin America, through the Cuban Revolution and its consequences, and through domestic politics in the United States, Canada and Latin American states. This guide uses **Cuba** as the main example of a Latin American country, with Guatemala, Chile and Nicaragua for the US interventions, and Canada for the second part.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: the Truman and Eisenhower presidencies; the Cuban Revolution; the Kennedy and Johnson presidencies; US foreign policy from 1968 to 1988; the impact on Canada; and the impact on one Latin American country. Key concepts are **cause and consequence**, **significance** and **perspectives**. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1947: Truman Doctrine; Rio Pact. 1948: Organization of American States (OAS) founded at Bogotá.
- 1950: McCarthy's speech at Wheeling. 1954: CIA-backed coup in Guatemala.
- 1959: Castro takes power in Cuba. April 1961: Bay of Pigs. October 1962: Cuban Missile Crisis.
- 1961: Alliance for Progress. 1965: US intervention in the Dominican Republic; escalation in Vietnam.
- 1970: Allende elected in Chile. 1973: coup in Chile. 1975: Operation Condor.
- 1977: Panama Canal Treaties. 1979: Sandinistas take power in Nicaragua.
- 1981 to 1989: Reagan and the Contras; Iran-Contra revealed 1986.
- 1991: end of the Cold War.

## Truman and Eisenhower

**Truman's containment.** The policy, set out in the Truman Doctrine (1947) and supported by the Marshall Plan and NSC-68, aimed to stop the spread of communism. In the Americas, it led to the Rio Pact (1947), a mutual defence treaty, and the OAS (1948). Latin American governments expected economic aid. They received much less than Europe, which caused resentment.

**McCarthyism.** In the Red Scare of the late 1940s and 1950s, fear of communist infiltration led to loyalty programmes, investigations by the House Un-American Activities Committee, the trial of Alger Hiss and the execution of the Rosenbergs (1953). From 1950, Senator Joseph McCarthy made unproven accusations against officials and others. He was censured by the Senate in 1954 after the televised Army-McCarthy hearings. The effects were damaged careers, a climate of conformity and limits on dissent.

**Eisenhower's New Look.** Eisenhower and Secretary of State John Foster Dulles aimed to contain communism more cheaply, relying on nuclear deterrence, alliances and covert action by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), rather than large conventional forces.

**Intervention in Latin America.** In Guatemala, President Jacobo Árbenz carried out land reform (Decree 900, 1952) that affected the US-owned United Fruit Company, and was suspected of communist ties. A CIA operation in 1954 helped to overthrow him and install Carlos Castillo Armas. The coup ended reform and led to decades of repression and civil war. Historians debate how far the main aim was protecting business interests or stopping communism. A hostile reception for Vice-President Nixon in Caracas in 1958 showed anti-US feeling.

## The Cuban Revolution and its impact

**Regional reactions.** Castro's seizure of power in January 1959 inspired many in Latin America, especially students and the left, and alarmed conservative governments and the US. Relations soured as Cuba nationalised US property in 1960, the US imposed an embargo, and Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for oil and trade. Castro declared himself a Marxist-Leninist in December 1961.

**Bay of Pigs (April 1961).** A force of about 1,400 Cuban exiles, trained and equipped by the CIA, landed in Cuba. Kennedy withheld full US air support. The invasion failed in about three days, no uprising followed, and Castro's position was strengthened. It humiliated Kennedy and made Castro look for stronger Soviet protection.

**Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962).** The USSR secretly placed nuclear missiles in Cuba. Kennedy announced a naval "quarantine" and demanded their removal. After a tense 13 days, Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in exchange for a US pledge not to invade Cuba and, secretly, the removal of US missiles from Turkey. The world came close to nuclear war. Castro felt sidelined by the deal.

**Diplomatic impact.** The OAS voted in January 1962 to exclude Cuba, but Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Chile and others abstained, and in 1964 the OAS required members to break relations. Mexico kept relations. Latin American governments feared both Cuban-inspired guerrillas and US intervention, and many strengthened their armies.

## Kennedy and Johnson

**Alliance for Progress.** Launched in 1961, it promised about $20 billion over ten years for economic growth and social reform to undercut the appeal of revolution. Results were mixed. Growth was uneven, elites resisted land reform, and the US also backed military governments when it feared communism. After Kennedy's death, emphasis shifted to security.

**The Dominican Republic (1965).** After a revolt to restore the elected Juan Bosch, Johnson sent about 22,000 troops, saying he feared "another Cuba". An OAS force later replaced them. The intervention (the Johnson Doctrine) drew criticism across Latin America.

**Domestic impact of Vietnam.** Johnson escalated the war after the Gulf of Tonkin incident (1964). Costs rose, casualties grew and the "credibility gap" widened between the government's claims and reality. Anti-war protests spread through campuses. The draft was resented. Spending on war limited the Great Society. After the Tet Offensive in 1968, Johnson decided not to seek re-election.

**Regional support and opposition.** Few Latin American governments supported the war. Canada's government criticised bombing in 1965 (Pearson's speech at Temple University) and became a refuge for tens of thousands of US war resisters. Some leaders supported the US quietly in return for aid.

## US foreign policy, 1968 to 1988

**School of the Americas and Operation Condor.** The US Army School of the Americas trained thousands of Latin American officers in counterinsurgency. Some graduates later committed human rights abuses. Operation Condor (from 1975) was a cooperation among the security services of South American military regimes (including Chile, Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Bolivia and Brazil) to track and kill opponents across borders. How much the US knew and supported is debated, but the US gave intelligence and technical help.

**Nixon and Chile.** Salvador Allende, a socialist, won the 1970 election with about 37 per cent of the vote. Nixon and Kissinger feared another Cuba. The CIA tried to prevent his taking office, and the US supported economic pressure. In September 1973, General Augusto Pinochet led a coup in which Allende died. The Pinochet regime killed or disappeared thousands. Historians debate the degree of direct US involvement in the coup, but few doubt it supported opposition to Allende.

**Carter and human rights.** Carter made human rights a stated aim, cutting aid to some military regimes. The **Panama Canal Treaties** (1977), negotiated with Omar Torrijos, committed the US to transfer the canal to Panama by the end of 1999. The Senate approved them narrowly in 1978. Critics said Carter was weak, and supporters said it ended a colonial grievance.

**Reagan and the Contras.** After the Sandinista revolution of 1979 overthrew Somoza in Nicaragua, Reagan backed the Contras, anti-Sandinista rebels. When Congress restricted aid (the Boland Amendments), officials secretly funded the Contras with money from arms sales to Iran, the Iran-Contra affair (revealed in 1986). The International Court of Justice ruled against the US in 1986. Reagan also supported the government of El Salvador and invaded Grenada in 1983.

## Impact of the Cold War on Canada

**Domestic anti-communism.** The defection of Igor Gouzenko in 1945 revealed Soviet spying in Ottawa and led to arrests. Canada saw security checks and some persecution, but less than the US.

**NATO and NORAD.** Canada helped found NATO (1949) and pressed for its economic and political purposes (Article 2). It joined the North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD) in 1957 to 1958, which tied Canadian air defence to the US.

**UN peacekeeping.** After the Suez Crisis of 1956, Lester Pearson proposed a UN emergency force, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1957. Peacekeeping became central to Canada's identity as a middle power.

**Cooperation and tension.** Canada accepted US leadership but disputed the details. John Diefenbaker's reluctance to accept nuclear warheads on Bomarc missiles in 1962 to 1963 caused a clash with Kennedy and contributed to his government's fall. Canada kept trade with Cuba and refused to join the embargo.

## Impact on one Latin American country: Cuba

**Regional collaboration.** Cuba was a signatory of the Rio Pact but expelled from the OAS in 1962.

**Domestic political impact.** The Cold War shaped Castro's one-party state, the repression of opponents and the 1976 Constitution. Fear of US attack justified a large military and tight control.

**Cooperation and tension with the USA.** The embargo (begun in 1960 and tightened in 1962) and the Bay of Pigs left lasting hostility, but the two states cooperated at times, for example on migration (the Freedom Flights from 1965 and a migration agreement in 1984).

**Cooperation and tension with the USSR.** Soviet oil, aid and sugar purchases sustained the Cuban economy, and Cuba joined Comecon in 1972. Cuban troops fought in Angola from 1975. Tension arose over the missile crisis and Cuba's independent line. The end of Soviet aid in 1991 led to the "Special Period" of severe hardship.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was US intervention in Latin America driven by anti-communism or by economic and strategic interest?
- Did US policy create the radicalism it feared?
- Was the Cuban Missile Crisis a diplomatic success for Kennedy or a mutual compromise?
- Did Carter really change US policy?

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Cold War in the Americas as a US story only.
- Confusing the Bay of Pigs and the Missile Crisis.
- Ignoring Canada or the Latin American country when the question asks for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which Latin American country should I study?**
Cuba is rich in evidence. Chile, Guatemala, Nicaragua or Argentina also work.
