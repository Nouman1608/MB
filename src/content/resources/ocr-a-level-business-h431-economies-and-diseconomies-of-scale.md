## Core meanings

**Economies of scale** are falls in the average (unit) cost of production as a business increases its output. **Diseconomies of scale** are rises in average cost when output grows beyond a point where the business is hard to manage. Average cost is total cost divided by output.

The word "scale" matters. These ideas describe what happens when a business changes its size or output over the long run, so that it can change all its inputs. They are not the same as spreading fixed costs over more units in the short run, although that effect is often part of the story.

**Internal** economies and diseconomies come from the growth of the business itself. A business has some control over them. **External** economies and diseconomies come from the growth of the whole industry or the area where the business operates. A single business has little control over them.

## Internal economies

Internal economies come in several types. Learn the cause of each, not just the name.

- **Purchasing (bulk-buying) economies.** Large orders earn discounts from suppliers, because the supplier saves on handling and delivery and wants to keep a big customer.
- **Technical economies.** Larger firms can afford bigger or more specialised machines, and can use them fully. A large bakery can run an automated line that a small shop could never keep busy.
- **Managerial economies.** A large firm can employ specialist managers, for example in finance or marketing, who are more efficient than a single owner doing every job.
- **Financial economies.** Large firms are seen as lower risk, so banks may lend at lower interest rates and the firm may raise money by selling shares.
- **Marketing economies.** The cost of advertising can be spread over a large volume of sales. A national campaign costs a fixed sum whether the firm sells 200,000 or 800,000 units.
- **Risk-bearing economies.** A firm with many products or markets can survive the failure of one.

## Internal diseconomies

- **Communication problems.** More layers of management and more sites mean messages are slow, distorted or lost.
- **Coordination and control problems.** Managers cannot supervise every activity, so mistakes and waste increase.
- **Lack of motivation or commitment.** In a large firm, workers may feel remote from decisions and see little link between their effort and results. Absenteeism, labour turnover and poor quality can rise.
- **Slow decision making.** Decisions pass through many people, so the business reacts slowly to change.

Diseconomies are not certain. A well-run firm can delay them with good communication systems, decentralised decisions and strong training, but each of these has a cost.

## External economies and diseconomies

**External economies** include:

- a pool of skilled labour in the area, as in a cluster of technology firms near a university;
- specialist suppliers and support services setting up nearby;
- better transport, broadband and other infrastructure paid for by government or shared among firms;
- a reputation for the area, such as a region known for pottery or engineering, which attracts customers.

**External diseconomies** include:

- congestion and slow deliveries as more firms use the same roads;
- rising land rents and wages as firms compete for sites and workers;
- shortage of suitable workers;
- strain on local services and community opposition to expansion, which may lead to tougher planning rules.

External effects can reverse. An area can enjoy external economies while an industry is small and suffer external diseconomies when it becomes crowded.

## Worked example 1: unit cost as output grows

Brackwell Biscuits (invented) has fixed costs of £150,000 a year. Its variable cost is £0.50 a pack, but a supplier gives a bulk discount that cuts it to £0.46 a pack once annual output reaches 600,000 packs. The pack sells for £0.80.

Average cost = (fixed costs + variable cost per pack × output) ÷ output.

| Output (packs) | Variable cost per pack | Total cost | Average cost per pack |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100,000 | £0.50 | £200,000 | £2.00 |
| 300,000 | £0.50 | £300,000 | £1.00 |
| 600,000 | £0.46 | £426,000 | £0.71 |
| 1,200,000 | £0.46 | £702,000 | £0.585 |

Working for 600,000 packs: £150,000 + (£0.46 × 600,000) = £150,000 + £276,000 = £426,000. Divided by 600,000 this gives £0.71.

Two economies are at work. Spreading the fixed cost is the main reason for the fall from £2.00 to £1.00. A purchasing economy then reduces the variable cost. Between 300,000 and 600,000 packs the average cost falls by £0.29, which is 29% of £1.00.

The effect on profit is large. At 600,000 packs revenue is £480,000 and total cost is £426,000, a profit of £54,000. At 1,200,000 packs revenue is £960,000, cost is £702,000 and profit is £258,000. Doubling output multiplied profit by more than four, because unit cost fell while the price stayed the same.

## Worked example 2: diseconomies

Brackwell wants to grow to 1,800,000 packs. It must add a second site and a layer of managers, which adds £90,000 to fixed costs. Poor communication between the sites raises variable cost back to £0.50 a pack because of waste and rework.

Total cost = (£150,000 + £90,000) + (£0.50 × 1,800,000) = £240,000 + £900,000 = £1,140,000.

Average cost = £1,140,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = **£0.633**.

This is higher than the £0.585 at 1,200,000 packs, a rise of £0.048, or 8.3%. At a price of £0.80 profit is £1,440,000 − £1,140,000 = £300,000. Profit is still higher than at 1,200,000 packs because the extra volume earns a margin, but it grew by less than output did, and each extra pack now earns less.

The lesson is that average cost can rise while profit still rises. Diseconomies reduce the benefit of growth; they do not always make growth unprofitable. They do matter if the price is under pressure. If rivals force the price down to £0.65, Brackwell would make only £30,000 at 1,800,000 packs, and the extra site would have been a poor decision.

The average cost curve is therefore U-shaped in the long run. Cost falls while economies dominate, is lowest at the **minimum efficient scale**, and rises when diseconomies dominate. This is a standard idea in economics. The OCR specification does not use the term, but it helps to explain the pattern.

## Worked example 3: evaluating a decision to grow

Marsh & Fenn Joinery (invented) has 30 employees and is considering buying a rival and merging the two workshops. The directors expect:

- timber purchasing discounts of 6% on a £500,000 annual timber bill;
- the loss of a duplicated administration team, saving £60,000 a year;
- extra managers costing £75,000 a year;
- one-off costs of redundancy and moving machinery of £120,000.

Annual saving from purchasing: 6% × £500,000 = £30,000.

Annual saving in total: £30,000 + £60,000 = £90,000. Annual extra cost: £75,000. Net annual benefit: £90,000 − £75,000 = **£15,000**.

Payback on the one-off costs: £120,000 ÷ £15,000 = **8 years**.

On these figures the economies are real but small once the new management cost is counted. The judgement depends on:

- **Whether the savings are certain.** The purchasing discount depends on suppliers agreeing, which is not guaranteed.
- **Time.** An eight-year payback is long for a small firm in a changing market.
- **Non-financial effects.** Merging two teams may harm morale and communication, a diseconomy not shown in the figures. Customers may value the personal service of a small firm.
- **Other benefits.** A larger firm could bid for bigger contracts, which the figures do not capture.

A balanced conclusion: the merger is not justified by cost savings alone, but could be if it opens contracts the firms cannot win separately.

## Evaluating costs and benefits

When you are asked to evaluate, check the following.

1. **Which type is it?** Internal or external, and which kind. Name the cause.
2. **How big is it?** Use figures if given, and compare unit costs or total savings with the cost of achieving them.
3. **Who benefits?** Customers gain if savings are passed on in lower prices. Owners gain from higher profit. Employees may gain from stable jobs, or lose if the economies come from fewer staff. Suppliers may gain a large order but lose bargaining power if one buyer dominates. The local community faces congestion and noise if the site grows.
4. **What does it cost to get there?** Economies usually need investment, such as machines, premises or finance. Costs come first and savings later.
5. **What could go wrong?** Diseconomies, falling demand and external diseconomies can all cancel the gain.
6. **Judgement.** Say which side outweighs the other for this business, and what the answer depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "bigger means cheaper" without saying that average cost may rise again at higher output.
- Mixing up internal and external. A skilled labour pool in an area is external; a bulk discount for one firm is internal.
- Describing an economy without saying why unit cost falls. "Technical economies" is a label; the explanation is that larger machines or fuller use of equipment cut cost per unit.
- Treating economies of scale as certain. They depend on demand being high enough to use the extra capacity.
- Forgetting the costs that come with growth, such as finance, training and redundancy.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs a cause and a result, ideally in a case context. "Evaluate" needs both sides, a judgement and what it depends on. Calculations of average cost are common, so write the formula and the working. Link your answer to the business in the case, for example its sector, size and objectives. A small firm in a niche market may have little to gain from scale, while a firm making a standard product in volume may gain a great deal.
