The specification describes Question 4 as requiring students "to analyse and evaluate a given issue or development using evidence from a range of the sources (both unseen and in the pre-release pack) and their own knowledge".

## Section A: the method

**1.** State the dates on which the pre-release booklet is made available for the May/June and the October/November series. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between using a range of sources and linking sources in Question 4. **[2]**

**3.** Give two ways in which provenance can help you decide how much weight to give a source in Question 4. **[2]**

## Section B: five-source sets

### Set 1: Peacemaking following the First World War, 1919-23

**Source A** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From a speech imagined as given by a German member of the National Assembly, June 1919.
"We are told to sign that Germany alone caused the war. We are to pay sums no one has yet named, give up our colonies, our coal and our fleet, and keep an army fit only for a police force. This is not a peace. It is a sentence."

**Source B** *(pre-release, invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in a French magazine in July 1919. Clemenceau, small and tired, holds a tiny shield labelled "Treaty" while a huge German soldier in the background sharpens a sword. The caption reads: "Is this all the protection we get?"

**Source C** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From a history of the peace conference written in 1990 by a British historian.
"Germany lost about an eighth of its territory and a tenth of its population, but kept its industrial heartland intact. Compared with the treaty Germany itself had imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in 1918, Versailles was moderate."

**Source D** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a private letter imagined as written by a member of the American delegation in Paris, May 1919.
"The President's Fourteen Points have been traded away one by one. Self-determination applies to everyone except the Germans. I fear we are building resentment that will outlast us all."

**Source E** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a memorandum imagined as written by a British Treasury official, 1920.
"If Germany is stripped of her capacity to trade, she cannot pay reparations, and Britain loses her best pre-war customer. A Germany that recovers is in our own interest."

**4.** Identify which of Sources A-E support, and which challenge, the view that the Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany. Give a brief reason for each source. **[5]**

**5.** "The Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany." Using the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

**6.** "The peacemakers of 1919 achieved their aims." Using the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

### Set 2: Threats to peace in the 1960s

**Source F** *(pre-release, invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in a Soviet magazine in May 1960. Khrushchev holds up a broken toy aeroplane labelled "U2" in front of a red-faced Eisenhower, who is hiding a camera behind his back. The caption reads: "Weather research, Mr President?"

**Source G** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From a television broadcast imagined as given by President Kennedy, July 1961.
"West Berlin is the great testing place of Western courage. We will not be driven out by force. I am asking Congress for more men, more ships and more aircraft, because we seek peace, but we shall not surrender."

**Source H** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From a West Berlin newspaper editorial imagined as published on 14 August 1961.
"Barbed wire divides our city. Families waved to one another across it yesterday, weeping. The Western powers protest, but their tanks stay in their barracks."

**Source I** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a private letter imagined as written by an American official to a colleague, November 1962.
"After Cuba, both men have looked into the abyss. The talk now is of a direct telephone line and a limit on testing. Strange to say, I think the world is safer than it was a year ago."

**Source J** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a statement imagined as issued by the East German government in 1963.
"Since the protective wall was built, the flight of our skilled workers has been stopped and the provocations from West Berlin have ended. The wall has brought calm to the heart of Europe."

**7.** Identify which of Sources F-J support, and which challenge, the view that the building of the Berlin Wall reduced tension between the superpowers. Give a brief reason for each source. **[5]**

**8.** "The building of the Berlin Wall reduced tension between the superpowers." Using the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

**9.** "Kennedy dealt successfully with the threats to peace in the years 1961-63." Using the sources and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

**10.** Choose two sources from Set 2 that give different impressions of the situation in Berlin. Explain how their provenance helps to account for the difference. **[4]**

## Answers

**How the 12-mark guides work.** Range of sources used and linked, pre-released and unseen [4]; provenance used to weigh at least two sources [2]; precise own knowledge used to test and extend the sources [4]; a supported judgement answering the question [2]. Sorting tasks: [1] per source correctly placed with a reason.

**1.** 1 February for the May/June series [1]; 1 July for the October/November series [1].

**2.** Range means drawing on several of the five sources, pre-released and unseen [1]; linking means showing how sources relate to each other, for example by confirming or contradicting one another, rather than using each in isolation [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: an author with no reason to support a view who still supports it carries more weight; a public statement is strong evidence of what its author wanted believed but weaker evidence of private views; a source written later with hindsight gives an overview but not contemporary feelings; an insider's report shows what officials knew.

**4.** [1] each. **A supports**: a German politician describes the treaty as "a sentence", listing war guilt, reparations and losses. **B challenges**: French opinion saw the treaty as too weak to protect France. **C challenges**: the historian calls it moderate compared with Brest-Litovsk and notes Germany kept its industry. **D supports**: an American insider says self-determination was denied to Germans and resentment would last. **E** can be used **either way**: it implies some British officials thought the economic terms risked crippling Germany, but its main concern is British trade, so it is better evidence of British self-interest than of harshness.

**5.** Range and linking [4]: A and D support the view, and their agreement is significant because D is written by an American, from the victorious side; B and C challenge it, B showing French fears that the treaty was too weak, C comparing it with Brest-Litovsk; E links to A, suggesting reparations could harm everyone. Provenance [2]: A is a German politician speaking in June 1919 to rally opposition, so it shows anger rather than a balanced view; C has the advantage of hindsight and comparison. Own knowledge [4]: Article 231 (war guilt); reparations later set at £6,600 million (1921); the army limited to 100,000, with no air force or submarines; loss of Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor and all colonies; the Saar under League control; *Anschluss* forbidden; but the Rhineland was demilitarised rather than separated, Germany's industry survived, and Germany later recovered in the 1920s. Judgement [2]: for example, the terms were harsh in their humiliation of Germany, but not so harsh that Germany could not recover, so the treaty was more resented than crippling.

**6.** Range and linking [4]: B shows Clemenceau failed to gain the security French opinion wanted; D shows Wilson's Fourteen Points were compromised; E shows Lloyd George's government worried about the economic consequences; A shows that Germany was punished, which all three wanted to some degree; C suggests a compromise. Provenance [2]: D is a private letter by a member of Wilson's own delegation, so its criticism of the President's success is strong evidence; B is a French cartoon reflecting public opinion, not Clemenceau's own view. Own knowledge [4]: Clemenceau gained Alsace-Lorraine, a demilitarised Rhineland and reparations, but not the separation of the Rhineland, and was criticised in France; Lloyd George gained naval supremacy and some colonies, and avoided total destruction of German trade; Wilson secured the League of Nations, but the US Senate refused to ratify the treaty and the USA never joined. Judgement [2]: for example, each achieved some aims but none achieved all; Wilson was least successful because his own country rejected his League.

**7.** [1] each. **F challenges**: the U2 cartoon shows tension before the Wall, so it shows tension was already high, not that the Wall reduced it. **G challenges**: Kennedy's broadcast shows a military build-up over Berlin in July 1961. **H challenges**: the Wall caused distress and Western anger in August 1961. **I** can be used **either way**: it says the world was safer by late 1962, but because of Cuba rather than Berlin. **J supports**: the East German government claims the Wall brought "calm".

**8.** Range and linking [4]: J supports the view, and H, written from West Berlin, partly corroborates it, since Western tanks "stay in their barracks", showing the Western powers would not fight over the Wall; G shows how dangerous the Berlin crisis was before the Wall, so stopping the flow of refugees removed one cause of confrontation; F and I show that other crises, the U2 and Cuba, drove tension. Provenance [2]: J is East German propaganda written to justify the Wall, so it is weak evidence of its effects but shows the regime's motives; H is a West Berlin editorial written the day after the border closed, strong on immediate feelings. Own knowledge [4]: about three million people had left East Germany through Berlin since 1949; Khrushchev's ultimatum at Vienna in June 1961; the Wall began on 13 August 1961; US and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie in October 1961; Kennedy accepted the Wall as preferable to war and visited West Berlin in June 1963. Judgement [2]: for example, in the short term the Wall raised tension, as Checkpoint Charlie showed, but in the longer term it reduced the danger of war over Berlin by ending the refugee crisis, even though it became a symbol of division.

**9.** Range and linking [4]: G shows Kennedy standing firm over Berlin while seeking peace; H suggests the West did little to stop the Wall; I shows that after Cuba the superpowers moved towards a hotline and a test ban; F reminds the historian that Kennedy inherited a crisis from Eisenhower. Provenance [2]: G is Kennedy's own public broadcast, so it shows his intended image of firmness; I is a private letter by an American official, frank but from Kennedy's side. Own knowledge [4]: the failed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961) and the difficult Vienna summit made Khrushchev think Kennedy weak; Kennedy increased defence spending in 1961; in October 1962 he chose a naval quarantine rather than air strikes, and the crisis ended with Soviet missiles removed, a pledge not to invade Cuba and the secret removal of US missiles from Turkey; the hotline and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in 1963. Judgement [2]: for example, Kennedy made serious mistakes in 1961, but his handling of Cuba in 1962 and the agreements of 1963 show that he dealt successfully with the most dangerous threat.

**10.** Two sources identified [1], for example H and J. Difference explained [1]: H stresses distress and division, while J claims the Wall brought calm. Provenance explains it [2]: H is a West Berlin editorial written the day after the border closed, expressing the shock of people cut off from family; J is a statement by the East German government two years later, designed to justify the Wall by presenting it as protection, so each reflects its author's position and purpose.
