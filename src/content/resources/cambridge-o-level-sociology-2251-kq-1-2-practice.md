These questions practise key question 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What types of data
and evidence do sociologists use?* Section A follows the shape the syllabus describes for the
compulsory Paper 1 Question 1, parts (a) to (e). Section B gives extra practice on reading a table and
judging secondary evidence.

---

## Source A (invented for practice)

*A researcher studied how women in one city combine paid work and housework. She used a national
labour force survey, which showed that the share of women aged 25–54 in paid work rose from 38% in
2010 to 47% in 2024. She also read the diaries of 15 working mothers, which a women's charity had
collected and published, and carried out a content analysis of 300 television advertisements for
household products broadcast in 2024.*

## Source B (invented for practice)

*Burglaries in one country, in thousands: the number recorded by the police and the number estimated
from a national victim survey, in which a sample of households is asked whether they have been
burgled.*

| Year | Recorded by police | Estimated by victim survey |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 120 | 260 |
| 2020 | 95 | 210 |
| 2025 | 80 | 190 |

---

## Section A — Research methods question

**(a)(i)** Using Source A, identify the percentage of women aged 25–54 in paid work in 2024. **[1]**

**(a)(ii)** Using Source A, explain why the researcher's study is based on secondary data. **[3]**

**(a)(iii)** Using Source A, explain **two** problems with the evidence in the study. **[4]**

**(b)** Describe **two** differences between quantitative and qualitative data. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **three** limitations of official statistics for sociologists. **[6]**

**(d)** Evaluate the view that media content is useful evidence for sociologists. **[14]**

**(e)** You have been asked to investigate why some young people spend a lot of time in online gaming
communities. Explain the primary research methods, sampling and secondary evidence you would use, and
give reasons for your choices. **[8]**

## Section B — Data and documents

**(f)** Using Source B, describe the trend in burglaries recorded by the police between 2015 and 2025.
**[2]**

**(g)** Using Source B, explain **two** reasons why the victim survey estimates are higher than the
police figures. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** strengths of historical documents as sociological evidence. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain how **two** of Scott's criteria could be used to judge a personal diary. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** strengths of digital sources for sociological research. **[4]**

## Answers

**(a)(i) [1]** 47% (1).

**(a)(ii) [3]** The researcher did not collect any of the data herself (1). The labour force survey was
produced by a national body, the diaries were collected and published by a charity, and the
advertisements were made by companies for selling products (1). All existed before the study and
were created for other purposes, which is what makes them secondary (1).

**(a)(iii) [4]** Any two problems, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for applying it to the source):
- The 15 diaries come from mothers chosen by one charity, so they are unlikely to be representative of
  working mothers in the city, and findings cannot be generalised (2).
- Diaries written for publication may present a selected or idealised picture, lowering their
  credibility and validity (2).
- The labour force survey is national, not city-level, so it may not describe women in this city (2).
- Advertisements show how products are marketed, not how households actually divide housework, so
  the content analysis may not measure what the study claims (2).

**(b) [4]** Two differences, 2 marks each:
- Quantitative data is numerical and can be counted and compared statistically; qualitative data is
  in words, images or sound (2).
- Quantitative data shows patterns and trends across large samples; qualitative data gives depth and
  shows the meanings people attach to their actions (2).
- Quantitative data is generally more reliable and easier to repeat; qualitative data is generally
  more valid but harder to compare (2).

**(c) [6]** Three limitations, 2 marks each:
- Definitions are chosen by governments and can change, for example who counts as unemployed, so
  comparisons over time may be misleading (2).
- Some behaviour is hidden: crime statistics miss the dark figure of unreported and unrecorded
  offences (2).
- Interpretivists argue they are socially constructed, reflecting the decisions of officials such as
  coroners or police officers, as Atkinson (1978) argued for suicide (2).
- They may be collected for political purposes and presented to show government success (2).
- They give numbers but not the meanings or reasons behind behaviour (2).

**(d) [14]** Levels: 11–14 a balanced, developed answer with accurate concepts and a supported
conclusion; 7–10 developed but less balanced or with limited evaluation; 4–6 limited points, mainly
descriptive; 1–3 basic or one-sided.

*Useful:*
- Plentiful, cheap and accessible at any time; past content allows comparison over time.
- Content analysis is systematic and can be repeated, so findings can be reliable.
- Reveals how groups are represented: Tuchman (1978) described the "symbolic annihilation" of women;
  the Glasgow University Media Group (1976) analysed how television news presented industrial
  disputes.
- Representations may shape identities and attitudes, so they matter in their own right.
- No observer effect, since the content was produced without a researcher present.

*Limitations:*
- Media content reflects the priorities of owners, editors and advertisers, not a direct picture of
  society.
- Choosing categories for content analysis involves the researcher's judgement, so bias is possible.
- Counting content says little about how audiences interpret it; meanings require interviews or
  observation.
- Online content may be anonymous or fake, so authenticity is hard to establish.

*Conclusion:* useful for studying representation and how it changes, but limited as evidence of
behaviour or attitudes unless combined with primary methods.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear plan with methods, sampling and secondary evidence all explained with
reasons; 4–6 a plan with some reasons; 1–3 a list of methods with little justification. Evaluation of
the plan is not required. Example points:
- **Primary method:** unstructured or semi-structured interviews, because the aim is to understand
  reasons and meanings, which needs depth; possibly overt participant observation in a gaming
  community, with consent.
- **Sampling:** snowball sampling through players, because there is no sampling frame of gamers; or
  volunteers recruited through a school or youth club, stratified by age and gender.
- **Secondary evidence:** public forum posts or published survey data on young people's screen time,
  to give context and compare with interview findings.
- **Ethics:** informed consent, including parental consent for under-18s; anonymity for usernames.

**(f) [2]** Recorded burglaries fell steadily (1), from 120 thousand in 2015 to 80 thousand in 2025, a
fall of 40 thousand or one third (1).

**(g) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- Many victims do not report burglaries, for example when nothing valuable was taken, when they do
  not trust the police or when they think nothing will be done (2).
- The police may not record every reported incident as a crime, for example if they judge it too
  minor or lack evidence (2).
- Victim surveys ask people directly, so they capture part of the dark figure that never reaches the
  police (2).

**(h) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each:
- They may be the only evidence available about the past, allowing historical study of family,
  work or migration (2).
- They allow comparison between past and present, showing how society has changed (2).
- They were produced without a researcher present, so there is no observer or interviewer effect (2).

**(i) [4]** Any two criteria, 2 marks each:
- **Authenticity:** checking that the diary is genuine and written by the person claimed, for example
  by examining its origin (2).
- **Credibility:** asking whether the writer was sincere and accurate, or exaggerating to impress a
  future reader (2).
- **Representativeness:** asking whether this diarist is typical, since only some people keep
  diaries (2).
- **Meaning:** asking whether the researcher can understand the writer's words and references in
  their original context (2).

**(j) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each:
- Very large amounts of current material are available quickly and often free (2).
- They can reach hidden or scattered groups, such as members of online subcultures, who are hard to
  contact by other methods (2).
- Posts are produced without a researcher present, so they show people's views in their own words
  without an interviewer effect (2).

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should an (a)(i) answer be?** One item, taken accurately from the source with its unit. No
explanation is needed.

**Do I need to name Scott in my answers?** Naming him is not required, but using his criteria gives a
clear structure for evaluating any document.
