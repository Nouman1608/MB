These questions cover section **3.1.9.1 Rate equations** of the AQA A-level Chemistry specification (7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Rate equations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-rate-equations/). Use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. The Arrhenius equation is k = Ae^(−Eₐ/RT).

## Questions

**1.** Define each term.

**(a)** Order of reaction with respect to a reactant **[1]**

**(b)** Rate constant **[1]**

**2.** The rate equation for a reaction is rate = k[A][B]².

**(a)** State the order with respect to A, the order with respect to B and the overall order. **[2]**

**(b)** Deduce the units of k. **[2]**

**3.** For the reaction in question 2, the initial concentrations are changed. State and explain the effect on the rate of:

**(a)** doubling [A] only **[1]**

**(b)** doubling [B] only **[1]**

**(c)** doubling both [A] and [B] **[1]**

**4.** A reaction has the rate equation rate = k[X]²[Y]. When [X] = 0.15 mol dm⁻³ and [Y] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³, the initial rate is 2.7 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate k and give its units. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the rate when [X] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³ and [Y] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³ at the same temperature. **[2]**

**5.** A first-order reaction has k = 0.025 s⁻¹. Calculate the rate when the concentration is 0.060 mol dm⁻³. **[2]**

**6.** For a zero-order reaction, state the units of k and explain why changing the concentration of the reactant has no effect on the rate. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why the rate constant increases when the temperature is raised. **[3]**

**8.** A reaction has A = 4.0 × 10¹² s⁻¹ and Eₐ = 75.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the rate constant at 298 K. **[3]**

**9.** The rate constant of a reaction at 298 K is 1.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. The activation energy is 50.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the factor by which k increases when the temperature is raised to 308 K. Show the expression you use, ln(k₂ ÷ k₁) = (Eₐ ÷ R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). **[3]**

**10.** The table shows the rate constant of a reaction at different temperatures.

| T / K | 300 | 360 |
|---|---|---|
| k / s⁻¹ | 0.0860 | 12.9 |

**(a)** Write the logarithmic form of the Arrhenius equation. **[1]**

**(b)** State what would be plotted on each axis to give a straight line, and what the gradient of the line is equal to. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the activation energy, in kJ mol⁻¹, using the two points. **[4]**

**11.** The value of k for a reaction is found to be twice as large at 310 K as at 300 K. A student states that the rate constant doubles for every 10 K rise for every reaction. Comment. **[2]**

**12.** Explain why the units of k for a second-order reaction are mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The power to which the concentration of that reactant is raised in the rate equation [1]

**(b)** The constant of proportionality in the rate equation [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Order in A is 1; order in B is 2; overall order is 3 [1 + 1]

**(b)** k = rate ÷ ([A][B]²), so units = (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)³ [1] = **mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹** [1]. **[4]**

**3. (a)** The rate doubles (first order in A) [1]

**(b)** The rate increases by a factor of four (second order in B) [1]

**(c)** The rate increases by a factor of 2 × 4 = eight [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** k = 2.7 × 10⁻³ ÷ (0.15² × 0.30) [1] = 2.7 × 10⁻³ ÷ 6.75 × 10⁻³ = **0.40** [1]; units mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ [1]

**(b)** rate = 0.40 × 0.30² × 0.10 [1] = **3.6 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[5]**

**5.** rate = k[A] = 0.025 × 0.060 [1] = **1.5 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The units of k are mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ [1]; the rate equation is rate = k, with no concentration term, so the rate does not depend on the concentration [1]. **[2]**

**7.** A greater proportion of molecules has energy equal to or greater than the activation energy at a higher temperature [1]; so a greater proportion of collisions is successful [1]; the rate at the same concentrations is greater, so k, which is the rate at unit concentrations, is greater [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Eₐ ÷ RT = 75 000 ÷ (8.31 × 298) [1] = 30.29; e^(−30.29) = 6.97 × 10⁻¹⁴ [1]; k = 4.0 × 10¹² × 6.97 × 10⁻¹⁴ = **0.28 s⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** 1/T₁ − 1/T₂ = 1/298 − 1/308 = 1.0896 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ [1]; ln(k₂ ÷ k₁) = (50 000 ÷ 8.31) × 1.0896 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.656 [1]; k₂ ÷ k₁ = e^0.656 = **1.9** (k₂ = 1.9 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹) [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** ln k = −Eₐ/RT + ln A [1]

**(b)** ln k on the y-axis against 1/T on the x-axis [1]; the gradient equals −Eₐ/R [1]

**(c)** 1/T values: 1/300 = 3.333 × 10⁻³ and 1/360 = 2.778 × 10⁻³ [1]; ln k values: −2.454 and +2.557 [1]; gradient = (2.557 − (−2.454)) ÷ (2.778 × 10⁻³ − 3.333 × 10⁻³) = 5.011 ÷ (−5.56 × 10⁻⁴) = −9020 K [1]; Eₐ = 9020 × 8.31 = 74 900 J mol⁻¹ = **about 75 kJ mol⁻¹** (accept 74 to 76) [1]. **[7]**

**11.** The statement is wrong [1]; the factor depends on the activation energy and the temperature, so the doubling per 10 K is only an approximation for some reactions near room temperature [1]. **[2]**

**12.** For second order, rate = k[A]² or k[A][B], so k = rate ÷ concentration² [1]; units = (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)² = mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the orders from the balanced equation.
- Units of k that do not match the overall order.
- Using Eₐ in kJ mol⁻¹ with R in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- Using °C for temperature.
- Getting the sign of the gradient wrong (the gradient is −Eₐ/R).
