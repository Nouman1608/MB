## Diffusion (4.1.3.1)

Substances move into and out of cells across the cell membrane by diffusion. Learn the specification's definition:

> Diffusion is the spreading out of the particles of any substance in solution, or particles of a gas, resulting
> in a net movement from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.

Particles move randomly, so more leave a crowded area than enter it: the **net** movement is down the
concentration gradient. Diffusion needs no energy from the cell.

The specification names three substances that move by diffusion:

- **oxygen** and **carbon dioxide** in gas exchange (for example, oxygen diffuses from the air in the lungs into
  the blood)
- **urea**, a waste product, which diffuses from cells into the **blood plasma** so that it can be removed by the
  kidney.

### Factors that affect the rate of diffusion

| Factor | Change that increases the rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration gradient | A bigger difference in concentration | More particles move down the gradient than move back each second |
| Temperature | A higher temperature | Particles have more kinetic energy, so they move faster |
| Surface area of the membrane | A larger surface area | More particles can cross at the same time |

When you explain a factor, give the change and the reason, not just "temperature affects diffusion".

### Surface area to volume ratio

A single-celled organism has a **relatively large surface area to volume ratio**, so diffusion across its membrane
can supply enough oxygen and remove enough waste to meet its needs.

Model organisms as cubes or cuboids:

```
surface area of a cube = 6 × (side length)²
volume of a cube       = (side length)³
surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume, written as  x : 1
```

**Worked example 1.** Compare cubes with sides of 1 cm, 2 cm and 4 cm.

```
Side 1 cm: SA = 6 × 1² = 6 cm²     V = 1³ = 1 cm³     ratio 6 ÷ 1  = 6 : 1
Side 2 cm: SA = 6 × 2² = 24 cm²    V = 2³ = 8 cm³     ratio 24 ÷ 8 = 3 : 1
Side 4 cm: SA = 6 × 4² = 96 cm²    V = 4³ = 64 cm³    ratio 96 ÷ 64 = 1.5 : 1
```

Each time the side doubles, the ratio halves.

**Worked example 2.** Shape matters as well as size. Compare a cube of side 4 mm with a flat cuboid
1 mm × 8 mm × 8 mm. Both have a volume of 64 mm³.

```
Cuboid SA = 2 × (1×8 + 8×8 + 1×8) = 2 × 80 = 160 mm²
Cuboid ratio = 160 ÷ 64 = 2.5 : 1
Cube ratio   = 96 ÷ 64  = 1.5 : 1
```

The flat shape has the larger ratio, which is why thin, flat structures make good exchange surfaces.

### Why multicellular organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems

A large multicellular organism has a small surface area to volume ratio. Its outer surface is too small to supply
all its cells by diffusion, and inner cells are too far from the surface. So it needs:

1. **Specialised exchange surfaces** (organs such as the lungs or the small intestine) that give a large area for
   substances to move into and out of the body.
2. **A transport system** (such as the blood in animals) that carries substances between the exchange surface and
   every cell, so each cell is only a short distance from a supply.

### Adaptations of exchange surfaces

The specification lists four features that increase the effectiveness of an exchange surface:

- a **large surface area**
- a **thin membrane**, giving a short diffusion path
- (in animals) an **efficient blood supply**
- (in animals, for gaseous exchange) being **ventilated**.

Blood supply and ventilation keep the **concentration gradient steep** by removing absorbed substances and
bringing in fresh air or water.

| Exchange surface | Large surface area | Short diffusion path | Other features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small intestine | Millions of villi lining the inner wall | Villus wall is one cell thick | Good capillary network carries absorbed food away |
| Lungs | Millions of alveoli | Alveolar and capillary walls are thin | Rich blood supply; breathing ventilates the alveoli |
| Fish gills | Many gill filaments, each with many thin plates | Thin layer of cells between water and blood | Rich blood supply; water flows over the gills continuously |
| Roots | Root hair cells with long projections | Thin cell walls | Ions and water are taken from the soil |
| Leaves | Flat, thin shape; air spaces inside the leaf | Gases only travel a short distance | Stomata let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapour out |

Link each feature to its effect: "villi give a large surface area, so more glucose is absorbed at once".

## Osmosis (4.1.3.2)

Water moves across cell membranes by osmosis:

> Osmosis is the diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution through a partially
> permeable membrane.

A partially permeable membrane lets water through but not larger solute molecules such as sucrose. A **dilute**
solution has more water and less solute than a **concentrated** one. Osmosis needs no energy.

Applied to plant tissue:

- In a solution **more dilute** than the cell contents, the cells gain water and the tissue **gains mass**.
- In a solution **more concentrated** than the cell contents, the cells lose water and the tissue **loses mass**.
- In a solution at the **same concentration** as the cell contents, there is no net movement of water and the mass
  does not change.

### Percentage change in mass

Pieces of tissue start at different masses, so compare **percentage** change:

```
percentage change in mass = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100
```

A gain gives a positive answer and a loss gives a negative answer. Keep the sign.

**Worked example 3.** A potato cylinder goes from 2.50 g to 2.80 g. A second goes from 2.40 g to 2.04 g.

```
Cylinder 1: (2.80 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = 0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +12.0 %
Cylinder 2: (2.04 − 2.40) ÷ 2.40 × 100 = −0.36 ÷ 2.40 × 100 = −15.0 %
```

If three repeats at one concentration give +11.5 %, +12.5 % and +12.0 %, the mean is
(11.5 + 12.5 + 12.0) ÷ 3 = **+12.0 %**.

### Rate of water uptake

A rate is a compound measure: an amount divided by a time.

**Worked example 4.** A potato cylinder gains 0.36 g of water in 30 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake.

```
rate of water uptake = 0.36 g ÷ 30 min = 0.012 g/min
in grams per hour: 0.36 g ÷ 0.5 h = 0.72 g/h
```

Always give the unit.

## Required practical activity 3

The specification asks you to investigate the effect of a range of concentrations of salt or sugar solutions on
the mass of plant tissue. A typical method:

1. Cut potato cylinders with a cork borer and trim them to the same length.
2. Blot each dry and record its mass.
3. Place one in each of a range of sugar or salt solutions (for example 0.0 to 0.8 mol/dm³), same volume in each.
4. Leave for the same time at the same temperature.
5. Remove, blot dry the same way, and record the final mass.
6. Calculate percentage change in mass, repeat, and find the mean for each concentration.

**Variables.** Independent: concentration of the solution. Dependent: percentage change in mass. Control: type
of tissue, size and shape of the cylinders, volume of solution, time, temperature, and how the cylinders are
blotted.

**Graph.** Plot concentration on the x-axis and percentage change in mass on the y-axis, with a zero line across
the middle. Draw a line or curve of best fit. Where the line crosses zero, the solution has the same
concentration as the cell contents.

**Worked example 5.** At 0.2 mol/dm³ the mean change is +4.0 %; at 0.4 mol/dm³ it is −6.0 %. Assuming a
straight line between these points, where is zero?

```
The change falls by 10.0 percentage points over 0.2 mol/dm³.
From +4.0 % to 0 is 4.0 of those 10.0 points: 4.0 ÷ 10.0 × 0.2 = 0.08
Concentration ≈ 0.2 + 0.08 = 0.28 mol/dm³
```

**Improvements.** Surface water left after blotting adds to the final mass, so blot every cylinder the same way.
Use more concentrations near the crossing point, and repeat to spot anomalies.

## Active transport (4.1.3.3)

> Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution (against a
> concentration gradient). This requires energy from respiration.

The specification gives two examples.

- **Root hair cells** absorb **mineral ions** from very dilute solutions in the soil. The concentration of ions
  inside the cell is higher, so the ions must be moved against the gradient. Plants need these ions for healthy
  growth.
- In the **gut**, sugar molecules are absorbed from **lower** concentrations in the gut into the blood, which has a
  **higher** sugar concentration. Cells use the sugar for respiration.

Anything that reduces respiration reduces active transport. Roots in waterlogged soil get little oxygen, so
aerobic respiration slows and mineral ion uptake falls.

## Comparing the three processes

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| What moves | Particles of any substance in solution or a gas | Water only | Substances such as mineral ions and sugar |
| Direction | Higher → lower concentration | Dilute → concentrated solution | Dilute → concentrated solution (against the gradient) |
| Membrane | Any membrane the particle can cross | Partially permeable membrane needed | Cell membrane |
| Energy from respiration | Not needed | Not needed | Needed |
| Example | Oxygen into blood in the lungs | Water into root hair cells | Mineral ions into root hair cells |

The trap: osmosis and active transport both go "dilute to concentrated" in terms of the **solution**, but in osmosis
water moves down its own gradient, while in active transport the solute moves up its gradient.

## Common errors

- Writing "from high to low" without the word **concentration**.
- Defining osmosis without "partially permeable membrane", or saying solute moves in osmosis.
- Calculating surface area of a cube as side² rather than 6 × side².
- Dropping the minus sign on a percentage loss in mass.
- Dividing by the final mass instead of the initial mass.
- Saying active transport "uses energy" without saying the energy comes from **respiration**.
- Listing exchange-surface features with no link to how they increase the rate of diffusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is osmosis?**
Osmosis is the diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.

**What is active transport?**
Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against the concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.

**What are the two examples of active transport in AQA GCSE Biology?**
Root hair cells absorbing mineral ions from very dilute soil water, and the absorption of sugar molecules from the gut into the blood.

**Why do multicellular organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems?**
A large multicellular organism has a small surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its outer surface is too slow to supply all its cells.

**How does shape affect surface area to volume ratio?**
Thin, flat shapes have a larger surface area to volume ratio than compact shapes of the same volume, which is why many exchange surfaces are thin and flat.
