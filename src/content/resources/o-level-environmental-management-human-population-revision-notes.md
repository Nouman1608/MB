> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Topic 7 of Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) explains how people are spread across the
world, how the age and sex structure of a population is shown and compared, what makes populations grow,
how growth affects cities and the environment, and how governments try to manage population size. Many
questions are data-based, so practise turning maps, tables and pyramids into precise descriptions.

## 7.1 Where do people live?

- **Densely populated** places have many people; **sparsely populated** places have few.
- **Population density** = number of people in an area, usually people per km².
- **Population distribution** = how people are spread over an area.

**Worked example.** Region A: 2 400 000 people on 8 000 km² → 300 per km². Region B: 600 000 people on
30 000 km² → 20 per km². Region A is densely populated; Region B is sparsely populated. Density alone hides
the fact that most of Region B's people may live in one river valley - that is distribution.

## How do you describe a population pyramid?

Use a three-part description - **base, middle, top** - with figures.

| Part of pyramid | What it shows | Group |
|---|---|---|
| Base (0-14) | Young dependants; birth rate | Dependent population |
| Middle (15-64) | People of working age | Working population |
| Top (65+) | Elderly dependants; life expectancy | Dependent population |
| Left v right | Males v females | Sex balance |

## How do low- and high-income countries differ?

World Bank income groups: **LICs**, **MICs**, **HICs**.

- **LIC pattern**: wide base, sides slope in quickly, narrow top - high birth rate, lower life expectancy,
  large young dependent group.
- **HIC pattern**: narrower base, straight sides, wide top - low birth rate, high life expectancy, large
  elderly dependent group.
- **MICs** often show a narrowing base as birth rates fall.

**Worked example (7.1.4).** Two pyramids show these shares of the population (illustrative figures):
Country X has 42% aged 0-14, 55% aged 15-64 and 3% aged 65+; Country Y has 16%, 63% and 21%.
A full-mark comparison uses the figures: "Country X has a much larger young dependent population (42%
against 16%), suggesting a higher birth rate, so it is likely to be the LIC. Country Y has seven times the
share of elderly dependants (21% against 3%), showing higher life expectancy, so it is likely to be the HIC.
Both have a similar working population (55% and 63%)."

## 7.2 What makes a population change?

**Natural change** = births - deaths. **Migration** adds (immigration) or removes (emigration) people.

| Factor | Raises population when... | Lowers population when... |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate (children per woman) | Little contraception or education; children needed for work | Women educated; contraception available; children costly |
| Death rate (life expectancy) | Healthcare, clean water, sanitation, food improve | Disease, famine, conflict |
| Migration | Pull factors: jobs, services, safety | Push factors: poverty, conflict, hazards |

**Rural-urban migration** and **urbanisation** concentrate growth in cities.

## What are the impacts of rapid population growth?

Group the twelve listed impacts into three clusters.

- **Housing and services**: informal urban settlements; megacities; pressure on facilities and
  infrastructure; changing employment opportunities.
- **Resources**: availability of resources; energy usage; water supply; availability of land.
- **Environment**: sanitation; waste management; air, land, light, noise, visual and water pollution;
  traffic congestion.

Answer tip: name the impact, then add a consequence - e.g. "inadequate sanitation in informal settlements
lets sewage enter streams, spreading disease".

## 7.3 How can population size be managed?

| Strategy | How it works | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access to contraception | Couples plan family size | Lowers birth rate; improves women's health | Supply and cost; cultural or religious resistance |
| Anti-natalist policy | Incentives or limits to reduce births | Can slow growth quickly | Ageing population; gender imbalance; rights concerns |
| Pro-natalist policy | Incentives such as child benefits, paid parental leave | Counters ageing and labour shortages | Costly; effect often small |
| Access to education | Especially for girls | Later marriage, smaller families, higher incomes | Takes a generation |
| Access to healthcare | Lower infant mortality | Families need fewer births | Population rises first as deaths fall |
| Control of migration | Limits or encourages movement | Matches labour to need | Political tension; labour shortages; brain drain |

A well-known anti-natalist example is China's one-child policy, in force from 1980 until 2015, which
slowed growth but contributed to rapid ageing and a gender imbalance.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Describing a pyramid shape ("triangle") without explaining what it shows about birth and death rates.
- Forgetting that a large elderly population is also dependent.
- Writing "overpopulation causes pollution" without naming the type of pollution and its cause.
- Saying migration control affects world population - it only moves people between countries.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a dependent population?**
People who rely on the working population, usually children aged 0-14
and people aged 65 and over.

**What is the difference between urbanisation and rural-urban migration?**
Rural-urban migration is the
movement of people from the countryside to towns. Urbanisation is the resulting growth in the proportion
of people living in urban areas.

**What is the difference between push and pull factors?**
Push factors are reasons to leave a place, such
as conflict, poverty or natural hazards. Pull factors are reasons to move to a place, such as jobs, safety and
better services. Most migration answers need one of each.

**Which population strategies does 5014 list?**
Access to contraception, pro-natalist and anti-natalist
policies, access to education, access to healthcare and control of migration.
