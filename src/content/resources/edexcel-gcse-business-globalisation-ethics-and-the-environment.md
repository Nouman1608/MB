This guide follows the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It covers Theme 2: Building a business, topic 2.1 Growing the business, content items 2.1.3 Business and globalisation and 2.1.4 Ethics, the environment and business. The specification has two written papers: Theme 1 Investigating small business (1BS0/01) and Theme 2 Building a business (1BS0/02). Each lasts 1 hour 45 minutes, carries 90 marks and is 50% of the qualification. Each has three sections (A 35 marks, B 30 marks, C 25 marks) of calculations, multiple-choice, short-answer and extended-writing questions, and Sections B and C use business contexts given in the paper. Calculators may be used, and formulae are listed in the specification (Appendix 3) but are not provided in the examinations.

The specification does not define sustainability, tariffs, trade blocs, multinationals, e-commerce or pressure groups; the definitions here and the European Union example are standard usage, not specification text. Globalisation means that businesses and markets in different countries become more closely linked, so a business can buy, sell, make and compete across borders.

## 1. Imports (2.1.3)

Imports are goods or services bought from overseas. Imports affect a business in two ways.

Competition from overseas. Overseas rivals can sell in the home market, often with lower costs. A small business then faces more choice for customers, pressure to cut prices and a risk of losing sales. Competition can also push a business to improve.

Buying from overseas. A business can buy materials, components or finished goods abroad. The reasons are lower prices, a wider choice or goods that are not available at home. The costs to include are freight, delays, quality control and any tariff.

### Worked example: the true cost of buying abroad

Ferncott Textiles needs 12,000 metres of cloth. A home supplier charges £6.40 a metre. An overseas supplier charges £5.10 a metre, a tariff of 8% of the cloth value applies, and shipping costs £3,300.

```
Home supplier:   12,000 x 6.40               = £76,800
Overseas cloth:  12,000 x 5.10               = £61,200
Tariff:          8% of 61,200                = £4,896
Shipping                                     = £3,300
Overseas total:  61,200 + 4,896 + 3,300      = £69,396
Saving:          76,800 - 69,396             = £7,404
```

The saving is 9.6% of the home price (7,404 / 76,800 x 100). Without the tariff it would be £12,300. The overseas route is cheaper, but a good answer still weighs delivery times, reliability and quality before recommending it.

## 2. Exports (2.1.3)

Exports are goods or services sold to overseas markets. Exporting gives a business more potential customers, a way to grow when the home market is crowded and less dependence on one market. The drawbacks are higher transport and administration costs, the need to understand different tastes and languages, and barriers such as tariffs.

### Worked example: export share and a tariff

Brindle Ceramics has sales revenue of £480,000, of which £72,000 comes from exports.

```
Export share = 72,000 / 480,000 x 100 = 15%
```

Next year exports rise to £99,000. The change is (99,000 - 72,000) / 72,000 x 100 = 37.5%.

A customer country charges a 12% tariff on a mug that Brindle sells for £25. The tariff is 12% of £25 = £3.00, so the mug costs £28.00 if the importer passes the whole tariff on. The mug is now 12% dearer than before, which may cut demand.

## 3. Changing business locations (2.1.3)

Globalisation lets a business change where it operates. It may move production abroad, open an overseas office or shop, or use overseas call centres or suppliers. The usual reasons are lower labour costs, cheaper land or materials, being closer to a growing market and avoiding tariffs by making goods inside the target country. The costs are redundancy payments at home, harm to reputation if local jobs are lost, longer supply chains and the need to manage staff from a distance.

### Worked example: moving production

Kelso Garments makes 50,000 shirts a year. Each shirt needs 0.8 hours of labour. UK labour costs £11.50 an hour; the overseas site would cost £3.10 an hour, and extra freight is £1.35 a shirt.

```
UK labour per shirt:        0.8 x 11.50 = £9.20
Overseas labour per shirt:  0.8 x 3.10  = £2.48
Labour saving per shirt:    9.20 - 2.48 = £6.72   (x 50,000 = £336,000)
Extra freight:              1.35 x 50,000 = £67,500
Net saving:                 336,000 - 67,500 = £268,500
```

Redundancy costs, set-up costs and quality problems could reduce the saving.

## 4. Multinationals (2.1.3)

A multinational is a business that has operations (such as factories, shops or offices) in more than one country. It usually has a head office in one country.

For the business: access to new customers, lower costs, spreading risk across countries and the ability to produce near each market. Possible problems are the cost and difficulty of managing operations in several countries, different cultures and rules, and being blamed in the news for conditions anywhere in its supply chain.

For host countries: jobs, spending, new skills and tax receipts. Possible problems include competition that squeezes local firms, profits that leave the country and pressure on the environment.

## 5. Barriers to international trade (2.1.3)

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It raises the price of the import compared with home products, which protects home producers and raises money for a government. For an importer it raises costs; for an exporter it makes the product dearer in the customer country. The Ferncott and Brindle examples above show each effect.

A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to trade with each other on easier terms, for example with low or no tariffs between members. Members commonly keep tariffs on goods from outside the group. The European Union is an example. Two effects follow:

- Inside the bloc: exporting to other members is easier and cheaper, which helps growth.
- Outside the bloc: a business faces the bloc's tariffs, so a trade bloc acts as a barrier. One response is to produce inside the bloc (see section 3).

## 6. How businesses compete internationally (2.1.3)

### The internet and e-commerce

E-commerce means selling over the internet. A website can reach overseas customers without a shop in each country. It is open all day and can link to online marketplaces and social media. Limits include delivery cost and time, returns, language and payment preferences, and the need to build trust with unfamiliar buyers.

### Worked example: overseas web sales

Orla's Teas gets 48,000 overseas visits to its site in a year. Of these, 2.5% place an order. The average order is £22, the tea in each order costs £9.10 and delivery costs £4.20 an order.

```
Orders:   48,000 x 2.5% = 1,200
Revenue:  1,200 x 22    = £26,400
Tea cost: 1,200 x 9.10  = £10,920
Delivery: 1,200 x 4.20  = £5,040
Left over: 26,400 - 10,920 - 5,040 = £10,440
```

The £10,440 must still cover website costs, marketing and other overheads.

### Changing the marketing mix

Competing abroad often means adjusting each element of the mix:

- Product: change size, flavour, labelling or language to suit local tastes and needs.
- Price: set it for local incomes and competitors, allowing for freight and tariffs.
- Promotion: use the local language, local media and cultural references that fit.
- Place: use a local distributor, an overseas warehouse or a local online marketplace.

## 7. Ethical considerations (2.1.4)

Ethical considerations are questions of right and wrong in how a business behaves, such as how it treats employees and suppliers, how honest its advertising is and where it buys its goods. Ethical behaviour can cost money in the short run. There is a possible trade-off between ethics and profit.

Possible gains: better reputation, loyal customers, motivated staff and an easier time recruiting. Possible costs: higher costs, lower profit in the short run and possibly higher prices.

### Worked example: paying suppliers a fairer price

Hobsford Chocolate sells 400,000 bars at £1.80. Each bar costs £0.95 to make.

```
Profit before: (1.80 - 0.95) x 400,000 = 0.85 x 400,000 = £340,000
Fairer price adds £0.12 a bar: 0.73 x 400,000 = £292,000
Fall in profit: 340,000 - 292,000 = £48,000  (14.1%)
If sales rise 6%: 424,000 x 0.73 = £309,520
```

Profit is still £30,480 below the starting point even after the sales rise. The ethical choice costs profit in the short run. A business might accept that if it expects loyal customers and a stronger reputation to pay back over time, and it should say so in the answer.

## 8. Environmental considerations and sustainability (2.1.4)

Environmental considerations are the effects of a business on the natural environment: waste, pollution, energy and resource use. Sustainability means operating so that resources are not used up and the environment is not damaged beyond repair, so that future generations can still meet their needs.

Actions include using recycled packaging, cutting energy use or reducing waste. The trade-off is between the environment, sustainability and profit. The environmental choice can raise costs, but it can also attract customers and cut running costs.

### Worked example: refillable packaging

Solent Soaps sells 250,000 bars a year with a profit of £0.90 a bar. Switching to refillable packaging adds £0.06 to the cost of every bar and brings 20,000 extra sales.

```
Profit before:  250,000 x 0.90 = £225,000
After:          270,000 x 0.84 = £226,800
Change:         +£1,800
```

The profit gain is small. The business also cuts waste, which would support a sustainability claim.

## 9. Pressure groups and the marketing mix (2.1.4)

A pressure group is an organised group that tries to change how businesses or governments behave. It uses campaigns, petitions, social media, protests and media coverage. If the group's message spreads, customers may stop buying, which gives the business a reason to respond.

| Element | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Product | Change an ingredient, material or design |
| Price | A fairer price to suppliers raises costs and may raise prices |
| Promotion | Bad publicity must be answered; advertising may feature the changes |
| Place | Retailers may stop stocking the product, or stock it more widely if it is approved |

Suppose a campaign leads a retailer to stop selling Bramley Foods' range. If this cut 8% of sales revenue of £2.5 million, the business would lose £200,000 a year.

## Common errors

- Writing that a tariff is paid by the exporter. A tariff is a tax on imports; the importer pays it, though prices may be passed on.
- Using the word "export" for something bought from abroad.
- Saying that moving production abroad always saves money without counting redundancy, freight and quality control.
- Assuming that ethical or environmental choices always cost profit. They can bring sales.
- Listing four marketing mix points without tying them to the business in the question.
