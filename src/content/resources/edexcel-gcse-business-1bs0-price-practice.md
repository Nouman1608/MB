## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term penetration pricing. **[2]**

**2.** A shop prices a jacket at £39.99 rather than £40. Which **one** pricing strategy is this? **[1]**

A: Price skimming

B: Psychological pricing

C: Cost-plus pricing

D: Penetration pricing

**3.** Which **two** of the following are influences on pricing strategies named in the specification? **[2]**

A: Technology

B: The weather

C: Competition

D: The owner's age

E: The number of shareholders

**4.** Moorfield Mugs makes a mug for £3.60 and uses cost-plus pricing with a 40% mark-up. Calculate the selling price. **[2]**

**5.** Larch Lane Bakery sells 300 loaves a day at £2.80. It cuts the price to £2.40 and sales rise to 340 loaves a day.

**(a)** Calculate daily revenue before the price cut. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in daily revenue after the price cut. **[2]**

**6.** Zest Juice Bar sells smoothies at £4.99 and gives students 20% off on weekdays. State **one** way Zest Juice Bar sets different prices for different market segments. **[1]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why a business might use price skimming. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Voltway sells kitchen appliances only through its website. Outline **one** way technology could influence Voltway's pricing. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **one** way the product life cycle can influence a business's pricing strategy. **[3]**

**10.** Copse Coffee is an independent café that charges £3.40 for a latte. A national coffee chain has opened two doors away, charging £2.60.

**(a)** Calculate how much higher Copse Coffee's price is than the chain's, as a percentage of the chain's price. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse how this competition could influence Copse Coffee's pricing strategy. **[6]**

**11.** Lumen Labs is launching a smart desk lamp with a new eye-strain sensor. Each lamp costs £45 to make. Market research suggests rivals could launch similar lamps within about twelve months.

Option 1: skimming at £120, with forecast sales of 2,000 in the first year.

Option 2: penetration at £70, with forecast sales of 5,000 in the first year.

Justify which pricing strategy Lumen Labs should use. **[9]**

**12.** Kestle Water Park charges £18 for every visit. Weekdays are quiet, with about 800 visitors a month, while weekends are often full. The manager proposes a £10 weekday price for all visitors, forecast to bring 3,000 extra weekday visitors a month. Each visitor adds £3 in variable costs. Weekend prices would stay at £18.

Evaluate whether Kestle Water Park should introduce the £10 weekday price. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Setting a low price when a product is launched [1] to attract customers quickly and win market share, with the price usually raised later [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The other two named influences are market segments and the product life cycle.

**4.** Mark-up = £3.60 × 40% = £1.44 [1]. Price = £3.60 + £1.44 = **£5.04** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** 300 × £2.80 = **£840** [1]

**(b)** New revenue = 340 × £2.40 = £816 [1]. Change = **−£24**, a fall of £24 a day [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Sales rose by about 13% but the price fell by about 14%, so revenue fell. A lower price does not always mean more revenue.

**6.** Students get 20% off on weekdays [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* £4.99 is psychological pricing, which applies to everyone, so it does not answer a question about segments.

**7.** A new, innovative product may have no direct rivals at launch [1], so some customers who want to be first will pay a high price [1]. This lets the business earn a high profit per unit early on, helping it recover its development costs before rivals arrive and prices fall [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Customers can compare Voltway's prices with other online sellers in seconds [1], so Voltway may have to keep its prices close to the cheapest rivals or lose sales to them [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Another valid point is that Voltway can change prices online quickly, for example for sales events, which supports promotional pricing.

**9.** In the introduction phase an innovative product may be priced high (skimming) because it has few rivals [1]. As it moves into growth and maturity, competitors enter the market [1], so the business usually lowers its price towards rivals' prices or uses promotional offers to defend its market share [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** (£3.40 − £2.60) ÷ £2.60 × 100 [1] = **30.8%** higher [1]

**(b)** Copse is 80p a latte dearer than a chain two doors away, so price-sensitive customers may switch [1]. Copse cannot easily match £2.60 because a small café is unlikely to have the chain's buying power and lower costs [1], so a price war would squeeze its profit per cup [1]. Instead Copse could keep its price and differentiate, for example with locally roasted beans or a friendlier service, so that loyal customers accept paying more [1]. It might use targeted prices for particular segments, such as a loyalty card or a lower morning price for regulars, to keep its busiest customers without cutting every price [1]. If Copse's customers are mainly price-sensitive office workers, though, it may have to move its price closer to £3 to survive [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers should use the figures: the 80p gap and the chain's location are what make the competition so direct.

**11.** Option 1 contribution = (£120 − £45) × 2,000 = **£150,000** [1][1]. Option 2 contribution = (£70 − £45) × 5,000 = **£125,000** [1][1]. Skimming earns £25,000 more in year 1 [1]. The lamp's new sensor has no rivals yet, so some buyers will pay a high price to have it first, which is the situation skimming suits [1]. However, rivals may arrive within twelve months, and penetration would give Lumen 3,000 more customers and a stronger market share before they do [1]. Skimming also leaves room to cut the price later when rivals arrive, while it is harder to raise a penetration price without losing customers [1]. **Judgement:** choose skimming, because it earns more while Lumen has the market to itself and still allows a price cut when competitors launch; this depends on the forecast of 2,000 buyers at £120 being accurate [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Both options must be calculated and compared. The twelve-month warning about rivals is the key piece of context to weigh.

**12.** Extra contribution from new visitors = 3,000 × (£10 − £3) = £21,000 a month [1]. Existing weekday visitors would pay £8 less: 800 × £8 = £6,400 lost [1]. Net gain = £21,000 − £6,400 = **£14,600** a month [1]. For: weekdays are quiet, so the park has spare capacity and extra visitors add little cost beyond £3 each [1]. The lower price targets price-sensitive segments, such as families with young children or retired visitors, who may then return at weekends [1]. Against: some weekend visitors may switch to cheaper weekdays, which would cut revenue at full price [1]. The 3,000 forecast is uncertain; if only 1,000 extra came, the net gain would be £7,000 − £6,400 = £600 [1]. Busier weekdays may need more lifeguards, adding fixed costs not in the figures [1]. **Conclusion:** introduce the weekday price, because the park has empty capacity and the gain is large on the forecast, but it should review results after a few months and could limit the offer to term-time weekdays to stop weekend visitors switching [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers include the revenue lost from existing customers, not only the gain from new ones.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating a mark-up but forgetting to add it to the cost.
- Assuming that higher sales after a price cut always mean higher revenue.
- Confusing psychological pricing with pricing for segments.
- Leaving out the revenue lost from existing customers when a price is cut.
- Naming an influence without showing how it changes this business's price.
