## What 5.1 covers

The syllabus asks for an understanding of: the advantages and disadvantages of
operating as a sole trader; how a sole trader can be a trading, service or
manufacturing business, or a combination; the importance of the statement of
profit or loss and the statement of financial position; how to prepare both
statements; the content of the statement of financial position (non-current
assets, intangible assets, current assets, current liabilities, non-current
liabilities and capital); the effect of a change in one balance on others; and
adjustments for depreciation (straight line, reducing balance and revaluation),
accrued and prepaid expenses and income, irrecoverable debts and the allowance,
and drawings.

## Operating as a sole trader

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Owner takes all decisions quickly | Unlimited liability: personal possessions can be used to pay business debts |
| Owner keeps all the profit | Capital limited to the owner's savings and what lenders will provide |
| Few legal formalities to start | The business depends on one person's skills and health |
| Financial statements stay private | Long hours and no one to share responsibility |

## Why the two statements matter

The **statement of profit or loss** measures performance over a period: it shows
whether the business made a profit and how much the owner can safely withdraw.
The **statement of financial position** records assets, liabilities and capital
on one date, showing what the business owns and owes and whether it can pay its
debts. A lender usually needs both.

## Combination businesses

A sole trader who sells goods **and** charges for services prepares a trading
section for the goods (revenue less cost of sales gives gross profit) and then
adds the service income below gross profit, alongside other income. Service
income has no cost of sales line of its own: the cost of providing it (for
example mechanics' wages) is among the expenses.

**Adjustments at 31 December 2028**

1. Inventory 13,250.
2. Wages accrued 650; insurance prepaid 380.
3. A full year's loan interest is due; only 900 has been paid.
4. Write off a further debt of 200; allowance to be 4% of remaining receivables.
5. Depreciation: equipment 10% straight line on cost; van 25% reducing balance;
   hand tools revalued at 1,250.
6. Tomas took a bicycle costing 600 for his son.
7. General expenses include Tomas's private phone bill of 350.

**Workings**

```
Purchases            92,400 − 600 (goods for own use)          = 91,800
Loan interest        15,000 × 8% = 1,200; accrued 1,200 − 900  =    300
Allowance            (8,600 − 200) × 4% = 336; increase 336 − 260 = 76
Equipment            24,000 × 10%                                = 2,400
Van                  (18,000 − 7,200) × 25%                      = 2,700
Hand tools           1,500 + 400 − 1,250                         =   650
Drawings             14,000 + 600 + 350                          = 14,950
```

Points to notice:

- Goods taken by the owner are removed from **purchases** at cost, not added
  to revenue.
- The private phone bill is moved out of expenses into drawings, so profit
  rises by 350.
- Hand tools under the revaluation method show only the valuation; depreciation is
  the fall in value after allowing for additions.
- The trademark is an **intangible** asset: it has no physical form but brings
  future benefit, so it is listed separately from tangible non-current assets.
- The loan is a non-current liability because repayment is more than twelve
  months away; its accrued interest is a current liability.

## The effect of a change in one balance

Every change has at least two effects. Take three changes to Marlow Bikes' figures:

| Change | Effects |
|---|---|
| Closing inventory found to be 500 higher | Cost of sales −500; gross profit and profit +500; current assets +500; capital +500 |
| A further debt of 100 written off | Expenses +100 (plus a 4 fall in the allowance); profit −96; net trade receivables −96; capital −96 |
| Tomas pays 2,000 of trade payables | Bank −2,000; trade payables −2,000; no effect on profit or capital |

The second row shows why you must follow a change all the way through: writing
off 100 also reduces the allowance by 4% × 100.

## Common mistakes

- Adding goods taken by the owner to revenue instead of deducting them from
  purchases at cost.
- Leaving private expenses in the statement of profit or loss.
- Using reducing balance on cost instead of on net book value.
- Charging the full allowance instead of the change.
- Putting service income above gross profit.
- Listing intangible assets among current assets.
- Adding the loan back to capital: it is a liability.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare:** use the full titles, including the business name and date, and show
  workings beside or below each adjusted figure.
- **Explain** the importance of each statement by naming what it shows and who
  uses it.
- For **advantages and disadvantages**, tie each point to the business in the
  question: "Tomas can decide quickly to start offering repairs."
