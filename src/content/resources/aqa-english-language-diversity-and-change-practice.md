## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between sociolect and dialect, giving the basis of variation for each. **[3]**

**2.** Name the five lenses in the specification's analytical framework for approaching an unfamiliar text. **[5]**

## Section B

**3.** An extract of internal messages between hospital nurses includes the abbreviations "obs", "NBM" and "handover", used without explanation.

**(a)** Identify the type of language variation shown, and explain your reasoning. **[3]**
**(b)** Analyse the social function of this variation for the group using it. **[5]**

**4.** A transcript of a radio interview includes a speaker saying: "aye, that's reet, we allus do it that way round 'ere." Analyse how this extract demonstrates dialect variation, referring to at least **two** specific features. **[5]**

**5.** Explain why a language-change essay must draw on examples from across the specification's full timeframe, rather than only present-day usage. **[3]**

**6.** A word's recorded frequency in a corpus falls from 340 occurrences per million words in 1960 to 12 occurrences per million words in 2020. Explain what kind of evidence this represents, and what caution should be applied before drawing a conclusion about language change from it alone. **[5]**

**7.** Explain the concept of **representation** and how it might differ between a text produced by an in-group member and a text produced by an outsider describing the same group. **[5]**

**8.** Name and briefly explain **two** named debates or attitudes that could be referenced in an evaluative essay on language change. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the claim that language change should be resisted in order to preserve standards of "correct" English. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. Sociolect** varies according to **social group membership** — occupation, gender, ethnicity — while **dialect** varies according to **geography** — regional, national or international variation [1] [1] [1].

**2.** **Personal** context (who produced it) [1]; **social** context (what group or setting shaped it) [1]; **geographical** context (relevant to dialect) [1]; **temporal** context (relevant to change, when it was produced) [1]; **audience, purpose, genre and mode** [1].

**3. (a)** This is an example of **occupational sociolect**, since the abbreviations vary according to **professional group membership rather than geography** [1] [1] [1]. **(b)** Specialist lexis such as this **creates efficient, rapid communication among group members who share the same background knowledge**, allowing information to be conveyed quickly in a time-pressured environment [1] [1]. It simultaneously **constructs in-group identity**, since using the abbreviations correctly signals membership of the professional community [1] [1], while potentially **excluding or confusing outsiders** unfamiliar with the terms — connecting to the specification's focus on how language is used to enact relationships [1].

**4.** **"Reet" for "right" and "'ere" for "here"** show **non-standard pronunciation/spelling representing a regional accent feature**, a marker of geographical dialect [1] [1]. **"Allus" (always)** and the phrase **"we allus do it that way round 'ere"** show **regional lexical and grammatical variation** distinct from Standard English phrasing [1] [1]. Together these features place the speaker within a **specific regional speech community**, which is the defining basis of dialect variation [1].

**5.** The specification's timeframe for language change runs from **1600 to the present day** [1], so an essay confined to present-day examples **cannot demonstrate genuine change over time**, only a snapshot of current usage [1]. Examiners expect at least **one historical comparison point** to show the candidate understands change as a process observed across periods, not simply variation that exists today [1].

**6.** This is an example of **corpus/frequency data**, a legitimate form of research evidence named in the specification alongside dictionaries and other language collections [1] [1]. The caution required is that a **fall in frequency alone does not explain why** the change occurred, or **rule out other factors** such as a shift in the topics the corpus happens to cover, changes in what is being written about, or the specific texts sampled in each period [1] [1]; frequency data should be treated as evidence to be **interpreted alongside other sources**, not as self-explanatory proof of a cause [1].

**7. Representation** is how language is used to **construct a particular image or portrayal of a group**, which may reinforce or challenge existing social attitudes [1] [1]. An **in-group member's** text is more likely to use **insider terminology neutrally or with pride**, reflecting lived experience and shared identity [1] [1]; an **outsider's** text describing the same group may rely on **generalisation, unfamiliar terminology used inaccurately, or framing that reflects the outsider's own assumptions** rather than the group's self-understanding, which can produce a less accurate or even stigmatising representation [1].

**8.** Any two: the **prescriptivist versus descriptivist debate** — prescriptivists view language change as decline from a correct standard, while descriptivists view it as natural, neutral variation to be recorded rather than judged [1] [1]; the debate over whether a particular **dialect or sociolect is unfairly stigmatised** — for example, whether a regional or social variety is treated as "incorrect" rather than simply different, reflecting social attitudes toward the group who speaks it rather than any inherent linguistic deficiency [1] [1].

**9.** **Case for resisting change:** a **prescriptivist** view holds that a shared, stable standard supports clear communication across contexts and generations, and that some changes (such as the erosion of a grammatical distinction) can genuinely reduce precision in meaning [1] [1]. **Case against resisting change:** a **descriptivist** view holds that language change is a natural, continuous process that has always occurred — the English of 1600 differs enormously from today's, and no point in that continuous shift can be objectively fixed as the "correct" one to preserve [1] [1]; attempts to label certain dialects or forms as incorrect often **reflect social attitudes towards the speakers of those varieties rather than genuine linguistic deficiency**, so "preserving standards" can function as a way of stigmatising particular social or regional groups [1] [1]. **Judgement:** since the specification itself frames prescriptivism and descriptivism as **competing, both academically recognised positions** rather than a settled question, a strong evaluative answer should conclude that "correctness" in language is best understood as **socially constructed rather than objectively fixed**, while still acknowledging that shared conventions have genuine practical value for clear communication in formal contexts [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Identifying a feature (a dialect word, a sociolect term) without linking it to its social function or the identity/relationship concepts the specification names.
- Blending diversity and change into one undifferentiated answer rather than treating them as two related but distinct lenses.
- Confining a change-focused answer to present-day examples only, missing the required 1600-to-present timeframe.
- Treating quantitative or corpus evidence as self-explanatory, without noting what caution is needed before drawing a conclusion from it.
- Ending an evaluative essay without a reasoned judgement that engages with named debates such as prescriptivism versus descriptivism.

## Approaching diversity and change questions

Work through the five-lens framework — personal, social, geographical and temporal context, then audience/purpose/genre/mode — before analysing individual language features in any unfamiliar text, since this establishes the context a strong answer needs before it can explain why a feature matters. When a question presents dialect features specifically, look for non-standard spelling, pronunciation markers, regional lexis and grammar together rather than relying on a single feature, and always state which basis of variation (social versus geographical) is being tested, since sociolect/dialect confusion is one of the most heavily penalised errors in this content area. For the evaluative essay style, keep two or three named debates prepared in advance — prescriptivist versus descriptivist attitudes to change, and debates about stigmatised dialects or sociolects are the two most reliably useful — so a specific, credited reference point is always available regardless of which particular question or text the exam provides.
