## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Opportunity cost is best defined as: **[1]**

A: The money price paid for a good.

B: The total value of all the alternatives not chosen.

C: The value of the next best alternative given up.

D: The cost of producing one more unit.

**2.** Which of the following would shift a country's production possibility curve outward? **[1]**

A: A fall in unemployment.

B: The discovery of new mineral deposits.

C: A rise in the general price level.

D: A switch of resources from consumer goods to defence.

**3.** Using the schedule for Country N, what is the opportunity cost of increasing operations from 40 thousand to 60 thousand? **[1]**

A: 20 thousand school places.

B: 82 thousand school places.

C: 102 thousand school places.

D: 12 thousand school places.

**4.** A point outside a country's production possibility curve shows a combination of goods that is: **[1]**

A: Productively efficient.

B: Produced with unemployed resources.

C: Unattainable with current resources and technology.

D: Produced with constant opportunity cost.

**5.** Explain the difference between opportunity cost and accounting cost, using an example. **[3]**

**6.** A graduate is offered a job paying £24,000 a year. Instead she takes a one-year course with fees of £9,000. Assuming the job was her next best alternative, calculate the full cost of the course to her and explain your answer. **[3]**

### Section B

**7.** Use the schedule for Country N.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing operations from 40 to 60 thousand, and from 80 to 100 thousand. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what the pattern of opportunity costs shows and why it occurs. **[3]**

**(c)** Country N produces 50 thousand operations and 85 thousand school places. Explain whether this combination is efficient. **[2]**

**8.** A new surgical technique doubles the number of operations each surgeon can perform but has no effect on schools. Explain, with the aid of a diagram described in words, the effect on Country N's production possibility curve. **[4]**

**9.** A council has £600 million to spend. It can build one new hospital, or 30 new schools at £20 million each, or a new ring road. It chooses the hospital. Explain the opportunity cost of its choice and why it may be hard to measure. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a country's choice between capital goods and consumer goods today affects its production possibilities in the future. **[4]**

**11.** Explain two reasons why an economy may be producing inside its production possibility curve. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the concept of opportunity cost to a government making spending decisions. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Option B adds up all rejected options, which overstates the cost. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A fall in unemployment moves the economy toward the curve; it does not shift it. **[1]**

**3.** **A** [1]. School places fall from 102 thousand to 82 thousand. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Accounting cost is the money actually paid out [1]. Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative given up, including resources already owned [1]. For example, a shop owner who works in her own shop pays herself no wage, but the opportunity cost includes the salary she could have earned elsewhere [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Full cost = fees + earnings forgone = 9,000 + 24,000 = **£33,000** [1] [1]. The fees are an accounting cost, but the larger cost is the salary she gives up by not taking the job, which is her opportunity cost [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the forgone salary is the most common error; the money paid out is only part of the cost.

**7. (a)** 40 to 60 thousand: 102 - 82 = **20 thousand** school places [1]. 80 to 100 thousand: 50 - 0 = **50 thousand** school places [1].

**(b)** Opportunity cost rises as more operations are produced (6, 12, 20, 32, then 50 thousand places for each extra 20 thousand operations) [1]. Resources are not equally suited to both uses [1]: as health care expands, resources better suited to teaching, such as staff and buildings, are switched into hospitals, where they are less productive, so more school places are lost for each extra operation [1].

**(c)** At 50 thousand operations, Country N could provide at least 92 thousand school places, halfway between C and D [1]. With only 85 thousand places, it is inside the curve, so resources are unemployed or used inefficiently [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Because the curve bows outward, the true maximum at 50 thousand operations is at least the halfway figure, so 85 thousand is certainly inside it.

**8.** Draw education on one axis and health care on the other, with the original curve [1]. The new technique raises the maximum operations possible, so the curve's end point on the health-care axis moves outward, to 200 thousand operations [1]. The end point on the education axis stays at 120 thousand places, because schools are unaffected [1]. The curve pivots outward from the education intercept, so at any number of school places more operations are now possible [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A technology that helps only one good pivots the curve; drawing a parallel outward shift loses marks.

**9.** The opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up [1]: if the schools were the council's second choice, it is the 30 schools and the education they would have provided [1]. It is hard to measure because the benefits of the forgone options, such as better learning or shorter journeys, are not easily valued in money [1], and the council may not know which option was truly next best, since people disagree about priorities [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Capital goods, such as machines and infrastructure, are used to produce other goods [1]. Producing more of them today means giving up some consumer goods now, the opportunity cost [1]. But a larger capital stock raises the economy's capacity in future [1], so its PPC shifts outward further than if it had produced mostly consumer goods, allowing more of both goods later [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Unemployment: if aggregate demand is weak, workers and machines are idle [1], so output is below what the economy could produce [1]. Inefficiency: resources may be used in the wrong places or with poor methods [1], for example skilled workers doing jobs that do not use their skills, so output is lower than possible [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Useful: every spending choice has an opportunity cost, so the concept forces government to compare options rather than judge each in isolation [1]. For example, £600 million on a hospital could have built 30 schools [1]. It reminds government that "free" services still use resources [1], and that borrowing today has a cost later [1]. Limits: the next best alternative is hard to identify when there are many options and conflicting priorities [1]. Many benefits, such as health or the environment, are hard to value in money [1]. Government may lack information about costs and benefits [1], and political pressure may lead it to ignore opportunity cost [1]. Judgement: the concept is essential as a way of thinking about choices, but it rarely produces a precise figure [1], so it is most useful alongside other tools that try to value costs and benefits [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a specific example to show the concept at work before weighing its limits.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding up all rejected options.
- Forgetting forgone earnings in personal decisions.
- Showing a fall in unemployment as a shift of the curve.
- Drawing a parallel shift for technology that affects one good.
- Not using figures from the schedule.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
