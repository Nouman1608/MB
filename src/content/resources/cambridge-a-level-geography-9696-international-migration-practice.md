## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between voluntary and forced (involuntary) migration. **[2]**

**2.** State the meaning of "remittances". **[1]**

**3.** Explain why most refugees are hosted by countries neighbouring their own. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why voluntary and forced migration are often described as two ends of a continuum. **[3]**

**5.** The table shows the main destinations of emigrants from the fictional country of Ostrava in 2024.

| Destination | Type of country | Distance from Ostrava (km) | Emigrants (thousands) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Belen | Neighbouring MIC | 300 | 210 |
| Corfa | HIC, former colonial power, shares a language | 6 500 | 140 |
| Daru | Oil-rich HIC with labour recruitment schemes | 3 800 | 95 |
| Elm | HIC, no historical or language links | 5 200 | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of these emigrants who went to Belen. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons why far more emigrants went to Corfa than to Elm, although the two countries are at similar distances. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** reason why Daru attracts a large number of emigrants. **[2]**

**6.** The table shows data for the fictional country of Velmar.

| Year | Remittances (% of GDP) | Nurses per 10 000 people |
|---|---|---|
| 2004 | 3 | 18 |
| 2014 | 7 | 15 |
| 2024 | 12 | 12 |

**(a)** Describe the changes shown in the table. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how emigration may explain these changes. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the causes of international economic migration. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the demographic impacts of international migration on **both** source and destination countries. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why the impacts of a refugee flow on a host area may differ between groups of people living there. **[4]**

**10.** "International migration brings more benefits to destination countries than to source countries." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Voluntary migrants choose to move, usually for work, study or family [1]. Forced migrants have little or no choice because staying would threaten their life or freedom, for example through war, persecution or disaster [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Money sent by migrants to family or others in their country of origin [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Refugees often flee quickly on foot or by road, so they cross the nearest border [1]. They have little money for long journeys and may lack documents to enter distant countries [1]. Many hope to return home when it is safe, so they stay close and may share language or ethnic links with people across the border [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Many moves involve some choice but under heavy pressure [1], such as leaving after repeated crop failure or rising violence combined with unemployment [1]. So it is often hard to place a migrant in only one category, and the same route may carry both refugees and economic migrants (mixed migration) [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Total = 210 + 140 + 95 + 15 = 460 thousand [1]; 210 ÷ 460 × 100 = 45.7% [1].

**(b)** Corfa is a former colonial power, so historical ties mean established communities from Ostrava already live there [1]. Shared language makes finding work and settling easier [1]. Chain migration through these networks keeps the flow going, while Elm has no such links [1].

**(c)** Its recruitment schemes actively bring in workers, often on contracts arranged before departure [1], and oil wealth funds high wages in construction and services [1]. **[7]**

**6. (a)** Remittances rose from 3% to 12% of GDP between 2004 and 2024, a fourfold increase [1]. Nurses per 10 000 people fell from 18 to 12, a fall of one third [1].

**(b)** More people are emigrating and sending money home, so remittances form a growing share of the economy [1]. Many emigrants are skilled health workers recruited abroad for higher wages, a brain drain [1]. This leaves Velmar with fewer nurses, so health care may suffer even as household incomes rise [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Wage gaps mean the same work earns much more in the destination [1]. Labour shortages in ageing HICs, for example in care work and farming, create demand for workers [1]. Employers and governments recruit workers abroad [1]. Networks of earlier migrants supply information, housing and jobs [1]. Historical and language links make some destinations easier [1]. Policies such as free movement areas or points-based visas open routes for some groups [1]. **[6]**

**8.** In source countries, emigration of young adults reduces the number of births [1] and ages the population, raising the old-age dependency ratio [1]. If one sex dominates emigration, the sex ratio becomes unbalanced [1]. In destination countries, young adult migrants lower the average age [1] and often raise the birth rate [1], which slows population ageing and adds to the working-age population [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Landowners and traders may gain from higher demand for rented land, shelter and goods [1]. Poor casual workers may lose as refugees compete for informal jobs and wages fall [1]. Consumers may face higher food and rent prices [1]. People employed by aid agencies gain new jobs and income [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* destination countries receive migrants; source countries lose them [1]. Benefits can be economic, demographic and social [1].

*Agree:* destinations gain workers for sectors with shortages, such as health care and farming [1]. Young migrants slow population ageing and add taxpayers [1]. Destinations gain skilled workers whose education was paid for elsewhere [1]. Migrants start businesses and add cultural diversity [1].

*Disagree:* remittances to source countries can be very large; India receives more than any other country [1]. Emigration relieves unemployment and pressure on land in source areas [1]. Returning migrants bring savings, skills and ideas [1]. Destinations also face costs, such as pressure on housing and services and social tension [1].

*Variation:* source countries lose more when skilled workers leave and do not return, as with health workers [1]. Impacts differ by group: employers in destinations gain, while some low-paid local workers may face competition [1].

*Use of examples:* a named stream with specific detail on remittances or labour [1].

*Judgement:* balance depends on the skill level of migrants, whether they return and how remittances are used [1]. A reasoned final view on "more benefits" [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers compare the two sides directly instead of describing each in turn.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling refugees "economic migrants" or the reverse.
- Explaining a destination pattern by distance alone when the data show networks or history matter.
- Describing changes in a table without quoting the start and end values.
- Covering only source or only destination when both are asked for.
- Essays that list benefits without a judgement on "more".
