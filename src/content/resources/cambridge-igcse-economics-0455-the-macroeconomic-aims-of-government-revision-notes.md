These notes condense section 4.2, The macroeconomic aims of government, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026: the five aims, the reasons for choosing them, the criteria set for each, and the three named conflicts. All figures are invented. ## The five aims

| Aim | Meaning | Why chosen |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Rise in real GDP over time | Higher living standards, jobs, tax revenue |
| Full employment / low unemployment | Those willing and able to work can find jobs | Avoid wasted labour, lost output, hardship, benefit costs |
| Stable prices / low inflation | Low, steady rise in the general price level | Protect purchasing power and savings; help planning; keep exports competitive |
| Balance of payments stability | No large, lasting current account deficit or surplus | Avoid foreign borrowing, asset sales and exchange-rate pressure |
| Redistribution of income | Narrowing the gap between rich and poor | Reduce poverty; improve health, education and social stability |

## Formulae

```
Growth (%) = (real GDP this year - real GDP last year) / real GDP last year x 100
Unemployment rate (%) = unemployed / labour force x 100
Labour force = employed + unemployed
Inflation (%) = (price index this year - last year) / last year x 100
Current account as % of GDP = balance / GDP x 100
```

Worked reminder: employed 19.2 million, unemployed 0.8 million.

```
Labour force = 20 million; unemployment rate = 0.8 / 20 x 100 = 4%
```

Worked reminder: price index rises from 125 to 130.

```
Inflation = 5 / 125 x 100 = 4%
```

## Criteria (targets)

- Usually a number or range: for example a growth rate, a maximum unemployment rate, an inflation range, a limit on the current account as a share of GDP.
- Differ between countries: fast-developing economies may target faster growth; countries with a history of high inflation may set strict inflation targets.
- Change over time: priorities shift in a recession or when prices are rising quickly.

## Reading a performance table

1. Calculate each indicator for each year.
2. Compare each with its target and state "met" or "not met" with the figure.
3. Look for links between indicators (for example, falling unemployment and rising inflation).
4. Name the conflict the data shows.

## The three conflicts (4.2.2)

| Conflict | Chain |
|---|---|
| Full employment vs stable prices | More spending → more jobs → workers scarce → wages rise → costs and prices rise |
| Economic growth vs balance of payments stability | Higher incomes → more imports (consumer goods and materials) → current account worsens |
| Full employment vs balance of payments stability | More people in work → higher incomes → more imports; rising prices may hurt exports → current account worsens |

**In reverse:** policies to cut inflation or a current account deficit reduce spending, which can raise unemployment and slow growth.

Worked reminder: GDP rises from 200 to 220 billion; imports are 25% of GDP; exports stay at 52.

```
Imports: 50 → 55; trade balance: +2 → -3
```

## When conflicts may not happen

- Spare capacity: growth and employment can rise without much inflation.
- Export-led growth: the current account may improve.
- Supply-side improvements: productivity rises, so costs and prices need not rise.

## Redistribution of income: how it is pursued

- Progressive income taxes take a larger share of higher incomes.
- Benefits and pensions raise the incomes of the poorest households.
- Free or subsidised education and health care help low-income families most.
- Criteria might be fewer households below a poverty line or a narrower gap between the top and bottom incomes.

## Exam traps

- **Labour force, not population,** is the base for the unemployment rate.
- **Stable prices ≠ zero inflation.** Targets are usually low and positive.
- **Only three named conflicts.** Develop these rather than inventing others.
- **Show the steps.** A conflict answer needs the links between the two aims.
- **Real, not nominal, GDP** measures growth.
- **Percentage-point change versus percentage change.** Unemployment falling from 6% to 4% is 2 percentage points, a one-third fall in the rate.

## Self-test

1. List the five macroeconomic aims.
2. Give one reason why governments aim for low inflation.
3. Give one reason why governments aim for low unemployment.
4. Real GDP rises from 640 to 656 billion. Calculate the growth rate.
5. Employed 27.6 million, unemployed 2.4 million. Calculate the unemployment rate.
6. A price index rises from 160 to 164. Calculate inflation.
7. A current account deficit is 18 billion and GDP 450 billion. Express the deficit as a percentage of GDP.
8. Why might a fast-developing country set a higher growth target?
9. Explain the conflict between full employment and stable prices in one chain.
10. Why can economic growth worsen the current account?
11. Name one condition under which growth may not cause inflation.
12. Unemployment falls from 8% to 6%. State the change in percentage points and as a percentage.

### Answers

1. Economic growth; full employment/low unemployment; stable prices/low inflation; balance of payments stability; redistribution of income.
2. Protects purchasing power and savings; helps firms plan; keeps exports competitive.
3. Unemployment wastes resources, lowers output and income and raises benefit spending.
4. 16 / 640 x 100 = 2.5%.
5. Labour force 30 million; 2.4 / 30 x 100 = 8%.
6. 4 / 160 x 100 = 2.5%.
7. 18 / 450 x 100 = 4%.
8. To raise living standards quickly from a low base.
9. More spending → firms hire more → labour scarce → wages rise → firms raise prices.
10. Higher incomes raise spending on imports, and firms import more materials, so imports may rise faster than exports.
11. Spare capacity; or productivity rising through supply-side improvements.
12. 2 percentage points; a 25% fall in the rate.
