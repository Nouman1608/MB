This guide goes deeper into subtopic **9.2 Uses of metals** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Alloys and Extraction of Metals](/resources/alloys-and-extraction-of-metals/) guide summarises the four named uses in a single table. This page explains *why* each property matters for each job, shows how to answer data-based "suggest" questions, and sets out the reasoning examiners reward. For quick recall use the [uses of metals revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-uses-of-metals-revision-notes/), and practise with the [uses of metals practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-uses-of-metals-practice/).

## What 9.2 asks you to do

Subtopic 9.2 has one learning outcome, and it is **Core**: describe the uses of metals in terms of their physical properties. There is no Supplement content in 9.2. The syllabus names four uses that you must know:

| Metal | Use named in the syllabus | Property or properties named |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium | manufacture of aircraft | low density |
| Aluminium | manufacture of overhead electrical cables | low density **and** good electrical conductivity |
| Aluminium | food containers | resistance to corrosion |
| Copper | electrical wiring | good electrical conductivity **and** ductility |

The word "including" in the syllabus means these four are the minimum. A question can give you data about another metal and ask you to apply the same reasoning.

## The rule examiners apply: use plus property

A mark for a use is almost always tied to the property that explains it. "Aluminium is used for aircraft" is a fact, not a description in terms of properties, and on its own earns nothing. "Aluminium is used for aircraft because it has a low density" earns the mark. When two properties are named in the syllabus, as for overhead cables and copper wiring, expect a question to want both.

## Why each property matters

### Aluminium in aircraft: low density

Density is mass per unit volume. A metal with a low density gives a lighter part of the same size. For an aircraft, a lighter body means less fuel is needed to fly it, or more passengers and cargo can be carried for the same fuel. The density of aluminium is about 2.7 g/cm³, roughly a third of that of copper (about 9.0 g/cm³) or iron (about 7.9 g/cm³).

### Aluminium in overhead cables: low density and good electrical conductivity

An overhead cable hangs between pylons and must carry a large current over long distances. It needs:

- **good electrical conductivity**, so the current passes easily;
- **low density**, so each length of cable is light and the pylons can support it.

Copper conducts electricity better than aluminium, so why not use copper overhead? Because copper is over three times as dense. For the same mass of metal, an aluminium cable conducts better than a copper one, and a lighter cable needs less support. This trade-off is a favourite "explain" question.

### Aluminium in food containers: resistance to corrosion

Foil trays and drink cans hold moist and sometimes acidic food for a long time. A metal that corroded would weaken the container and could contaminate the food. Aluminium resists corrosion. Extended candidates will meet the reason in 9.4: a thin, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide on the surface protects the metal underneath. Core candidates only need to state the property.

### Copper in electrical wiring: good electrical conductivity and ductility

Wiring inside buildings and appliances needs to carry current with little loss, so good electrical conductivity matters. It also has to be made into long, thin wires and bent around corners, so the metal must be **ductile**. Weight is not a problem for wiring inside a wall, so copper's higher density does not count against it here, unlike for overhead cables.

## Physical properties, not reactivity

The syllabus asks for uses "in terms of their physical properties": density, conductivity, ductility, malleability, strength and melting point. Corrosion resistance is the one chemical behaviour included in the list. Do not explain a use by quoting the reactivity series unless the question asks for it; "aluminium is reactive" is the opposite of what explains its use in food containers.

## Worked example 1: comparing two metals with data

**Question.** A 100 cm³ length of cable can be made from aluminium (density 2.70 g/cm³) or copper (density 8.96 g/cm³). Calculate the mass of each and use your answer to explain why aluminium is used for overhead cables. [3]

**Model answer, annotated.**

```
mass = density × volume
aluminium: 2.70 × 100 = 270 g        [1]
copper:    8.96 × 100 = 896 g        [1]
```

The copper cable is more than three times heavier, so aluminium is chosen because its low density means the pylons have less mass to support, while it still conducts electricity well [1].

*The last mark needs both properties: low density (from your calculation) and good electrical conductivity.*

## Worked example 2: choosing a metal from a table

*The data below are invented for practice.*

| Metal | Density / g/cm³ | Relative electrical conductivity | Resists corrosion? | Ductile? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2.3 | 55 | Yes | Yes |
| B | 8.7 | 95 | Slowly corrodes | Yes |
| C | 7.5 | 15 | No | No |
| D | 1.8 | 30 | No | Yes |

**Question.** Suggest which metal is most suitable for (a) wiring inside a house and (b) a container for storing fruit juice. Give a reason for each choice. [4]

**Model answer, annotated.**

(a) B [1]: it has the highest electrical conductivity and is ductile, so it can be drawn into wires [1]. *Its high density does not matter for wiring inside a wall.*

(b) A [1]: it resists corrosion, so it will not be attacked by the acidic juice [1]. *D is lighter, but it corrodes, which rules it out.*

*"Suggest" means there may be more than one acceptable answer, but each choice must be justified from the data. A choice with no reason scores at most one of the two marks.*

## Common mistakes

- **Giving a use without a property.** Every use answer needs "because…".
- **Mixing up the two aluminium electrical points.** Overhead cables need low density *and* good conductivity; household wiring uses copper for conductivity *and* ductility.
- **Writing "aluminium is light".** Use the scientific term **low density**. "Light" can be accepted in some answers, but "low density" is always correct.
- **Saying copper is used for overhead cables because it is a better conductor.** Copper is a better conductor, but it is too dense for overhead cables.
- **Saying malleable when the property needed is ductile.** Wires are drawn out, so ductility is the property; malleability is about hammering into sheets.
- **Explaining food containers with "aluminium is unreactive".** Aluminium is fairly high in the reactivity series. Say that it **resists corrosion**.

## Exam technique for 9.2 command words

- **State:** give the use or the property in a few words, such as "low density".
- **Describe:** give the use **and** the property together, in one sentence.
- **Explain:** link the property to why it matters for the job. "Low density, so the aircraft is lighter and uses less fuel" is a full explanation.
- **Suggest:** choose from data, then justify using the column that matters for that job. Ignore columns that do not matter, and say why if the question asks you to compare.
- **Calculate:** use density = mass ÷ volume, show the formula, and give units.
