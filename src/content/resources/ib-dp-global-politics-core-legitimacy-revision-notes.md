For full explanations and worked examples, read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-core-legitimacy/) first. These notes are for the final weeks before the exam.

They cover the core prescribed topic **Legitimacy in global politics** for IB Diploma Programme Global Politics. They follow the IB Diploma Programme *Global politics guide* (first assessment 2026, published February 2024), the course examined from the May 2026 session onwards. The topic is common to SL and HL and is examined mainly through Paper 1, with links into Paper 2 section B. Practise with the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-core-legitimacy-practice/), and track coverage with the [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/global-politics/) and the [course hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/global-politics/).

## The four items of prescribed content

1. Sources of state legitimacy, including government legitimacy.
2. Challenges to state and government legitimacy.
3. Sources of legitimacy of non-state actors.
4. Legitimation processes and loss of legitimacy of political actors.

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning to use in answers |
|---|---|
| Legitimacy | Broad acceptance of an actor or action as proper by a population; the rationale for governance |
| State legitimacy | Acceptance of the state itself: its right to exist and rule a territory |
| Government legitimacy | Acceptance of those in office and of how they gained and use power |
| Sovereignty | A state's independence and control over its territory |
| Legitimation | The process of gaining or strengthening legitimacy |
| Delegitimation | Action by others that reduces an actor's legitimacy |
| Stakeholder | Anyone affected by an issue, even without the means to act |

## Sources of state and government legitimacy

The guide lists these fifteen. The four groups are a memory aid, not an IB classification.

| Group | Sources | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Process | free and fair elections; representation; consent of governed people; transparency | How did power arrive, and is it open? |
| Performance | government performance; citizen welfare; order and stability; economic, political and social development; sustained development; quality of governance | Does it deliver? |
| Identity | history; ideology; national identity | Does it fit a shared story? |
| Recognition | leadership recognition; international recognition | Do others accept it? |

Remember the guide's framing: democracy or constitutionalism is the most widely accepted modern form of state legitimacy, but hereditary or traditional leadership can also be a source where that is the norm.

## Challenges to legitimacy

Split every challenge by **origin**.

| From within the state | From external stakeholders |
|---|---|
| Political, economic and social instability | Non-recognition of a government or state |
| Inability to govern effectively | Sanctions on a state or its officials |
| Inability to ensure citizens' well-being | Suspension or exclusion from regional bodies |
| Excessive use of force and threats | Criticism or resolutions from UN bodies |
| Violence, oppression, human-rights violations | Pressure from NGOs and foreign publics |

The left column comes from the guide's areas to consider. The right column lists common forms of external challenge.

## Non-state actors: the four areas

| Area | Question to ask | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | Whose interests does it represent, and how well? | Membership, leadership selection, who funds it |
| Interaction | How does it relate to states and other non-state actors? | Access to UN forums, partnerships, conflict with governments |
| Ability | What means does it have to exert influence? | Money, expertise, mobilisation, armed force, media reach |
| Efficacy | How far does it achieve its goals? | Outcomes delivered, policy changes, services provided |

The guide wants legitimacy judged towards governments **and** towards other actors: donors, members, communities and each other.

## Legitimation processes

| Process | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Formal recognition by an actor in an authority role | Top down | South Sudan admitted to the UN in 2011 |
| Self-legitimation | From the actor | Holding elections or referendums; national symbols |
| Organic recognition | Bottom up | Turnout, membership, donations, trust |
| (De)legitimation by opponents | Contest | Boycotts, NGO reports, rival claims |

## Method in steps

> **Analysing a legitimacy claim (any actor)**
> 1. Name the actor and the claim precisely.
> 2. Identify the source or sources it relies on.
> 3. Name the audience: citizens, other states, an IGO, members, donors.
> 4. Explain why that audience accepts or rejects the claim.
> 5. Test it: is the source durable, and what would undermine it?

> **Tracing a loss of legitimacy**
> 1. Which source was damaged?
> 2. Was the challenge internal, external or both?
> 3. Did the government lose legitimacy, the state, or both?
> 4. What was the outcome: fall from office, repression, reform?
> 5. Was legitimacy rebuilt, and through which source?

> **Paper 1 compare and contrast**
> Keep a running comparison. Each paragraph covers one point and refers to both sources. Say where they agree, where they differ, and why.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Legitimacy vs power.** Power is the ability to effect change; legitimacy is acceptance. Coercion can keep power without legitimacy.
- **Legitimacy vs legality.** Legal acts can be seen as illegitimate; illegal protest can be seen as legitimate.
- **Legitimacy vs sovereignty.** Control of territory can exist without wide recognition, and recognition without control.
- **State vs government.** A government can fall while the state stays accepted.
- **Process vs performance.** Elections versus delivery. Most governments rely on both.
- **Internal vs external challenge.** Citizens' protests versus sanctions and non-recognition.
- **Formal vs organic recognition.** Granted by an authority versus earned from audiences.

## Case bank

Use cases from roughly the last two decades, as the guide recommends. Explain each; do not just name it.

- **Sri Lanka 2022:** economic crisis, mass protests, president resigned. Government legitimacy lost; state legitimacy intact; renewed by the 2024 election.
- **Lebanon 2020:** Beirut port explosion; government resigned. Governance and transparency.
- **Myanmar 2021:** military coup; force against protesters; ASEAN excluded its leaders from summits.
- **Belarus 2020:** disputed presidential election; EU non-recognition and sanctions.
- **Afghanistan 2021:** Taliban control with very limited formal recognition. Sovereignty and legitimacy apart.
- **Kosovo 2008:** declared independence; recognition divided; not a UN member. State legitimacy contested.
- **Saudi Arabia:** hereditary monarchy; religious role; welfare and Vision 2030. Non-electoral sources.
- **Médecins Sans Frontières:** neutrality, expertise, private funding. Non-state actor legitimacy through interaction, ability and efficacy.
- **Fridays for Future:** youth climate movement; strong representation claim without an elected mandate.

## Linking legitimacy to the thematic studies

The guide notes that legitimacy may look different in each thematic study. Paper 2 section B essays reward these links.

- **Peace and conflict:** who has a legitimate claim to use force, and how international responses depend on that judgement. See the [Peace and conflict revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-peace-conflict-revision-notes/).
- **Rights and justice:** human-rights violations as a challenge to government legitimacy; NGOs gaining legitimacy by documenting abuses. See the [Rights and justice revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-rights-justice-revision-notes/).
- **Development and sustainability:** development and sustained development as sources of performance legitimacy; the legitimacy of companies and movements in climate debates.

## Paper 1 at a glance

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 15 minutes, carries 25 marks, and is 30% of the SL grade or 20% of the HL grade. It has four sources and four compulsory questions: understanding a source (3 marks), applying knowledge to a source (4 marks), comparing and/or contrasting two sources (6 marks) and evaluating the sources with your own knowledge (12 marks, marked against markbands).

## Quick self-test

1. Define legitimacy in one sentence.
2. Name two process sources and two performance sources of government legitimacy.
3. Give one reason why legitimacy is not the same as legality.
4. In Sri Lanka in 2022, was it the state or the government that lost legitimacy? Justify briefly.
5. List the four areas the guide gives for judging non-state actor legitimacy.
6. Give one external challenge to a government's legitimacy.
7. What is the difference between top-down recognition and organic recognition?
8. How can an actor have sovereignty without legitimacy? Give a case.
9. Which source of legitimacy does a disputed election most directly damage?
10. Why must you name an audience when judging legitimacy?
11. Which Paper 1 skill is AO3 and asks you to refer to two sources throughout?
12. Give one self-legitimation strategy.

### Answers

1. Legitimacy is broad acceptance by a population that an actor or action is proper and has the right to exercise power.
2. Process: free and fair elections; transparency (also representation, consent). Performance: citizen welfare; order and stability (also development, quality of governance).
3. A law can be passed through proper procedure yet be widely rejected; an illegal protest can be widely seen as justified.
4. The government. Protests targeted the president and his administration; power passed through constitutional procedures, so the state itself stayed accepted.
5. Representation, interaction, ability, efficacy.
6. Any one: sanctions on officials; non-recognition of an election result; suspension from a regional body; a critical UN resolution.
7. Top-down recognition is granted formally by an actor in an authority role, such as UN admission. Organic recognition grows from stakeholders, such as public trust or membership.
8. It can control territory without being accepted, for example the Taliban in Afghanistan after 2021, with very limited formal recognition.
9. Free and fair elections, and with it consent of governed people; it often damages international recognition too.
10. Legitimacy is acceptance by someone. The same actor can be accepted by one audience and rejected by another.
11. Question 3: compare and/or contrast.
12. Holding elections or a referendum; publishing performance reports; appealing to national identity or ideology.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining legitimacy as "legal" or "powerful" instead of "accepted".
- Mixing up state and government legitimacy in a case where only one changed.
- Listing several sources with no explanation of how each works in the case.
- Naming an example with no date, actor or detail. The guide expects contextualisation.
- Never saying whose acceptance is at stake.
- Treating every non-elected government as illegitimate, ignoring the other sources the guide names.
- Judging non-state actors on one area only, usually efficacy, and skipping representation.
- In compare and contrast, writing about each source separately instead of a running comparison.
- In evaluation, giving one side only. Weigh strengths and limits and reach a judgement.
