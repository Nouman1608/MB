## What makes a polymer a condensation polymer

In a condensation polymerisation, monomers join through a reaction that releases a small molecule, usually **water**. Each time two monomers join, one water molecule is lost. Unlike addition polymerisation, there is more than one product, so the atom economy is below 100 %.

To form a long chain each monomer needs **two reactive groups**, one at each end.

| Type of polymer | Monomers | Link formed | Small molecule lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polyester | Dicarboxylic acid + diol | Ester, –CO–O– | H₂O |
| Polyamide | Dicarboxylic acid + diamine | Amide, –CO–NH– | H₂O |
| Polyamide (polypeptide) | Amino acid (one –NH₂ and one –COOH) | Amide (peptide), –CO–NH– | H₂O |

(If a diacyl chloride is used instead of the diacid, hydrogen chloride is lost instead of water. The specification names the dicarboxylic acids.)

## Polyesters: Terylene

Terylene (poly(ethylene terephthalate)) is made from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and ethane-1,2-diol.

```
n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH + n HOCH₂CH₂OH → –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O]ₙ– + 2n H₂O
```

The ester link –CO–O– joins each acid group to an alcohol group. The chain alternates acid unit and diol unit. Uses: textile fibres for clothing, and plastic drinks bottles.

## Polyamides: nylon 6,6 and Kevlar

**Nylon 6,6** is made from hexanedioic acid, HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH, and 1,6-diaminohexane, H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂. The two 6s show six carbon atoms in each monomer.

```
repeating unit: –[NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO]ₙ–
```

Uses: ropes, fabrics, carpets and mouldings.

**Kevlar** is made from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and benzene-1,4-diamine (1,4-diaminobenzene).

```
repeating unit: –[NH–C₆H₄–NH–CO–C₆H₄–CO]ₙ–
```

Uses: bulletproof vests, protective clothing, and as reinforcement in tyres and ropes.

## Polypeptides from amino acids

An amino acid has an –NH₂ group at one end and a –COOH group at the other, so it can react with itself. For glycine, H₂N–CH₂–COOH:

```
n H₂N–CH₂–COOH → –[NH–CH₂–CO]ₙ– + n H₂O
```

The link –CO–NH– is the same amide link, called a peptide link in proteins. Amino acids and proteins are developed in the Amino acids and Proteins sub-topics.

## Three drawing skills

### 1. Repeating unit from monomers

1. Draw the two monomers with their functional groups at the ends.
2. Remove –OH from the acid group of one end, and H from the –OH (or –NH₂) of the other, so a molecule of water is removed.
3. Join the two ends by the new ester or amide link.
4. Draw the repeating unit with the link at both ends, in brackets with extended bonds, so that it contains one unit of each monomer.

### 2. Repeating unit from a section of chain

Look for the repeating pattern, which contains **one unit of each monomer** for a two-monomer polymer. Start and finish your bracket in the same position along the chain. Draw the bonds extending out of the brackets.

### 3. Monomers from a section of chain

1. Break each ester or amide link.
2. Add –OH to the carbonyl (C=O) carbon, giving –COOH.
3. Add –H to the oxygen of the ester (giving –OH) or to the nitrogen of the amide (giving –NH₂).

This is the reverse of hydrolysis.

## Intermolecular forces

| Polymer | Link | Forces between chains |
|---|---|---|
| Polyester (Terylene) | –CO–O– | Permanent dipole–dipole attractions between the polar C=O and C–O bonds, plus London forces |
| Polyamide (nylon 6,6) | –CO–NH– | **Hydrogen bonds** between N–H of one chain and C=O of another, plus London forces |
| Kevlar | –CO–NH– | Hydrogen bonds, with chains made rigid and straight by the benzene rings so they line up and pack closely |

The hydrogen bonds in polyamides are stronger than the dipole–dipole forces in polyesters. This is why nylon fibres are strong, and why Kevlar, with extra rigidity from the benzene rings and regular hydrogen bonding, is exceptionally strong for its mass. Condensation polymers differ from polyalkenes, whose chains are held only by London forces.

## Comparing addition and condensation polymers

| Feature | Addition polymer | Condensation polymer |
|---|---|---|
| Monomers | Alkenes or substituted alkenes with C=C | Two functional groups per monomer (diacid and diol, diacid and diamine, or amino acid) |
| Number of products | One (the polymer) | Polymer plus a small molecule (usually water) |
| Atom economy | 100 % | Less than 100 % |
| Backbone | Carbon–carbon chain | Contains ester or amide links |
| Intermolecular forces | London forces (plus dipole–dipole if polar) | Dipole–dipole (polyester) or hydrogen bonding (polyamide) |
| Breakdown by hydrolysis | No | Yes (see the Biodegradability and disposal sub-topic) |

The last row is the reason that condensation polymers can be broken down by water, acids or alkalis and addition polymers cannot. The links in the chain are polar and can be attacked by nucleophiles, whereas a chain of C–C bonds has no such site.

## Naming the linkages

An ester link is –CO–O– and an amide link is –CO–NH–. Both contain a carbonyl group, C=O. In an exam, always draw the link in full, including the C=O, because a link drawn without it will not earn the mark. A useful check is to count the links in a chain section: the number of links equals the number of monomer units minus one in a chain that starts and ends with a monomer.

## Worked example 1: nylon 6,6 repeating unit

**Question.** Draw the repeating unit formed from hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane.

The acid end –COOH of one monomer reacts with the amine end –NH₂ of the other, losing H₂O and forming –CO–NH–. The repeating unit contains one of each monomer:

–[CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH]ₙ–

## Worked example 2: monomers from a section of chain

**Question.** Draw the monomers from this section:

–O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–

The links are esters, –CO–O–. Break each ester link, adding –OH to the C=O carbon and H to the O. The monomers are **ethane-1,2-diol**, HOCH₂CH₂OH, and **benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid**, HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH. Identify the polymer as Terylene.

## Worked example 3: mass balance

14.6 g of hexanedioic acid (Mᵣ = 146.0) reacts with 11.6 g of 1,6-diaminohexane (Mᵣ = 116.0).

```
n(acid)   = 14.6 ÷ 146.0 = 0.100 mol
n(amine)  = 11.6 ÷ 116.0 = 0.100 mol    (1 : 1, so neither is in excess)
repeating unit: C₁₂H₂₂N₂O₂, Mr = 262.0 − 2(18.0) = 226.0
maximum mass of polymer = 0.100 × 226.0 = 22.6 g
mass of water lost      = 0.200 × 18.0 = 3.6 g
check: 14.6 + 11.6 = 26.2 g = 22.6 g + 3.6 g ✓
```

## Worked example 4: atom economy

For the repeating unit of Terylene, C₁₀H₈O₄ (Mᵣ = 192.0), from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (Mᵣ = 166.0) and ethane-1,2-diol (Mᵣ = 62.0):

```
atom economy = 192.0 ÷ (166.0 + 62.0) × 100 = 84.2 %
```

It is below 100 % because 2 water molecules are lost for each repeating unit.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a repeating unit with only half of a link, or ending the bracket in the middle of an ester or amide link.
- Including both monomers' full structures with no loss of water.
- Using an addition-style repeating unit with a C=C bond.
- Writing a monomer with only one functional group, which could not form a chain.
- Saying nylon has hydrogen bonds between the "C=O and C=O" or "N–H and N–H". The bond forms between N–H (donor) and C=O (acceptor).
- Naming Terylene as a polyamide, or nylon as a polyester.
- Forgetting that the forces in polyesters are dipole–dipole, not hydrogen bonds (no N–H or O–H group in the chain).
- Hydrolysing in reverse: adding –OH to the wrong atom when finding monomers.

## Exam technique

- **Draw a repeating unit**: show the link, extended bonds and n. Check that it contains one of each monomer.
- **Find monomers**: break the links and restore –COOH, –OH or –NH₂.
- **Explain properties**: name the force, name the groups responsible, and relate it to strength.
- **Link to uses**: strong fibres (nylon, Kevlar) depend on hydrogen bonding.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are two functional groups needed on each monomer?**
So that each monomer can bond at both ends and the chain can keep growing.

**Is a polypeptide a polyamide?**
Yes. A peptide link is an amide link.

**Why is Kevlar stronger than nylon 6,6?**
Its chains are rigid and straight because of the benzene rings, so they pack closely and line up, giving many hydrogen bonds between chains.
