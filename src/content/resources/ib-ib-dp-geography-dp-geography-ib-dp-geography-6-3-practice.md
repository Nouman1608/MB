## Section A

**1.** Define buyer power, with reference to supermarkets. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between the Fairtrade minimum price and the Fairtrade premium. **[2]**

**3.** Outline one advantage and one disadvantage of contract farming for a smallholder. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by tied aid. **[2]**

**5.** Outline why pharmaceutical patents can limit access to medicines in low-income countries. **[2]**

**6.** State the role of community health workers in a health system. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An agency has 9,000,000 currency units to feed people for one season. Each person needs 0.15 tonnes of food. Food shipped from the donor country costs 1,000 per tonne delivered; food bought regionally costs 600 per tonne delivered.

**(a)** Calculate how many people each option can feed. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in people reached by choosing regional purchase. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one situation in which shipping food from the donor country might still be the better choice. **[2]**

**8.** A region has suffered drought. Markets are still functioning and traders can bring food from neighbouring districts.

**(a)** Explain why cash transfers may be more suitable than in-kind food aid here. **[2]**

**(b)** Outline one risk of cash transfers in this situation. **[2]**

**9.** Using a power-interest grid, explain the position of smallholder farmers in a national food system, and suggest how they could increase their influence. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the case of antiretroviral drugs for HIV shows conflict between stakeholders in global health. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the role of non-governmental organisations in improving food security. **[10]**

**12.** "Governments, not corporations, are the most important stakeholders in improving health." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Buyer power is the ability of a large purchaser to influence the price and terms it pays suppliers [1]; supermarkets that sell most of a country's food can demand low prices, strict standards or special terms from farmers and processors [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The minimum price is a floor paid to producer organisations, intended to cover the cost of sustainable production when market prices fall [1]; the premium is an extra sum paid on top, which producer groups invest in community or business projects [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Advantage: a guaranteed buyer and agreed price, often with seed and advice supplied [1]. Disadvantage: loss of independence and the risk of being dropped for missing quality standards [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Aid given on the condition that it is spent on goods or services from the donor country [1], which usually raises costs and may not suit the recipient's needs [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Patents give the developer exclusive rights to sell a medicine for a period, so there is no cheaper competition [1]; high prices set to recover research costs may be unaffordable for low-income governments and households [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Local people trained to deliver basic care, vaccination and nutrition advice [1], extending health services to communities that clinics and hospitals cannot easily reach [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Donor-shipped: cost per person = 1,000 x 0.15 = 150; 9,000,000 / 150 = **60,000 people** [1]. Regional: 600 x 0.15 = 90; 9,000,000 / 90 = **100,000 people** [1].

**(b)** (100,000 - 60,000) / 60,000 x 100 = **66.7%** [1].

**(c)** For example, if regional markets are also short of food, buying there could push up prices for people not receiving aid [1], harming food access across the region, so importing food adds to total supply instead [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Food is available in functioning markets, so the problem is access, not availability [1]; cash lets households buy what they need and supports local traders and farmers instead of undercutting them [1].

**(b)** Extra cash may raise local food prices if traders cannot increase supply fast enough [1], harming households who do not receive transfers [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Smallholders have high interest, because their livelihoods and food depend on the system [1], but low power, because they are dispersed, have little capital and limited political voice [1]. They could increase influence by forming cooperatives to sell together and bargain with buyers [1], or by working with NGOs and farmer movements to campaign on prices, land rights or subsidies [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Pharmaceutical firms held patents and sold antiretrovirals at prices that recovered research costs but were unaffordable in much of Africa [1]. Generic manufacturers, many in India, produced far cheaper versions [1]. NGOs and affected governments campaigned for access, while firms defended their patents [1]. In 2001 WTO members agreed the Doha Declaration, confirming that countries could act to protect public health, after which prices fell and treatment expanded [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Strengths:** NGOs often work at community scale and can design bottom-up projects that fit local needs [1]; they can reach groups that governments neglect [1].
- **Relief:** they deliver emergency food and nutrition support quickly [1].
- **Advocacy:** they campaign on land rights, prices and corporate behaviour, as in the infant formula marketing campaign of the 1970s [1].
- **Development projects:** farmer training, storage and water projects can improve availability and utilisation [1].
- **Limits:** small scale and dependence on donations [1]; projects may end when funding stops [1]; coordination with governments can be weak [1].
- **Accountability:** NGOs are not elected, raising questions about whose priorities they follow [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, NGOs are valuable at local scale and in emergencies, but lasting national food security depends on government policy [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Government role:** funds and runs health systems, sets regulation and public-health policy such as vaccination programmes, sanitation and sugar taxes [1] [1].
- **Government limits:** budgets, weak governance or conflict, as with polio in Pakistan and Afghanistan [1].
- **Corporate role:** pharmaceutical firms develop medicines and vaccines [1]; food firms shape diets through products and marketing [1].
- **Corporate limits and conflicts:** patents and pricing can restrict access, as with antiretrovirals around 2000 [1]; marketing of unhealthy foods adds to non-communicable disease [1].
- **Other stakeholders:** international organisations, foundations and public-private partnerships such as Gavi, and community health workers [1].
- **Interaction:** governments regulate corporations, so their influence often works through them [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, agree to a large extent, because governments set the rules and fund universal services, but corporate innovation is essential and must be regulated [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "to what extent" requires a clear position; the best answers show how stakeholders interact rather than treating them separately.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming stakeholders without their powers and interests.
- Forgetting to multiply by tonnes per person before dividing the budget.
- Assuming cash or in-kind aid is always better.
- Ignoring the limits on a stakeholder's power.
