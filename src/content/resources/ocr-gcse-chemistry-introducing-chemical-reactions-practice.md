These questions cover sub-topic **C3.1 Introducing chemical reactions** of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Chemistry A (Gateway Science) J248 specification, Version 4.0 (August 2026): statements C3.1a to C3.1l and the maths outcomes CM3.1i to CM3.1iv. Questions on C3.1e, C3.1g, C3.1h, C3.1k and C3.1l are marked **(Higher Tier only)**; the others suit both tiers. Relative atomic masses are given in each question where you need them.

## Questions

**1.** Use the ions Li⁺, Mg²⁺, NH₄⁺, F⁻, OH⁻ and SO₄²⁻ to write the formula of each compound.

**(a)** lithium fluoride **[1]**
**(b)** magnesium hydroxide **[1]**
**(c)** ammonium sulfate **[1]**

**2.** Balance each equation.

**(a)** Na + O₂ → Na₂O **[1]**
**(b)** C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (the combustion of butane) **[2]**

**3.** Half equations.

**(a)** Write the half equation for zinc ions, Zn²⁺, forming zinc atoms. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for iodide ions forming iodine molecules. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why there is a 2 in front of the iodide ion in your answer to (b). **[1]**

**4.** Aluminium reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give a solution of aluminium chloride, AlCl₃, and hydrogen gas. Write the balanced equation, including state symbols. **[3]**

**5.** (Higher Tier only)

**(a)** Iron(III) chloride solution is added to sodium hydroxide solution. A solid of iron(III) hydroxide, Fe(OH)₃, forms. Write the ionic equation, including state symbols. **[2]**
**(b)** Chlorine solution is added to potassium bromide solution. The solution turns orange as bromine forms. Write the ionic equation. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the spectator ion in reaction (b). **[1]**

**6.** A student puts zinc carbonate and dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask and seals it with a bung. The flask and contents have a mass of 85.62 g. Fizzing is seen until the reaction stops.

**(a)** State the mass of the sealed flask and contents at the end. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your answer to (a). **[1]**
**(c)** The student removes the bung and weighs the flask again later. The reading is 85.18 g. Calculate the change in mass. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain the change in (c) using the particle model. **[2]**

**7.** A crucible containing copper powder has a mass of 24.50 g. It is heated in air without a lid, then cooled and reweighed. The new mass is 24.82 g.

**(a)** Calculate the change in mass. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the mass increased, using ideas about particles. **[2]**

**8.** (Higher Tier only) Use the Avogadro constant 6.02 × 10²³ per mole.

**(a)** State what is meant by the Avogadro constant. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of one silver atom. Ar of Ag = 108. Write the answer in standard form, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the number of atoms in 2.70 g of aluminium. Ar of Al = 27. **[2]**

**9.** (Higher Tier only) Use Ar: H = 1, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Ca = 40.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, in 2.96 g. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of 0.125 mol of magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄. **[2]**

**10.** (Higher Tier only) A student heats 0.84 g of lithium in nitrogen. The lithium reacts completely with 0.56 g of nitrogen, N₂, to make 1.40 g of lithium nitride, Li₃N. Use Ar: Li = 7, N = 14. Use the masses to deduce the balanced equation for the reaction. Show your working. **[5]**

**11.** (Higher Tier only) Aluminium reacts with iodine to form aluminium iodide, AlI₃. A teacher mixes 0.81 g of aluminium with 6.35 g of iodine, I₂. Use Ar: Al = 27, I = 127.

**(a)** Write the balanced equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that iodine is the limiting reactant. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the maximum mass of aluminium iodide that can form. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the mass of aluminium left over. **[1]**
**(e)** The teacher repeats the reaction with 1.62 g of aluminium and the same mass of iodine. Explain why the mass of product does not change. **[1]**

**12.** (Higher Tier only) A strip of magnesium ribbon is left in silver nitrate solution, AgNO₃(aq). Grey crystals of silver grow on the strip, and magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO₃)₂, stays dissolved. Use Ar: N = 14, O = 16, Mg = 24, Ag = 108.

**(a)** Write the ionic equation, including state symbols. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of magnesium that reacts when 5.40 g of silver forms. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of magnesium nitrate made at the same time. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** One Li⁺ balances one F⁻: **LiF** [1]
**(b)** One Mg²⁺ needs two OH⁻, with brackets: **Mg(OH)₂** [1]
**(c)** Two NH₄⁺ balance one SO₄²⁻: **(NH₄)₂SO₄** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A formula without the brackets, such as MgOH₂, usually gains no credit because it describes a different particle.

**2. (a)** **4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O** [1]
**(b)** Carbon and hydrogen balanced: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O, with oxygen still unbalanced [1]; doubling to clear the half: **2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers with 6½O₂ are correct in ratio, but whole-number coefficients are the expected form, so double if a half appears.

**3. (a)** **Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn** [1]
**(b)** **2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻** [1]
**(c)** Iodine exists as I₂ molecules, so two iodide ions are needed to make one molecule (and two electrons are released) [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Check charges: a half equation that balances atoms but not charge, such as 2I⁻ → I₂ + e⁻, is not accepted as balanced.

**4.** Correct formulae: Al, HCl, AlCl₃ and H₂ [1]; balanced: 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂ [1]; state symbols: **2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Get the formulae right before balancing; writing AlCl in place of the given AlCl₃ spoils the whole equation, however good the state symbols are.

**5. (a)** Correct species: Fe³⁺ and OH⁻ forming Fe(OH)₃ [1]; balanced with states: **Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)** [1]
**(b)** Correct species: Cl₂ and Br⁻ forming Cl⁻ and Br₂ [1]; balanced: **Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂** [1]
**(c)** **Potassium ion, K⁺** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An ionic equation that still contains Na⁺ or K⁺ on both sides is not fully simplified; remove every spectator ion.

**6. (a)** **85.62 g** [1]
**(b)** The flask is sealed, so no particles can enter or leave; total mass is conserved [1]
**(c)** 85.62 − 85.18 = **0.44 g decrease** [1]
**(d)** Carbon dioxide gas formed in the reaction and its molecules escaped from the open flask into the air [1]; the atoms were not destroyed, so total mass is still conserved but the escaped gas is no longer weighed [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the gas and saying it left the container is what earns credit; "mass was lost in the reaction" contradicts conservation of mass.

**7. (a)** 24.82 − 24.50 = **0.32 g increase** [1]
**(b)** Oxygen molecules from the air react with the copper to form copper oxide [1]; the oxygen atoms are now bonded in the solid in the crucible, so their mass is added to the reading [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Full credit needs both where the extra mass comes from (oxygen in the air) and why the balance now detects it.

**8. (a)** The number of particles (atoms, molecules or ions) in one mole of a substance, about 6.02 × 10²³ per mole [1]
**(b)** Mass of one atom = 108 ÷ (6.02 × 10²³) [1] = **1.79 × 10⁻²² g** [1]
**(c)** Amount = 2.70 ÷ 27 = 0.100 mol [1]; atoms = 0.100 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **6.02 × 10²² atoms** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A mass of 1.79 g, or a positive power of ten, shows the standard form went wrong; one atom must have a tiny mass.

**9. (a)** Mr of Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74 [1]; amount = 2.96 ÷ 74 = **0.0400 mol** [1]
**(b)** Mr of MgSO₄ = 24 + 32 + 4 × 16 = 120 [1]; mass = 0.125 × 120 = **15.0 g** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An Mr of 57 for Ca(OH)₂ comes from ignoring the bracket; show the Mr working so a slip there is visible.

**10.** Amount of Li = 0.84 ÷ 7 = 0.12 mol [1]; amount of N₂ = 0.56 ÷ 28 = 0.020 mol [1]; Mr of Li₃N = 35, so amount = 1.40 ÷ 35 = 0.040 mol [1]; dividing by 0.020 gives the ratio Li : N₂ : Li₃N = 6 : 1 : 2 [1]; equation: **6Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 14 instead of 28 for nitrogen gas halves the ratio for N₂; always use the formula as it appears in the equation.

**11. (a)** **2Al + 3I₂ → 2AlI₃** [1]
**(b)** Amount of Al = 0.81 ÷ 27 = 0.030 mol [1]; amount of I₂ = 6.35 ÷ 254 = 0.025 mol [1]; 0.030 mol Al would need 0.030 × 3/2 = 0.045 mol I₂, but only 0.025 mol is present, so **iodine is limiting** [1]
**(c)** Amount of AlI₃ = 0.025 × 2/3 = 0.01667 mol; Mr of AlI₃ = 27 + 3 × 127 = 408 [1]; mass = 0.01667 × 408 = **6.80 g** [1]
**(d)** Al used = 0.01667 × 27 = 0.45 g, so Al left = 0.81 − 0.45 = **0.36 g** [1]
**(e)** Iodine is still the limiting reactant and its amount has not changed, so the same amount of product forms; the extra aluminium is simply left over [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In "show that" parts, the comparison with the equation ratio must be written out; stating "iodine has fewer moles" without the 3 : 2 ratio does not prove it is limiting.

**12. (a)** Correct species, with nitrate ions left out: Mg, Ag⁺, Mg²⁺, Ag [1]; balanced with states: **Mg(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)** [1]
**(b)** Amount of Ag = 5.40 ÷ 108 = 0.0500 mol [1]; ratio Mg : Ag = 1 : 2, so 0.0250 mol Mg [1]; mass = 0.0250 × 24 = **0.600 g** [1]
**(c)** Mr of Mg(NO₃)₂ = 24 + 2 × (14 + 3 × 16) = 148 [1]; 0.0250 mol × 148 = **3.70 g** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the 1 : 2 ratio and using 0.0500 mol of magnesium doubles both later answers; write the ratio line out explicitly.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing formulae without brackets, such as NH₄₂SO₄ for ammonium sulfate.
- Changing subscripts to balance an equation instead of adding coefficients.
- Writing I or Cl instead of I₂ or Cl₂ in equations, and using 127 instead of 254 as the Mr of iodine.
- Half equations with electrons written as products when they should be reactants, or with unequal total charge.
- Leaving spectator ions in an ionic equation, or splitting a precipitate into ions.
- Explaining a mass change as "mass destroyed" rather than "gas escaped" or "oxygen gained from the air".
- Comparing masses instead of moles to find the limiting reactant.
- Final answers with too many significant figures, or in ordinary form when the value is very small.
