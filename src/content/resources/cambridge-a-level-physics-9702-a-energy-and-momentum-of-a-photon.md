This guide covers subtopic **22.1 Energy and momentum of a photon** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Quantum Physics study guide](/resources/a-physics-quantum-physics/) introduces the whole of Topic 22 and states E = hf and p = E/c; this page goes further into the 22.1 outcomes alone: what "particulate nature" and "quantum" mean, converting fluently between joules, electronvolts, frequencies and wavelengths, counting photons in a beam, and using photon momentum to find the force exerted by light. For quick recall use the [Energy and momentum of a photon revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-energy-and-momentum-of-a-photon-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Energy and momentum of a photon practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-energy-and-momentum-of-a-photon-practice/).

## What 22.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists five outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- **understand that electromagnetic radiation has a particulate nature**
- **understand that a photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy**
- **recall and use E = hf**
- **use the electronvolt (eV) as a unit of energy**
- **understand that a photon has momentum and that the momentum is given by p = E/c**

## Particulate nature and quanta

At AS Level, electromagnetic radiation is treated as a wave: it diffracts, interferes and can be polarised. Topic 22 adds that radiation also behaves as a stream of particles. Energy is carried, emitted and absorbed in discrete packets, not continuously.

- A **quantum** is the smallest discrete amount of a quantity that can exist on its own.
- A **photon** is a quantum of electromagnetic energy: a single packet of energy hf.

So a beam of light of frequency f can carry energy hf, 2hf, 3hf and so on, but never 1.5hf. Brighter light of the same frequency means **more photons per second**, not bigger photons. The strongest evidence for this particulate behaviour, the photoelectric effect, is the subject of 22.2.

## E = hf and its partners

```
E = hf          h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s (Planck constant)
c = fλ   →   E = hc/λ
```

Energy is proportional to frequency and inversely proportional to wavelength. Across the electromagnetic spectrum:

| Radiation | Typical wavelength | Photon energy |
|---|---|---|
| Radio (100 MHz) | 3 m | 6.6 × 10⁻²⁶ J (about 4 × 10⁻⁷ eV) |
| Red light | 700 nm | 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (1.8 eV) |
| Violet light | 400 nm | 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (3.1 eV) |
| X-rays (50 keV) | 2.5 × 10⁻¹¹ m | 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ J |
| Gamma rays (1.2 MeV) | 1.0 × 10⁻¹² m | 1.9 × 10⁻¹³ J |

Visible photons carry a few electronvolts; this is the same order of energy as the energies involved in electron transitions in atoms (22.4), which is why visible light interacts so strongly with matter.

## The electronvolt

The **electronvolt** is the energy transferred when an electron moves through a potential difference of one volt:

```
1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
```

It follows from W = QV with Q = e and V = 1 V. Conversions:

- **eV → J**: multiply by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
- **J → eV**: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
- **keV and MeV**: 1 keV = 10³ eV, 1 MeV = 10⁶ eV.

An electron accelerated from rest through 5.0 V gains 5.0 eV, which is 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. An alpha particle (charge 2e) accelerated through the same p.d. gains 10 eV.

A useful shortcut: with h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, **hc ≈ 1.24 × 10⁻⁶ eV m**, so the energy in eV is about 1240 divided by the wavelength in nanometres. Use it to check answers, but show full working with SI values in the exam.

## Photon momentum

A photon has no rest mass, but it does carry momentum:

```
p = E / c
```

Combining with E = hc/λ gives p = h/λ, which is the starting point for the de Broglie relation in 22.3. Photon momenta are tiny: a red-light photon has p ≈ 1 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m s⁻¹.

### The force exerted by light

When a beam of power P is **absorbed** by a surface, each second the beam delivers energy P and therefore momentum P/c. Rate of change of momentum is force (Newton's second law), so:

```
force on an absorbing surface = P / c
force on a perfectly reflecting surface = 2P / c
```

The reflecting case doubles because each photon's momentum is reversed: the change is from +p to −p, which is 2p.

## Worked example 1: a laser pointer

A laser pointer emits 1.0 mW of light of wavelength 650 nm. Calculate **(a)** the energy of one photon in J and in eV, **(b)** the number of photons emitted per second, **(c)** the momentum of one photon, **(d)** the force on a target that absorbs the whole beam.

```
(a) E = hc/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 650 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    in eV: 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.91 eV

(b) N per second = P / E = 1.0 × 10⁻³ / 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.3 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹

(c) p = E/c = 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 1.02 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m s⁻¹

(d) F = P/c = 1.0 × 10⁻³ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 3.3 × 10⁻¹² N
    (check: N × p = 3.27 × 10¹⁵ × 1.02 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.3 × 10⁻¹² N)
```

*Annotation.* A milliwatt beam contains trillions of photons per second, which is why light normally appears continuous. The force check in (d) shows the two routes (power over c, or number per second times momentum each) agree.

## Worked example 2: an X-ray photon

An X-ray photon has energy 50 keV. Calculate its energy in joules, its frequency, its wavelength and its momentum.

```
E = 50 × 10³ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ J
f = E/h = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 1.2 × 10¹⁹ Hz
λ = c/f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 1.21 × 10¹⁹ = 2.5 × 10⁻¹¹ m
p = E/c = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 2.7 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹
```

*Annotation.* Convert the prefix (kilo) and the unit (eV to J) in one line before anything else. The X-ray photon has about 26 000 times the energy and momentum of the red photon in Worked example 1.

## Worked example 3: pressure of sunlight

Take the intensity of sunlight reaching a spacecraft as 1.4 kW m⁻². Estimate the force on a flat, perfectly reflecting sail of area 100 m² facing the Sun.

```
Power on sail P = 1.4 × 10³ × 100 = 1.4 × 10⁵ W
F = 2P/c = 2 × 1.4 × 10⁵ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 9.3 × 10⁻⁴ N
```

*Annotation.* The force is under a millinewton, yet it acts continuously and needs no fuel. The factor 2 is for reflection; an absorbing sail would feel half this force.

## Common mistakes

- **Using E = hf with wavelength** in place of frequency. Use E = hc/λ for wavelength.
- **Forgetting the eV conversion** or applying it twice.
- **Writing nm as 10⁻⁶ m.** A nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m.
- **Saying brighter light has more energetic photons.** Intensity changes the number per second, not the energy each.
- **Writing p = mv for a photon.** A photon has no rest mass; use p = E/c.
- **Forgetting the factor 2** for a reflecting surface.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (what is meant by a photon): a quantum (discrete packet) of electromagnetic energy, of energy hf.
- **Define** (the electronvolt): the energy gained (transferred) when an electron moves through a p.d. of one volt.
- **Calculate**: write E = hf or E = hc/λ, convert units, and give the final unit; give eV answers only when asked.
- **Show that**: give one more significant figure than the value quoted in the question.
- **Explain** (why a beam exerts a force): photons carry momentum; absorption (or reflection) changes their momentum; the rate of change of momentum is the force.
