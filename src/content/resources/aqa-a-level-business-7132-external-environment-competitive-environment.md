This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.7 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: the competitive environment** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **Porter's five forces, how and why these might change, and the implications of these forces for strategic and functional decision making and profits**
- an understanding of the five forces to include **entry threat (barriers to entry), buyer power, supplier power, rivalry and substitute threat**
- how **the five forces shape competitive strategy**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## The idea behind the model

Michael Porter argued that the profit potential of an industry depends on the strength of five competitive forces. The stronger the forces, the more profit is competed away to customers (through lower prices), suppliers (through higher input costs) or rivals (through higher costs of competing). A business that understands the forces can choose where and how to compete, and which forces to reduce.

The model is an industry-level tool. It tells you why some industries (such as pharmaceutical patents) are generally profitable and others (such as budget airlines) are tough, and why a firm's own position within an industry matters.

## The five forces

### 1. Threat of new entrants (barriers to entry)

New firms add capacity and compete for customers, which reduces prices and profit. The threat is lower when **barriers to entry** are high:

- **economies of scale** that a new firm cannot match
- **high start-up costs** (plants, equipment, advertising)
- **brand loyalty** and customer switching costs
- **access to distribution channels** controlled by existing firms
- **legal barriers**, such as patents, licences and regulation
- **know-how** and experience
- **the likelihood of retaliation** by existing firms, such as price cuts.

The threat is higher when entry is cheap, for example when online selling needs little capital.

### 2. Buyer power

Powerful buyers force prices down, demand higher quality or better terms, and play suppliers against each other. Buyer power is high when:

- few buyers account for a large share of the supplier's sales
- products are standardised, so buyers can easily switch
- switching costs are low
- buyers have information (price comparison websites) and could produce the product themselves ("backward integration")
- the product is a small part of the buyer's costs (it makes little difference), or a big part (they bargain hard).

### 3. Supplier power

Powerful suppliers can raise prices or reduce quality. Supplier power is high when:

- there are few suppliers, or one supplies something unique
- switching costs are high
- the supplier could move into the buyer's market ("forward integration")
- the industry is not an important customer for the supplier
- there are no substitutes for the input.

### 4. Rivalry among existing competitors

Intense rivalry leads to price wars, heavy advertising and rapid product launches, all of which cut profit. Rivalry is high when:

- there are many competitors of similar size
- industry growth is slow, so firms can grow only by taking share
- products are undifferentiated
- fixed costs are high, so firms need to run at full capacity
- exit barriers are high (specialised assets, redundancy costs), so weak firms stay.

### 5. Threat of substitutes

Substitutes are different products that meet the same need, for example video calls instead of business flights. The threat is high when substitutes offer a similar benefit at a lower price or higher quality and the cost of switching is low. Substitutes put a ceiling on the prices an industry can charge.

## Summary table

| Force | High when | Effect on industry profit | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry threat | Low barriers | Lower prices, lower profit | Build brand, scale, patents; make switching costly |
| Buyer power | Few large buyers, easy switching | Prices pushed down | Widen customer base; differentiate; loyalty schemes |
| Supplier power | Few suppliers, high switching cost | Input costs rise | Multiple suppliers; long-term contracts; vertical integration |
| Rivalry | Many similar firms; slow growth | Price wars | Differentiate; avoid direct competition |
| Substitutes | Close alternatives | Price ceiling | Improve value; extend into the substitutes |

## Worked example: Fenwick Biscuits

Fenwick Biscuits sells £10m of biscuits a year, 40% of it to one supermarket chain, and makes £1m operating profit.

**Buyer power.** The supermarket demands a 5% price cut on its purchases.

- Revenue lost: 40% × £10m × 5% = **£200,000**
- Because costs do not fall when the price is cut, this comes straight from profit: £1m − £0.2m = **£800,000**, a fall of **20%**.

**Supplier power.** A key ingredient costs £15 of a £45 unit cost, with a selling price of £50. The supplier raises its price 8%.

- Extra cost per unit: £15 × 8% = **£1.20**
- Margin per unit falls from £5 to **£3.80**, a fall of 24%.

**Rivalry and entry.** The biscuit market is growing slowly, there are many similar brands, and supermarket own-label products are close substitutes.

**Implications for strategy.** Fenwick is under pressure from three forces: powerful buyers, powerful suppliers, and strong rivalry. It could:

- develop **differentiated products** (for example a premium range) to reduce buyer power and rivalry
- **widen its customer base** (cafes, export, online) so one buyer is less than 40% of sales
- **find alternative ingredient suppliers** or sign long-term contracts to cap input prices
- **invest in brand** so shoppers insist on Fenwick in-store.

**Functional implications:** marketing builds the brand; operations raises efficiency to protect margins; finance prepares for lower profit; purchasing (part of operations) negotiates.

## How and why the forces might change

The forces are not fixed. Change comes from:

- **Technology:** the internet cuts barriers to entry in retail but raises buyer power through price comparison.
- **Regulation:** deregulation opens markets to new entrants; patent expiry opens the market to generics.
- **Economic change:** a recession increases rivalry as firms fight for fewer customers.
- **Mergers and growth:** consolidation among buyers or suppliers raises their power; a firm's own growth can strengthen its position.
- **Customer behaviour:** loyalty changes and switching becomes easier.
- **Globalisation:** foreign competitors enter the market.

A thorough analysis considers whether the forces are rising or falling, not just their current level.

## Implications for decisions and profits

- **Strategy:** choose markets where forces are weak (see Ansoff and Porter's generic strategies in section 3.8); decide whether to compete on cost, differentiation or focus; consider acquiring suppliers or buyers.
- **Marketing:** differentiation and branding reduce rivalry and buyer power.
- **Operations:** cost reduction improves resilience; supplier management reduces supplier power.
- **Finance:** profit forecasts reflect the pressure on prices and costs.
- **Human resources:** skills as a barrier to entry and a source of advantage.

The overall effect on **profit** depends on the balance of forces. An industry with high barriers, fragmented buyers and suppliers, differentiated products and few substitutes is likely to be very profitable.

## Evaluating the model

**Strengths:** a structured way of analysing the industry; covers customers, suppliers and potential competitors, not only current rivals; links directly to strategy.

**Limitations:**

- It is a snapshot, while industries change quickly.
- Industry boundaries are hard to draw (are streaming services rivals of cinemas?).
- It ignores government, complementary products and cooperation between firms.
- It does not measure the forces. Judging "high" or "low" is subjective.
- It focuses on the industry, not the particular strengths of the firm.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** choose the force that matters most in the case, say why it is strong, and trace the effect on prices, costs and profit.
- **Evaluate:** compare the forces, say which is most important and why, and consider how they might change.
- Quote evidence from the case: the number of buyers, the level of start-up costs, the share of sales.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing suppliers with buyers.
- Listing the five forces with no application.
- Calling every force "high".
- Treating substitutes as the same product from another firm; substitutes meet the same need in a different way.
- Not drawing a conclusion about profit or strategy.
