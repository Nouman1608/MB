## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why the Daewongun followed a policy of isolation. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract, which is *constructed for practice* and is not a real quotation.

> "The peasants did not rise because of a foreign book. They rose because the magistrate took their rice and the clerks took their seed. The teaching only gave them a name and a faith." (A historian's summary, constructed for practice)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the causes of the Donghak Peasant Revolution. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of the view that only local grievances caused the revolution. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the impact of Japanese assimilation policies on Korean society in the 1930s and early 1940s. **[6]**

**4.** To what extent was the Treaty of Ganghwa (1876) the main reason for the loss of Korean independence by 1910? **[15]**

**5.** Evaluate the impact of Japanese rule on the Korean economy between 1910 and 1945. **[15]**

**6.** Explain the significance of the March First Movement (1919). **[6]**

**7.** Explain two consequences of the Gapsin Coup (1884) for Korea's position in the region. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the reason, 1 for explanation). Fear of Western influence on Confucian society, shown in the persecution of Catholics; the wish to keep the new, weaker monarchy secure while he rebuilt royal authority; the experience of China, defeated by Britain, which suggested the danger of contact; the French and American attacks of 1866 and 1871, which he saw as threats that could be repelled. **[4]**

**2.** **(a)** The extract suggests that the revolution had local, economic causes (1): corrupt officials taking rice and seed (1). It suggests that the religious teaching of Donghak provided an identity and organisation rather than the root cause (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Award up to 3. The revolution also had wider causes: resentment of foreign merchants and Japanese influence, and Donghak's own demands for social equality and the removal of foreigners (1); the weak central government and Sedo politics created conditions in which local abuses were not checked (1); the movement's national spread after the Gobu uprising, and its organisation by Jeon Bong-jun, suggest more than a local response (1). **[3]**

**3.** Marking points (up to 6): the requirement to visit Shinto shrines and show loyalty to the emperor conflicted with Korean beliefs (up to 1); Korean was removed from schools and public life, and from 1940 Koreans were pressed to take Japanese names (up to 2); Korean newspapers were closed, and education stressed Japanese identity (up to 1); the policies provoked resistance and hardened nationalism, as well as some accommodation by those who sought advancement (up to 1); the policies fitted wartime mobilisation (up to 1). **[6]**

**4.** Marking points (up to 15):
- A thesis and criteria for "main reason" (up to 2).
- Ganghwa: opened ports to Japan, extraterritoriality, ended Korea's isolation, treated Korea as independent of China (up to 3).
- Later stages: Imo Incident, Gapsin Coup, the Sino-Japanese War of 1894 to 1895, the Russo-Japanese War, the Eulsa Treaty of 1905, annexation in 1910 (up to 4).
- Internal weakness: Sedo politics, financial limits, factional struggle, the failure of the Gwangmu reforms to build enough strength (up to 3).
- Perspectives: historians who stress Japanese imperialism and those who stress Korean internal weakness (up to 1).
- A reasoned judgement that ranks the factors (up to 2). **[15]**

**5.** Marking points (up to 15):
- A thesis addressing both gains and costs (up to 2).
- Developments: land survey, railways, ports, banking, industry in the north in the 1930s, higher rice output (up to 4).
- Costs: land loss and tenancy, rice exports to Japan, profits flowing to Japanese owners, wartime exploitation and forced labour (up to 4).
- Who benefited: Japanese settlers and firms, some Korean landlords and businessmen, but most farmers did not (up to 2).
- Perspectives: "colonial modernisation" against exploitation, and "colonial modernity" (up to 1).
- A judgement consistent with the evidence (up to 2). **[15]**

**6.** Marking points (up to 6): it was the largest peaceful protest against Japanese rule, with participants from many classes, religions and regions (up to 2); it led Japan to alter its policy, moving to "cultural rule" while still using force (up to 1); it led to the Provisional Government in Shanghai, which gave nationalists an organisation (up to 1); it became a lasting symbol of national identity, though it did not achieve independence (up to 2). **[6]**

**7.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the consequence, 1 for explanation). The failure of the coup showed that Qing troops under Yuan Shikai could decide Korean politics, which began the Chinese decade of 1885 to 1894; Japan's influence fell for a time, and it agreed with China in the Tianjin Convention (1885) that both would withdraw troops, which later allowed either to send forces back if the other did; the radical reformers were killed or fled (Kim Ok-gyun went to Japan), so the reform movement lost its leaders and conservatives were strengthened; the rivalry of the powers deepened, with Britain briefly occupying Port Hamilton and Russia gaining attention. **[4]**

## Frequently asked questions

**What counts as a "reason" for loss of independence?**
Anything that explains why Korea could not resist: internal weakness, great-power rivalry and Japanese strength. Rank them.

**Should I include the division of Korea in 1945?**
Yes, briefly, as the end of the period, but the main marks are for the years 1840 to 1945.
