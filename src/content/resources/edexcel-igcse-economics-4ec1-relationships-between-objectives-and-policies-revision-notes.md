These notes condense section 2.1.3 a of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026: the impact of policies and the trade-off between macroeconomic objectives. Fuller explanations and worked examples are in the [study guide on relationships between objectives and policies](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-relationships-between-objectives-and-policies/); test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-relationships-between-objectives-and-policies-practice/). All figures are invented.

## The four named trade-offs

| Pair | The conflict in one line |
|---|---|
| Unemployment and inflation | More spending creates jobs, but as spare workers run out, wages and prices rise |
| Economic growth and inflation | Demand growing faster than capacity causes shortages and rising prices |
| Economic growth and environmental protection | More output uses more resources and creates more pollution; protecting the environment raises costs and can slow growth |
| Inflation and the current account | Prices rising faster than abroad make exports dearer and imports relatively cheaper, so the current account worsens |

**Root cause:** demand-side policies change total spending, and spending moves output, jobs, prices and imports together. A policy cannot choose only the good effects.

## Policy-by-policy summary

| Policy | Helps | Harms |
|---|---|---|
| Expansionary fiscal (raise spending, cut taxes) | Growth, unemployment | Inflation, current account, environment; fiscal deficit widens |
| Contractionary fiscal (cut spending, raise taxes) | Inflation, current account | Growth, unemployment |
| Interest rate cut / asset purchases | Growth, unemployment | Inflation, current account |
| Interest rate rise | Inflation, current account (through lower import spending) | Growth, unemployment |
| Environmental tax | Environment | Growth (higher costs); inflation (cost-push) |
| Regulation, fines, pollution permits | Environment | Growth; possibly jobs and prices |
| Supply-side policy | Can help growth, unemployment, inflation and the current account together | Slow to work; costly; some (deregulation) may weaken environmental protection |

## Chains to learn

**Interest rate rise:** borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding → consumer spending and investment fall → demand-pull pressure eases → inflation falls → firms cut output and hiring → unemployment rises → fewer imports bought → current account improves.

**Tax cut:** disposable income rises → consumer spending rises → firms expand and hire → unemployment falls → near full capacity, prices rise → more imports bought → current account worsens.

**Environmental tax:** cost of polluting rises → firms cut emissions or switch methods → environment improves → higher costs are passed on in prices → cost-push inflation; some firms cut output → growth slows.

**Training or infrastructure:** productivity and capacity rise → output grows without the same price pressure → exports become more competitive → several objectives improve, but only after years.

## Worked numbers (from the study guide, invented data)

- **Interest rate rise from 2% to 4%:** inflation 6.0% → 3.5% (down 2.5 percentage points); growth 4.0% → 1.5% (down 2.5 points); unemployment 4.0% → 5.5% (up 1.5 points); current account -3.0% → -2.0% of GDP.
- **Energy tax:** energy has a 10% weight in the CPI and its price rises 8%, so it adds 0.10 x 8 = 0.8 percentage points to inflation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Trade-off | Improving one objective makes another worse |
| Expansionary policy | Policy that raises total spending in the economy |
| Contractionary policy | Policy that reduces total spending in the economy |
| Demand-pull inflation | Prices rise because total demand grows faster than supply |
| Cost-push inflation | Prices rise because firms' costs rise, for example from taxes or dearer energy |
| Spare capacity | Unused workers and machinery, which let output rise without much inflation |
| Policy mix | Using two or more policies together to balance objectives |
| Time lag | The delay between a policy change and its effect |
| Percentage point | The simple difference between two percentages, such as 6% to 3.5% = 2.5 points |
| Asset purchasing | Central bank buying of bonds to increase the money supply, working like an interest rate cut |

## Deciding factors for evaluation

1. **The main problem.** The objective furthest from target usually takes priority.
2. **Type of inflation.** Contractionary policy suits demand-pull; with cost-push, cutting demand costs many jobs for a small fall in prices.
3. **Spare capacity / stage of the economic cycle.** In a recession, expansion raises output with little inflation; in a boom, it mostly raises prices.
4. **Speed needed.** Interest rates act faster than supply-side measures.
5. **Size of the change.** A small rise in interest rates may barely move spending.
6. **Exchange rate.** Higher interest rates may raise the exchange rate, making exports dearer and offsetting the current account gain.
7. **Combination.** A policy mix can control inflation now and build capacity for later.

## Exam traps

- Saying "inflation and unemployment both go up" with no mechanism: the trade-off is that cutting one tends to raise the other.
- Claiming an interest rate rise worsens the current account through lower inflation. In the specification's trade-off it is **higher** inflation that worsens the current account.
- Treating environmental policy as costless: taxes and regulations raise costs and can add to inflation.
- Praising supply-side policy with no reference to time lags or the cost to the budget.
- Quoting a percentage change when the question compares two rates.
- Ending an "Evaluate" answer without saying what the conclusion depends on.

## Self-test

1. Name the four trade-offs listed in section 2.1.3 a.
2. Why do demand-side policies create trade-offs?
3. State two effects of a cut in income tax on macroeconomic objectives.
4. Explain why an interest rate rise can improve the current account.
5. Give one reason the current account gain from higher interest rates might be smaller than expected.
6. How can an environmental tax raise inflation?
7. Why is supply-side policy said to ease trade-offs?
8. Give two limitations of supply-side policy when inflation is high now.
9. Unemployment falls from 7% to 4.5%. State the change in percentage points.
10. Food has a 20% weight in a price index and food prices rise 5%. Calculate the contribution to inflation.
11. Why would contractionary policy be a costly response to cost-push inflation?
12. What is a policy mix? Give one example.

## Answers

1. Unemployment and inflation; economic growth and inflation; economic growth and environmental protection; inflation and the current account.
2. They change total spending, which moves output, jobs, prices and imports together, so they cannot improve one objective without affecting the others.
3. For example: unemployment falls because higher disposable income raises spending and firms hire; inflation may rise as demand outpaces supply (also: imports rise, current account worsens).
4. Households and firms spend less, including on imports, so import spending falls; lower inflation also keeps exports price competitive.
5. Higher interest rates can attract money from abroad and raise the exchange rate, making exports dearer and imports cheaper.
6. It raises firms' costs, and firms pass some of the cost on in higher prices (cost-push inflation).
7. It raises productive capacity, so output and jobs can grow without the same pressure on prices, and exports can become more competitive.
8. Time lags (training and infrastructure take years) and cost (it widens the fiscal deficit).
9. A fall of 2.5 percentage points.
10. 0.20 x 5 = 1 percentage point.
11. Prices are rising because costs rose, not because demand is too high, so cutting demand lowers prices only slowly while causing large falls in output and jobs.
12. Using more than one policy together to balance objectives, for example raising interest rates to cut inflation while spending on training to raise future productivity.
