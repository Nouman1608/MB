## Following the carbon

| Stage | Site | Carbon in | Carbon out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | Cytoplasm | Glucose (6C) | 2 × pyruvate (3C) |
| Link reaction | Matrix | Pyruvate (3C) | Acetyl (2C) on coenzyme A + CO₂ |
| Krebs cycle | Matrix | Acetyl (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) | 2 CO₂; oxaloacetate regenerated |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Inner membrane | No carbon involved | — |

### Glycolysis in more detail

1. Glucose is phosphorylated twice using **2 ATP**, giving **fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C)**. Phosphorylation makes the sugar more reactive and traps it in the cell.
2. It splits into **two triose phosphate (3C)** molecules.
3. Each triose phosphate is **oxidised** by removal of hydrogen, which reduces NAD.
4. Each is converted to **pyruvate (3C)**, producing **2 ATP** by substrate-linked phosphorylation.

Per glucose: 4 ATP made, 2 used, **net 2 ATP**, and **2 reduced NAD**.

### The link reaction

When oxygen is available, pyruvate moves into the mitochondrial matrix. Each pyruvate is **decarboxylated** (CO₂ removed) and **dehydrogenated** (hydrogen passed to NAD). The remaining 2C acetyl group joins **coenzyme A**, forming acetyl coenzyme A, which carries the acetyl group into the Krebs cycle.

### The Krebs cycle

1. Acetyl coenzyme A passes its 2C group to **oxaloacetate (4C)**, forming **citrate (6C)**; coenzyme A is released for reuse.
2. Citrate is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated to a **5C** compound (CO₂ out, NAD reduced).
3. The 5C compound is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated to a **4C** compound (CO₂ out, NAD reduced); one ATP forms by substrate-linked phosphorylation.
4. The 4C compound is converted back to oxaloacetate in further small steps, with more dehydrogenations: one reduces **FAD**, another reduces NAD.

Per turn: 2 CO₂, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP.

### Worked example 1: totals per glucose

Two pyruvates are made, so the link reaction and the Krebs cycle each run **twice** per glucose.

| Stage | CO₂ | Reduced NAD | Reduced FAD | ATP (substrate-linked, net) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | 0 | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Link reaction (× 2) | 2 | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| Krebs cycle (× 2) | 4 | 6 | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | **6** | **10** | **2** | **4** |

Six CO₂ account for all six carbons of glucose. Only 4 ATP come directly from the pathway; most energy is still held in the 10 reduced NAD and 2 reduced FAD, which is why oxidative phosphorylation matters so much.

## NAD, FAD and oxidative phosphorylation

**NAD** and **FAD** are coenzymes. They accept hydrogen atoms removed by dehydrogenase enzymes, becoming reduced, and carry them to the carriers in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**. There they are oxidised and return to accept more hydrogen.

The chain of causes in oxidative phosphorylation:

1. Hydrogen atoms from reduced NAD and FAD **split into protons (H⁺) and energetic electrons**.
2. The electrons pass along the **electron transport chain** of carriers, **releasing energy** at each step.
3. That energy is used to **transfer protons** from the matrix into the intermembrane space, building a high proton concentration there.
4. Protons **return** to the matrix by **facilitated diffusion through ATP synthase**; their movement provides energy to make ATP from ADP and Pi (**chemiosmosis**).
5. At the end of the chain, **oxygen is the final electron acceptor**: it combines with electrons and protons to form **water**.

### What happens if a link is broken?

If oxygen is absent, electrons cannot leave the chain, so the chain stops. Reduced NAD and FAD cannot be oxidised, so there is no NAD or FAD to accept hydrogen; the Krebs cycle and link reaction stop. Only glycolysis can continue, and only if NAD is regenerated by fermentation. Questions on poisons that block the chain follow the same logic.

## Mitochondria: structure and function

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Outer membrane | Separates the mitochondrion from the cytoplasm; lets pyruvate in |
| Inner membrane folded into **cristae** | Large surface area for electron transport chains and ATP synthase |
| Inner membrane impermeable to protons except at ATP synthase | Maintains the proton gradient |
| Narrow intermembrane space | Proton concentration builds up quickly |
| Matrix | Contains enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle, plus DNA and ribosomes |

Active cells such as muscle have mitochondria with more densely packed cristae.

## Anaerobic respiration

| | Lactate fermentation (mammals) | Ethanol fermentation (yeast; plant roots) |
|---|---|---|
| Pyruvate converted to | Lactate | Ethanal (CO₂ released), then ethanol |
| Reduced NAD used to | Reduce pyruvate | Reduce ethanal |
| CO₂ produced? | No | Yes |
| Purpose | Regenerate NAD so glycolysis continues | Regenerate NAD so glycolysis continues |

**Why aerobic yield is much greater**: anaerobically, ATP comes only from glycolysis (net 2 per glucose), and most of the energy stays in lactate or ethanol. Aerobically, pyruvate is completely oxidised to CO₂ and water, and the hydrogen in the 10 reduced NAD and 2 reduced FAD drives oxidative phosphorylation, which makes far more ATP.

## Rice in flooded fields

The syllabus limits this to three adaptations:

- **Aerenchyma**: tissue in stems and roots with large air spaces, through which oxygen diffuses from the air down to submerged root cells.
- **Ethanol fermentation in roots**: root cells can respire anaerobically and tolerate higher ethanol concentrations than most plants.
- **Faster growth of stems**: as water rises, stems elongate quickly so that leaves stay above the surface, where they can exchange gases.

## Investigating rate with redox indicators

DCPIP and methylene blue are blue when oxidised and colourless when reduced. Dehydrogenases in respiring yeast remove hydrogen from the substrate; the dye accepts it and decolourises. The faster respiration is, the sooner the blue disappears.

### Worked example 2: temperature and methylene blue

Equal volumes of yeast suspension, glucose solution and methylene blue were mixed at each temperature. The data are invented for practice.

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to decolourise / s | 600 | 280 | 150 | 220 | no change in 900 s |
| Rate (1000 ÷ time) / arbitrary units | 1.7 | 3.6 | 6.7 | 4.5 | 0 |

```
rate at 30 °C = 1000 ÷ 280 = 3.57 ≈ 3.6
```

Rate rises from 20 to 40 °C because molecules have more kinetic energy, so enzyme–substrate collisions are more frequent. Above about 40 °C, dehydrogenases and other enzymes begin to denature, so the rate falls; at 60 °C no respiration is detected. Controls: boiled yeast (shows decolourisation needs living cells); same volumes and concentrations; tubes not shaken, because oxygen from air re-oxidises methylene blue. A colour standard helps judge the end point consistently.

To vary **substrate concentration** instead, keep temperature constant and use a range of glucose concentrations; the rate rises and then levels off when another factor, such as the quantity of enzyme, limits it.

## Respirometers and temperature

A respirometer with potassium hydroxide measures oxygen uptake. Place sets in water baths at several temperatures, equilibrate each, take repeat readings over a fixed time, and calculate the rate in mm³ min⁻¹ using πr² × distance. Use a control tube at each temperature, because gas volume changes with temperature.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the Krebs cycle on the cristae or oxidative phosphorylation in the matrix.
- Forgetting that the link reaction and Krebs cycle run twice per glucose.
- Saying ATP synthase pumps protons; protons are moved by energy from electrons and **flow back** through ATP synthase.
- Saying lactate fermentation "produces ATP"; it regenerates NAD so that glycolysis can.
- Writing "oxygen is needed to make ATP" without its role as final electron acceptor.
- Using time, not 1/time, as the rate.
