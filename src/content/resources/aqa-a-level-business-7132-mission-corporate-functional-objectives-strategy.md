This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.1 Mission, corporate objectives, functional objectives and strategy** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **influences on the mission of a business**
- **internal and external influences on corporate objectives and decisions**, including the pressures for short-termism, business ownership, and the external and internal environment
- **the links between mission, corporate objectives and strategy**
- **the distinction between strategy and tactics**
- **the impact of strategic decision making on functional decision making**
- **internal and external influences on functional objectives and decisions**
- **the value of SWOT analysis**.

All three A-level papers can assess this content, so expect it in multiple-choice, short-answer, data-response and essay questions. All businesses and figures on this page are invented.

## The cascade from mission to tactics

Strategic planning works as a chain. Each link must make sense in the light of the one above it.

| Level | Question it answers | Time scale | Example for a fictional cycle maker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mission | Why do we exist? | Very long term | "Make cycling the easiest way to get around any town" |
| Corporate objectives | What overall results do we want? | 3 to 10 years | Reach 15% of the UK commuter bike market; 8% return on capital employed |
| Strategy | How will we achieve those objectives? | Several years | Launch an electric range; move into Germany |
| Functional objectives | What must each department achieve? | 1 to 3 years | Operations: cut unit cost 6%; Marketing: raise brand awareness to 40% |
| Tactics | What will we do this month or quarter? | Days to months | A discount weekend; a new supplier for batteries |

A **mission** is a statement of the purpose and values of the business. It is not usually measurable. A **corporate objective** is a measurable target for the whole business. A **strategy** is a medium- to long-term plan that commits significant resources to achieve those objectives. **Functional objectives** are targets for finance, marketing, operations and human resources, set so that, if each is met, the corporate objectives are met too.

## Influences on the mission

Missions rarely change often, but they are shaped by several things:

- **Founders and owners.** A founder's values may define the mission for decades. A family business may stress longevity; a plc may stress growth for investors.
- **Culture and history.** What the business has always done well, and what its staff believe it stands for.
- **Stakeholders.** Customers, employees, communities and campaign groups may all expect the mission to reflect their concerns, including sustainability.
- **The market.** A business in a fast-changing market, such as digital services, may write a broad mission so it does not have to change it when products change.
- **Resources and capabilities.** A mission that the business cannot deliver is empty and damages credibility.

A mission also does work. It guides decisions when no rule applies, it helps with recruitment, and it gives customers a reason to choose the business. Its weakness is vagueness: "to be the best" tells managers very little.

## Influences on corporate objectives

### Internal influences

- **Ownership.** Shareholders of a plc expect returns, so objectives often include profit, dividends and share price. A social enterprise sets objectives for social impact. A sole trader may be content with a steady income.
- **Resources.** Finance, skills and technology limit what can be targeted. A business with little cash cannot set an objective of opening twenty new stores.
- **Culture and leadership.** A risk-taking leader sets bolder targets than a cautious one.
- **Past performance.** Objectives are usually set relative to what has been achieved.

### External influences

- **The economy.** Weak demand makes growth targets harder; a boom encourages them.
- **Competitors.** A rival's move may force a business to set market share targets.
- **Legislation and regulation.** Environmental rules may add an objective such as a cut in emissions.
- **Technology and social change.** Customers moving online may lead to an objective on online sales.

### Pressures for short-termism

**Short-termism** means giving more weight to quick results than to long-term success. It arises when:

- shareholders and analysts judge the company on quarterly or half-yearly results
- directors' bonuses depend on current-year profit or share price
- a low share price exposes the company to a takeover bid
- lenders demand quick repayment.

The effects can be damaging. A business might cut research spending to lift this year's profit, or delay investment in staff training. The long-term cost may be lost innovation. Businesses that are privately owned, or have patient owners, find it easier to set long-term objectives. A fair evaluation notes that some pressure for short-term results is healthy because it prevents waste and drift.

## Links between mission, objectives and strategy

The test of a good link is consistency. If a company's mission is "affordable healthy food for every family", an objective to double profit margins by moving to premium products conflicts with it. Where a strategy contradicts the mission, staff and customers notice, and trust falls.

Strategy should also follow from the objectives. If the objective is 15% market share in five years from 9% now, a strategy that only maintains the current product range is unlikely to deliver.

## Strategy and tactics

| | Strategy | Tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | The whole business or a major part of it | One function or one situation |
| Time | Medium to long term | Short term |
| Resources | Large commitments, often hard to reverse | Small, flexible |
| Who decides | Senior managers and the board | Middle and junior managers |
| Example | Enter a new overseas market | Run a two-week social media promotion in that market |

Tactics are how a strategy is carried out day to day. They can be changed quickly if they do not work. Strategic decisions are harder and costlier to reverse. A common error is to call any important decision "strategic". The test is scale, time and the commitment of resources.

## The impact of strategy on functional decisions

A strategy sets the agenda for every department.

**Worked example.** The cycle maker adopts a strategy of launching an electric range and entering Germany.

- **Operations** must source batteries, adapt the factory and possibly outsource assembly. Objective: unit cost of the electric model within 12% of the standard model by year two.
- **Marketing** must research German buyers, adapt branding and build a dealer network. Objective: 25% brand awareness among target German commuters in year two.
- **Finance** must fund the investment through retained profit or a loan. Objective: keep gearing under 40%.
- **Human resources** must recruit staff with battery and German-market skills and train existing workers. Objective: fill 90% of new technical roles within six months.

If any department fails, the strategy suffers. Functional objectives should therefore be consistent with each other. A marketing objective of rapid growth is unrealistic if operations cannot raise output.

## Influences on functional objectives and decisions

- **Internal:** the corporate strategy, budgets, the skills and morale of the workforce, and the capacity of the other functions.
- **External:** customers' expectations, competitors' actions, supplier reliability, employment law, interest rates and technology.

For example, an HR objective on pay is shaped by the labour market (external) and by the profit the business can afford to distribute (internal).

## The value of SWOT analysis

**SWOT** sorts information into internal **strengths** and **weaknesses** and external **opportunities** and **threats**. It is used to link the internal position to the external environment, and to choose strategies that exploit strengths and opportunities while limiting weaknesses and threats.

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| Internal | Strength: loyal customers; skilled staff | Weakness: ageing factory; one major supplier |
| External | Opportunity: grants for electric transport | Threat: new low-cost imports |

**Value:** it is quick and cheap, forces managers to consider both inside and outside the business, can involve many staff, and gives a clear starting point for objectives.

**Limits:**

- It is a list, not an analysis. It does not rank issues or show how they connect.
- It is subjective, so two managers may produce different SWOTs from the same facts.
- It captures one moment, while the environment changes.
- Items can be placed in the wrong box. A strength in one market can be a weakness in another.
- It does not tell managers what to do.

Its value rises when each item is backed by data, ranked by importance, and used to set specific objectives.

## Exam technique

- **Define or state:** give the precise meaning, for example "a strategy is a medium- to long-term plan that commits significant resources".
- **Analyse:** build a chain of reasoning. For short-termism: shareholders judge results quarterly, so directors cut research spending, so fewer new products, so lower long-term growth.
- **Assess or evaluate:** weigh the effect, say what the effect depends on (for example how patient the owners are), and reach a judgement about this business.

Always refer to the business in the case. Marks for application are lost when answers could be about any firm.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the mission as a measurable target.
- Confusing strategy with tactics, or calling every big decision strategic.
- Writing that short-termism is always bad.
- Listing SWOT items without linking them to a decision.
- Describing functional objectives without explaining how they follow from the strategy.
- Ignoring ownership when asked what influences objectives.
