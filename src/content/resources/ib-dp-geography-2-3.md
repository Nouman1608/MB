This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 2.3 of Option B, Oceans and coastal margins, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): managing coastal margins. The [2.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-managing-coastal-margins-revision-notes/) already explain how each hard and soft measure works, the four shoreline management policy options, integrated coastal zone management, a simple benefit-cost ratio, and cases at Mappleton, Medmerry and in the Netherlands. This page adds what you need to **evaluate** management at a higher level: why coastal margins create conflict between users, how whole-life costs change the comparison between strategies, how plans change over time, and two contrasting cases from tropical coasts, mangrove restoration and the Maldives. Practise with the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-2-3-practice/); the [Oceans and coastal margins study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-oceans-coastal-margins-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the pressures on coastal margins and the conflicts between users.
- Evaluate hard engineering, soft engineering and planning approaches.
- Evaluate how coastal management involves stakeholders and balances competing interests.

## Why coastal margins create conflict

The coastal margin is a narrow zone where many valuable uses compete for the same land and water. Each use has its own priorities, so a decision that helps one can harm another.

| User | Wants | Typical conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Residents and homeowners | Protection of property and access | With conservation groups over managed realignment |
| Tourism businesses | Wide, attractive beaches, clean water | With ports over dredging and with defences that spoil views |
| Ports and shipping | Deep, stable channels | With fishers and conservationists over dredging and reclamation |
| Fishers | Healthy nursery habitats and fish stocks | With aquaculture and development that removes mangroves or marsh |
| Farmers | Protected low-lying farmland | With habitat creation on their land |
| Conservation bodies | Natural processes and habitats | With almost any hard engineering |
| Governments | Value for money and safety | With communities who are told their area will not be defended |

When an exam asks you to discuss management, name at least two stakeholders, say **what each wants**, and explain **why** their aims clash in that place.

## Whole-life cost: the comparison changes over time

A benefit-cost ratio based only on construction cost can mislead, because strategies differ in how much they cost to keep working.

**Worked example (invented town, costs in millions of currency units over 50 years).** The assets protected are valued at 12.0.

| Option | Capital cost | Recurring cost | Whole-life cost (50 years) | Benefit-cost ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: sea wall | 4.0 | 0.1 a year maintenance | 4.0 + (0.1 x 50) = 9.0 | 12.0 / 9.0 = 1.33 |
| B: beach nourishment | 0 | 0.6 every 5 years, plus 0.5 monitoring in total | (0.6 x 10) + 0.5 = 6.5 | 12.0 / 6.5 = 1.85 |

On capital cost alone, nourishment looks almost free and the wall expensive. Over 50 years the gap narrows, but nourishment still has the better ratio. Three cautions belong in any evaluation of figures like these:

- **Future costs are uncertain.** If sea-level rise means nourishment must be repeated every three years rather than every five, its cost rises to about 17 campaigns, or roughly 10.2 plus monitoring, and the ratio falls below the wall's.
- **Some values are missing.** The wall may scour the beach that tourists use; nourishment may disturb the seabed where sand is dredged. Neither appears in the ratio.
- **Distribution is hidden.** A ratio says nothing about who pays and who benefits.

## Planning over time

Coastal plans increasingly set different policies for different time periods. In England and Wales, shoreline management plans set a policy for each stretch of coast over three periods, or **epochs**: up to 20 years, 20 to 50 years, and 50 to 100 years. A frontage might be "hold the line" for the first epoch, while people and infrastructure are given time to adapt, then "managed realignment" later. This approach:

- recognises that defences built today cannot be maintained forever as sea level rises;
- gives communities warning, though it can lower property values and cause distress once a future "no defence" policy is published;
- allows policy to be revised as evidence improves, a form of **adaptive management**.

Managed realignment can also provide **compensatory habitat**. Where defences elsewhere cause coastal squeeze, creating new intertidal habitat by realigning a different frontage can offset the loss.

## Tropical coasts: restoring mangroves

Mangrove forests line many tropical coasts and estuaries. Their dense roots slow water, trap sediment and absorb wave energy, while supporting fisheries and storing carbon. Large areas have been cleared for aquaculture ponds, farming and development. Restoration is now a common **nature-based** strategy.

Evaluation:

- **Strengths:** low cost compared with sea walls; benefits for fisheries and carbon storage; can keep pace with sea-level rise if sediment supply is enough; involves local communities in planting and protection.
- **Limitations:** seedlings take years to provide protection; planting fails where the site is wrong (too exposed, too deep or too saline), so restoration must restore the right water and sediment conditions, not only plant trees; mangroves reduce but do not stop large storm surges; pressure to clear land for ponds continues while aquaculture pays.

## Small island states: the Maldives

The Maldives is a nation of low-lying coral atolls, where most land lies only about a metre or so above sea level. It shows management choices in their sharpest form.

- **Hard protection of the capital.** After swell waves flooded parts of Male in 1987, a sea wall was built around the island with funding from Japan. It protects the densely populated capital but costs a great deal and could not be extended to the country's many inhabited islands.
- **Building higher land.** From 1997, a new island, **Hulhumale**, was reclaimed from the lagoon near Male using dredged sand and built up to around two metres above sea level, to house a growing population on higher ground.
- **Natural defences.** Healthy coral reefs break waves and supply the sand that keeps islands in place, so reef damage from warming and from coral and sand mining increases vulnerability.

The case shows that strategies depend on **scale and resources**. Hard defences can protect one critical island; across a whole nation, land raising, reef protection and, in the long term, relocation are part of the debate.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the view that soft engineering is more sustainable than hard engineering for managing coastal margins.* **(sample conclusion)**

"Soft approaches such as nourishment and mangrove restoration work with coastal processes, keep sediment in the system and support ecosystems, so in environmental terms they are usually more sustainable **[environmental criterion]**. However, they need repeated spending, and whole-life costs can rise sharply if sea-level rise shortens the time between nourishment campaigns **[economic criterion with a condition]**. Hard defences remain essential where dense, high-value settlement cannot move, as around Male **[located counter-argument]**. Sustainability therefore depends on the place: soft and planning approaches suit undeveloped or lightly developed frontages, while critical urban coasts need a mix **[supported judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Naming stakeholders without saying what each wants and why they conflict.
- Comparing strategies on capital cost alone.
- Treating a benefit-cost ratio as the final answer rather than one input to a decision.
- Describing mangrove restoration as simply planting trees.
- Writing about one temperate case only; examiners reward a contrasting setting.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a strategy with explicit criteria: effectiveness, whole-life cost, environmental impact, equity and adaptability to sea-level rise.
- **Discuss** management by setting out more than one stakeholder view before concluding.
- **To what extent** questions need a position that depends on the type of coast and the value at risk.
- Use the vocabulary precisely: managed realignment, epoch, compensatory habitat, nature-based solution.
