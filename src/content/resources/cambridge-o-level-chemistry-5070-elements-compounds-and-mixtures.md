Section 2.1 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has a single learning outcome: "Describe the differences between elements, compounds and mixtures." One line in the syllabus, but it is tested in several ways: classifying particle diagrams, classifying named substances, and using experimental evidence to decide what a sample is. This guide prepares you for each of those question styles.

The [Atomic Structure overview for O Level 5070](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-atomic-structure/) introduces the three categories alongside the rest of sections 2.2 and 2.3. Here the examples and questions are new, and the focus stays on 2.1.

## The three definitions

- An **element** is a substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by a chemical reaction.
- A **compound** is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio.
- A **mixture** contains two or more substances (elements, compounds or both) that are not chemically bonded together.

The words "substance", "chemically bonded" and "fixed ratio" carry the marks. Elements and compounds are both **pure substances**: every particle in the sample is the same. A mixture is not a pure substance.

## Five differences to learn

| Difference | Compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | fixed: always the same ratio of elements by mass and by number of atoms | variable: components can be present in any proportions |
| Properties | new properties, different from the elements it is made from | each component keeps its own properties |
| Forming it | a chemical reaction; usually an energy change (heat given out or taken in) | simply mixing; usually no significant energy change |
| Separating it | only by a chemical reaction (for example electrolysis) | physical methods such as filtration, evaporation, distillation, chromatography or a magnet |
| Formula | has a chemical formula, for example MgO | no single formula; described by naming its components |

Elements can be compared too: they contain one kind of atom, cannot be separated into anything simpler, and have their own fixed properties.

### Properties change completely in a compound

Sodium is a soft, highly reactive metal that reacts violently with water. Chlorine is a toxic, green gas. Bonded together, they form sodium chloride: a white, unreactive solid we sprinkle on food. Nothing about sodium chloride resembles either element. That is the clearest sign of a compound: its properties belong to the compound, not to its elements.

In a mixture, by contrast, you can still detect each component. Salt water tastes salty and still boils away to leave salt; sand in water still settles and can be filtered out.

### Melting and boiling behaviour

A pure substance, whether element or compound, melts and boils at a **fixed temperature**. Pure water boils at 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure. A mixture usually melts or boils over a **range** of temperatures, and the values depend on the proportions. Seawater, for example, boils above 100 °C, and its boiling point changes as the water boils away and the salt becomes more concentrated. This is a useful piece of evidence when a question asks how you could tell a pure compound from a mixture.

## Classifying particle diagrams

Many questions show boxes of circles, with different colours or shadings for different atoms. Ask two questions about each box.

1. **How many different kinds of particle (molecule or unbonded atom) are there?** One kind means a pure substance; more than one means a mixture.
2. **Is each particle made of one kind of atom or more than one?** One kind means an element; two or more bonded together means a compound.

**Worked example 1.** Classify each box.

| Box | What the box contains | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| A | separate single atoms, all the same kind | element (for example neon) |
| B | pairs of identical atoms joined together, all the same | element made of molecules (for example oxygen, O₂) |
| C | identical molecules, each one large atom joined to two small atoms of a different kind | compound (for example water, H₂O) |
| D | some pairs of identical atoms of one kind, some pairs of identical atoms of another kind | mixture of two elements (for example hydrogen and oxygen gases) |
| E | some molecules of one compound and some molecules of another compound | mixture of compounds (for example carbon dioxide and water vapour) |

Box B is the classic trap. Atoms joined together do not make a compound unless they are **different** atoms. Oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen and chlorine all exist as diatomic molecules, yet they are elements.

Box D is the other trap: a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen contains the same atoms as water, but they are not bonded to each other. Spark the mixture and it reacts to make water, a compound, giving out a large amount of energy.

## Classifying named substances

**Worked example 2.** Classify each substance as an element, a compound or a mixture: diamond, distilled water, brass, aqueous sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, ozone (O₃), crude oil.

*Answer.*
- **Diamond**: element. It is a giant structure, but every atom is carbon.
- **Distilled water**: compound. Only H₂O molecules are present.
- **Brass**: mixture. It is an alloy of copper and zinc, and the proportions can vary.
- **Aqueous sodium chloride**: mixture. A solution is a mixture of a solute and a solvent.
- **Magnesium oxide**: compound. Magnesium and oxygen are bonded in a fixed 1 : 1 ratio.
- **Ozone**: element. All three atoms in each molecule are oxygen.
- **Crude oil**: mixture of many different compounds.

Notice that alloys and solutions are mixtures. Students often call brass or salt water compounds because they "look like one substance". Looking uniform does not make something pure.

## Using evidence

**Worked example 3.** A student heats a grey powder in air. It glows brightly and forms a white powder that weighs more than the grey powder. The white powder does not change on further heating, and it melts at one fixed, very high temperature. Is the grey powder an element, a compound or a mixture, and what is the white powder?

*Annotated answer.* The grey powder has combined with something from the air (oxygen), because the product is heavier: this is a chemical reaction. The bright glow shows energy given out, which is typical of forming a compound. The white powder melts at a fixed temperature, so it is a pure substance; it was made by combining two elements, so it is a **compound** (an oxide). The grey powder could be an element, for example magnesium, that reacts with oxygen. The evidence alone cannot prove it is an element, so a careful answer says "the white powder is a compound; the grey powder is likely to be an element".

That last sentence shows exam technique: state what the evidence supports, and do not claim more than it shows.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any substance with molecules a compound. O₂, N₂, Cl₂ and O₃ are elements.
- Calling a solution or an alloy a compound because it looks uniform.
- Saying a compound "contains the properties of its elements".
- Saying a mixture can be separated "easily". The mark is for **physical** methods, not ease.
- Forgetting that a compound has a **fixed** ratio of elements; a mixture of the same elements can have any ratio.
- Writing "compounds are made of different atoms" without "chemically bonded".

## Exam technique

- **Describe the differences**: write comparative sentences, for example "a compound has a fixed composition, whereas a mixture has a variable composition". One-sided lists lose marks.
- **State** or **identify**: one word is enough (element, compound or mixture).
- **Explain** a classification: give the reason from the definition, such as "two different atoms are chemically bonded".
- With particle diagrams, count the kinds of particle first, then look inside each particle.
