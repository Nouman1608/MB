Class practicals are the hands-on strand of the IB Psychology course for first assessment 2027. Every SL and HL student must take part in a practical for **each of the four contexts**, and each context uses a different research method: an interview in health and well-being, an observation in human development, a survey or questionnaire in human relationships and an experiment in learning and cognition. They feed directly into **Paper 2 Section A** (four compulsory questions on the practicals) and they prepare you for the internal assessment, where you design a research proposal using one of these four methods. Class practicals take about 30 of the recommended teaching hours at both SL and HL (a minimum of four).

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-class-practicals-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-class-practicals-practice/).

## What the guide requires

| Context | Type of practical | Minimum sample for a student-led practical |
|---|---|---|
| Health and well-being | Interview (structured, semi-structured or focus group) | One participant (individual interview) or three to eight (one focus group) |
| Human development | Observation (naturalistic or controlled, overt or covert, participant or non-participant) | One participant |
| Human relationships | Survey/questionnaire (used interchangeably) | 10 participants |
| Learning and cognition | Experiment (true or quasi-) | Five participants |

Points the guide makes about running them:

- Practicals are expected to take place **during lesson time**. The teacher can be the researcher and the students the participants.
- Extra student-led practicals are allowed but not compulsory. They need explicit teacher permission on a class practical research form and must follow ethical guidelines.
- The examples listed in each context are **suggestions only**. Teachers may choose another practical if it relates to the context.
- Students are encouraged to write a brief record of each practical: the aim, procedure, sampling technique, sample findings and ethical considerations. It is not assessed, but it helps with Paper 2 Section A and the internal assessment.

## The four methods

### Interview

An interview gathers verbal data by asking questions. **Structured** interviews use fixed questions in a fixed order, which makes results comparable but allows little exploration. **Semi-structured** interviews have a set of planned questions with room for follow-up questions, which gives rich qualitative data but is harder to compare and depends on the interviewer's skill. A **focus group** is a group discussion led by a moderator. It shows how people discuss and influence each other but can suffer from dominant members and conformity.

Guide examples include focus groups on how students manage stress or physical health, an interview on social media use and self-esteem and an interview on exercise habits and mood. The guide warns that it is inappropriate for students to interview each other on mental health concerns, diagnoses, bullying or self-harm. Stress reduction techniques, healthy lifestyle habits and media consumption are more suitable.

### Observation

Observation records behaviour as it happens. It can be **naturalistic** (in the real setting) or **controlled**; **overt** (participants know) or **covert** (they do not); **participant** (the observer joins in) or **non-participant**. Strengths include high ecological validity and the chance to see what people do rather than what they say. Limitations include observer bias, no control of extraneous variables and the problem of interpreting behaviour. Guide examples are language-learning classes, playground observations and group dynamics in a public setting.

### Survey or questionnaire

A set of written questions answered by a sample. Closed questions give quantitative data and are easy to analyse; open questions give qualitative data. Strengths are the ability to reach many people quickly and anonymity. Limitations are social desirability, response bias, and misunderstanding of questions. The guide suggests the Hazan and Shaver (1987) love quiz and Buss (1989) on cross-cultural differences in mate preference. In Hazan and Shaver's work, a "love quiz" published in a newspaper asked readers to choose which description of adult romantic experience fitted them best, and the choices were related to attachment styles. Buss surveyed thousands of people in dozens of cultures about the qualities they wanted in a partner. These are only examples.

### Experiment

An experiment manipulates an **independent variable** and measures a **dependent variable**. A **true experiment** allocates participants randomly to conditions, so it supports causal claims. A **quasi-experiment** uses groups that already exist (for example two age groups), so the independent variable is not manipulated and causality is weaker. Guide examples: Loftus and Palmer (1974), Masuda and Nisbett (2001), Tversky and Kahneman (1974) and the Mozart effect work of Rauscher et al.

## Sampling techniques

The research methodology section lists five. Be ready to describe each and give one advantage and one disadvantage.

- **Self-selected (volunteer):** participants choose to take part, for example by replying to an advert. Easy and ethical, but biased towards the motivated.
- **Opportunity (convenience):** whoever is available, such as classmates. Quick, but unrepresentative.
- **Stratified:** the population is divided into groups (strata) and each is sampled in proportion. Representative, but needs detailed information about the population.
- **Random:** every member of the population has an equal chance of selection. Least biased, but needs a full list of the population and is often impractical.
- **Snowball:** participants recruit others. Good for hard-to-reach groups, but the sample is not representative.

## Ethics in class practicals

The guide gives firm rules. Learn these.

- **Protection from harm.** No practical that causes anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort. A teacher supervises. Conformity or obedience studies are not permitted under any circumstances. Partial deception may be allowed where full knowledge would change the outcome, provided there is no harm and a full debrief, and participants can withdraw their data afterwards.
- **Informed consent.** Explicit consent through a consent form; implied consent is not acceptable. Participants must know the aims and their right to withdraw at any time and must not be pressured to continue. Participants aged 16 or older do not need parental consent. For under-16s, written consent from parents or guardians is required, and written consent from teachers if the study is in school. Animals must not be used. Participants must be in a fit state of mind.
- **Naturalistic observation.** Participants cannot consent, and debriefing is hard. The teacher decides which situations are appropriate; public places where people expect to be seen by strangers (a shopping centre, public transport, a street) are generally acceptable.
- **Surveys.** Fewer ethical issues, but consent is still needed.
- **Data protection.** Store data confidentially, keep participants anonymous even after the practical, use data only for the agreed purpose and delete online data once the work is complete.

## Paper 2 Section A: the four questions

Paper 2 Section A has four compulsory questions on the practicals (the guide's rubric labels are Question 1a to 1d). The focus is on the research method, not on the context in which you conducted the practical. The mark scheme uses markbands.

| Part | Skill | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Knowledge and understanding of the research methodology | 4 |
| 1b | Application of a concept to the practical | 4 |
| 1c | Compare and contrast | 6 |
| 1d | Design | 6 |

How to prepare:

1. Keep a one-page record of each practical: aim, method, sampling technique, procedure, a sample result, ethics.
2. For 1a, define the method and its key terms accurately (for example semi-structured interview, quasi-experiment, overt observation).
3. For 1b, choose a concept (bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective or responsibility) and make at least two clear links to your practical.
4. For 1c, plan similarities and differences between two methods: purpose, data type, sampling, ethics, validity, ability to show causality.
5. For 1d, describe a procedure in an ordered sequence with enough detail that someone else could repeat it: participants and sampling, materials, instructions, consent and debrief, how data are collected and analysed.

## Worked example: comparing two methods

Compare a semi-structured interview with a survey.

- **Similarity:** both ask participants questions, so both rely on self-reported data and are exposed to social desirability bias.
- **Difference:** an interview collects detailed qualitative data from a few people; a survey with closed questions collects quantitative data from many and is easier to analyse statistically.
- **Difference:** an interviewer can ask follow-up questions and clarify meaning, but may unintentionally influence answers (interviewer bias); a survey is standardised, so each person answers the same questions, but misunderstandings cannot be corrected.
- **Ethics:** in an interview, anonymity is harder to maintain, so confidentiality must be explained; in an anonymous survey, risk of harm is lower, but consent is still required.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the topic of the practical instead of the method.
- Calling a quasi-experiment a "true experiment".
- Listing ethics without saying how they applied to your practical.
- Giving a design answer with no sampling, no ethics and no explanation of the analysis.
- Forgetting that the class practicals underpin the internal assessment.
