## 1. Types of business (item 1a)

| Type | Key point | Typical aim |
|---|---|---|
| Private sector | Owned by individuals or shareholders | Profit or return to owners |
| State-owned (public sector) | Owned by government | Public service, jobs, correcting market failure |
| For-profit | Surplus goes to owners | Distribute profit |
| Not-for-profit | Surplus reinvested in the purpose | Social or charitable aim |
| Co-operative | Owned by members (workers, consumers or suppliers), often one member one vote | Member benefit |
| Joint venture | Two or more firms share a project, costs and risks | Entry to markets, shared expertise |

**State-owned enterprises:** may pursue public interest goals, fund loss-making but socially valuable services and keep essential industries under public control. Weaknesses: weaker cost discipline, political interference and reliance on taxpayers. **Private sector firms** face competitive pressure and profit incentives, but may ignore external costs and unprofitable customers.

**Co-operatives:** members share profit and decisions, which builds loyalty. Raising capital is harder because outside investors get no control. **Joint ventures:** each partner contributes assets or skills. They end when the project ends or one partner buys the other out.

Must-know distinction: a not-for-profit body may earn a surplus. The question is where the surplus goes.

## 2. Size and growth (items 2a to 2g)

- **SME or large:** state the measure (employees, turnover or assets). Definitions differ between countries.
- **Organic growth:** growth from within, funded by retained profit. Slow, low risk, control kept.
- **Merger:** agreed combination. **Takeover:** purchase of control, agreed or hostile.

| Integration | Direction | One advantage | One disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Same stage, same industry | Economies of scale, market share | Regulator, job cuts |
| Forward vertical | Towards customers | Secures outlets and the retail margin | Unfamiliar skills |
| Backward vertical | Towards suppliers | Secure inputs, lower costs | Complacent in-house supplier |
| Conglomerate | Unrelated industry | Spreads risk | No synergies, loss of focus |

**Constraints on growth:** size of market; access to finance; owner objectives; government regulation and bureaucracy.

**Why firms stay small:** niche or local demand, personal service, flexibility, owner choice, lack of finance. **Why firms grow:** economies of scale, market power, diversification, survival, manager or shareholder pressure.

**Impact of growth:** businesses gain scale but may suffer diseconomies. Workers gain security and promotion, or lose jobs after mergers. Consumers gain if cost savings are passed on, and lose if market power lifts prices or cuts choice.

**Demergers:** reasons are diseconomies of scale, refocusing on core activities, raising cash, releasing hidden value and regulation. Impact: sharper focus, lost scale economies, uncertainty for workers, uncertain effects on prices and choice.

### Merger arithmetic reminders

| Measure | Formula | Illustration (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Market share | firm sales / market sales × 100 | 30 / 200 = 15% |
| Merged share | (sales A + sales B) / market × 100 | (30 + 20) / 200 = 25% |
| Percentage growth | change / original × 100 | (3.0 − 2.5) / 2.5 = 20% |
| Takeover premium | (offer − market price) / market price × 100 | (5.50 − 4.00) / 4.00 = 37.5% |
| Cost saving | combined costs before − costs after | 9.0 + 6.0 − 12.0 = £3.0m |
| Vertical saving | (supplier price − in-house cost) × volume | (8.00 − 6.50) × 100,000 = £150,000 |

Always write the unit and say what the base is. In a percentage change question the base is the starting figure; in a share question it is the whole market.

### Method for evaluating a merger (steps)

1. Name the type and the motive.
2. Chain the gain: lower unit costs, then lower prices only if competition forces a pass-on.
3. Chain the harm: market power, higher prices, job losses.
4. Judge with a condition: market contestability, regulator, size of the savings.

## 3. Objectives (items 3a to 3c)

| Objective | Condition | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Profit maximisation | MC = MR, MC cutting MR from below | Profit = TR − TC |
| Revenue maximisation | MR = 0 | TR = P × Q |
| Sales volume maximisation | Largest Q where AR ≥ AC | AR = AC at the limit |

At maximum revenue, price elasticity of demand equals 1 for a straight-line demand curve.

*Worked reminder.* A printer faces P = 66 − 3Q, with Q in thousands and money in £000. Total cost is TC = 20 + 12Q + 1.5Q², so MR = 66 − 6Q and MC = 12 + 3Q.

| Q | P | TR | TC | Profit | MR | MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 51 | 255 | 117.5 | 137.5 | 36 | 27 |
| 6 | 48 | 288 | 146.0 | 142.0 | 30 | 30 |
| 7 | 45 | 315 | 177.5 | 137.5 | 24 | 33 |
| 11 | 33 | 363 | 333.5 | 29.5 | 0 | 45 |

- Profit maximum: 66 − 6Q = 12 + 3Q, so **Q = 6, P = 48, profit £142k**.
- Revenue maximum: MR = 0, so **Q = 11, P = 33, TR £363k**, profit £29.5k.
- Sales volume maximum with no loss: 4.5Q² − 54Q + 20 = 0 gives **Q ≈ 11.6, P ≈ 31.1**, profit zero.

Pattern: output rises and price falls as you move from profit to revenue to sales volume.

**Satisficing:** managers aim for acceptable profit, sales and staff satisfaction, not the maximum. With a profit target of £100k or more, output from about 3 to 9 thousand units qualifies (profit is £101.5k at 3 and £101.5k at 9).

**Principal-agent problem:** owners (principals) and managers (agents) have different aims, and managers know more (asymmetric information). Managers may chase revenue, growth, pay or status.

| Fix | Limit |
|---|---|
| Profit-related pay, share options | Short-termism, manipulation of results |
| Independent directors, audits | Cost of monitoring |
| Takeover threat | Only works if shares are widely traded |

### Worked chain: horizontal merger and consumers

Two rival bus companies merge. Cost chain: one head office and shared depots, so average cost falls, so the firm can cut fares. Harm chain: fewer rivals, so a higher market share, so more market power, so fares may rise and services on weak routes may be cut. Judgement: consumers gain only if rivals or new entrants keep the merged firm under pressure and the regulator watches fares.

### Worked chain: demerger

A group that makes tools, toys and tiles struggles to manage three unrelated businesses. Managers lose focus, so costs rise and profit lags. After a demerger, each firm has a clear aim and its own management. The tile business is valued more highly and can raise finance easily. Workers may fear job losses in the old head office but may gain a clearer career path in the smaller firm.

## Quick self-test

1. A charity-run shop reinvests its surplus. Is it for-profit or not-for-profit?
2. Name the type of integration when a brewer buys a chain of pubs.
3. Two firms sell £15m and £10m in a market worth £200m. What is the merged market share?
4. Revenue rises from £2.5m to £3.0m. Calculate the percentage growth.
5. A bidder offers £5.50 for shares trading at £4.00. Calculate the premium.
6. Give two constraints on the growth of a small firm.
7. Demand is P = 84 − 6Q. At what output is revenue maximised, and what is the price?
8. Using the same demand, find profit-maximising output if MC is constant at 12.
9. A firm's revenue is £480k and its total cost is £350k. Calculate profit.
10. Explain in one sentence why a manager paid on sales may prefer a different output from the owners.

### Answers

1. Not-for-profit.
2. Forward vertical integration.
3. (15 + 10) / 200 = 12.5%.
4. (3.0 − 2.5) / 2.5 = 20%.
5. (5.50 − 4.00) / 4.00 = 37.5%.
6. Any two: small market, difficulty raising finance, owner objectives, regulation or bureaucracy.
7. MR = 84 − 12Q = 0, so Q = 7 and P = 84 − 42 = 42. Revenue is £294.
8. MR = 84 − 12Q = 12, so Q = 6 (price 48).
9. 480 − 350 = £130k.
10. Pay linked to sales rewards extra revenue even if profit falls, so the manager pushes output beyond the owners' preferred level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a merger type without naming which stage of production the firms occupy.
- Saying growth lowers prices without explaining that cost savings must be passed on.
- Treating profit maximisation as the only objective and ignoring satisficing and the principal-agent problem.
- Stating MR = MC for every objective.
- Giving a one-sided answer on mergers, with no judgement on which effect is larger.
- Mixing up units or leaving out the unit (£, £000, thousands) in calculations.
- Using real firms as examples without applying economic concepts to them.
- Writing "not-for-profit" as "makes no money".
