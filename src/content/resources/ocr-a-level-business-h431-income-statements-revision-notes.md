## What the specification lists

- Explain what is meant by an income statement and what it contains.
- Calculate, interpret and analyse gross profit, operating profit and net profit.
- Complete, interpret and analyse an income statement.
- Evaluate the usefulness of income statements for a business and its stakeholders.

## Core recall

### What it is

- Shows revenue, costs and profit for a **period** (usually a year).
- Follows accruals: revenue when earned, costs when incurred.
- Contrast: the statement of financial position is a snapshot on one date.

### Layout (in order)

| Line | How found |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Sales for the period |
| Cost of sales | Opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating expenses | Distribution, administration, marketing, depreciation |
| Other operating income | Rent received and similar |
| **Operating profit** | Gross profit − operating expenses + other operating income |
| Finance costs | Interest paid |
| Profit before tax | Operating profit − finance costs |
| Tax | Rate × profit before tax |
| **Profit for the year (net profit)** | Profit before tax − tax |
| Dividends | Paid to shareholders |
| Retained profit | Profit for the year − dividends |

### Margins

| Margin | Formula |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Operating profit margin | Operating profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Net profit margin | Profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100 |

### Analysing a statement

1. Compare each profit line with last year (amount and %).
2. Compare margins: did profit grow faster or slower than revenue?
3. A rising gross margin means cost of sales grew more slowly than revenue (price rise, cheaper supplies, better mix).
4. A rising operating margin means running costs grew more slowly than sales (efficiency, or cuts that may harm the future).
5. Check one-off items.
6. Compare with competitors and targets.

### Usefulness

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Standard format, comparable | Historic |
| Shows the stages of profit | Profit is not cash |
| Allows margin analysis | Depends on judgement (depreciation, provisions) |
| Required by law for companies | Hides one-off items; ignores non-financial factors |

Users: owners, lenders (interest cover), managers, employees, suppliers, tax authority.

## Quick worked reminder

Brunel Cycles plc (invented): revenue £1,250,000; cost of sales 80,000 + 640,000 − 95,000 = £625,000; gross profit £625,000 (50.0%); operating profit 625,000 − 95,000 − 180,000 + 10,000 = £360,000 (28.8%); profit before tax £336,000 after £24,000 interest; tax at 25% £84,000; profit for the year £252,000 (20.2%); dividends £100,000; retained £152,000. Last year the margins were 47.3%, 25.5% and 17.3%, so profit grew faster than revenue.

## Method checklist for completing a statement

1. Find cost of sales: opening inventory plus purchases less closing inventory.
2. Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales. Label it.
3. Deduct each operating expense (distribution, administration, depreciation) and add any other operating income to reach operating profit.
4. Deduct interest paid to reach profit before tax.
5. Calculate tax on profit before tax, then deduct it to reach profit for the year.
6. Deduct dividends to reach retained profit.
7. Calculate the margins and compare them with last year.

## Writing the analysis

- Say which profit measure you are using and give its value.
- State the percentage change and compare it with the change in revenue.
- Give a likely cause for any change in a margin, and say whether it is controllable.
- Name a stakeholder (lender, shareholder) and say what the result means for them.
- Finish with a judgement and a warning that profit is not cash.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Income statement | A statement of revenue, costs and profit for a period |
| Cost of sales | The direct cost of the goods sold |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales |
| Operating profit | Profit from normal trading before interest and tax |
| Net profit | Profit for the year after interest and tax |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business after dividends |
| Dividend | A payment to shareholders from profit |
| Finance costs | Interest paid on borrowing |
| Margin | A profit measure as a percentage of revenue |

## Exam traps

- Cost of sales needs opening and closing inventory.
- Tax is taken from profit before tax.
- Dividends are paid from profit; they are not a cost in finding profit for the year.
- Three different profits: use the right one for each margin.
- A higher profit does not mean more cash.

## Self-test

1. What does an income statement show?
2. How is cost of sales calculated?
3. Revenue £800,000; cost of sales £410,000. Calculate gross profit and gross margin.
4. Gross profit £390,000; operating expenses £200,000. Calculate operating profit.
5. Operating profit £190,000; interest £15,000. Calculate profit before tax.
6. Tax is 25%. Calculate profit for the year.
7. Calculate the net profit margin.
8. Dividends are £50,000. Calculate retained profit.
9. Why is profit not the same as cash?
10. Give one reason why a gross margin might rise.
11. Give one limitation of an income statement for a lender.
12. Gross profit rises 20% and revenue rises 11%. What does this suggest?

### Answers

1. Revenue, costs and profit over a period, usually a year.
2. Opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory.
3. £800,000 − £410,000 = **£390,000**; 390,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **48.75%**.
4. £390,000 − £200,000 = **£190,000**.
5. £190,000 − £15,000 = **£175,000**.
6. 25% × £175,000 = £43,750; £175,000 − £43,750 = **£131,250**.
7. 131,250 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **16.4%** (16.41%).
8. £131,250 − £50,000 = **£81,250**.
9. Profit follows accruals (credit sales, depreciation), while cash is what is actually received and paid.
10. Higher prices, cheaper supplies or a shift to higher-margin products.
11. It is historic and does not show cash, so it does not prove the business can repay.
12. Cost of sales grew more slowly than revenue, so the gross margin improved.
