## Section A

**1.** State **two** reasons, other than religion, why knights joined the First Crusade. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the dispute over Gascony that contributed to the Hundred Years' War. **[3]**

**3.** Identify **three** English victories in the Hundred Years' War and the date of each. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what a *chevauchée* was and why English commanders used it. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **two** reasons why the Mongol invasions of Japan failed. **[4]**

**6.** Identify **two** methods Mehmed II used to capture Constantinople in 1453. **[2]**

**7.** Define "dynastic war" and give **one** example from 750 to 1500. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain why the crusaders were able to capture Jerusalem in 1099. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why France recovered and won the Hundred Years' War after 1429. **[6]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the role of religion in causing **two** medieval wars, each chosen from a different region. **[6]**

**11.** "Technology decided the outcome of medieval wars." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view, using examples from **two** wars. **[8]**

**12.** Examine the political effects of **one** medieval war. **[15]**

**13.** Compare and contrast the causes of **two** medieval wars, each chosen from a different region. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: hope of gaining land, especially for younger sons with no inheritance (1); plunder and wealth (1); prestige and adventure (1); obligation to follow their lord (1); escape from debts or legal trouble (1). **[2]**

**2.** English kings held Gascony as vassals of the French king, so they owed him homage for it (1). French kings used their rights as overlords to intervene in Gascon affairs and hear appeals, which English kings resented (1). In 1337 Philip VI declared Gascony confiscated, which began the war (1). **[3]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each for battle and correct date: Sluys (1340, naval) (1); Crécy (1346) (1); Poitiers (1356) (1); Agincourt (1415) (1); Verneuil (1424) (1). **[3]**

**4.** A *chevauchée* was a large mounted raid through enemy territory, burning crops and villages and taking plunder (1). It weakened the French king's resources and revenues (1). It also showed that he could not protect his subjects, undermining his authority, and could provoke the French into battle on English terms (1). **[3]**

**5.** 2 marks for each developed reason: storms destroyed much of the fleet in both 1274 and 1281, and in 1281 the ships, many built in haste, were especially vulnerable (2); Japanese warriors resisted fiercely, and after 1274 a defensive wall at Hakata Bay prevented the invaders from establishing a beachhead (2); supplying a large army across the sea was very difficult, and the invading force combined Mongol, Chinese and Korean troops with different commanders (2). **[4]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: bombardment of the land walls with great cannon (1); a blockade by sea (1); ships moved overland into the Golden Horn to bypass the chain across its entrance (1); repeated mass assaults, ending in the final attack of 29 May (1). **[2]**

**7.** A war fought over rights of succession or inheritance to a throne or lands (1). Example: the Hundred Years' War or the Wars of the Roses (1). **[2]**

**8.** Up to 4 marks for developed explanation: Muslim rulers were divided between rival Seljuk princes and the Fatimids of Egypt, who had taken Jerusalem only in 1098 (1); the capture of Antioch (1098) and the defeat of a relief army gave the crusaders a base and confidence (1); Genoese ships arrived with timber and supplies, allowing siege towers to be built (1); religious fervour sustained the army through hardship, and the final assault in July 1099 broke through the walls (1). **[4]**

**9.** 3 marks for each developed reason, up to 6: Joan of Arc's relief of Orléans (1429) and the coronation of Charles VII at Reims gave the French monarchy legitimacy and morale (3); the Burgundians abandoned the English alliance at Arras (1435), removing England's key ally (3); permanent taxation and the companies of ordinance (1445) gave France a professional army with effective artillery, which won at Formigny (1450) and Castillon (1453) (3); England was weakened by the minority and weak rule of Henry VI and by financial strain (3). **[6]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example the First Crusade (Europe and the Middle East) and the Mongol invasions of Japan (Asia and Oceania). Similarities (up to 2): religious ideas were used to justify war on both sides, such as papal crusade preaching and Japanese prayers for divine protection (1); in both, religion combined with political ambition (1). Differences (up to 4): religion was a central cause of the First Crusade, but not of the Mongol invasions, which arose from imperial demands for tribute (1); the crusade was launched by a religious leader, the Mongol attack by a secular emperor (1); crusaders were promised spiritual reward for fighting, while the Mongols were generally tolerant of religions (1); in Japan religion mattered more in explaining the outcome, through belief in the divine winds, than in causing the war (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: the English longbow won Crécy and Agincourt against larger armies (2); great cannon breached Constantinople's walls in 1453, which had resisted earlier sieges (2). Against: England lost the Hundred Years' War despite the longbow, because France had better finances, alliances and leadership after 1429 (2); weather and logistics, not technology, defeated the Mongol invasions of Japan (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *For example, the Hundred Years' War.* France: stronger monarchy, permanent taxation (the *taille*) and a standing army; the defeat of English and Burgundian power; growing royal control of the nobility. England: loss of all French lands except Calais (1453); weakened crown under Henry VI; noble factions and returning soldiers contributing to the Wars of the Roses. Both: national identity strengthened. A strong answer distinguishes short- from long-term effects and effects on each kingdom, and judges which were most significant. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, the First Crusade and the Mongol invasions of Japan.* Causes of the crusade: Byzantine appeal, papal ambition, religious enthusiasm, land and wealth; trigger: Clermont (1095). Causes of the Mongol invasions: Kublai Khan's demands for submission, Mongol imperial ideology, the war against the Song, Japanese refusal and execution of envoys. Comparison: both combined ideological justification with political ambition; the crusade was a mass movement drawing volunteers from across Christendom, while the Mongol invasion was a state enterprise ordered by one ruler; both had a clear trigger. A strong answer compares in every paragraph and judges which type of cause mattered most in each. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the First Crusade in Europe or the Middle East?**
Its causes lay largely in Europe, and its fighting took place in Anatolia and the Levant. If a question requires different regions, pair it with a war clearly located in another region, such as one in East Asia.

**How many wars should I revise?**
At least three, from at least two regions, so that you can answer both single-war and comparative questions.
