## The measure everything depends on

The specification defines both kinds of economy in terms of **average total cost** (ATC), the cost of producing one unit:

**Average total cost = total cost ÷ output**

"Scale" means the size of the business's operations. An economy of scale is a fall in ATC that happens **because** the business operates on a larger scale. A diseconomy of scale is a rise in ATC that happens **because** the scale has grown too large. A cost change caused by something else, such as a rise in world fuel prices, is neither.

## Worked example 1: splitting average cost

Ashgrove Bottling has fixed costs of 30,000 a month. Its total costs at different monthly outputs:

| Output (bottles) | Total cost | ATC | Fixed cost per bottle | Variable cost per bottle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5,000 | 55,000 | 11.00 | 6.00 | 5.00 |
| 10,000 | 80,000 | 8.00 | 3.00 | 5.00 |
| 20,000 | 120,000 | 6.00 | 1.50 | 4.50 |
| 30,000 | 165,000 | 5.50 | 1.00 | 4.50 |
| 40,000 | 240,000 | 6.00 | 0.75 | 5.25 |

Method: ATC = total cost ÷ output; fixed cost per bottle = 30,000 ÷ output; variable cost per bottle = (total cost − 30,000) ÷ output.

What the columns show:

- **Fixed cost per bottle falls all the way**, from 6.00 to 0.75, because the same 30,000 is spread over more bottles.
- **Variable cost per bottle falls** from 5.00 to 4.50 between 10,000 and 20,000. That is consistent with cheaper materials bought in larger amounts.
- **ATC is lowest at 30,000 bottles** (5.50). Up to that output Ashgrove gains internal economies of scale.
- **Between 30,000 and 40,000, ATC rises** to 6.00 even though fixed cost per bottle is still falling. Variable cost per bottle has jumped to 5.25: perhaps overtime, extra shifts or machines running past their best. That rise is the sign of diseconomies of scale.

The skill this tests: reading a table to say where economies end and diseconomies begin, and explaining why.

## Internal economies: classifying the sources

The specification does not list types of internal economy, so the categories below are standard teaching. Use them to explain *why* ATC falls, always ending with the effect on average cost.

| Source | How ATC falls | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing (bulk buying) | Larger orders win lower prices per unit | A supermarket chain pays less per tin than a corner shop |
| Technical | Larger firms can afford specialised, faster machinery that lowers cost per unit | A large bakery uses an automated oven line |
| Financial | Larger firms are seen as lower risk, so they may borrow at lower interest | A large company obtains a loan at a lower rate than a small one |
| Marketing | Promotion costs are spread over more units | A national advert costs less per unit sold for a firm selling millions |
| Managerial | Large firms can employ specialist managers who work more efficiently | A dedicated buyer negotiates better supplier deals |

### Worked example 2: a purchasing economy

Ashgrove buys glass bottles at 0.30 each for orders under 50,000 a month, and 0.24 each for orders of 50,000 or more. It raises monthly orders from 40,000 to 60,000.

- Bottle cost before: 40,000 × 0.30 = 12,000 (0.30 per bottle).
- Bottle cost after: 60,000 × 0.24 = 14,400 (0.24 per bottle).

Total spending on bottles rises, but the cost **per bottle** falls by 0.06. That fall in a unit cost, caused by a larger scale of buying, is an internal economy of scale.

## External economies

External economies come from outside the business, usually from the growth of the industry in an area. Every firm in that area can benefit, whatever its own size.

- **Skilled labour pool**: when many firms in one industry are close together, workers with the right skills live nearby, so recruitment and training cost less.
- **Specialist suppliers and services**: suppliers set up near a cluster of customers, cutting delivery times and costs.
- **Infrastructure**: better roads, ports or internet links built for the area lower transport and communication costs.
- **Training and research**: a local college may start courses that match the industry's needs.

### Worked example 3: an external economy

A regional college starts a course for food technicians. Lyndale Foods, which hires 40 technicians a year, used to spend 2,500 training each one; trained college leavers need only 1,000 of further training.

- Yearly training saving: 40 × (2,500 − 1,000) = **60,000**.
- Lyndale produces 200,000 units a year, so ATC falls by 60,000 ÷ 200,000 = **0.30** per unit.

Lyndale did nothing to cause this. The cause is external, and its competitors in the area gain too, so it does not give Lyndale an advantage over local rivals; it can give the area an advantage over firms elsewhere.

## Diseconomies of scale and the limits of growth

Diseconomies of scale explain why a business cannot keep growing and keep cutting average cost forever. Standard causes:

- **Communication**: more layers of management slow messages down and distort them, so decisions are late or wrong.
- **Co-ordination and control**: many sites, departments and products are harder to keep working together, so effort is duplicated and waste increases.
- **Motivation**: employees in a very large organisation may feel distant from decision makers and less valued, so absence, errors and staff turnover rise.

### Worked example 4: growth that raised average cost

Lindqvist Furniture expanded from one factory to four. Output rose from 10,000 to 36,000 units a year, and total costs rose from 900,000 to 3,456,000.

- ATC before: 900,000 ÷ 10,000 = **90**.
- ATC after: 3,456,000 ÷ 36,000 = **96**.

Average cost rose by 6 per unit. Lindqvist found that each factory ordered its own materials, so bulk-buying power was lost, and decisions passed through a new regional management layer. A response could be a single central purchasing team and fewer management layers, to recover economies and reduce diseconomies.

**Limits of growth** are wider than cost. Even before diseconomies set in, growth can be limited by the size of the market (there may not be enough customers), the finance available, and the owner's wish to keep control or a manageable workload.

## Using the ideas in an evaluation

When a case asks whether a business should grow, weigh:

1. Which economies of scale it could gain, with the effect on ATC.
2. Which diseconomies it risks, and whether they can be managed.
3. Whether the market is large enough to buy the extra output.
4. Whether it can finance the growth.

### Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether Ashgrove Bottling should increase output from 30,000 to 40,000 bottles a month.*

> At 30,000 bottles Ashgrove is at its lowest average total cost, 5.50. Producing 40,000 raises ATC to 6.00, because variable cost per bottle rises from 4.50 to 5.25, which suggests overtime or strain on the machines. However, fixed cost per bottle still falls, and if Ashgrove can sell the extra 10,000 bottles at a price above 6.00, total profit could still rise. It should only expand if demand is strong and it can fix the cause of the higher variable cost, for example by adding a second shift on normal pay rates rather than overtime. Otherwise, staying at 30,000 is the more efficient choice.

## Common mistakes

- Defining economies of scale as "lower total costs". Total costs usually rise with output; it is **average** cost that falls.
- Calling any cost rise a diseconomy without linking it to the scale of the business.
- Mixing up internal (from the firm's own growth) and external (from outside the firm).
- Naming a type of economy without explaining how it lowers cost per unit.
- Ignoring the limits of growth that are not about cost, such as market size.
