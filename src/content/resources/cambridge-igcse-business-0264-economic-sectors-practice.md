## Questions

### Section A: short answer

**1.** Identify the activity sector (primary, secondary or tertiary) of each business.

**(a)** A quarry that digs limestone. **[1]**

**(b)** A firm that builds offices. **[1]**

**(c)** A taxi company. **[1]**

**(d)** A sawmill that cuts logs into planks. **[1]**

**2.** Define the term **tertiary sector**. **[2]**

**3.** State two features that show an organisation is in the public sector. **[2]**

**4.** Outline why a car repair garage is in the tertiary sector and not the secondary sector. **[2]**

**5.** Halvorsen Shipping is a public limited company owned by thousands of shareholders. Explain whether it is in the private or the public sector. **[2]**

**6.** For each organisation, state its activity sector and its ownership sector.

**(a)** A prison run by the national government. **[2]**

**(b)** A family-owned dairy farm. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Quillon Orchards grows apples on its own land. It also presses some of the apples into juice in a small factory on the farm, and sells bottles of juice in its farm shop.

**(a)** Identify the three activity sectors that Quillon Orchards works in, giving the activity that places it in each. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain one advantage to Quillon Orchards of pressing its own apples instead of selling all of them to a juice factory. **[3]**

**8.** Lorvane Copper mines copper ore. It sells the ore to Brecca Cables, which makes electrical cable. Brecca Cables sells its cable through Fenwold Supplies, a chain of building-supply shops.

**(a)** State the activity sector of each business. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how a fall in house building could affect Lorvane Copper. **[3]**

**9.** The figures show the value of one consignment of products at each stage of a chain of production.

| Stage | Business | Value of the consignment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seldon Fibres grows and sells raw wool | $1,200 |
| 2 | Marrick Mills spins the wool and weaves it into blankets | $4,500 |
| 3 | Ashlow Home sells the blankets to the public | $7,100 |

**(a)** Calculate the value added at stage 2 and at stage 3. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the stage at which the most value is added, and state its sector. **[2]**

**10.** Explain one difference between the way a private sector business and a public sector organisation is usually funded. **[3]**

**11.** Kestrel Town's bus services are run by the local council. The council is considering selling the bus service to a private company, Tyndale Buses.

**(a)** Explain one way the aims of the bus service might change if it moved into the private sector. **[3]**

**(b)** Consider the advantages and disadvantages to local residents of the bus service moving into the private sector. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**12.** Explain why it is important for a manufacturer to consider businesses in the other activity sectors when planning its production. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** Primary [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Secondary [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Tertiary [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** Secondary [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* Part (b) is the one most often lost: building is a secondary activity, not a service. Part (d) processes a natural resource into a new product, so it is secondary even though it handles timber.

**2.** The part of the economy made up of businesses that provide services [1], such as retail, transport, banking or education, rather than extracting resources or making goods [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It is owned by national or local government [1]. It is mainly funded by the government from taxes [1]. **[2]**

Also accept, as either feature: it is controlled by government or by managers the government appoints; its main aim is to provide a service to the public rather than to make a profit.

**4.** It repairs and services cars that have already been made, so it provides a service [1]. It does not manufacture or assemble the cars, which would be a secondary activity [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It is in the private sector [1], because it is owned by shareholders, who are private individuals or groups, not by the government; "public" in "public limited company" means its shares can be bought by the public [1]. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** Tertiary [1] and public [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Primary [1] and private [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Primary, because it grows apples [1]. Secondary, because it presses the apples into juice and bottles it [1]. Tertiary, because it sells juice in its farm shop [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Pressing its own apples lets Quillon add value to the fruit itself [1]. Juice sells for more than the raw apples it contains, so the farm keeps the value that a juice factory would otherwise add [1]. This could raise its revenue and profit, and make it less dependent on the price a factory is willing to pay for apples [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* A reason that uses the case (apples, juice, a factory buyer) earns the application mark; "it makes more money" alone does not.

**8.** **(a)** Lorvane Copper: primary [1]. Brecca Cables: secondary [1]. Fenwold Supplies: tertiary [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Fewer houses being built means builders buy less cable from Fenwold Supplies [1]. Brecca Cables then makes less cable and so buys less copper ore [1]. Lorvane Copper's sales and revenue fall, even though the original change happened in another part of the chain [1]. **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Stage 2: 4,500 - 1,200 = **$3,300** [1]. Stage 3: 7,100 - 4,500 = **$2,600** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Stage 2, Marrick Mills [1], which is in the secondary sector [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Value added at a stage is the value leaving that stage minus the value coming in. Subtracting from the first stage every time is a common slip.

**10.** A private sector business is usually funded by its owners' investment, loans and the revenue it earns from sales [1]. A public sector organisation is usually funded mainly by the government from taxes [1]. This means a private business must cover its costs from sales to survive, while a public organisation can provide a service even if users pay little or nothing [1]. **[3]**

**11.** **(a)** The council's main aim is likely to be providing a service to residents [1]. Tyndale Buses, as a private company, would usually aim mainly for profit [1]. It might therefore cut routes that make a loss, such as a quiet evening service, or raise fares [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Advantage: a profit-seeking firm has a reason to keep costs down and attract passengers, for example by improving busy routes [1], which could give residents on those routes a better service [1]. Disadvantage: routes that make a loss may be cut or fares raised [1], which would hit residents who rely on buses, such as elderly people or those without cars [1]. Judgement: residents on busy routes may gain, but those on quiet routes are likely to lose [1], so the change is only good for residents overall if the council requires Tyndale Buses to keep essential routes running [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The final two marks are for a judgement that follows from both sides and adds a condition. "It depends" with no reason earns nothing.

**12.** A manufacturer depends on the primary sector for its materials [1], so it needs to know whether supplies will be available and at what price, or production may stop or costs rise [1]. It also depends on the tertiary sector to transport, finance and sell its goods [1], so it must plan output in line with what transport firms and retailers can handle and what shops expect to sell [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classing construction as tertiary or a repair garage as secondary.
- Defining the public sector as organisations that "serve the public".
- Treating a public limited company as public sector.
- Giving only one classification when both are asked for.
- Working out value added from the first stage each time instead of from the stage before.
- Weighing only one side in a "consider" question, or giving a judgement without a reason.
