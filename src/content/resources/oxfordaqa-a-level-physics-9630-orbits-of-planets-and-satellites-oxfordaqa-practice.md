These questions cover section **3.7.4 Orbits of planets and satellites** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: orbital period and speed against radius, energy of an orbiting satellite and the significance of a geosynchronous orbit. Section 3.7 is International A-level only. Marks are shown for each part. All orbits are circular. The planet and moon in Question 8 are invented for practice.

Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², GM(Earth) = 3.98 × 10¹⁴ N m² kg⁻¹, Earth radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m and the speed of electromagnetic waves 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Revise first with the [orbits of planets and satellites study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-orbits-of-planets-and-satellites-oxfordaqa/) and the [orbits revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-orbits-of-planets-and-satellites-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain why two satellites of different masses in the same circular orbit move at the same speed. **[2]**

**2.** A satellite moves to a circular orbit of larger radius. Its orbital speed

A: increases

B: decreases

C: stays the same

D: becomes zero **[1]**

**3.** Calculate the orbital speed of a satellite 8.0 × 10⁶ m from the Earth's centre. **[2]**

**4.** The radius of a satellite's orbit is increased by a factor of 9. State the factor by which its period changes. **[1]**

**5.** For a satellite to stay above one point on the Earth's surface, its orbit must be geosynchronous and also

A: polar

B: equatorial and in the direction of the Earth's rotation

C: as low as possible

D: elliptical **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the total energy of a satellite in orbit is negative. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.**

**(a)** Show that for a circular orbit of radius r around a body of mass M, T² = (4π²/GM) r³. **[3]**

**(b)** State what the gradient of a graph of T² against r³ represents. **[1]**

**8.** A moon orbits a planet with radius 4.2 × 10⁸ m and period 2.5 days.

**(a)** Convert the period to seconds. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of the planet. **[2]**

**(c)** A second moon orbits the same planet at twice the radius. Calculate its period in days. **[2]**

**9.** A satellite of mass 900 kg orbits the Earth at a radius of 9.0 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Calculate its kinetic energy, potential energy and total energy. **[3]**

**(b)** It is moved to an orbit of radius 1.8 × 10⁷ m. Calculate the energy that must be supplied. **[2]**

**(c)** State and explain what happens to its speed. **[2]**

**10.**

**(a)** Explain the significance of a geostationary orbit for communications satellites. **[3]**

**(b)** A geostationary satellite is 3.58 × 10⁷ m above the equator. Calculate the minimum time for a signal to travel from a ground station up to the satellite and back down. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why satellites used for detailed imaging of the ground are placed in low orbits instead. **[1]**

**11.** A 300 kg satellite is launched from the Earth's surface into a circular orbit of radius 6.8 × 10⁶ m. Ignoring air resistance and the Earth's rotation, calculate the minimum energy that must be supplied. **[3]**

**12.** Satellite A has a period of 2.0 hours. Satellite B, orbiting the same planet, has a period of 16 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the ratio of B's orbital radius to A's. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio of B's orbital speed to A's. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The gravitational force provides the centripetal force, GMm/r² = mv²/r [1]; the satellite's mass m cancels, so v = √(GM/r) depends only on M and r [1].

**2.** **B** [1]

**3.** v = √(GM/r) = √(3.98 × 10¹⁴/8.0 × 10⁶) [1] = **7.1 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]

**4.** 9^1.5 = **27** [1]

**5.** **B** [1]

**6.** Potential energy is zero at infinity and negative in orbit, and Ep = −GMm/r is twice the size of Ek = GMm/(2r) [1]; so E = Ek + Ep = −GMm/(2r) is negative, meaning energy would have to be supplied for the satellite to escape: it is bound [1].

**7.**

**(a)** Gravitational force provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v² = GM/r [1]; v = 2πr/T, so 4π²r²/T² = GM/r [1]; rearranging gives T² = (4π²/GM) r³ [1].

**(b)** 4π²/GM, which depends only on the mass of the central body [1].

**8.**

**(a)** T = 2.5 × 24 × 3600 = **2.16 × 10⁵ s** [1]

**(b)** M = 4π²r³/(GT²) = 4π² × (4.2 × 10⁸)³/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.16 × 10⁵)²) [1] = **9.4 × 10²⁶ kg** [1]

**(c)** T ∝ r^1.5, so the period rises by 2^1.5 = 2.83 [1]; T = 2.83 × 2.5 = **7.1 days** [1]

**9.**

**(a)** Ek = GMm/(2r) = 3.98 × 10¹⁴ × 900/(2 × 9.0 × 10⁶) = **1.99 × 10¹⁰ J** [1]; Ep = −GMm/r = **−3.98 × 10¹⁰ J** [1]; E = Ek + Ep = **−1.99 × 10¹⁰ J** [1]

**(b)** New E = −3.98 × 10¹⁴ × 900/(2 × 1.8 × 10⁷) = −9.95 × 10⁹ J [1]; energy supplied = −9.95 × 10⁹ − (−1.99 × 10¹⁰) = **9.95 × 10⁹ J** [1]

**(c)** It **decreases**, from 6.65 km s⁻¹ to 4.70 km s⁻¹ [1], because v = √(GM/r) falls as r increases; the supplied energy, and some kinetic energy, go into potential energy [1].

**10.**

**(a)** The satellite has the same period as the Earth's rotation and stays above the same point on the equator [1]; ground aerials can point in a fixed direction without tracking it [1]; it gives continuous coverage of the same region at all times [1].

**(b)** Distance ≥ 2 × 3.58 × 10⁷ = 7.16 × 10⁷ m [1]; t = 7.16 × 10⁷/3.00 × 10⁸ = **0.24 s** [1]

**(c)** A low orbit is much closer to the ground, so finer detail can be resolved (and the satellite passes over many different regions) [1].

**11.** On the ground, E = −GMm/R = −3.98 × 10¹⁴ × 300/6.37 × 10⁶ = −1.87 × 10¹⁰ J [1]; in orbit, E = −GMm/(2r) = −3.98 × 10¹⁴ × 300/(2 × 6.8 × 10⁶) = −8.78 × 10⁹ J [1]; energy supplied = **1.0 × 10¹⁰ J** [1]

**12.**

**(a)** r ∝ T^(2/3) [1]; r(B)/r(A) = (16/2.0)^(2/3) = 8^(2/3) = **4** [1]

**(b)** v ∝ 1/√r, so v(B)/v(A) = 1/√4 = **0.5** [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using altitude for r (Questions 10 and 11).
- Leaving the period in days in M = 4π²r³/(GT²) (Question 8).
- Saying a heavier satellite must go faster or slower (Question 1).
- Saying the satellite speeds up when moved to a higher orbit (Question 9).
- Forgetting the potential energy at the surface in a launch calculation (Question 11).
- Describing a geosynchronous orbit without saying it matches the Earth's rotation, or without linking it to fixed dishes (Question 10).
