## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Rents for flats in a city rise sharply, and builders start converting offices into flats. Which function of price is mainly shown by builders' response to the rise in rents? **[1]**

A: Rationing.

B: Incentive.

C: Measure of value.

D: Store of value.

**2.** Which of the following is an example of non-price rationing? **[1]**

A: An auction for a painting.

B: A waiting list for council housing.

C: A rise in the price of coffee after a poor harvest.

D: A sale in a clothes shop.

**3.** A maximum price is set below the equilibrium price. The most likely result is: **[1]**

A: A surplus.

B: A shortage.

C: A rise in quantity supplied.

D: No change in the market.

**4.** In a planned economy, resources are mainly allocated by: **[1]**

A: Consumer spending.

B: Competition between firms.

C: Decisions of a central authority.

D: The rationing function of price.

**5.** Explain the incentive function of price, using an example. **[2]**

**6.** A council has 600 allotment plots. Demand for plots is Qd = 1,200 - 20P, where P is the annual rent in pounds.

**(a)** Calculate the rent at which all plots would be let with no excess demand. **[1]**

**(b)** The council sets the rent at £10. Calculate the excess demand. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain one way the council might allocate the plots and one drawback of that method. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** *Extract (invented).* During a long drought, Country W's water company keeps its price unchanged and bans the use of hosepipes. Reservoirs fall to 30% of capacity. Some economists argue that the company should instead raise the price of water used above a basic allowance per household.

**(a)** Explain how two functions of price would operate if the price of water were raised. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether a price rise is a better way than a ban to allocate water during a drought. **[6]**

**8.** A city adds a 20p refundable deposit to plastic bottles. The share of bottles returned rises from 45% to 85%. In the first year 300 million bottles are sold.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the return rate in percentage points and as a percentage. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the value of deposits left unclaimed in the first year. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain two factors that may explain why this incentive was effective. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a planned economy may produce surpluses of some goods and shortages of others. **[4]**

**10.** Demand for petrol cars falls as buyers switch to electric cars. Explain how the price mechanism reallocates resources in response. **[4]**

**11.** Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a mixed economy. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether health care is better allocated by the market or by the government. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Higher rents reward supplying more flats, so builders convert offices. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A waiting list allocates by time spent waiting, not price. **[1]**

**3.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A higher price raises the reward for producing a good, so firms expand output and new firms enter [1]. For example, if the price of oat milk rises, dairies find it more profitable and switch some capacity to producing it [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 1,200 - 20P = 600, so P = **£30** [1].

**(b)** Qd = 1,200 - 200 = 1,000, so excess demand = 1,000 - 600 = **400 plots** [1].

**(c)** A waiting list, first come first served [1]; but people may wait years, and the plots may not go to those who would use them most [1]. Other answers: a ballot (random, ignores need), priority for local residents. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Rationing: a higher price would reduce the quantity of water demanded [1], as households cut non-essential uses such as filling pools, so use falls toward the amount the reservoirs can supply [1]. Signalling: the higher price tells users that water has become scarcer [1], and tells the company and others that investment in storage or leak repair is more valuable [1].

**(b)** For a price rise: it lets each household decide which uses matter most, so water goes to its most valued uses [1], and raises revenue that could fund repairs [1]. For a ban: water is a necessity with inelastic demand, so a price rise might need to be large to cut use enough [1], and it would hit low-income households hardest, although a basic allowance at the old price reduces this [1]. A ban is simple and immediate but needs enforcing and treats all users the same [1]. Judgement: a price rise above a basic allowance combines efficient rationing with protection for essential use, so it is likely to be better, provided households can monitor their use [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The basic allowance in the extract is the key to a strong judgement; use it.

**8. (a)** 85 - 45 = **40 percentage points** [1]; 40 / 45 x 100 = **88.9%** [1].

**(b)** 15% of 300 million = 45 million bottles; 45 million x £0.20 = **£9 million** [1].

**(c)** The reward is easy to collect, for example at shop counters, so the cost of changing behaviour is low [1], and even a small payment is enough to make returning bottles worthwhile [1]. The scheme was well publicised, so people knew about it and understood it [1]; and some people collect bottles others discard, so the incentive also works through those who value the money most [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Planners must decide output for thousands of goods without price signals [1]. They lack information about what consumers want and how wants change [1], so they may plan too much of goods people do not want, creating surpluses [1], and too little of goods in demand, creating shortages and queues, because prices are not allowed to adjust [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Lower demand means petrol car prices fall [1], so producing them becomes less profitable [1]. Firms cut output and some leave the industry, releasing workers, factories and capital [1]. Rising demand and prices for electric cars make them more profitable, so those resources move into producing electric cars [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Advantage: markets allocate most goods, keeping incentives and choice [1], while government provides public goods and corrects failures that markets would leave [1]. Disadvantage: government intervention can be inefficient or poorly targeted [1], and higher taxes to pay for it may weaken incentives to work and invest [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Market: prices give providers an incentive to be efficient and to respond to patients' wants [1]; competition can bring choice and innovation [1]. But health care allocated by ability to pay leaves low-income patients without treatment [1]; patients lack the information to judge treatments [1]; and vaccination and treatment of infectious disease benefit others, so a market would provide too little [1]. Government: can allocate by need, free at the point of use [1], and can take wider benefits into account [1]. But without prices, demand exceeds supply, so waiting lists ration care [1], and weak incentives can reduce efficiency [1]. Judgement: because of information problems and external benefits, government has a strong case to fund health care, but using some market elements, such as competition between providers, may improve efficiency, so a mixed approach is likely best [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the general strengths and weaknesses of each system to features of health care in particular.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up the incentive and signalling functions.
- Calling a 40-point rise a 40% rise.
- Ignoring non-price rationing in a price-cap question.
- Evaluating without using the extract's details, such as the basic allowance.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
