## How pollutants behave

Classify a pollutant by its behaviour, because behaviour decides how it should be managed:

- **Biodegradable** pollutants (sewage, food waste, paper-mill waste) are broken down by decomposers. They harm ecosystems mainly by using up oxygen as they decay, and a river can recover downstream if loading is not too high.
- **Persistent** pollutants (many pesticides, PCBs, heavy metals such as mercury and lead, most plastics) break down very slowly or not at all. They accumulate in sediments and organisms and can travel far from their source. The Stockholm Convention (2001) restricts a list of persistent organic pollutants for this reason.
- **Energy as a pollutant.** Heated cooling water reduces the solubility of oxygen, raises the metabolic rate of organisms (so they need more oxygen) and can exclude cold-water species.
- **Changes in pH.** Acid mine drainage, from water flowing through exposed sulfide minerals in mines, lowers pH and dissolves metals that are toxic to aquatic life.

## Measuring: two calculations

### Worked example 1: biochemical oxygen demand

A water sample is collected below a fictional food-processing plant. Its dissolved oxygen is measured at once and again after five days sealed in the dark at 20 °C.

```
dissolved oxygen on day 0 = 9.2 mg L⁻¹
dissolved oxygen on day 5 = 3.6 mg L⁻¹
BOD₅ = 9.2 − 3.6 = 5.6 mg L⁻¹
```

**Why the method works.** Keeping the sample dark stops photosynthesis adding oxygen, and sealing it stops oxygen entering from the air, so all the fall is due to respiration by microorganisms decomposing organic matter. A higher BOD means more organic pollution. If the sample's BOD is very high, it is diluted first, because all the oxygen would otherwise be used up before day 5 and the result would be an underestimate.

### Worked example 2: nutrient load

Concentration alone does not show how much pollutant a river delivers. The **load** combines concentration with discharge. A fictional river carries nitrate at 6 mg L⁻¹ with a discharge of 12 m³ s⁻¹.

```
6 mg L⁻¹ = 6 g m⁻³   (1 m³ = 1000 L)
load = 6 g m⁻³ × 12 m³ s⁻¹       = 72 g s⁻¹
per day = 72 × 86 400 s          = 6 220 800 g
        = 6220.8 kg ≈ 6.2 tonnes of nitrate per day
```

**Interpretation.** A storm that doubles discharge can double the load even if concentration stays the same, and runoff from fields during storms often raises concentration as well. This is why monitoring designed around dry-weather samples underestimates non-point pollution.

### Designing a fair survey

When comparing sites, sample upstream of a source as a control, at several distances downstream, at the same time of day (oxygen varies between day and night because of photosynthesis) and on several dates. Combine direct measures with a biotic index so that short-lived pollution events and long-term conditions are both captured.

## Limiting nutrients and eutrophication in more depth

Algal growth is controlled by whichever nutrient is in shortest supply relative to need: the **limiting nutrient**. In most lakes and rivers, phosphorus is limiting, so phosphate inputs from sewage, detergents and farm runoff trigger blooms. In many coastal and marine waters nitrogen is more often limiting, so nitrate from farm fertilisers has the greater effect. Managing the limiting nutrient gives the largest benefit for the cost.

Two consequences deserve named examples:

- **Dead zones.** Where dissolved oxygen falls below about 2 mg L⁻¹ (hypoxia), most fish and invertebrates die or leave. A large seasonal dead zone forms each summer in the northern Gulf of Mexico, fed by nutrients carried by the Mississippi River from farmland in its basin. The Baltic Sea also has extensive hypoxic areas.
- **Harmful algal blooms.** Some cyanobacteria release toxins that poison fish, livestock, pets and people, and force drinking-water intakes to close.

Internal loading slows recovery: phosphorus stored in lake-bed sediments is released when bottom water loses oxygen, so a lake can stay eutrophic for years after external inputs are cut.

## Plastics

Plastics are persistent and enter water from litter, poorly managed waste, lost fishing gear and wastewater. In the sea, wind-driven gyres collect floating debris in accumulation zones, such as the area in the North Pacific known as the Great Pacific Garbage Patch.

- **Macroplastics** entangle animals (seals, turtles, seabirds) and are swallowed, blocking digestive systems.
- **Microplastics** (pieces smaller than 5 mm) come from the breakdown of larger items, synthetic textile fibres and tyre wear. They are eaten by filter feeders and zooplankton and pass up food chains.
- Plastics can adsorb persistent chemicals from seawater and carry them into organisms.

Management at the three levels: reduce single-use plastic and design for reuse; improve waste collection and add filters to washing machines and drains; clean beaches and remove ghost nets. Because microplastics are almost impossible to remove once dispersed, the first level matters most.

## Case study: mercury at Minamata

In the 1950s, people in Minamata, Japan, developed severe neurological disease. A chemical factory had been releasing mercury compounds into Minamata Bay. Mercury converted to methylmercury, which bioaccumulated in fish and shellfish and biomagnified up the food chain to the people who ate them. The disease became known as Minamata disease. The **Minamata Convention on Mercury**, agreed in 2013, now controls the use and release of mercury worldwide. The case shows why persistent, fat-soluble pollutants are most dangerous to top consumers, including humans.

## Recovery is possible

**Lake Erie** in North America suffered severe eutrophication by the 1960s. Under the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement of 1972 between Canada and the United States, phosphorus from sewage treatment and detergents was cut, and the lake improved substantially. Since the 1990s, harmful blooms have returned, driven largely by non-point runoff of dissolved phosphorus from farmland. The case shows that point sources are easier to control than non-point sources, and that management must adapt as sources change.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain why discharge of warm water from a power station can reduce the number of fish species in a river. **[3]**

**Answer.** Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen [1]. At the same time, higher temperatures raise the metabolic rate of fish and invertebrates, so they need more oxygen [1]. Species adapted to cold, well-oxygenated water, such as trout, cannot survive, so only tolerant species remain and species richness falls [1].

## Common mistakes

- Calling every pollutant "toxic". Many harm ecosystems indirectly, for example by removing oxygen or blocking light.
- Calculating BOD as the final oxygen reading instead of the drop in oxygen.
- Comparing pollution between rivers by concentration only, ignoring discharge.
- Writing that nitrate is always the cause of freshwater blooms; phosphorus is usually limiting in lakes.
- Saying that cutting inputs immediately cures a eutrophic lake; internal loading delays recovery.
- Describing Minamata without the conversion to methylmercury and biomagnification.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** BOD or load: show conversions (mg L⁻¹ to g m⁻³; seconds to days).
- **Explain** a data pattern downstream of an outfall: link BOD, oxygen and indicator species in sequence.
- **Evaluate** a management strategy: say which level it acts at, its cost and effectiveness, who pays and what it misses.
- **To what extent**: weigh point-source successes against non-point challenges, then judge.
