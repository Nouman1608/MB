Condensed recall for **3.1.3 Section C: Coastal systems and landscapes** (OxfordAQA International AS
and A-level Geography 9635, Unit 1, option 1b). For the full explanation, read the
[Physical Geography 1 study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-geography-physical-geography-1/).

## Coasts as systems

| Systems term | Coastal example |
|---|---|
| **Inputs** | Wave, wind and tidal energy; sediment from rivers, cliff erosion and offshore |
| **Stores** | Beaches, spits, dunes, offshore bars, mudflats |
| **Flows / transfers** | Longshore drift, onshore and offshore movement, wind blowing sand inland |
| **Outputs** | Sediment lost offshore or to another cell, energy dissipated |
| **Negative feedback** | Storm waves comb sand offshore into a bar; the bar makes waves break early, reducing erosion until calmer waves return the sand |
| **Dynamic equilibrium** | A beach whose shape changes with each storm but stays roughly balanced over time |

## Energy at the coast

- **Waves** form as wind blows over the sea; their energy depends on wind speed, duration and
  **fetch**.
- **Tides** raise and lower the level at which waves attack; a large tidal range spreads wave energy
  over a wider zone.
- **Currents** (including rip currents and longshore currents) move water and sediment.
- **High-energy coasts** face long fetches and powerful waves: erosion exceeds deposition, giving
  cliffs and rocky platforms. **Low-energy coasts** are sheltered: deposition exceeds erosion, giving
  beaches, mudflats and saltmarsh.

## Erosion, transport and waves

- **Erosion:** hydraulic action (water compresses air in cracks), abrasion (rock fragments scour),
  attrition (fragments wear each other down), solution (chemical dissolving of soluble rock).
- **Longshore drift:** waves approach at an angle, swash carries sediment obliquely up the beach,
  backwash returns it straight down under gravity, giving net movement along the coast.

The specification also lists **wave quarrying** (waves removing blocks of rock already loosened along
joints) and **cavitation** (collapsing air bubbles in breaking waves release shock waves that weaken
rock). **Transport** is by traction, suspension and longshore (littoral) drift; **deposition** occurs
where energy falls.

| | Constructive waves | Destructive waves |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Low, long wavelength | High, short wavelength |
| Swash and backwash | Swash stronger | Backwash stronger |
| Effect | Build beaches | Erode beaches |

**Sub-aerial processes** act on the land above the waves: weathering (freeze-thaw, salt
crystallisation, biological and chemical weathering), **mass movement** (rockfall, slumping on clay
cliffs, landslides) and **runoff**. Cliff shape depends on the balance between marine erosion at the
base and sub-aerial processes on the face: where marine erosion dominates, cliffs are steep; where
sub-aerial processes dominate, they are gentler.

## Landforms

**Erosional**
- **Headlands and bays:** differential erosion of alternating resistant and less resistant rock; soft
  rock becomes bays, hard rock remains as headlands.
- **Cave → arch → stack → stump:** a line of weakness becomes a cave; the cave is cut through to an
  arch; the roof collapses to leave a stack; the base erodes to a stump.
- **Wave-cut platform:** a gently sloping surface left at the base of a retreating cliff.

**Factors in cliff development:** rock type and structure (joints, faults, the dip of the beds
seaward or landward), wave energy, beach width in front of the cliff, and sub-aerial processes. As a
wave-cut platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy, so cliff retreat slows -- a
negative feedback.

Examples from beyond the UK: the limestone stacks of the **Twelve Apostles** on Victoria's coast in
Australia show the cave-arch-stack sequence and how quickly stacks can collapse.

**Depositional**
- **Spit:** deposition builds out into open water where the coastline changes direction.
- **Tombolo:** a spit that joins the mainland to an offshore island.
- **Compound spit:** a spit with several recurved ridges, each marking a former end as wind and wave
  direction changed.
- **Offshore bar:** a ridge of sediment below or just above the water offshore, often formed when
  destructive waves drag sand seaward.
- **Barrier beach / bar:** a spit or bar that grows across a bay, enclosing a lagoon.
- **Barrier islands:** long, narrow sand islands parallel to the coast, separated from it by lagoons,
  as along much of the Atlantic coast of the USA.
- **Sand dunes:** wind blows dry sand inland; pioneer plants trap it, and a succession of embryo,
  fore, yellow and grey dunes develops away from the sea.

**Estuarine mudflats and saltmarsh:** in sheltered estuaries and behind spits, fine silt settles at
slack water, aided by **flocculation** (clay particles clump together in salt water). Mudflats are
colonised by salt-tolerant pioneer plants, which trap more sediment and raise the surface, allowing a
succession of plants as the marsh is flooded less often.

## Sea level change

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Eustatic** | A global change in the volume of sea water: ice sheets growing or melting, thermal expansion |
| **Isostatic** | A local rise or fall of the land: crust depressed by ice sheets rebounds after they melt |
| **Tectonic** | Land raised or lowered by plate movement and earthquakes |

At the end of the last glacial period, melting ice sheets raised sea level greatly; most of that
rise occurred before roughly 6 000 years ago.

- **Coastlines of emergence** (land rising relative to sea): **raised beaches** and **marine
  platforms**, often with old cliffs and caves now above the reach of waves.
- **Coastlines of submergence** (sea rising relative to land): **rias** (drowned river valleys, as on
  the coast of Galicia in north-west Spain), **fjords** (drowned glacial troughs, as in Norway) and
  **Dalmatian coasts** (drowned parallel ridges forming long islands, as on the Croatian coast).
- **Recent and predicted climate change:** thermal expansion and melting ice raise sea level, and
  storms may intensify, increasing flooding and erosion on low coasts, deltas and islands.

## Sediment cells

A **sediment cell** is a largely closed system of **sources, transfers and sinks** of sediment.
Management in one place affects others. **Groynes** trap sediment moving by longshore drift and
starve the beach further along the drift direction, which becomes more vulnerable to erosion. This
knock-on effect is the key evaluation point.

A **sediment budget** compares inputs with outputs: a positive budget means the coast is building
out; a negative budget means it is eroding.

## Management

| Hard engineering | Soft engineering |
|---|---|
| Sea walls, groynes, rip-rap | Beach nourishment, dune stabilisation, managed retreat |
| Protect land directly; costly; can move the problem along the coast | Work with natural processes; cheaper and more sustainable; less immediate protection |

**Managed retreat** lets the sea reclaim land instead of defending it. It is favoured on cost and
sustainability but is socially contentious, because it means abandoning land and property.

**Sustainable approaches:** **shoreline management plans** divide the coast by sediment cell and
choose, for each stretch, to **hold the line**, **advance the line**, carry out **managed
realignment** or take **no active intervention**. **Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM)**
coordinates all users and agencies -- fishing, tourism, ports, conservation, housing -- over a whole
coastline rather than defending one town at a time.

## Case studies

A local-scale coastal environment (processes, landscape outcomes and sustainable management
challenges) and a contrasting coastal landscape (risks and opportunities for human occupation, and
human responses of resilience, mitigation and adaptation). Name places and use only figures you are
sure of.

**Contrasting landscape example: the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta and Sundarbans (Bangladesh and
India).** **Opportunities:** fertile land for rice, fishing, and the Sundarbans mangrove forest for
timber, honey and protection from waves. **Risks:** low elevation, tropical cyclones and storm
surges -- the 1970 Bhola cyclone killed hundreds of thousands of people -- plus river flooding,
subsidence and rising sea level. **Responses:** a cyclone preparedness programme with volunteer
warning networks, raised **cyclone shelters**, embankments around polders, and protection and
replanting of mangroves. Deaths from later cyclones have been far lower, a strong example of
increased resilience.

## Exam traps

- Describing the cave-to-stump sequence without the process behind each step.
- Swapping constructive and destructive wave features.
- Evaluating management without the sediment cell knock-on effect.
- Treating hard engineering as always better or always worse.
- Writing about management in general when a named coast is required.
- Confusing eustatic (global sea volume) with isostatic (local land movement).
- Using only UK examples: the specification requires landscapes from beyond the UK.

## Self-test

1. Describe longshore drift.
2. How do headlands and bays form?
3. Distinguish a spit from a tombolo.
4. What is a sediment cell?
5. Why can groynes cause erosion elsewhere?
6. Distinguish a high-energy from a low-energy coast.
7. What is cavitation?
8. Give one example of negative feedback on a coast.
9. Distinguish eustatic from isostatic sea level change.
10. Name two landforms of a coastline of submergence and one of emergence.
11. How does saltmarsh develop?
12. What are the four shoreline management policy options?

**Answers:** 1. Waves arrive at an angle, swash moves sediment obliquely up the beach, backwash
returns it straight down, so sediment moves along the coast. 2. Differential erosion of hard and soft
rock. 3. A spit is a ridge built out from the coast; a tombolo is a spit that links the mainland to an
island. 4. A largely closed system of sediment sources, transfers and sinks. 5. They trap sediment
and starve beaches further along the drift direction. 6. High-energy coasts have powerful waves and
erosion dominates; low-energy coasts are sheltered and deposition dominates. 7. Air bubbles in waves
collapse, releasing shock waves that weaken rock. 8. A widening wave-cut platform makes waves break
earlier, slowing cliff retreat (or offshore bars reducing wave energy). 9. Eustatic is a global
change in sea volume; isostatic is a local rise or fall of the land. 10. Submergence: rias and fjords
(or Dalmatian coasts); emergence: raised beaches. 11. Silt settles in sheltered water, pioneer
salt-tolerant plants colonise and trap more sediment, the surface rises and a succession of plants
follows. 12. Hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment and no active intervention.
