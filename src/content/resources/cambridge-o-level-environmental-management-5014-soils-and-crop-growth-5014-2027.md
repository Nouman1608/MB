> **Syllabus edition note.** This guide follows the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 2.1 explains why crops grow well in some places and badly in others: the soil they root in, and
the weather and seasons above it. The
[Topic 2 Land guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-land/) summarises each feature
in a table. This page explains the links between particle size, pore spaces, water and air, compares soil
types side by side, and practises interpreting climate data, which is how the "interpret" outcome is
examined.

## What the syllabus asks

- **2.1.1** Describe the composition of soils: mineral particles (sand, silt and clay); organic content
  (living organisms, namely plants, animals, fungi and bacteria, and organic matter from decomposition);
  gases; water. Knowledge of percentage composition of soils is not required.
- **2.1.2** Describe how soil composition is important for crop growth: mineral particles; nutrient
  content (organic content and inorganic ions: nitrogen as nitrate ions NO₃⁻, phosphorus as phosphate ions
  PO₄³⁻, potassium as potassium ions K⁺); pH (acidic, neutral and alkaline); pore spaces; gas content;
  water content and drainage; ease of cultivation.
- **2.1.3** Explain why loam soils are a good medium for crop growth: ideal combination of mineral
  particles and pore spaces; retains moisture; contains organic content and inorganic ions; allows excess
  water to drain; easy to cultivate.
- **2.1.4** Define weather as "the day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere in a location".
- **2.1.5** State that some areas of the world experience wet seasons and dry seasons that impact crop
  growth.
- **2.1.6** Describe and interpret conditions suitable for the optimum rate of photosynthesis and crop
  growth: length of growing season; optimum weather conditions; daylight hours.

## What soil is made of

Soil has four components, and each one does a job for the crop:

| Component | What it includes | Job for the crop |
|---|---|---|
| Mineral particles | Sand (largest), silt, clay (smallest), from weathered rock | Anchor roots; their size controls pore spaces, drainage and nutrient holding |
| Organic content | Living plants, animals (such as earthworms), fungi and bacteria; organic matter from decomposition | Decomposers release nutrients; organic matter holds water and binds particles into crumbs |
| Gases | Air in the pore spaces | Oxygen for respiration by roots and soil organisms |
| Water | Held in the pore spaces and as films around particles | Taken up by roots for photosynthesis; carries dissolved ions into the plant |

You do not need the percentages of each, but you should be able to say what each component is and why it
matters.

## Particle size: the key to everything

Particle size decides the size of the **pore spaces** between particles, and pore size decides how water
and air behave.

- **Sand** particles are large, so the pores between them are large. Water drains through quickly and the
  soil is well aerated, but water and dissolved nutrients are easily lost (leached), so sandy soils dry out
  and are often infertile.
- **Clay** particles are tiny, so the pores are tiny. Clay holds water and nutrients well, but drains
  slowly. When wet it can become waterlogged, with little air, and is sticky and heavy to plough; when dry
  it sets hard and cracks.
- **Silt** is in between in size and behaviour.

| Property | Sandy soil | Clay soil | Loam |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pore spaces | Large | Very small | Mixture of large and small |
| Drainage | Fast | Slow; can waterlog | Excess water drains |
| Water retention | Poor | High | Retains moisture |
| Air content | High | Low when wet | Good |
| Nutrient holding | Poor; nutrients leached | Good | Good |
| Ease of cultivation | Easy, light | Difficult, heavy | Easy |

## Nutrients and pH

Crops take up nutrients as **inorganic ions** dissolved in soil water. The syllabus names three:

| Ion | Element supplied | Background: main use in the plant |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrate, NO₃⁻ | Nitrogen | Making proteins for growth, especially leaves |
| Phosphate, PO₄³⁻ | Phosphorus | Root growth and healthy development |
| Potassium, K⁺ | Potassium | Healthy flowers and fruit; resistance to disease |

**Organic content** supplies nutrients too: decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down dead plants,
animal waste and other organic matter, releasing ions into the soil.

**pH** describes how acidic or alkaline the soil is. Most crops grow best in soil that is close to
**neutral**. In very acidic or very alkaline soil, some nutrients become less available to roots, and
growth slows.

## Why loam is ideal

Loam contains a balanced mixture of sand, silt and clay, plus organic matter. The syllabus gives five
reasons it is a good medium for crop growth, and each one is explained by its composition:

1. **Ideal combination of mineral particles and pore spaces**: sand gives large pores for air and drainage;
   clay gives small pores that hold water.
2. **Retains moisture**: the clay and organic matter hold water, so roots do not dry out between rains.
3. **Contains organic content and inorganic ions**: decomposition supplies nutrients, and clay holds them.
4. **Allows excess water to drain**: the larger pores stop waterlogging, so roots get oxygen.
5. **Easy to cultivate**: it is neither sticky like clay nor loose like sand, so it is easy to plough and
   plant.

## Weather, seasons and growing conditions

**Weather** is "the day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere in a location": temperature, rainfall,
sunshine, wind and humidity today or this week. (Climate, in Topic 4, is based on weather over many years.)

**Wet and dry seasons.** In some parts of the world, much of the year's rain falls in a wet season,
followed by a long dry season. Farmers usually plant at the start of the wet season so crops grow while
water is available; in the dry season crops can only grow with irrigation, or the land is left unplanted.
If the wet season starts late or ends early, yields fall.

**Conditions for the optimum rate of photosynthesis and crop growth.** Photosynthesis needs light, carbon
dioxide, water and a suitable temperature. The syllabus names three conditions:

- **Length of growing season**: the number of months when temperature and water allow growth. A longer
  season allows larger yields or more than one harvest a year.
- **Optimum weather conditions**: warm (but not too hot) temperatures, enough rain or irrigation, and
  sunshine. Frost, drought, very high temperatures and storms all reduce growth.
- **Daylight hours**: more hours of light each day mean more time for photosynthesis.

## Worked example: interpreting climate data

*Invented data* for a farming region:

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean temperature (°C) | 24 | 25 | 27 | 29 | 30 | 28 | 27 | 27 | 27 | 26 | 25 | 24 |
| Rainfall (mm) | 5 | 8 | 15 | 40 | 120 | 260 | 300 | 280 | 190 | 60 | 10 | 5 |

*Question (original):* A crop needs at least 100 mm of rain a month and temperatures between 20 °C and
32 °C. Identify the months suitable for growing it without irrigation, and describe the pattern of rainfall.
[4]

*Model answer:* May to September are suitable: every month from May (120 mm) to September (190 mm) has at
least 100 mm of rain [1], and temperatures in those months, 27 °C to 30 °C, are within the range [1]. The
region has a wet season from May to September, with the highest rainfall in July (300 mm) [1], and a dry
season from November to February with 10 mm or less each month [1].

*Technique:* quote figures with units, and check both conditions for every month before answering.

## Common mistakes

- Giving percentages of soil components; they are not required.
- Saying clay "drains well" or sand "holds water".
- Forgetting gases as a soil component, or not saying why roots need oxygen.
- Writing "NPK" without naming the ions when the question asks for them.
- Calling loam ideal without giving reasons linked to its composition.
- Defining weather in a way that describes climate ("average conditions over 30 years").
- Interpreting data without quoting figures.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** composition: four components, with examples.
- **Explain** why loam is good: give the reason, then link it to the crop's needs (air, water, nutrients).
- **Define** weather with the syllabus wording.
- **Interpret**: read off values, quote them, and apply the conditions given.
