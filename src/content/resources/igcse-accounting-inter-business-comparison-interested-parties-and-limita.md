## What the syllabus asks for

| Section | You must understand |
|---|---|
| 6.3 | why two businesses' ratios can differ, and what goes wrong when you compare them |
| 6.4 | the decisions that ten kinds of reader make from the figures: owners, managers, employees, banks, suppliers, customers, investors and lenders, club members, governments / tax authorities, and others such as public and environmental bodies |
| 6.5 | why statements fall short: historic cost, how accounting policies are applied, and non-financial aspects such as workforce skill, business location and the economic climate |

## 6.3 Inter-business comparison

A ratio on its own says little. Setting it beside the same ratio for a
similar business gives a yardstick. The trouble is that two businesses can
return different ratios for reasons that have nothing to do with
management skill. The syllabus asks for two things: the factors behind the
differences, and the problems of making the comparison at all.

### A worked comparison

Dalbeck Pharmacy Limited and Meridew Tiles Limited are both trading
companies. Their latest results are summarised below (figures in $).

```
                              Dalbeck Pharmacy   Meridew Tiles
Revenue                              480,000         900,000
Cost of sales                        336,000         585,000
Gross profit                         144,000         315,000
Profit for the year                   40,000          99,000
Interest paid                              0          18,000
Average inventory                     30,000         140,000
Credit sales                          36,000         780,000
Trade receivables                      1,000         130,000
Credit purchases                     300,000         600,000
Trade payables                        25,000          90,000
Capital employed                     250,000         960,000
```

Capital employed is issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities.
Dalbeck has shares of 150,000 and reserves of 100,000 and no loans.
Meridew has shares of 400,000, reserves of 260,000 and a loan of 300,000.

| Ratio | Dalbeck Pharmacy | Meridew Tiles |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 30.00% | 35.00% |
| Profit margin | 8.33% | 11.00% |
| ROCE | 16.00% | 12.19% |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 11.20 times | 4.18 times |
| Inventory turnover (days) | 32.59 days | 87.35 days |
| Trade receivables turnover (days) | 10.14 days | 60.83 days |
| Trade payables turnover (days) | 30.42 days | 54.75 days |

ROCE uses profit for the year before interest, so Meridew's is
(99,000 + 18,000) ÷ 960,000 × 100 = 12.19%.

Read the table and the differences are large. Linking each one to a factor
is the skill the syllabus tests.

- Type of goods. Tiles are bulky and bought rarely, so inventory sits for
about 87 days. Medicines are bought constantly, so inventory is replaced
every 33 days or so.
- Cash or credit selling. Dalbeck takes most payments over the counter, so
only 10.14 days of credit sales are outstanding. Meridew sells to builders
on credit, so receivables take 60.83 days.
- Financing. Meridew's loan raises its capital employed, which lowers ROCE
even though its profit margin is higher.
- Pricing and margin. Meridew's gross profit margin is higher (35.00% against
30.00%), which can reflect what each sells and the prices it charges.
- Size. Meridew's larger purchases may earn a better price from suppliers
and longer credit, which fits its 54.75 payables days.

None of this shows that either business is run better. That is why a
conclusion should say what the ratios suggest and then name the
differences that limit the comparison.

### Accounting policies change the ratios

Suppose Meridew bought a delivery van for 60,000 at the start of the year.
Straight line over five years with no residual value gives depreciation of
12,000. A 25% reducing balance gives 15,000, which is 3,000 more.

```
                          Straight line    Reducing balance
Depreciation                     12,000              15,000
Profit before interest          117,000             114,000
Capital employed                960,000             957,000
ROCE                             12.19%              11.91%
```

Capital employed falls by 3,000 because the extra charge reduces reserves.
The same van and the same year give two ROCE figures. If Dalbeck chose
differently from Meridew, any gap between them would partly be a policy gap.
The size of the allowance for irrecoverable debts and the way inventory is
valued work in the same way.

### Problems of inter-business comparison

- Information may be missing. Another business may publish only summarised
statements, with no credit sales, credit purchases or opening inventory,
so some ratios cannot be calculated.
- Different year ends. A toy shop with a year end in January and one with a
year end in July show very different inventory and receivables.
- Different policies, as above.
- The businesses may not be alike in size, activity, age of assets, premises
owned or rented, or location.
- One year may be unusual for one of them.
- Non-financial aspects are ignored (see 6.5).

## 6.4 Interested parties

Every party reads the same statements but asks a different question, so
the answer to a 6.4 question is the decision plus the figure that supports
it.

### One set of figures, many readers

Thornquist Bakery is a sole trader. Its year-end figures (in $) are:
current assets 41,000 including inventory 9,000; current liabilities
30,000; trade payables 18,000; credit purchases 146,000. Its suppliers allow
30 days' credit.

- Current ratio = 41,000 ÷ 30,000 = 1.37 : 1.
- Acid test ratio = (41,000 − 9,000) ÷ 30,000 = 1.07 : 1.
- Trade payables turnover = 18,000 ÷ 146,000 × 365 = 45.00 days.

| Party | Figure it looks at here | Decision it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Bank | acid test 1.07 : 1 and profit | Whether to lend or extend an overdraft, since 1.07 : 1 suggests current liabilities can be met without selling inventory |
| Supplier | payables at 45.00 days against 30 allowed | Whether to keep offering credit or ask for payment sooner |
| Owner | profit for the year and capital | Whether the profit justifies the money tied up, and how much to withdraw |
| Manager | each expense against last year | Which costs to cut and what prices to set |
| Employees | profit and cash position | Whether jobs and a pay rise look safe |
| Tax authority | profit for the year | The tax to collect |

### The remaining parties

- Investors and lenders. An investor buying shares wants profit growth and
dividend prospects. A lender wants interest paid on time and repayment at
the end, so the lender studies liquidity, assets and the existing
non-current liabilities. State both, because a question on "investors and
lenders" may reward the difference.
- Customers. They want to know that the business will survive to honour
guarantees, supply spare parts and keep service going.
- Governments / tax authorities. Beyond tax, they use statements to decide
on grants and to gather statistics on business activity.
- Club members. Ibsley Chess Club has 60 members paying 60 each, so
subscriptions are 3,600. Its running costs for the year were 4,100. The
deficit is 500, or 500 ÷ 60 = 8.33 per member. Members use this to decide
whether to raise the subscription to about 68.33, cut costs or run a
fund-raising event.
- Other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies. A local
council or environmental body may use the statements to judge whether the
business can keep providing jobs and meet its obligations to the local
community and environment.

Parties can want opposite things. Owners may want a high withdrawal while
lenders prefer cash to stay in the business. A good answer names the party,
the decision, and the figure used.

## 6.5 Limitations of accounting statements

The statements are a useful but incomplete picture. The syllabus names
three causes.

### Historic cost

Assets are recorded at what they cost. Lowick Mill Limited has a profit
before interest of 54,000 and capital employed of 400,000 (shares 200,000,
reserves 100,000, loan 100,000). Its land cost 80,000 many years ago and is
now worth 240,000.

- ROCE with the land at historic cost = 54,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = 13.50%.
- If the land were shown at 240,000, reserves would rise by 160,000, capital
employed would be 560,000 and ROCE would be 54,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = 9.64%.

Same business, same profit, two answers. Historic cost also means figures
from different years use money of different purchasing power, so a rise in
revenue may partly be higher prices.

### Application of accounting policies

The van example above shows it. Depreciation method, inventory valuation
and the allowance for irrecoverable debts are choices and estimates. The
statements are only as comparable as the policies behind them, and a change
of policy breaks the link with earlier years.

### Non-financial aspects

The statements record what can be measured in money. Three examples from
the syllabus:

- Skill of the workforce. A well-trained team may improve quality and
service, but its skill is not an asset in the statement.
- Location of the business. A site near passing trade can raise sales for
years, and a road closure can ruin it. Neither appears.
- Economic climate. A recession lowers customers' spending, and rising
interest rates raise borrowing costs.

## Common errors

- Writing "Meridew is better" from one ratio. Give the ratio, the reason it
might differ, and a limit on the comparison.
- Listing parties without a decision. "Banks use the statements" earns no
mark; "banks use the acid test to decide whether to lend" does.
- Treating "investors and lenders" as identical.
- Naming only two of the three 6.5 limitations when asked for the
limitations in the syllabus.
- Giving a non-financial aspect as a figure. It must be something the
statements cannot show.
