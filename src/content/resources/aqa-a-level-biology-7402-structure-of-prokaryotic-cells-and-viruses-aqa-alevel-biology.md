This guide goes deeper on one sub-topic of AQA AS and A-level Biology (7401/7402): **3.2.1.2 Structure of prokaryotic cells and of viruses**, part of 3.2.1 Cell structure in Topic 2 (Cells). It is AS content, so it can appear on both AS papers and, at A-level, on Paper 1 and Paper 3. For the wider picture of eukaryotic organelles, read the [eukaryotic and prokaryotic cell structure guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-biology-eukaryotic-and-prokaryotic-cell-structure/). Condensed recall for this sub-topic is in the [prokaryotic cells and viruses revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-structure-of-prokaryotic-cells-and-viruses-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [cell structure practice questions](/resources/a-level-aqa-biology-eukaryotic-and-prokaryotic-cell-structure-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Specification point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells | Use size as a first distinguishing feature, and use micrograph measurements to show it |
| Cytoplasm that lacks membrane-bound organelles | Say which structures are absent, and which (ribosomes) are still present |
| Smaller ribosomes | Compare ribosome size between the two cell types |
| No nucleus; a single circular DNA molecule free in the cytoplasm and not associated with proteins | Contrast with linear, protein-bound DNA in a eukaryotic nucleus |
| A cell wall that contains murein, a glycoprotein | Contrast with cellulose (plants, algae) and chitin (fungi) walls |
| Many prokaryotic cells have plasmids, a capsule and one or more flagella | Name and recognise them; structural details are not required |
| Viruses are acellular and non-living | Explain what acellular means and why viruses are not classed as living cells |
| Virus structure: genetic material, capsid and attachment protein | Label and state the role of each part |

The specification limits what you need: you do not need the internal structure of a flagellum or a capsule, and there is no list of named bacteria or viruses to learn here. How bacteria divide (binary fission) and how viruses replicate belong to 3.2.2, *All cells arise from other cells*, and the structure of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is examined in 3.2.4 with the immune system.

## The four named differences, one at a time

### 1. Membrane-bound organelles

A prokaryotic cell has a cell-surface membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes, but no internal membranes forming organelles. That means no mitochondria, no chloroplasts, no endoplasmic reticulum, no Golgi apparatus and no lysosomes. The processes those organelles carry out in eukaryotes still happen: respiration enzymes, for example, work in the cytoplasm and on the cell-surface membrane. In an exam answer, "prokaryotes have no organelles" is wrong, because ribosomes are organelles that are not membrane-bound. Write "no membrane-bound organelles".

### 2. Smaller ribosomes

Prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller than those in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. Textbooks describe them as 70S compared with 80S; the specification itself only says "smaller", so either form scores. Ribosomes in mitochondria and chloroplasts are also the smaller type, which is one reason these organelles are compared with prokaryotes.

### 3. DNA: no nucleus, circular, free, not protein-bound

This bullet carries three separate ideas, and examiners reward each one:

- there is no nucleus and no nuclear envelope, so the DNA lies in the cytoplasm;
- the main DNA molecule is circular, not linear;
- it is not associated with proteins, whereas eukaryotic nuclear DNA is wound around histones to form chromosomes.

The same description (short, circular, not associated with protein) also applies to the DNA inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, which you meet again in 3.4.1.

### 4. A murein cell wall

All prokaryotic cells in this course have a cell wall containing murein, which the specification calls a glycoprotein. Plant and algal walls are made of cellulose and fungal walls of chitin, so "has a cell wall" never distinguishes a bacterium from a plant cell. Name the material.

## The optional extras: plasmids, capsule and flagella

The specification says *many* prokaryotic cells have these, so never state that all bacteria possess them.

| Structure | What it is | Function you can give |
|---|---|---|
| Plasmid | Small, circular loop of DNA separate from the main DNA molecule | Carries a few genes, for example for antibiotic resistance; can be passed between cells |
| Capsule | Layer outside the cell wall | Protects the cell and helps it stick to surfaces |
| Flagellum (plural flagella) | Long, thin extension from the cell surface | Rotates to move the cell |

## Size and how to show it with data

Bacterial cells are usually a few micrometres long, while most eukaryotic cells are tens of micrometres across. Viruses are smaller again, typically tens to a few hundred nanometres, which is why they can be seen only with an electron microscope.

**Worked example (invented data).** An electron micrograph at × 12 000 shows a rod-shaped bacterium 30 mm long.

```
Convert:   30 mm = 30 000 µm
Actual:    image size ÷ magnification = 30 000 ÷ 12 000
Answer:    2.5 µm
Compare:   a cheek cell about 60 µm across is 60 ÷ 2.5 = 24 times longer
```

Questions often ask you to use such a figure to "suggest whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic". A length of 2.5 µm supports prokaryotic, but size alone is weak evidence: add a structural feature visible in the image, such as no nucleus.

## Viruses: acellular and non-living

Acellular means not made of cells. A virus has no cell-surface membrane, no cytoplasm and no ribosomes, so it cannot make proteins or release energy by itself. It can only be replicated inside a living host cell, using that cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP. This is why the specification treats viruses separately and calls them non-living.

| Part | Description | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic material | DNA or RNA | Carries the genes for making new virus particles |
| Capsid | Protein coat around the genetic material | Protects the genetic material |
| Attachment protein | Protein on the outer surface | Binds to complementary receptor molecules on a host cell surface, so only certain cell types are infected |

Some viruses also have a lipid envelope taken from the host's cell-surface membrane. HIV is an example, and its full structure is covered in 3.2.4. The attachment-protein idea links forward to immunology: the shape of the attachment protein is what an antibody or a vaccine targets.

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** Compare the structure of a bacterium with that of a virus. **[4]**

> Both contain genetic material and are surrounded by an outer protective layer *(similarity first: "compare" means similarities as well as differences)*. A bacterium is a cell with a cell-surface membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes, but a virus is acellular and has none of these *(paired difference in one sentence)*. A bacterium has a murein cell wall, whereas a virus has a protein capsid *(names the material in both)*. A virus has attachment proteins on its surface for binding to host cells; bacteria may have a capsule or flagella instead *(uses "may" for optional structures)*.

Each sentence makes a direct comparison using "whereas" or "but". Separate descriptions of each organism, with the reader left to do the comparing, usually score less.

## Common mistakes

- Saying prokaryotes "have no organelles" or "no ribosomes".
- Writing "bacteria have no DNA" when meaning no nucleus.
- Describing the bacterial wall as cellulose, or as murein in plant cells.
- Stating that all bacteria have plasmids, capsules or flagella.
- Calling a virus "a very small bacterium" or "a prokaryote".
- Describing the capsid as a membrane or as lipid; it is protein.
- Saying a virus "uses its own ribosomes".

## Exam technique for AQA command words

| Command word | What earns the marks on this sub-topic |
|---|---|
| Name / Give | One precise term per mark: "murein", "capsid", "plasmid" |
| Describe | Features only, for example "circular DNA, not associated with proteins, free in the cytoplasm" |
| Compare | Paired statements with "whereas", including at least one similarity |
| Explain | A feature plus its consequence, for example "no ribosomes, so a virus cannot make its own proteins" |
| Suggest | Apply to an unfamiliar organism or micrograph, using size and named structures as evidence |
