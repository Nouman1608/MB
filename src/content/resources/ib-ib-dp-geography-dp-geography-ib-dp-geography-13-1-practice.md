## Section A

**1.** Define sovereign debt. **[1]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by weaponised interdependence. **[2]**

**3.** State two reasons why a country's debt can become harder to service without any new borrowing. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between a geopolitical risk and an economic risk, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one indicator that shows a country's exposure to a fall in commodity prices. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Country F has export earnings of 32 billion dollars a year and must pay 8 billion dollars a year in foreign debt service.

**(a)** Calculate its debt service ratio. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the new ratio if export earnings fall by 25%. **[2]**

**(c)** The currency falls from 40 to 60 local units per dollar. Calculate the percentage increase in the local-currency cost of the 8 billion dollars of debt service. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how a fall in tourism can lead to a shortage of imported goods. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why the lease of Hambantota port in Sri Lanka is a contested example of geopolitical risk. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest two ways in which a country could reduce its exposure to economic shocks transmitted through trade. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine how interdependence both creates and reduces geopolitical and economic risks. **[10]**

**11.** "Economic crises are caused more by global forces than by national decisions." Discuss this statement with reference to examples. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Money owed by a national government to lenders, at home or abroad [1]. **[1]**

**2.** States that control central nodes of global networks, such as financial payment systems or technology supply chains [1], can use that position to monitor others or to cut them off, turning interdependence into a source of power and risk [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The country's currency falls in value, so foreign-currency debt costs more in local money [1]; its export earnings fall, so less foreign currency is available to pay [1]. (Also credit a rise in interest rates on variable-rate debt.) **[2]**

**4.** A geopolitical risk arises from political relations between states or groups, such as armed conflict or sanctions [1]; an economic risk arises from financial and trade relationships, such as a debt crisis or a commodity price collapse [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, the share of export earnings that comes from a single commodity [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 8 / 32 x 100 = **25%** [1].

**(b)** New earnings: 32 x 0.75 = 24 billion dollars [1]. Ratio: 8 / 24 x 100 = **33.3%** [1].

**(c)** Before: 8 x 40 = 320 billion units; after: 8 x 60 = 480 billion units [1]. Increase: (480 - 320) / 320 x 100 = **50%** [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Tourism earns foreign currency when visitors spend money in the country [1]. If tourist numbers fall, as in Sri Lanka after the 2019 bombings and during the COVID-19 pandemic, foreign-currency reserves run down [1], so the country cannot afford to pay for imports such as fuel, food and medicine, causing shortages [1]. **[3]**

**8.** In 2017 Sri Lanka leased the port, built with Chinese loans, to a Chinese state-owned company for 99 years [1]. Some commentators saw this as debt-trap diplomacy, a loss of sovereignty over a strategic asset [1]; other researchers argue there is no evidence of a deliberate strategy, noting that the project was Sri Lanka's proposal and that most of its debt was owed to other lenders [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Diversify exports so that income does not depend on one commodity or service [1], which means a fall in one price has a smaller effect on total earnings [1]. Build up foreign-currency reserves [1], so that imports can still be paid for during a temporary shock [1]. (Also credit diversifying suppliers or borrowing in local currency.) **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines interdependence and identifies types of risk [1].
- Creates risk: shocks travel through financial systems, as in the 2008 financial crisis [1].
- Creates risk: dependence on one supplier or route exposes countries to disruption, as with chokepoints [1].
- Creates risk: states controlling network hubs can weaponise interdependence through sanctions or export controls [1].
- Creates risk: foreign-currency debt transmits global interest-rate and currency shocks [1].
- Reduces risk: trade ties can raise the cost of conflict between partners [1].
- Reduces risk: international institutions and coordination provide emergency finance and joint responses [1].
- Reduces risk: diversified global supply can replace a failed local harvest or supplier [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that interdependence redistributes risk, increasing it for dependent states and reducing it for diversified ones [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines an economic crisis and identifies global and national causes [1].
- Global: the 2008 financial crisis spread from US housing to countries with sound domestic policies [1].
- Global: falling commodity prices and rising global interest rates hit dependent and indebted states [1].
- Global: pandemics and wars disrupt tourism, trade and supply chains [1].
- National: Sri Lanka's tax cuts and fertiliser ban worsened its 2022 crisis [1].
- National: Venezuela's economic management deepened the effects of the oil price fall from 2014 [1].
- National: weak regulation of banks allowed risks to build up, as in Iceland before 2008 [1].
- Global and national causes interact: shocks become crises where domestic weaknesses exist [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence for both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on which matters more in the examples discussed [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" rewards showing how global and national causes interact. A single cause rarely explains a crisis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating a percentage fall in earnings but then dividing by the old figure.
- Forgetting that currency depreciation raises the local cost of debt, not the amount owed in dollars.
- Presenting a contested interpretation as fact.
- Explaining a crisis without the transmission mechanism.
