This guide covers subtopic **8.1 Diet** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to list the principal sources of, and describe the dietary importance of, carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, vitamins (C and D only), mineral salts (calcium and iron only), fibre (roughage) and water. You must name the diseases and describe the symptoms resulting from deficiencies of vitamin C (scurvy), vitamin D (rickets), calcium (rickets) and iron (anaemia), and understand the concept of a balanced diet.

The [Human nutrition guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-human-nutrition/) covers the whole topic. This page gives each nutrient in full, with the exam wording for sources, importance and deficiency, and works through a diet calculation. Use the [8.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-diet-revision-notes/) for recall and the [8.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-diet-practice/) to test yourself.

## What a diet must provide

Food is needed for **energy**, for **growth and repair** of tissue, and to keep the body working. The nutrients in the syllabus are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, two vitamins, two mineral salts, fibre and water. Each has a specific role and specific sources. A good exam answer names the nutrient, gives at least one food source, and states the role precisely.

## The nutrients

### Carbohydrates

- **Sources**: bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, cereals, sugar.
- **Importance**: the main source of **energy**. Glucose is broken down in respiration to release energy.

### Lipids (fats and oils)

- **Sources**: butter, cooking oil, nuts, fatty meat, oily fish, cheese.
- **Importance**: an **energy source** and store (lipids contain more energy per gram than carbohydrates), **insulation** under the skin to reduce heat loss, and a part of **cell membranes**.

### Proteins

- **Sources**: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese, beans, peas, nuts.
- **Importance**: **growth and repair** of tissues. Proteins are also used to make **enzymes**, and to make hormones and antibodies. They are made of amino acids, and excess is broken down in the liver.

### Vitamin C

- **Sources**: citrus fruits such as oranges and lemons, blackcurrants, peppers and fresh vegetables.
- **Importance**: keeps the skin, gums and other body tissues healthy, and helps wounds to heal.
- **Deficiency disease**: **scurvy**. **Symptoms**: bleeding gums, loose teeth, bruising, slow healing of wounds, and painful joints.

### Vitamin D

- **Sources**: oily fish, eggs, margarine, and milk with added vitamin D. The skin also makes vitamin D when exposed to sunlight.
- **Importance**: helps the intestine to **absorb calcium**, so that bones and teeth form properly.
- **Deficiency disease**: **rickets** (mainly in children). **Symptoms**: soft, weak bones that bend under the weight of the body, so the legs are bowed.

### Calcium

- **Sources**: milk, cheese, yoghurt, green vegetables, and fish eaten with their bones.
- **Importance**: needed to build **strong bones and teeth**. It also helps blood to clot and muscles to work.
- **Deficiency disease**: **rickets**, with the same symptoms of soft, bent bones.

### Iron

- **Sources**: red meat, liver, spinach, beans and fortified cereals.
- **Importance**: needed to make **haemoglobin**, the red pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen.
- **Deficiency disease**: **anaemia**. **Symptoms**: tiredness and weakness, pale skin, and shortness of breath on exercise, because the blood carries less oxygen to the tissues.

### Fibre (roughage)

- **Sources**: vegetables, fruit, wholemeal bread, brown rice, whole grain cereals.
- **Importance**: fibre is mostly cellulose from plant cell walls. It cannot be digested, but it adds **bulk** to the food so that the muscles of the gut can push it along by peristalsis. A lack of fibre can lead to constipation.

### Water

- **Sources**: drinks, and most foods, especially fruit and vegetables.
- **Importance**: water is the **solvent** in which reactions take place, it is a major part of blood plasma and so carries substances, and it is used in sweat to cool the body. Water is lost in urine, sweat and breath and must be replaced.

## Choosing the wording

Compare a weak answer with a strong answer.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Protein is for muscles" | "Protein is needed for growth and repair of tissues, and for making enzymes" |
| "Iron is for blood" | "Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells" |
| "Vitamin D is for bones" | "Vitamin D helps the absorption of calcium, which makes bones hard" |
| "Fibre is good for digestion" | "Fibre adds bulk so that food can be moved through the gut by peristalsis" |
| "Lipids give energy" (for insulation) | "Lipids are an energy store and form a layer under the skin that reduces heat loss" |

## The balanced diet

A **balanced diet** contains **all the nutrients in the correct proportions**, and provides **the right amount of energy**, for the person's needs. A balanced diet includes carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, vitamins, mineral salts, fibre and water.

The correct amount depends on the person.

| Person | Why needs differ |
|---|---|
| Growing child or teenager | Need more protein for growth, and calcium and vitamin D for bones |
| Active worker or athlete | Needs more energy, because muscle contraction uses a lot |
| Pregnant woman | Needs more protein, calcium and iron to build the baby and replace blood |
| Older person | Less active, so needs less energy |

If a person eats **more energy than they use**, the excess is stored as fat. If they eat **too little**, the body uses its stores and the person loses mass.

### Worked example: energy in a meal

*Illustrative energy values: bread 1050 kJ per 100 g, butter 3000 kJ per 100 g, apple 220 kJ per 100 g. Calculate the energy in a snack of 80 g bread, 10 g butter and 150 g apple.*

1. Bread: 80 g is 0.8 of 100 g, so 0.8 x 1050 = **840 kJ**.
2. Butter: 10 g is 0.1 of 100 g, so 0.1 x 3000 = **300 kJ**.
3. Apple: 150 g is 1.5 of 100 g, so 1.5 x 220 = **330 kJ**.
4. Total = 840 + 300 + 330 = **1470 kJ**.

Butter makes up only 10 g of the snack, but contributes 300 kJ, which shows how energy-rich lipids are: 3000 kJ per 100 g compared with 1050 kJ for the bread. (The numbers here are for practice. Use the values in the question when you meet a table.)

### Worked example: choosing foods for a need

*A girl has anaemia. Suggest a food to add to her diet and explain.* Red meat, liver or spinach would help, because they contain iron. Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, so with more iron her red blood cells can carry more oxygen and her tiredness should lessen. Adding vitamin C-rich foods is often advised, but this goes beyond the syllabus statements, so the key answer is iron and haemoglobin.

## Linking nutrients to the rest of the course

Diet connects with several other topics. Seeing the links helps you to answer longer questions.

- **Iron and haemoglobin**: haemoglobin is in the red blood cells that carry oxygen (topic 11.4), so iron links diet to transport in humans.
- **Calcium and vitamin D**: both relate to bone and tooth structure. Teeth are also covered in topic 8.2.
- **Fibre**: peristalsis (topic 8.2) is the wave of contractions that fibre helps.
- **Proteins**: enzymes are proteins (topic 5), and the digestion of protein produces amino acids (topic 8.2).
- **Lipids and carbohydrates**: these are the energy sources for respiration (topic 10).
- **Water**: acts as a solvent in digestion, excretion and transport (topic 3.1).

## Reading data about diets

Exam questions often give a table of the energy needs of different people or the nutrient content of foods. Use the following approach.

1. **Read the units**: kilojoules (kJ) for energy, grams or milligrams for nutrients.
2. **Check the portion**: values are usually per 100 g, so scale up or down to the real portion.
3. **Compare like with like**: before saying one person needs more, check that both values are for the same time period, such as per day.
4. **Link to the cause**: more energy for an active person (muscle contraction), more protein and calcium for a growing child, more iron for a pregnant woman.

*Example.* A table shows that a teenage boy needs 12 000 kJ per day and an office worker needs 10 000 kJ per day (illustrative values). The difference is 2 000 kJ, which is 2 000 divided by 10 000 x 100 = 20 per cent more. The boy needs more energy because he is growing and probably more active. He also needs more protein and calcium, for the same reason.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a disease without the nutrient that is missing, or the other way round.
- Giving "anaemia" as a lack of vitamin C. Anaemia is a lack of iron; scurvy is a lack of vitamin C.
- Forgetting that rickets can come from a lack of vitamin D or calcium.
- Saying fibre is "digested" or "gives energy". It is not digested.
- Saying water gives energy.
- Using only one source when the question asks for "principal sources".
- Using "balanced diet" to mean "eating less". It means all nutrients in the right proportions and the right energy.

## Command words

- **List**: write the sources, one after another, without detail.
- **Describe**: state the importance and the symptoms of a deficiency.
- **Name**: give the disease.
- **Understand** and **explain**: say why a diet is balanced for a particular person.
