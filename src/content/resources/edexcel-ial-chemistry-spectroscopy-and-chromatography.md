## Shift values used on this page

The ranges below are approximate values for the examples on this page. In a question, use the shift values that question gives you.

| Carbon environment | Approximate ¹³C δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| Alkyl carbon (C–C, C–H only) | 5–45 |
| Carbon bonded to O (alcohols, ethers, esters) | 50–90 |
| C=C in alkenes; carbons in a benzene ring | 110–160 |
| Carbonyl carbon of an acid or ester | 160–185 |
| Carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde or ketone | 190–220 |

| Proton environment | Approximate ¹H δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| Alkyl C–H (CH₃, CH₂, CH away from functional groups) | 0.7–1.7 |
| H on the carbon beside a C=O (for example CH₃CO–) | 2.0–2.7 |
| H on a carbon bonded to O or Cl | 3.3–4.5 |
| O–H in alcohols (varies) | 1.0–5.0 |
| Alkene C–H | 4.5–6.5 |
| Aldehyde –CHO | 9.0–10.0 |
| Carboxylic acid –COOH | 10.0–12.0 |

Chemical shift is measured in ppm from a reference compound, tetramethylsilane (TMS), which is set at δ = 0. The more electronegative atoms near a nucleus, the higher its δ value tends to be.

## 15.17 Accurate relative molecular mass

In Topic 10D the molecular ion gave a whole-number Mr, which many formulae share. A high resolution mass spectrometer measures m/z to four decimal places. Atomic masses are not exact whole numbers, so each formula has its own accurate mass.

On the ¹²C scale, carbon-12 is exactly 12.0000. The other atoms differ slightly from whole numbers. On this page use: **H 1.0078, C 12.0000, O 15.9949, N 14.0031**.

### Worked example 1: calculating an accurate Mr

Calculate the accurate relative molecular mass of C₅H₁₁NO₂.

1. Carbon: 5 × 12.0000 = 60.0000
2. Hydrogen: 11 × 1.0078 = 11.0858
3. Nitrogen: 1 × 14.0031 = 14.0031
4. Oxygen: 2 × 15.9949 = 31.9898
5. Total: **117.0787**

Keep four decimal places in every term.

### Worked example 2: from accurate mass to structure

A neutral organic liquid gives a molecular ion at m/z = 114.1043. Four formulae have whole-number mass 114: C₇H₁₄O, C₆H₁₀O₂, C₆H₁₄N₂ and C₅H₁₀N₂O. Its IR spectrum has a strong absorption near 1715 cm⁻¹ and no broad O–H absorption.

| Formula | Accurate Mr | Difference from 114.1043 |
|---|---|---|
| C₇H₁₄O | 114.1041 | 0.0002 |
| C₆H₁₀O₂ | 114.0678 | 0.0365 |
| C₆H₁₄N₂ | 114.1154 | 0.0111 |
| C₅H₁₀N₂O | 114.0791 | 0.0252 |

1. The closest match is **C₇H₁₄O**.
2. C₇H₁₄O has one C=O or one C=C or one ring. The IR band near 1715 cm⁻¹ points to C=O, so it is an aldehyde or ketone.
3. Possible structures include heptanal, heptan-2-one, heptan-3-one and heptan-4-one. Isomers share an accurate mass, so NMR is needed to decide (worked example 4).

## 15.18 What carbon-13 NMR tells you

About 1% of carbon atoms are ¹³C. In a strong magnetic field, ¹³C nuclei absorb radio-frequency radiation at a frequency that depends on the surrounding atoms, so carbons in different positions give signals at different δ values.

A ¹³C NMR spectrum therefore shows:

- **how many different carbon environments** the molecule has (one peak for each)
- **what kind of environment each one is** (from its δ value), for example an alkyl carbon, a carbon bonded to oxygen or a C=O carbon.

## 15.19 Using ¹³C NMR data

Carbons related by symmetry are in the same environment and give one peak between them. So the number of peaks equals the number of different carbon environments, which can be fewer than the number of carbons.

### Worked example 3: 2-methylbutan-2-ol

Structure: (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₃, five carbons. Predict the ¹³C spectrum.

1. The two CH₃ groups on C2 are equivalent: one environment.
2. C2 carries the OH: one environment.
3. The CH₂: one environment.
4. The CH₃ at the end of the ethyl group: one environment.
5. **Four peaks** for five carbons.
6. Shifts from the table: C2 (bonded to O) falls in 50–90 ppm; the other three are alkyl carbons in 5–45 ppm.

To "justify" a number of peaks, name each environment and say which carbons are equivalent.

## 15.20 Proton NMR

### Low resolution: environments and peak areas

In a low resolution ¹H spectrum, each proton environment gives one peak. Its δ value tells you the type of proton (use the table). The **relative peak area** (or the ratio given on the spectrum) is proportional to the number of ¹H atoms in that environment.

### High resolution: the (n+1) rule

At high resolution, a peak splits because of protons on the **adjacent** carbon. If there are n non-equivalent protons on neighbouring carbons, the peak splits into **n + 1** lines.

| H atoms on adjacent carbons | Pattern you see |
|---|---|
| none | singlet (one line) |
| one | doublet (two lines) |
| two | triplet (three lines) |
| three | quartet (four lines) |
| six, from two CH₃ groups | septet (seven lines) |

- Equivalent protons do not split each other. The six H atoms in (CH₃)₂ give one peak.
- Count H on the neighbouring carbons, not on the carbon you are looking at.
- An O–H proton normally appears as a singlet and does not split its neighbours.
- A carbon with no H, such as a C=O carbon, passes on no splitting. A CH₃ next to C=O is a singlet.

### Worked example 4: predicting the spectrum of heptan-4-one

Heptan-4-one is CH₃CH₂CH₂COCH₂CH₂CH₃. It is symmetrical about the C=O.

| Environment | Number of H | Neighbouring H | Splitting | Approximate δ / ppm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CH₃ (two equivalent) | 6 | 2 | triplet | 0.9 |
| middle CH₂ (two equivalent) | 4 | 3 + 2 = 5 | sextet (6 lines) | 1.6 |
| CH₂ next to C=O (two equivalent) | 4 | 2 | triplet | 2.4 |

Peak area ratio 6 : 4 : 4, which simplifies to **3 : 2 : 2**. The ¹³C spectrum would show **four peaks**: C=O (190–220 ppm) and three alkyl carbons.

This separates the isomers from worked example 2: heptan-2-one, for instance, would show a 3H singlet near 2.1 ppm from CH₃CO.

### Worked example 5: deducing a structure

Compound P is a ketone, C₅H₁₀O. Its high resolution ¹H spectrum shows:

- δ 1.1, doublet, area 6
- δ 2.1, singlet, area 3
- δ 2.6, septet, area 1

1. Area 6 as a doublet: two equivalent CH₃ groups next to one H, so (CH₃)₂CH–.
2. Area 1 as a septet: one H with six neighbours, the CH of that same group. δ 2.6 places it next to C=O.
3. Area 3 as a singlet at δ 2.1: a CH₃ next to C=O with no H on the neighbouring carbon, so CH₃CO–.
4. Join the pieces: CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂, **3-methylbutan-2-one**.
5. Check: 6 + 3 + 1 = 10 H and five carbons, matching C₅H₁₀O.

## 15.21 What chromatography does

Chromatography separates the components of a mixture using a **mobile phase**, which moves, and a **stationary phase**, which does not. Each component is distributed between the two phases to a different extent: the more strongly it is held by the stationary phase, the more slowly it moves.

| Method | Stationary phase | Mobile phase |
|---|---|---|
| Paper | Water held in the paper fibres | Solvent soaking up the paper |
| Thin-layer (TLC) | Thin layer of solid such as silica or alumina on a plate | Solvent moving up the plate |

## 15.22 Rf values

For a one-way chromatogram:

**Rf = (baseline to centre of spot) ÷ (baseline to solvent front)**

As a ratio of two lengths, Rf has no units; its value is from 0 to 1.

### Worked example 6: a TLC plate

A TLC plate is run until the solvent front is 7.6 cm above the baseline. Three spots appear at 1.6 cm, 4.2 cm and 6.5 cm.

1. Spot 1: 1.6 ÷ 7.6 = **0.21**
2. Spot 2: 4.2 ÷ 7.6 = **0.55**
3. Spot 3: 6.5 ÷ 7.6 = **0.86**

On polar silica, spot 1 is held most strongly, so it is probably the most polar component.

### Why Rf values differ

- Substances differ in how strongly the stationary phase holds them (for example by hydrogen bonds to silica or to water in paper) and in how soluble they are in the mobile phase.
- The same substance gives a different Rf if you change the solvent, the stationary phase or the temperature. To compare an unknown with a known substance, run both on the same plate under the same conditions.
- In any one solvent, two different compounds may give the same Rf. A matching Rf suggests, but does not prove, that two spots are the same compound.

## 15.23 HPLC and GC

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and gas chromatography (GC) are both **column chromatography**. The stationary phase is packed into or coats the inside of a column, and the mobile phase carries the mixture through it. Components leave the column at different times. The time from injection to detection is the **retention time**, and components separate because their retention times differ.

| | HPLC | GC |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile phase | Liquid solvent pumped under high pressure | Unreactive carrier gas |
| Stationary phase | Small solid particles packed in the column | Liquid coating on a solid support or on the column wall |
| Sample | Dissolved in the solvent | Must vaporise in the heated column |

Retention time depends on the column, temperature and flow rate, so compare it with a standard run under the same conditions. A match is evidence, not proof, of identity.

**With mass spectrometry.** The column output can feed straight into a mass spectrometer (GC–MS or HPLC–MS). Each separated component gives its own mass spectrum, which is matched against a database of known spectra. This is far more reliable than retention time alone. The specification names two uses:

- **Forensics**: identifying drugs, poisons or fire accelerants in samples from a scene.
- **Drug testing in sport**: separating the substances in an athlete's urine sample and identifying any banned compound.

## Common errors

- Choosing a formula by whole-number mass when the question gives accurate masses.
- Counting carbon atoms instead of carbon environments for the number of ¹³C peaks.
- Measuring Rf from the bottom of the plate instead of from the baseline.
- Calling the GC mobile phase "air" rather than an unreactive carrier gas.
