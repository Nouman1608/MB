The 9675 specification lists "the significance of the natural world, its beauty and possibly its
despoliation" among the ideas that can be explored in Unit 2. This guide applies that angle to the
Section B poetry essay. The four selections and the essay task are covered in the
[Unit 2 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-2-section-b/);
this page goes further into one angle and shows which poems to pair for it. Quotations are kept very
short here: use your open-book copy for exact wording.

## Unpacking the specification's phrase

The wording has three parts, and a strong essay uses all three.

- **"Significance"**: the question is never just what nature looks like, but what the poem makes it
  mean. The specification uses significance to mean weighing construction, context and
  interpretation together.
- **"Its beauty"**: beauty can console, teach, inspire or mislead. Ask who finds it beautiful and
  why.
- **"Possibly its despoliation"**: the spoiling of nature by people, machines, cities, war or
  neglect. The word "possibly" matters: not every poem shows damage, so ask whether the threat is
  present, implied or absent.

## Four relationships between people and nature

Most poems in the selections set up one of these relationships, and many shift between them.

1. **Nature as teacher or moral guide.** The landscape shapes the speaker's mind and conscience.
2. **Nature as mirror.** The landscape reflects a mood, a loss or a historical moment.
3. **Nature as victim.** Human action damages a place, and the poem records the cost.
4. **Nature as indifferent force.** The natural world goes on without caring for human hopes, or
   destroys them.

## The four poets

**Wordsworth.** In "Lines composed a few miles above Tintern Abbey" the remembered landscape of the
Wye is a moral teacher that has sustained the speaker in towns and cities; the small signs of
human life, farms and smoke, are absorbed into the scene rather than spoiling it. "Nutting" is the
clearest poem of despoliation in the selection: a boy finds an untouched hazel bower and tears it
down, and his triumph gives way to a sense of pain at what he has done, so the poem ends by warning
that there is a spirit in the woods. "Michael" places the threat in economics: a shepherd's land is
put at risk by debt, his son leaves for the city, and the unfinished sheepfold remains as a ruin.
"Composed upon Westminster Bridge" reverses expectations by finding natural beauty in a city seen at
dawn, before the day's smoke begins.

**Frost.** Frost's nature is rarely simply beautiful. "A Brook in the City" imagines a stream buried
under urban building and asks whether it still troubles the city's sleep. "The Wood-Pile" finds a
stack of cut wood abandoned in a frozen swamp, slowly rotting back into the earth, so human labour
is reclaimed by nature. In "Out, Out –" a farm buzz saw kills a boy against a backdrop of mountain
ranges at sunset, setting rural beauty beside mechanised danger. "Desert Places" turns a snowfield
at night into an image of the emptiness within the speaker.

**Hardy.** Hardy often reads landscape as a sign of a historical or personal condition. "The
Darkling Thrush" presents a winter landscape at the turn of the century as a corpse, and the old
thrush's song offers a hope the speaker cannot share. "Neutral Tones" fixes a failed love in a
drained winter scene. "Channel Firing" lets naval gunnery practice wake the dead in a coastal
churchyard, so war disturbs the land and its history. "The Convergence of the Twain" presents the
iceberg and the ship as destined partners, a natural force punishing human pride. "In Time of 'The
Breaking of Nations'" turns to small rural acts that will outlast war.

**Heaney.** Heaney's land holds history. "Bogland" presents the bog as a preserving ground that
keeps the past, moving downward rather than outward like a prairie. In the bog poems ("The Tollund
Man", "The Graubelle Man", "Bog Queen", "Punishment") the land preserves bodies and makes the
reader confront old and modern violence together. "Requiem for the Croppies" ends with growth rising
from the graves of rebels. "The Toome Road" sets armoured vehicles on a country road at dawn, a
military presence intruding on the speaker's land; and "Act of Union" writes the political
relationship between England and Ireland through the image of land and body.

## Pairs that work for this angle

| Pairing | Why it works |
|---|---|
| "Nutting" and "Tintern Abbey" (Wordsworth) | The same poet's nature as victim and as teacher |
| "A Brook in the City" and "The Wood-Pile" (Frost) | Nature buried by people against people reclaimed by nature |
| "Channel Firing" and "The Darkling Thrush" (Hardy) | Land disturbed by war; land as a sign of the age |
| "Bogland" and "The Toome Road" (Heaney) | Land as preserver of history; land intruded on by force |

Pairs work best when they share a question and differ in their answer.

## Methods that carry the angle

- **Personification** of landscape gives it feeling, making damage feel like injury.
- **Semantic fields** of violence (tearing, burying, firing) against fields of growth or rest.
- **Structural turns** where the mood shifts from delight to guilt, or from despair to hope.
- **Sound**: harsh consonants for damage, slower open sounds for stillness.
- **Viewpoint**: a child, an adult remembering, a passer-by, a voice from the grave.

## Worked example on an original poem

The poem is **original**, written for this guide. It is not from any set selection.

> They cleared the orchard in a week of rain.
> The saws were yellow, and the men were kind;
> they left one tree to mark the boundary line.
> In spring it flowered, white and out of place,
> a wedding guest who has not heard the news.

**Model paragraph:**

> The poem presents despoliation as quick, practical and almost polite, which makes the loss harder
> to judge. "A week of rain" sets the destruction in ordinary time and weather, and the bright
> "yellow" saws are machines rather than villains, while "the men were kind" refuses to blame the
> workers. The single tree left "to mark the boundary line" turns a living thing into a surveyor's
> marker, so nature survives only as a sign of ownership. The turn comes with "In spring", when the
> tree's flowering is beautiful but "out of place", and the final simile of "a wedding guest who has
> not heard the news" gives it innocence and pathos: the natural world keeps its rhythm after the
> human decision that has destroyed its meaning. Beauty here does not console; it exposes the loss.

The paragraph covers all three parts of the phrase: beauty (the flowering), despoliation (the
clearing) and significance (the tree as boundary marker and the irony of the final image).

## Common mistakes

- **Describing scenery.** Always move from what nature is like to what the poem makes it mean.
- **Assuming all nature poems praise nature.** Frost and Hardy often show it as indifferent.
- **Forgetting "possibly".** Arguing that a poem shows no despoliation can be a strong point.
- **Biography in place of analysis.** Wordsworth's life in the Lakes explains nothing unless linked
  to a line.
- **Comparing two poems separately.** Integrate them around a shared question.

## Exam technique

Section B is an essay on your selection, usually discussing more than one poem. For this angle,
choose one poem of beauty and one of damage, then a third that complicates the distinction. Unit 2
is open book, so mark short phrases in advance and spend exam time on analysis. Allow about an hour.
