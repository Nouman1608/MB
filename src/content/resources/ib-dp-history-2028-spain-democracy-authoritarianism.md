**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 10, Democracy, authoritarianism and conflict in Spain (circa 1920 to 2020), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics: challenges to Spanish democracy in the 1920s and early 1930s; the immediate causes of the Spanish Civil War; the reasons for Nationalist victory; the impact of Franco's regime; the transition to and establishment of democracy (to 1982); and successes and challenges in Spain from 1982 to 2020.

## Challenges to democracy in the 1920s and early 1930s

*Political, social and economic factors.* Spain in 1920 was a constitutional monarchy under Alfonso XIII, but real politics was managed by local bosses (*caciques*) and elections were rigged. Society was deeply divided: great landowners and poor landless labourers in the south; industrial workers in Barcelona and the Basque country with strong anarchist and socialist unions; a powerful Church; an army with a political outlook; and regional nationalism in Catalonia and the Basque Country. Colonial war in Morocco, including the disaster at Annual in 1921, weakened the government.

*Primo de Rivera regime (1923 to 1930).* General **Miguel Primo de Rivera** seized power in September 1923 with the king's backing. He suspended the constitution, banned strikes and suppressed Catalan symbols but won socialist cooperation by arbitration committees. The Rif War was ended with France in 1925, and public works expanded. But he alienated intellectuals, many officers and Catalans. The Depression and a falling peseta undermined support; he resigned in January 1930, and the monarchy was damaged by association with him.

*The Second Republic and polarization.* After republican successes in the municipal elections of April 1931, Alfonso XIII left Spain and the **Second Republic** was proclaimed. The constitution of December 1931 was liberal and secular, and gave women the vote. Reforms divided Spain further.

*Azaña and Gil-Robles.* **Manuel Azaña**, prime minister 1931 to 1933, pursued army reform, church reforms (an end to state funding, restrictions on religious schools, divorce), the Catalan Statute (1932) and land reform. Each created enemies: army officers, the Church and landowners on the right, and impatient peasants and anarchists on the left who thought reform too slow. A coup attempt by General Sanjurjo failed in 1932. In November 1933 the right won elections. **José María Gil-Robles** led the Catholic party CEDA, which supported the government without taking power at first. In October 1934, when CEDA ministers entered government, socialists and miners rose in Asturias; the revolt was crushed by the army under Franco's direction, with severe repression. The Republic had become a battle between two blocs, with moderate centre parties collapsing.

## Immediate causes of the Civil War

*Election of 1936.* In February 1936 the left formed the **Popular Front**, a coalition of Republicans, socialists and communists, and narrowly won seats in a system that magnified small vote differences. The result was seen by the right as a threat.

*Policies and violence.* The new government released prisoners, restored Catalan autonomy and accelerated land seizures. Street violence rose: the Falange, a fascist party led by José Antonio Primo de Rivera, clashed with left-wing groups. Landowners and employers resisted. Political murders multiplied, including that of the monarchist leader **Calvo Sotelo** on 13 July 1936, in revenge for the killing of a left-wing officer.

*Military coup of July 1936.* Generals including Mola and Sanjurjo had been plotting. The rising began in Spanish Morocco on 17 July and spread to the mainland. It failed to take the major cities of Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia, so the country was split and civil war began.

*Emergence of Franco.* **Francisco Franco**, commander of the Army of Africa, was a prestigious general. After Sanjurjo died in an air crash and Mola was seen as weaker, the Nationalist generals chose Franco in September 1936 as commander, and he was made head of state on 1 October. In April 1937 he merged the Falange and Carlist movements into one party under his control.

## Reasons for Nationalist victory

*Foreign involvement.* Germany sent the Condor Legion, which bombed Guernica in April 1937, and Italy sent tens of thousands of troops, with aircraft and supplies. The USSR sent tanks, aircraft and advisers to the Republic, and some 35,000 or more volunteers of the **International Brigades** joined it. But Britain and France adopted non-intervention, and the Republic had to pay for Soviet arms, sending its gold reserves to Moscow. Foreign aid to the Nationalists was larger and more reliable.

*Military and strategic factors.* The Nationalists had the professional Army of Africa and a unified command. The Republic lost much of its army to the rebels and had to build a new force with militias. Key phases included the siege of Madrid (1936), Nationalist capture of the north in 1937, the Republic's Ebro offensive (1938), and the collapse of Catalonia in early 1939. Madrid surrendered at the end of March 1939.

*Economic factors.* The Republic held the industrial areas of Catalonia and the Basque region, but it lost the northern industry in 1937, and wartime collectivization in some areas disrupted production. The Nationalists held more farmland and received credit and fuel from sympathetic firms and states.

*Role of individuals.* Franco's patient, methodical leadership and ruthless determination contrasted with the Republic's divided leaders, such as Largo Caballero and Negrín. The Republic was weakened by fighting among its own supporters: anarchists, communists and socialists clashed, as in Barcelona in May 1937. The Nationalists shared a common enemy and were more disciplined. Historians debate whether foreign aid or Republican division mattered most.

## Impact of Franco's regime

*Political methods.* Franco held absolute power as *Caudillo*. He balanced the army, Falange, Church and monarchists. A single party and a controlled parliament (the Cortes, from 1942) gave a facade of institutions. The Law of Succession (1947) declared Spain a kingdom with Franco as regent for life. He used patronage and played groups against each other.

*Treatment of opposition and use of violence.* After 1939 tens of thousands were executed, and many more imprisoned, put in labour camps or forced into exile; estimates vary. Laws punished "political responsibility" and Freemasonry and communism. Trade unions were banned. Repression was heaviest in the 1940s and eased later but continued until the end.

*Economic policies.* Until 1959 the regime pursued **autarky**, aiming for self-sufficiency, with heavy state control. The result was stagnation and hunger in the 1940s. The Stabilization Plan of 1959, devised by technocrats, opened the economy to foreign investment, tourism and trade, producing rapid growth in the 1960s, urbanisation and emigration.

*Social and cultural impact.* The regime promoted "National Catholicism". The Church controlled much of education and moral life. Women were expected to be wives and mothers, with fewer legal rights. Censorship was strict. Catalan, Basque and Galician languages and identities were suppressed, which fuelled nationalism. ETA, a Basque armed group, was founded in 1959.

## Transition to democracy (to 1982)

*Political role of Juan Carlos.* Franco named Prince **Juan Carlos** as his successor in 1969, expecting continuity. After Franco's death on 20 November 1975, Juan Carlos became king and supported change. He appointed **Adolfo Suárez** prime minister in July 1976.

*Role of individuals.* **Manuel Fraga Iribarne**, a former Franco minister, represented reform from within and later led the conservative Alianza Popular. Suárez, also from the regime, negotiated with the opposition. The Law for Political Reform (1976), approved by the Francoist Cortes and a referendum, opened the way to elections. The Communist Party was legalised in April 1977, and free elections in June 1977 were won by Suárez's UCD. The Moncloa Pacts (1977) agreed economic measures, and the constitution of 1978 was approved by referendum, creating a parliamentary monarchy with regional autonomies.

*External support and social factors.* Western Europe and the USA encouraged democracy; Spain wished to join the European Community. Social change under late Francoism had created a larger middle class, secularisation and a desire for stability, and the Civil War memory made leaders cautious. Violence, notably ETA attacks and the attempted coup of 23 February 1981 by Colonel Tejero, tested the system. The king's television appearance helped end it.

## Successes and challenges (1982 to 2020)

*Party politics.* The PSOE under **Felipe González** won a landslide in 1982 and governed until 1996. The People's Party (PP) under **José María Aznar** governed from 1996 to 2004, and the PSOE under **José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero** from 2004 to 2011. Social reforms under Zapatero included same-sex marriage (2005).

*Economic and social change.* Spain modernised, with high growth in the late 1980s and the 1990s and a property boom in the 2000s. The financial crisis of 2008 brought mass unemployment, austerity and the 15-M protest movement of 2011, and new parties such as Podemos and Vox emerged.

*International relations.* Spain joined NATO in 1982; a referendum in 1986 kept it in. It joined the European Economic Community on 1 January 1986 and later the euro.

*Regional autonomy.* The "state of autonomies" gave Catalonia and the Basque Country self-government. ETA violence continued until it announced an end in 2011 and dissolved in 2018. In Catalonia, tension over a new Statute of Autonomy (2006) and a court ruling in 2010 led to an independence referendum in October 2017, which the central government held to be unlawful, and direct rule was imposed.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best single factor for the Civil War's outcome?**
Do not decide before the question. Rank foreign aid, unity and military factors.

**Was the transition a model?**
Historians debate it: praised for peaceful change but criticised for the "pact of forgetting".
