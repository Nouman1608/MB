Section B of Paper 3 asks for one essay on a non-Shakespeare set play. It is worth 25 of the paper's 50
marks and, like Section A, is assessed on **AO1 to AO5**, each 20%. The set plays here are modern, so
this guide concentrates on the theatrical methods of twentieth- and twenty-first-century drama and on how
to build AO5 from production choices and critical approaches. For the paper's format, see the
[Paper 3 overview](/resources/a-level-english-literature-paper-3-shakespeare-and-drama/).

## What the section asks of you

On each play you choose an **(a) question**, an essay on the whole play, or a **(b) question** on a
passage from the play printed on the paper. The play is not allowed in the exam room. For 2026 the set
plays are Lynn Nottage's *Sweat*, Eugene O'Neill's *Long Day's Journey Into Night* and Wole Soyinka's
*Kongi's Harvest*. For 2027 they are *Sweat*, *Kongi's Harvest* and Timberlake Wertenbaker's *Our
Country's Good*. Check the list for your examination year.

## How this differs from Paper 1 drama

The close-reading skills from Paper 1 still apply: stage directions as evidence, dialogue rhythm,
dramatic irony, structure. Two things change. First, **AO5** means your argument must engage with and
evaluate other interpretations. Second, the set plays here use a wide range of modern theatrical
methods, and knowing the conventions they use or resist is part of AO1 and AO2.

## Modern theatrical methods

### Naturalism and its limits

**Naturalism** aims to present life as it is lived: realistic settings, everyday speech, characters
shaped by environment and heredity. *Long Day's Journey Into Night* is a landmark of the mode: one day in
August 1912, four acts moving from morning to midnight, a single family in a single house. Within it,
O'Neill uses long, detailed stage directions that describe characters' appearance and inner states; ask
what these do that dialogue cannot, and how a production can realise them. Notice also where naturalism
strains: the fog and foghorn that thicken through the play work symbolically as well as realistically.

### Time and structure

Modern dramatists often break chronological order. *Sweat* moves between 2000 and 2008, so the audience
sees consequences before causes, and every earlier scene is watched with **structural dramatic irony**.
Ask what the play gains by revealing outcomes first, and how the final scenes reshape the opening.

### Ritual, music and non-naturalistic theatre

*Kongi's Harvest* satirises a dictator who seeks to take control of a traditional harvest festival, and
it draws on song, dance and ritual. Here the stage is not a realistic room but a space for public
performance, so analysis should consider spectacle, music and crowd scenes as dramatic language, and how
satire works through exaggeration and contrast between the regime and traditional authority.

### Metatheatre

*Our Country's Good* follows convicts and officers in the first penal colony in New South Wales as they
rehearse a play. Scenes of rehearsal invite the audience to think about theatre itself: what performance
can do for people denied a voice. Productions often have actors double roles across convicts and
officers, a staging choice that sharpens questions of class and power.

### Setting and space

A single bar, a family living room, a palace courtyard: the set defines who can meet whom. Ask who enters
the space, who is excluded, and what objects carry meaning across the play.

## Context for modern drama

Context is relevant when it explains a dramatic choice: the economic pressures on industrial towns that
*Sweat* stages; the autobiographical family history behind O'Neill's play; post-independence politics in
Soyinka's satire; the eighteenth-century colony in Wertenbaker's. Keep it brief and attach it to a scene.

## Building AO5 for modern plays

Modern plays have rich production histories, and their critical reception is part of what you revise.
Three reliable sources of alternative readings:

- **Production choices**: casting, design, doubling, cuts, and how a staging changes a scene's meaning.
- **Critical approaches**: a Marxist or economic reading of *Sweat*; a postcolonial reading of *Kongi's
  Harvest*; a feminist reading of Mary Tyrone; a reading of *Our Country's Good* that questions whether
  it celebrates theatre too optimistically.
- **Audience positions**: how the play might land with its first audience and with audiences now, or in
  different countries.

### Annotated model paragraph

On *Long Day's Journey Into Night*, responding to a question on blame within the family:

> O'Neill structures the play so that blame circulates and never settles. Its long confessional
> speeches are shared among all four Tyrones, and each explains, but does not excuse, the harm the
> speaker has done. One reading treats Mary as the play's centre and its victim, with the men's failures
> orbiting her addiction; a feminist reading might go further, arguing that the house she insists has
> never been a real home is her prison. Yet the structure resists any single centre: the final act gives the
> longest confessions to the men, and Mary enters only at the end, lost in the past. The play is
> stronger read as a study of a family in which everyone is both cause and casualty, which is why its
> ending offers recognition rather than resolution.

The paragraph uses **structure** (AO2), presents **two readings** and tests them against the text (AO5),
and ends with a **judgement** (AO3).

## The (b) passage answer

1. **Locate** the passage in the play's structure and, for non-chronological plays, in its timeline.
2. **Thesis** naming the passage's dramatic function in the question's terms.
3. **Analyse** dialogue, stage directions, movement, set and sound.
4. **Link outward**: echoes, contrasts and consequences elsewhere.
5. **AO5**: how a different staging or critical approach would change the passage, evaluated.

## The (a) essay

Turn the question into a claim before planning. Asked to discuss the dramatic presentation of
friendship in *Sweat*, a weak thesis says that friendship is important and is tested. A stronger one
argues something about method: "Nottage stages friendship in a single shared bar, so that when Cynthia's
promotion to management divides her from Tracey, the audience watches the space itself split; because
the play has already shown the outcome in 2008, every earlier scene of loyalty is shadowed by what we know
is coming." It names a staging choice, uses the play's structure, and gives the essay a line to argue.

- A thesis that takes a position, with a judgement if the question says "How far".
- Moments from across the play, including the ending.
- Theatrical method in every paragraph, not just character and theme.
- AO5 woven through two or three paragraphs.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading modern plays as novels**: discussing character psychology with no reference to staging.
- **Ignoring stage directions** in plays that rely heavily on them.
- **Flattening structure**: discussing a non-chronological play as if it were told in order.
- **AO5 as a list**: alternative readings mentioned but not weighed.
- **Context without a choice**: political or biographical background left unconnected.
