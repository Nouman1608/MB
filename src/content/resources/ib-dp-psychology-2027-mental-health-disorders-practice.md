> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the mental health disorders strand of the health and well-being context (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice. Answers use major depressive disorder, but you may use any disorder in the guide's list.

Related: [mental health disorders study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain one biological explanation of a mental health disorder. **[4]**

**2.** Outline the cognitive triad. **[3]**

**3.** Explain one reason why the prevalence of a mental health disorder may differ between two cultures. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the role of one environmental factor in a mental health disorder. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Sam, 17, has felt low for months. After failing a mock exam he told his sister: "I'm hopeless. Nothing I do ever works and it won't get better." He now avoids revision, and his results fall further. His father had depression as a young man, and Sam recently lost a close friend who moved away.

**(a)** Use a cognitive model to explain Sam's thinking and behaviour. **[3]**
**(b)** Use the diathesis-stress model to explain why Sam may be at risk. **[3]**

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> A survey reports that 6 per cent of adults in Country X have depression, compared with 12 per cent in Country Y. Researchers note that in Country X, many people describe emotional problems through tiredness and aches, and mental illness is rarely discussed openly.

**(a)** Suggest two reasons the reported rates may not reflect true differences. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one way the researchers could investigate the question more validly. **[3]**

**7.** Evaluate the use of twin studies to explain a mental health disorder. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the value of cognitive models in understanding a mental health disorder. **[4]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate research into mental health disorders in terms of how well the causes of one disorder are understood. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any one explanation, for example genetic vulnerability. Disorders can be partly inherited **[1]**. Twin studies compare concordance in identical twins (all genes shared) and non-identical twins (about half shared) **[1]**; higher concordance in identical twins suggests heritability **[1]**. Qualification, such as heritability being below 100 per cent so environment also matters, or a diathesis-stress link **[1]**. Equally creditable: neurotransmitter explanations (with the limit that a drug's effect does not prove cause), HPA axis and brain structure.

**2.** Beck's cognitive triad is a pattern of negative thinking **[1]** about the self, the world (experiences) and the future **[1]**. It is maintained by negative schemas and, for example, overgeneralising from one failure **[1]**.

**3.** Any one reason with explanation, up to **[3]**. For example: stigma, where mental illness is more stigmatised, fewer people seek help or report symptoms **[1]**, so recorded prevalence is lower **[1]**, even if the true rate is similar **[1]**. Others: somatisation (distress expressed physically and not recorded as depression), diagnostic tools developed in one culture, or real differences in exposure to stress.

**4.** Any one factor, for example stressful life events or adverse childhood experiences. The factor and what it is **[1]**; how it influences the disorder, such as chronic stress affecting thoughts and the stress system **[1]**; evidence, such as Brown and Harris finding that severe life events and lack of a confiding relationship were linked to depression **[1]**; a limitation, such as retrospective reports or the inability to randomise exposure **[1]**.

**5.** (a) Beck's model: Sam shows the cognitive triad **[1]**: negative view of self ("I'm hopeless"), world/experience ("nothing I do works") and future ("it won't get better") **[1]**. The negative schema leads to avoidance of revision, which confirms the belief, so the cycle continues **[1]**. Credit attributional style (internal, stable, global).

(b) Diathesis is a vulnerability **[1]**: Sam's father's history suggests a genetic vulnerability **[1]**. Stress, the failed exam and loss of a friend, can trigger the disorder in a vulnerable person **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Two reasons, each with explanation, up to **[4]**. Somatisation: people in Country X express distress through physical symptoms, so a depression screening tool may miss them **[1]**, lowering the reported rate **[1]**. Stigma: people may not report or seek help **[1]**, so under-diagnosis **[1]**. Also credit differences in measurement tools or sampling.

(b) Any one: use a local (emic) approach, such as interviews with local practitioners to adapt the measure **[1]**; check that the tool measures the same construct in both countries (construct validity) **[1]**; use the same method and sampling in both countries so the figures are comparable **[1]**.

**7.** Description (up to 2): twin studies compare identical and non-identical twins to estimate heritability **[1]**, and for depression, heritability is moderate, not total **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4): strengths include separating genetic from environmental influence and large samples **[1]**; limitations include the equal environments assumption **[1]**, reductionism (does not explain what is inherited or how) **[1]**, heritability applies to populations, not individuals **[1]**, and results depend on how the disorder is diagnosed **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**8.** Value includes explanation: they identify thinking patterns that maintain the disorder **[1]**. Prediction and testing: patterns can be measured, for example with questionnaires **[1]**. Application: models led to cognitive behavioural therapy **[1]**. Limitation or balance, such as correlational evidence meaning that negative thinking may be a result of the disorder, or the models underplaying biological and social causes **[1]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Biological: genetic vulnerability and stress system, with evidence from twins, balanced by reductionism and unclear cause in imaging **[2]**.
- Cognitive: negative schemas and thinking patterns, with the problem of whether they cause or result from depression **[2]**.
- Environmental: life events and adversity, with retrospective bias and inability to randomise **[2]**.
- Cultural: prevalence and expression vary, so measures and samples need caution **[2]**.
- Causality: factors interact (diathesis-stress), so single-cause claims are weak; correlational designs dominate **[2]**.
- Measurement and bias: diagnosis based on symptom checklists, self-report, gender or cultural bias in diagnosis **[2]**.
- Perspective and responsibility: an integrated approach avoids reductionism, and researchers must avoid stigma **[2]**.
- A conclusion on how much is understood, for example causal pathways are partly understood but not for all groups **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** describing the disorder rather than explaining a cause.
- **Question 5:** retelling the scenario instead of using the model's terms.
- **Question 6:** giving reasons without explaining how they change the figures.
- **Question 9:** a list of causes without weighing the evidence, or only one concept.
