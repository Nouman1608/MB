Use these values throughout: Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K; relative atomic masses H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0. Give pH values to two decimal places.

## Questions

**1.** Define a Brønsted–Lowry acid and a Brønsted–Lowry base. **[2]**

**2.** Dihydrogenphosphate ions react with ammonia:

H₂PO₄⁻(aq) + NH₃(aq) ⇌ HPO₄²⁻(aq) + NH₄⁺(aq)

**(a)** Identify the two conjugate acid-base pairs. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation to show H₂PO₄⁻ acting as a base when it reacts with H₃O⁺. **[1]**

**3.**

**(a)** Define the term pH. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of a solution with [H⁺] = 6.3 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate [H⁺] in a cleaning solution of pH 9.62. **[1]**

**4.** A bottle contains 0.0290 mol dm⁻³ nitric acid.

**(a)** Calculate its pH. **[1]**
**(b)** 10.0 cm³ of this acid is diluted to 500 cm³ with water. Calculate the pH of the diluted solution. **[2]**

**5.**

**(a)** Explain the difference between a strong acid and a weak acid in terms of dissociation. Use an equation for each, with HCl and HCOOH. **[2]**
**(b)** A student writes: "0.0010 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is a weak acid because it is so dilute." Explain why the student is wrong. **[1]**

**6.** 2-methylpropanoic acid, (CH₃)₂CHCOOH, is a weak monoprotic acid with pKa = 4.86 at 298 K.

**(a)** Write the expression for Ka of 2-methylpropanoic acid. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of a 0.0840 mol dm⁻³ solution of the acid. **[3]**
**(c)** State the two assumptions you made in part (b). **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage of the acid molecules that are dissociated, and comment on whether assumption 2 is justified. **[1]**

**7.**

**(a)** Define the ionic product of water, Kw. **[1]**
**(b)** Define pKw. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the pH of 0.0420 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide at 298 K. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the pH of 0.00875 mol dm⁻³ strontium hydroxide, Sr(OH)₂, at 298 K. **[2]**

**8.** Three weak acids have these values at 298 K: acid P, Ka = 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³; acid R, pKa = 4.20; acid S, Ka = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Place the acids in order of strength, strongest first, showing how you compared them. **[2]**

**9.** Seven solutions, each 0.0900 mol dm⁻³, were tested with a pH meter at 298 K. The solutions were hydrochloric acid, ethanoic acid, ammonium chloride, sodium chloride, sodium ethanoate, aqueous ammonia and sodium hydroxide.

| Solution | J | K | L | M | N | O | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 8.9 | 1.0 | 11.1 | 5.1 | 13.0 | 7.0 | 2.9 |

**(a)** Identify each solution J to P. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the ammonium chloride solution has a pH below 7. **[2]**

**10.** A student compared two acids, X and Y, both 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ at 298 K. Each was diluted 10, 100 and 1000 times and the pH measured.

| Dilution factor | none | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acid X | 1.30 | 2.30 | 3.30 | 4.30 |
| Acid Y | 2.76 | 3.27 | 3.83 | 4.48 |

**(a)** Identify which acid is strong. Explain your answer using the data. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the pH of Y rises by about 0.5 for each of the first two tenfold dilutions. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why the rise in pH for Y between 100 and 1000 times dilution is larger than 0.5. **[1]**
**(d)** Use the undiluted reading for Y to calculate Ka for acid Y. **[2]**

**11.** In an experiment to find Ka, 1.35 g of lactic acid, CH₃CH(OH)COOH, was dissolved in water and the solution made up to 250 cm³. The pH was 2.54. Lactic acid is monoprotic.

**(a)** Calculate Ka for lactic acid. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate pKa. **[1]**

**12.** A student mixes 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid with 30.0 cm³ of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide at 298 K.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of the mixture. **[4]**
**(b)** At 50 °C, Kw = 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ (use this value). Calculate the pH of pure water at 50 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why pure water at 50 °C is neutral even though its pH is not 7.00. **[1]**
**(d)** Use the values of Kw at 298 K and 50 °C to deduce whether the ionisation of water is exothermic or endothermic. Explain your answer. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor [1]. A base is a proton (H⁺) acceptor [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write "proton" or "H⁺"; writing "hydrogen" alone is too vague to describe what is transferred.

**2. (a)** H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ [1]; NH₄⁺ and NH₃ [1].
**(b)** **H₂PO₄⁻ + H₃O⁺ → H₃PO₄ + H₂O** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check that each pair differs by one H⁺ and that the charges in your equation balance.

**3. (a)** pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], with [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³ [1].
**(b)** pH = −log₁₀(6.3 × 10⁻³) = **2.20** [1]
**(c)** [H⁺] = 10⁻⁹·⁶² = **2.40 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A definition of pH needs the minus sign and the base-10 log; "the negative log of hydrogen" is incomplete.

**4. (a)** Nitric acid is fully dissociated, so [H⁺] = 0.0290 mol dm⁻³; pH = **1.54** [1].
**(b)** New concentration = 0.0290 × 10.0/500 = 5.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = −log₁₀(5.80 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.24** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the dilution factor clearly before taking the log, so the method is visible.

**5. (a)** HCl is a strong acid: it is fully dissociated, HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) [1]. HCOOH is a weak acid: it is only partly dissociated, HCOOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCOO⁻(aq), with most molecules undissociated [1].
**(b)** Strength depends on the degree of dissociation, not concentration; HCl is still fully dissociated however dilute it is, so it is a dilute strong acid [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use a one-way arrow for the strong acid and an equilibrium arrow for the weak acid; the arrows carry the meaning.

**6. (a)** Ka = [H⁺][(CH₃)₂CHCOO⁻] / [(CH₃)₂CHCOOH] [1]
**(b)** Ka = 10⁻⁴·⁸⁶ = 1.38 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = √(1.38 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0840) = 1.08 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **2.97** [1].
**(c)** [H⁺] = [(CH₃)₂CHCOO⁻], because the ionisation of water is negligible [1]. The equilibrium concentration of the acid equals 0.0840 mol dm⁻³, because very little of it dissociates [1].
**(d)** 1.08 × 10⁻³/0.0840 × 100 = **1.3%**, which is small, so the assumption is justified [1].
*Examiner insight:* State each assumption as an equality between named concentrations; "the acid is weak" on its own does not say what was assumed.

**7. (a)** Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] (the ionic product of water) [1].
**(b)** pKw = −log₁₀Kw [1].
**(c)** [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴/0.0420 = 2.38 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **12.62** [1].
**(d)** [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.00875 = 0.0175 mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴/0.0175 = 5.71 × 10⁻¹³, so pH = **12.24** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Strontium hydroxide releases two OH⁻ per formula; using 0.00875 directly gives 11.94, a common slip.

**8.** Convert to one scale: P has pKa 2.89 and S has pKa 4.70 (or R has Ka 6.3 × 10⁻⁵) [1]. Order: **P > R > S** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion; an order with no comparison on a common scale is a guess.

**9. (a)** Acids: K = hydrochloric acid, P = ethanoic acid [1]. Bases: N = sodium hydroxide, L = ammonia [1]. Salts: M = ammonium chloride, O = sodium chloride, J = sodium ethanoate [1].
**(b)** NH₄⁺ is a weak (Brønsted–Lowry) acid [1]; it donates H⁺ to water, NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺, so [H⁺] > [OH⁻] [1].
*Examiner insight:* Explain the salt's pH through the ion that donates or accepts protons; saying "it is made from a strong acid" alone is not enough.

**10. (a)** X is strong [1]; its pH rises by exactly 1.00 for each tenfold dilution, so [H⁺] falls tenfold, matching full dissociation with [H⁺] = c [1].
**(b)** For a weak acid [H⁺] = √(Ka × c) [1]; a tenfold fall in c lowers [H⁺] by √10, which raises pH by log₁₀√10 = 0.5 [1].
**(c)** At high dilution a larger fraction of Y dissociates, so [HA] is no longer close to c and the square-root approximation fails [1].
**(d)** [H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁷⁶ = 1.74 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; Ka = (1.74 × 10⁻³)²/0.0500 = **6.04 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³**, using the unrounded [H⁺] (6.0 × 10⁻⁵ to two significant figures) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote numbers from the table to support "strong" or "weak"; a description with no data does not use the evidence the question gives.

**11. (a)** Mr = 90.0, so n = 1.35/90.0 = 0.0150 mol [1]; c = 0.0150/0.250 = 0.0600 mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁵⁴ = 2.88 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; Ka = (2.88 × 10⁻³)²/0.0600 = **1.39 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**, using the unrounded [H⁺] [1].
**(b)** pKa = −log₁₀(1.39 × 10⁻⁴) = **3.86** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert 250 cm³ to 0.250 dm³; using 250 gives a concentration a thousand times too small.

**12. (a)** n(H⁺) = 0.0200 × 0.150 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.0300 × 0.120 = 3.60 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. Excess OH⁻ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1]; [OH⁻] = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴/0.0500 = 0.0120 mol dm⁻³ [1]; [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴/0.0120 = 8.33 × 10⁻¹³, so pH = **12.08** [1].
**(b)** In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺] = √(5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 2.35 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **6.63** [1].
**(c)** [H⁺] still equals [OH⁻], which is the meaning of neutral [1].
**(d)** Kw is larger at the higher temperature, so the equilibrium H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ shifts right on heating [1]; an equilibrium constant that increases with temperature means the forward reaction is **endothermic** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Divide the excess moles by the total volume of the mixture (50.0 cm³), not by the volume of one solution.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a conjugate pair whose members differ by two protons.
- Confusing "weak" (dissociation) with "dilute" (concentration).
- Taking log₁₀ of a mass or a moles value instead of a concentration in mol dm⁻³.
- Forgetting to double [OH⁻] for group 2 hydroxides.
- Rounding [H⁺] to two significant figures before squaring it for Ka.
- Leaving Ka without units, or giving pH or pKa units.
- In mixing questions, ignoring the total volume when finding the excess concentration.
- Using pH 7 as the test for neutral at temperatures other than 298 K.
