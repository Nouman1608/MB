These questions test the section 3.3 row on the theory of tort law: the policy behind the duty of care, the limits on economic loss and psychiatric injury claims, the objective standard of care, injunctions and the purpose of vicarious liability.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the part of the *Caparo* test that allows a court to take policy into account. **[1]**

**2.** Identify **two** aims of the law of tort. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the "floodgates" argument, giving an example of an area of tort where it is used. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why a learner driver is judged by the standard of a reasonably competent driver. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why an injunction is described as a discretionary remedy. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **three** policy reasons for imposing vicarious liability on an employer. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain, using **one** case, how the courts have used policy to deny a duty of care to a claimant who suffered foreseeable physical harm. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the policy reasons why the law refuses to allow recovery for pure economic loss caused by a negligent act. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the courts balance conflicting interests when deciding whether to grant an injunction in private nuisance, referring to **two** cases. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss whether the objective standard of care is fair to defendants. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the policy reasons that limit claims by secondary victims of psychiatric injury. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss whether the policy reasons for vicarious liability justify imposing liability on an employer who is not at fault. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty of care [1].

**2.** Any two from: compensating the claimant [1]; corrective justice, making the wrongdoer bear loss caused by fault; deterring careless conduct [1]; spreading losses through insurance; protecting interests in land.

**3.** The floodgates argument is the fear that recognising a type of claim will lead to an unmanageable number of claims [1], exposing defendants to liability out of proportion to their fault [1]. It is used to restrict claims for pure economic loss or by secondary victims of psychiatric injury, as in *Alcock* (1992) [1].

**4.** The standard of care is objective, so it does not vary with the defendant's experience [1]: *Nettleship v Weston* (1971) [1]. A reason given is that motor insurance is compulsory, so the loss falls on an insurer rather than the learner personally, and the standard gives other road users equal protection [1].

**5.** An injunction is an equitable remedy, so a claimant who proves a tort is not entitled to it as of right [1]; the court decides whether to grant it, may limit it, or may award damages instead [1].

**6.** Any three from: the employer is more likely to be able to pay and to be insured [1]; the tort arose from an activity on the employer's behalf [1]; the activity is part of the employer's business; the employer created the risk by employing the person [1]; the employer controls the employee to some degree (*Catholic Child Welfare Society*, 2012).

**7.** In *Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* (1989) the police failed to catch a serial killer before he killed the claimant's daughter [1]. Harm to a future victim was arguably foreseeable, but the House of Lords held that no duty was owed [1] because it was not fair, just and reasonable to impose one [1]. The policy reasons were that liability would encourage defensive policing and divert police time and money into defending claims [1]. (Credit *Marc Rich* or *Michael* with equivalent explanation.)

**8.** Financial loss from a single negligent act can spread to a large and unknown number of people [1], creating liability "in an indeterminate amount for an indeterminate time to an indeterminate class" (*Ultramares v Touche*, 1931) [1]. In *Spartan Steel v Martin* (1973) the court refused lost profits not linked to physical damage [1], reasoning that businesses can protect themselves by insurance [1] and that the loss is better spread thinly among many than imposed on one defendant [1].

**9.** An injunction is discretionary, so the court weighs the claimant's right to enjoy land against the defendant's activity and the public interest [1]. In *Miller v Jackson* (1977) a nuisance from cricket balls was proved [1], but the injunction was refused because of the value of the cricket ground to the community, and damages were awarded instead [1]. In *Kennaway v Thompson* (1981) the court granted an injunction [1] limiting the number and type of powerboat events, protecting the claimant without closing the club [1]. *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014) adds that the *Shelfer* guidance on damages in lieu should not be applied mechanically, so public benefit may be weighed [1].

**10.** The standard is objective: the defendant is judged against the reasonable person, not their own ability (*Blyth*, 1856) [1]. It can seem unfair because a learner who does their best is still in breach (*Nettleship*, 1971) [1]. However, it gives certainty and equal protection to claimants [1], and insurance usually bears the loss [1]. The courts adjust it where fairness requires, for children (*Mullin v Richards*, 1998) and professionals (*Bolam*, 1957) [1]. Conclusion: it is fair if seen as allocating risk rather than moral blame [1].

**11.** Secondary victims must meet the *Alcock* (1992) control mechanisms: close tie, proximity in time and space, own unaided senses, and reasonable fortitude [1]. The main policy is the floodgates fear: a disaster can be witnessed by huge numbers, including on television [1]. Courts also fear fraudulent or exaggerated claims [1] and liability out of proportion to the defendant's fault [1]. These reasons give certainty [1]. However, outcomes can be arbitrary: a sibling must prove a close tie that a parent is presumed to have [1], and in *White* (1998) officers failed largely to keep consistency with *Alcock* rather than on principle [1]. The Law Commission recommended reform in 1998, but it has not been enacted and *Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust* (2024) shows the courts will not extend liability, so the limits are justified for certainty but leave some genuine victims without a remedy [1].

**12.** Vicarious liability is strict: the employer is liable for the employee's tort without its own fault [1]. Justifications include the employer's ability to pay and insure [1] and enterprise risk, since the employer benefits from the activity and created the risk (*Cox v Ministry of Justice*, 2016) [1]. It also encourages careful recruitment and supervision [1]. Against this, it is contrary to the fault principle and its extension to relationships akin to employment has reduced certainty [1]. Conclusion: the policy reasons justify it where the tort is closely connected to the employer's enterprise, which is why limits such as *Barclays Bank* (2020) are important [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a policy without a case to show the courts using it.
- Treating "floodgates" as a complete answer without explaining the effect on defendants.
- Saying *Miller v Jackson* found no nuisance.
- Ending an evaluation question without a conclusion.
