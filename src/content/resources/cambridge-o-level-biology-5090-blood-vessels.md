This guide covers subtopic **11.3 Blood vessels** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to name the main blood vessels that carry blood to and from the heart, lungs, liver and kidneys (aorta, vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, hepatic vein, hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein, renal artery and renal vein), to describe and identify on diagrams and photomicrographs the structure of arteries, veins and capillaries (thickness of wall, composition of wall, diameter of lumen and presence of valves), and to explain how the structure of each is related to the pressure of the blood that it carries.

## The main vessels

An **artery** carries blood **away from** the heart. A **vein** carries blood **towards** the heart. The word describes the direction of flow, not the oxygen content.

| Organ | Vessel in | Vessel out |
|---|---|---|
| Heart | Vena cava (from the body), pulmonary vein (from the lungs) | Aorta (to the body), pulmonary artery (to the lungs) |
| Lungs | Pulmonary artery (deoxygenated blood) | Pulmonary vein (oxygenated blood) |
| Liver | Hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta), hepatic portal vein (blood rich in absorbed nutrients from the intestine) | Hepatic vein (to the vena cava) |
| Kidneys | Renal artery | Renal vein |

Points to notice:

- The **aorta** is the largest artery. It leaves the left ventricle and branches to the whole body.
- The **vena cava** is the largest vein. It returns blood from the body to the right atrium.
- The **pulmonary artery** is the only artery that carries deoxygenated blood (apart from its branches), and the **pulmonary vein** carries oxygenated blood.
- The liver has **two** vessels bringing blood in (the hepatic artery and the hepatic portal vein) and one taking blood out (the hepatic vein).
- The **hepatic portal vein** connects the gut to the liver (see subtopic 8.3). The **renal** vessels are the kidney's.

## Arteries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at **high pressure**, in surges as the heart beats.

- **Thick wall**: to withstand the high pressure without bursting.
- **Muscle and elastic tissue** in the wall: the elastic tissue stretches as each surge of blood arrives and then recoils, which smooths out the flow and helps to keep the blood moving between beats. The muscle can contract to change the width of the vessel.
- **Narrow lumen** (the space inside) relative to the thickness of the wall.
- **No valves** (apart from the semilunar valves at the heart), because the pressure is high enough to keep the blood moving forwards.

## Veins

Veins return blood to the heart at **low pressure**.

- **Thin wall**: the blood pressure is low, so a strong wall is not needed.
- **Less muscle and elastic tissue** than arteries.
- **Wide lumen**: this lowers the resistance to flow, so blood can move at low pressure.
- **Valves**: these stop blood from flowing backwards. Muscles around the veins squeeze them when they contract, pushing the blood towards the heart, and the valves ensure it can go only that way.

## Capillaries

Capillaries are the tiny vessels between arteries and veins. They pass close to every cell.

- **Wall one cell thick**: gives a short distance for diffusion of oxygen, glucose and carbon dioxide between the blood and the tissues.
- **Very narrow lumen**: just wide enough for red blood cells to pass through in single file, so the blood is close to the wall and flows slowly, allowing time for exchange.
- **No muscle or elastic tissue** and **no valves**.
- **Permeable walls**: the walls have tiny gaps that let some of the plasma leak out as tissue fluid (see subtopic 11.4).
- A huge number of capillaries give a large surface area for exchange.

## Comparison

| Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction of flow | Away from the heart | Towards the heart | Between arteries and veins |
| Blood pressure | High | Low | Falling (low by the venous end) |
| Wall thickness | Thick | Thin | One cell thick |
| Muscle and elastic tissue | Much | Little | None |
| Lumen | Narrow | Wide | Very narrow |
| Valves | No | Yes | No |
| Function | Carry blood quickly under pressure | Return blood to the heart | Exchange of substances |

## Relating structure to pressure

A model answer always links a **feature** to the **pressure** and gives the reason.

- *Why does an artery have a thick, elastic wall?* Blood leaves the heart at high pressure. The thick wall resists the pressure and stops the artery bursting, and the elastic tissue stretches and recoils to even out the surges.
- *Why does a vein have valves?* The pressure in veins is low, so the blood could flow backwards. The valves close if blood begins to flow backwards, so it moves only towards the heart.
- *Why is a capillary wall one cell thick?* The aim is exchange, not carrying under pressure. A thin wall gives a short diffusion distance, and the low pressure means that the thin wall does not burst.

## Worked example: measuring vessel sections

*A photomicrograph at a magnification of x50 shows an artery and a vein. In the image, the artery wall measures 12 mm and its lumen 8 mm across. The vein wall measures 3 mm and its lumen 14 mm.*

Use actual size = image size divided by magnification.

| | Image (mm) | Actual (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| Artery wall | 12 | 12 divided by 50 = 0.24 |
| Artery lumen | 8 | 8 divided by 50 = 0.16 |
| Vein wall | 3 | 3 divided by 50 = 0.06 |
| Vein lumen | 14 | 14 divided by 50 = 0.28 |

1. **Ratio of wall thickness to lumen diameter**: artery 12 divided by 8 = **1.5**; vein 3 divided by 14 = **0.21**.
2. **Interpretation**: the artery wall is 1.5 times as thick as its lumen is wide, which fits the high pressure it carries. The vein wall is much thinner than its lumen is wide, which fits the low pressure.
3. The actual wall of the artery is 0.24 divided by 0.06 = **4 times** as thick as the vein wall.

## Identifying vessels on a section

- A **round, thick-walled** vessel with a **small, round or star-shaped** lumen is an artery.
- A **flattened, thin-walled** vessel with a **large, irregular** lumen is a vein.
- A **tiny** tube barely wider than a red blood cell, with a wall of a single cell, is a capillary.
- If valves are visible in a longitudinal section, the vessel is a vein.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that arteries always carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood.
- Saying that arteries have valves.
- Saying that a capillary has "no wall". It has a wall one cell thick.
- Describing the wall of a vein as "weak" instead of "thin, because the pressure is low".
- Confusing the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
- Forgetting that the liver has two supply vessels.
- Giving the structure without the pressure link.
- Saying that the lumen of an artery is wide. It is narrow compared to the wall.

## Command words

- **Name**: give the vessel that joins two organs.
- **Identify**: pick out an artery, vein or capillary on a section.
- **Describe**: state the wall thickness, composition, lumen and valves.
- **Explain**: relate each feature to the pressure of the blood.
