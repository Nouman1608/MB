## Factors affecting the location of urban activities

| Factor | Examples of influence |
|---|---|
| Economic | Land price, accessibility to customers and workers, the need for large sites, agglomeration with similar firms |
| Social | Where target customers live, safety and image of an area, links to communities |
| Environmental | Flat land for large buildings, flood risk, attractive settings for offices and housing, avoiding pollution |
| Political (including planning) | Zoning, green belts, tax incentives in enterprise zones, policies that protect town centres or restrict out-of-town building |

Planning is named specifically. It can override market forces: a planning authority can refuse an out-of-town shopping centre that would have been profitable, or offer incentives that draw firms into a declining inner area.

## Bid rent and spatial competition

Bid rent theory, developed by William Alonso (1964), assumes that the centre of a city is the most accessible point, so land there is most valuable. Each land use can pay a different maximum rent at each distance from the centre, and land goes to the highest bidder.

- **Retailing** gains most from accessibility to many customers, so it bids highest at the centre, but its bid falls steeply with distance.
- **Offices and services** need accessibility to workers and clients, but less than shops, so their bid curve is lower and less steep.
- **Housing** needs space more than accessibility, so its curve is the flattest.
- **Manufacturing** needs large, cheap sites and road access, so it is often outside the inner residential zone in modern cities.

**Worked example (illustrative units).** At distance *d* km from the centre:

- retail can pay 1 000 − 200*d*;
- offices can pay 700 − 100*d*;
- housing can pay 400 − 50*d*.

Retail and offices bid equally where 1 000 − 200*d* = 700 − 100*d*, so 300 = 100*d* and *d* = **3 km**. Offices and housing bid equally where 700 − 100*d* = 400 − 50*d*, so 300 = 50*d* and *d* = **6 km**. Housing's bid reaches zero at 400 ÷ 50 = **8 km**. The result is a pattern of rings: retail from 0 to 3 km, offices from 3 to 6 km, housing from 6 to 8 km, and rural uses beyond. This is **functional zonation** produced by spatial competition.

**Limitations.** Real cities have several accessible points, such as ring roads, railway stations and suburban centres, so bid rent peaks occur away from the centre. Planning, historic buildings, physical barriers and inertia distort the pattern. In many LIC and MIC cities, the poorest live on the periphery not because they prefer space but because informal land there is the only land they can occupy.

## How locations change over time

### Retailing

In many HICs retailing has decentralised in phases. Russell Schiller (1986) described three "waves" in Britain:

1. **Food superstores** on the edge of town from the 1970s, needing car parks and large sites.
2. **Retail warehouses** for bulky goods such as DIY, furniture and electrical goods.
3. **Regional shopping centres** selling clothes and other comparison goods, such as the Metrocentre in Gateshead (1986) and Meadowhall in Sheffield (1990).

Later changes include factory outlet centres and, above all, online shopping, which has reduced demand for shop floor space everywhere. From the mid-1990s British planning policy began to favour town-centre sites first, slowing new out-of-town development.

### Services

Offices have moved from CBDs to **business parks** near motorway junctions and airports, where land is cheaper, parking is easy and buildings can be modern. Back-office work has moved to suburban sites or abroad. Public services such as hospitals have often been rebuilt on larger edge-of-city sites.

### Manufacturing

In nineteenth-century industrial cities, factories clustered near the centre, close to canals, railways and workers' housing. With deindustrialisation, many inner-city factories closed. New manufacturing and warehousing located on **industrial estates and science parks** at the urban edge, with road access, space for single-storey buildings and room to expand. Inner sites left behind became brownfield land, later reused for housing and offices.

In fast-growing LIC and MIC cities, manufacturing still expands, often in **export processing zones** and industrial corridors on the urban edge, linked to ports.

## The changing CBD

The CBD has the highest land values, the tallest buildings and the most retail and office floor space. It is often divided into a **core**, with the most intense use, and a **frame**, with lower-order shops, car parks, warehouses and institutions.

| Change | Causes | Responses |
|---|---|---|
| Loss of retail | Out-of-town centres, online shopping, high rents | More cafés, restaurants and leisure; pop-up shops |
| Office decline in some cities | Business parks, home working | Conversion of offices to apartments; mixed use |
| Residential growth | Re-urbanisation, brownfield policy | New apartments, student housing |
| Congestion | Car use, deliveries | Pedestrianisation, congestion charges, park-and-ride, light rail |
| Edge expansion and decline | The CBD grows towards wealthier areas and shrinks on its poorer side | Regeneration of declining edges |

In many LIC and MIC cities the CBD is still growing, with new office towers alongside dense informal trading on its streets and markets. Some cities have built new business districts away from the historic centre.

## Residential segregation

Segregation is the uneven distribution of social groups across a city. The syllabus names two **causes**, income and race/ethnicity, and several **processes**:

- **Operation of the housing market.** House prices and rents sort households by what they can pay. Lenders and estate agents may steer groups towards or away from areas.
- **Influence of family and friends.** Newcomers settle near relatives, who help with housing and jobs (chain migration).
- **Culture.** Groups may choose to live near places of worship, shops selling familiar food and schools teaching their language.
- **Planning.** Public housing allocation, zoning for large plots in wealthy suburbs, or historical laws that separated groups by race.

Segregation can be **voluntary** (choice for support and identity) or **involuntary** (discrimination, poverty). Most patterns combine both.

### Measuring segregation: the index of dissimilarity

The index of dissimilarity (D) shows what percentage of one group would need to move to give both groups the same distribution across the city:

D = ½ × Σ |(a ÷ A) − (b ÷ B)| × 100

where a and b are the numbers of each group in one area, A and B are the city totals, and the sum (Σ) is taken across all areas.

**Worked example (fictional city with four wards).**

| Ward | Group A | Group B | a/A | b/B | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 400 | 50 | 0.40 | 0.05 | 0.35 |
| 2 | 300 | 150 | 0.30 | 0.15 | 0.15 |
| 3 | 200 | 300 | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.10 |
| 4 | 100 | 500 | 0.10 | 0.50 | 0.40 |
| Total | 1 000 | 1 000 | | | 1.00 |

D = ½ × 1.00 × 100 = **50**. Half of either group would have to move to remove the difference. Values near 0 show little segregation; values near 100 show almost complete separation.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how the housing market can lead to residential segregation. [5]*

> House prices and rents vary between areas (**starting point**), so households can only live where they can afford (**sorting by income**). Where income differs between ethnic groups, segregation by income also produces segregation by ethnicity (**link between the two causes**). Lenders may refuse mortgages in some areas, or agents may steer buyers, restricting choice (**discrimination**). Wealthier groups can outbid others for the most attractive areas, such as gated communities (**exclusion**), so poorer groups are concentrated in the cheapest housing (**outcome**).

## Common mistakes

- Describing bid rent without explaining *why* accessibility matters to each land use.
- Drawing a bid rent graph without labelling axes (rent, distance from the centre) or the zones.
- Treating Burgess and Hoyt as descriptions of every city, rather than models with limits.
- Describing segregation only as discrimination, ignoring choice and family networks.
- Writing about "the CBD dying" with no evidence; many CBDs are changing function rather than declining.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** an index or a crossover point: show each step and the units.
- **Describe** a land-use map or transect: give the pattern, the distances and an anomaly.
- **Explain** a location change: link it to a named factor, economic, social, environmental or political.
- **Assess** the importance of one factor: weigh it against at least two others and reach a view.
