This guide covers subtopic **11.2, The chemical properties of the halogen elements and the hydrogen halides**, from Topic 11, Group 17, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Group 17: The Halogens](/resources/as-group-17-the-halogens/) guide covers all of Topic 11. This page goes deeper into 11.2: displacement reactions written as ionic equations and read with a solvent layer, a bond energy calculation that explains the reactions with hydrogen, and a careful explanation of thermal stability that avoids the polarity trap. Condensed notes are in the [11.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-chemical-properties-of-the-halogen-elements-revision-notes/), and the [11.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-chemical-properties-of-the-halogen-elements-practice/) test it.

## What 11.2 asks you to do

1. Describe the relative reactivity of the elements as **oxidising agents**.
2. Describe the reactions of the elements with **hydrogen** and explain their relative reactivity in these reactions.
3. Describe the relative **thermal stabilities of the hydrogen halides** and explain these in terms of **bond strengths**.

## The halogens as oxidising agents

A halogen acts as an oxidising agent by gaining electrons. It is itself reduced:

X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻

Oxidising power **decreases** down the group: Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. The reason is the attraction for the incoming electron:

- Down the group the atoms are **larger**, so an incoming electron enters a shell further from the nucleus.
- There are **more inner shells** shielding it.
- So the attraction for the extra electron is weaker, and the halogen gains electrons less readily.

### Evidence: displacement reactions

A halogen oxidises the halide ion of any halogen **below** it, displacing that halogen from solution. Writing **ionic equations** shows clearly what is oxidised and what is reduced:

Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq)

Cl₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

In each, the halogen (oxidation number 0) is reduced to −1 and the halide ion (−1) is oxidised to 0. No reaction occurs when a halogen is added to the halide of a halogen above it, for example I₂ with Br⁻ or Br₂ with Cl⁻.

**Reading the results.** In water, bromine (orange) and iodine (brown) can look similar at low concentration. Shaking the mixture with a hydrocarbon solvent such as cyclohexane separates the halogen into the upper organic layer, where bromine is **orange to red-brown** and iodine is **purple**. That makes the product easy to identify.

**Worked example: a results grid.** Each halogen solution is added to each potassium halide solution, then cyclohexane is added and shaken.

| Added to → | KCl(aq) | KBr(aq) | KI(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂(aq) | — | Organic layer orange: Br₂ formed | Organic layer purple: I₂ formed |
| Br₂(aq) | No reaction (orange of Br₂ itself) | — | Organic layer purple: I₂ formed |
| I₂(aq) | No reaction (purple of I₂ itself) | No reaction (purple of I₂ itself) | — |

Chlorine displaces two halogens, bromine one, iodine none. So the order of oxidising power is Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Notice that "no reaction" still leaves the colour of the halogen added. The evidence for no reaction is that the colour in the organic layer is that of the **original** halogen.

## Reactions with hydrogen

| Reaction | Conditions and observations |
|---|---|
| H₂ + F₂ → 2HF | Explosive, even in the cold and dark |
| H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | Explosive in sunlight or UV light; slow in the dark |
| H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr | Needs heating, with a platinum catalyst; much less vigorous |
| H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI | Needs heating with a platinum catalyst; slow and **reversible**, so an equilibrium mixture forms |

Reactivity with hydrogen **decreases** down the group.

### Explaining the trend with bond energies

Two explanations work together. First, oxidising power decreases down the group (hydrogen is oxidised from 0 to +1 in each reaction). Second, the H–X bond formed becomes **weaker**, so less energy is released when the product forms and the reaction becomes much less exothermic.

**Worked example.** Use bond energies to calculate ΔH for each reaction, H₂(g) + X₂(g) → 2HX(g). Bond energies (kJ mol⁻¹): H–H 436; Cl–Cl 242, Br–Br 193, I–I 151; H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299.

ΔH = (bonds broken) − (bonds formed)

- HCl: (436 + 242) − 2(431) = 678 − 862 = **−184 kJ mol⁻¹**
- HBr: (436 + 193) − 2(366) = 629 − 732 = **−103 kJ mol⁻¹**
- HI: (436 + 151) − 2(299) = 587 − 598 = **−11 kJ mol⁻¹**

The X–X bond broken gets weaker down the group, which on its own would make the reaction more exothermic. But the H–X bonds formed weaken much more (431 → 299 kJ mol⁻¹, and two of them form), so ΔH becomes much less negative. With HI, ΔH is close to zero, which fits a reaction that does not go to completion and reaches equilibrium instead.

## Thermal stability of the hydrogen halides

| Hydrogen halide | H–X bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Effect of heating |
|---|---|---|
| HF | 562 | Very stable |
| HCl | 431 | Stable at the temperature of a Bunsen flame |
| HBr | 366 | Decomposes slightly on strong heating; a little brown bromine may be seen |
| HI | 299 | Decomposes readily: a hot wire or glass rod plunged into HI gives purple iodine vapour |

The decomposition is the reverse of formation, for example 2HI → H₂ + I₂.

**Thermal stability decreases** down the group. The explanation must use bond strength:

1. Down the group, X is a larger atom, so the H–X bond is **longer**.
2. The bonding pair is further from the X nucleus and more shielded, so it is held less strongly.
3. The H–X bond is **weaker** (lower bond energy).
4. Less energy is needed to break it, so the hydrogen halide decomposes more easily on heating.

**The polarity trap.** The H–X bond also becomes **less polar** down the group, because the electronegativity of X falls. Polarity is not the explanation the syllabus asks for, and it does not explain the trend: the syllabus links thermal stability to **bond strength**. Use bond energies.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up oxidising agent and reducing agent.** Halogens are oxidising agents; the halide ions are the reducing agents (11.3).
- **Writing displacement equations with single atoms**, such as Cl + Br⁻. Halogens are diatomic.
- **Reporting "no reaction" when a colour is seen.** Check whether the colour is from the halogen added or a halogen formed.
- **Forgetting that H₂ + I₂ is reversible.**
- **Explaining thermal stability by polarity or electronegativity.** Use H–X bond energy.
- **Saying the reactions with hydrogen get slower because the X–X bond gets stronger.** It gets weaker; the H–X bond formed is the key.

## Exam technique: command words in 11.2

- **Describe** (reactions with hydrogen): give conditions and how vigorous each reaction is, with equations.
- **Explain** (relative reactivity): use oxidising power (atomic size, shielding, attraction for an electron) and the decreasing H–X bond energy.
- **Describe and explain** (thermal stability): state the trend with evidence, then bond length → bond energy → energy to break.
- **Deduce** (displacement results): state what each colour shows and conclude the order of oxidising power.
