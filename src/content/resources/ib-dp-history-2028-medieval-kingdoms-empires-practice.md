> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. The model case is the Carolingian Empire; if your class studied another medieval kingdom, adapt the answers.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 1 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). The real paper asks for 15-mark essays; the shorter questions below build the skills for them. Revise with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-medieval-kingdoms-empires-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-medieval-kingdoms-empires/).

## Questions

**1.** State two ways in which Charlemagne supervised the counts. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the coronation of 800 was important. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "The king sends out his envoys each year, a bishop and a count together, to hear the poor and to report on those in office. Let no man hide the truth from them." (Constructed for practice, in the style of a royal order.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about how the ruler checked his officials. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of using an order like this to show how government really worked. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why conquest helped the Carolingian rulers to keep the loyalty of nobles. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the ways in which religion supported royal authority. **[6]**

**6.** Compare the experiences of noblewomen and peasant women in the Carolingian world. **[4]**

**7.** "The Carolingian Empire fell mainly because Frankish custom divided the realm among sons." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Two historians disagree. One says Carolingian government worked because of a strong king imposing order. The other says it worked through negotiation with the nobility. Discuss how you would use these views in an essay on how a medieval ruler maintained authority. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: sent missi dominici on circuits; held annual assemblies of nobles; demanded oaths of loyalty; issued capitularies setting out duties. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4 marks: the pope crowned Charlemagne in Rome on Christmas Day 800 (1); it linked him with the Roman imperial past and raised his status above other kings (1); it showed the Church's blessing and tied his authority to Christian duty (1); it created a lasting argument over whether popes or emperors were superior, so it mattered for the papal and imperial relationship (1). Accept a well-developed point on Einhard's claim of reluctance as context. **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the ruler sent envoys regularly (1); they were a bishop and a count, so church and lay figures checked each other (1); they heard complaints from ordinary people and reported, so the king gained information beyond what his officials told him (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it states what the ruler wanted, not what happened (1); it does not show whether envoys were honest, or whether officials hid the truth (1); developed explanation of why a command can show intention rather than practice, supported with an example such as the need to repeat orders (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons with explanation. Plunder and tribute were shared out among warriors and nobles, giving them a stake in the king's success (2). Newly conquered land could be granted as benefices or offices, rewarding families and binding them to the ruler (2). Accept also: annual campaigns kept the aristocracy occupied and united against outsiders. **[4]**

**5.** Markband approach, with up to 6 marks. Look for: anointing and coronation gave rulers a sacred role (1); the king as defender of the faith, and his laws mixing civil and religious duty (1); bishops and abbots served as royal officials and wrote the documents of government (1); monasteries and cathedrals enriched by royal gifts stayed loyal (1); the pope's blessing, in 751 and 800, legitimized the dynasty (1); analysis that religion also limited the ruler, since the Church claimed its own authority, and forced conversion could provoke revolt as in Saxony (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 4: similarity, both worked within households and under male authority (1); difference, noblewomen could hold property, run estates and influence politics as empresses or abbesses (1); peasant women laboured on the land and at crafts with few legal rights (1); a comparative judgement, for example that status mattered more than gender alone, supported by Dhuoda's handbook as evidence of an educated noblewoman (1). **[4]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each. Understanding of the claim: partible inheritance and the civil wars of the 830s and the settlement at Verdun in 843 (3). Evaluation of other factors, shown as themes: external raids (Vikings from 793, Magyars, Muslim raiders), weak successors, and the loss of plunder after expansion ended (3 for one theme developed; a further 3 for two further themes treated briefly but accurately, 6 in all). Perspectives: a historian's view, for example on whether the system depended on Charlemagne's personal energy, or on whether local lords gained power from the need for defence (3). Judgement: a reasoned conclusion, for example that division was a major cause because it repeatedly split resources but did not act alone, since raids and weak rulers made the effect worse (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 6: states the first view (strong king) and gives evidence such as capitularies and missi (1); states the second view (negotiation) and gives evidence such as annual assemblies, oaths and gifts of land (1); explains why the views differ (types of sources used, the purpose of royal documents, emphasis on ideal or practice) (2); evaluates which is more convincing for the chosen example, or argues the two can be combined (2). **[6]**
