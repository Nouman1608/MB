Education is the process of learning knowledge, skills and values; schooling is the formal part of
it that happens in institutions. Key question 5.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What is
education and schooling?*, asks you to distinguish formal from informal education, explain the
official and hidden curriculum, assess the effectiveness of different types of schools, and weigh
alternative approaches such as online learning, homeschooling and vocational learning.

For the roles of education and differences in achievement (5.2 and 5.3), see the
[O Level family, education and crime guide](/resources/o-level-sociology-family-education-crime/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251 (2025–2027), **5.1 What is education and schooling?**

- **5.1.1 Formal and informal education** — the differences between them; the official curriculum
  (testing, syllabus content and subjects); the hidden curriculum (norms and values, punctuality,
  conformity, gender roles).
- **5.1.2 Different types of schools** — the effectiveness of, for example, state/public, private,
  selective/non-selective, single-sex, co-educational, faith and international schools; strengths and
  limitations of each.
- **5.1.3 Alternative approaches to education** — online learning, homeschooling, unschooling,
  vocational learning, progressive schooling; strengths and limitations.

## What is the difference between formal and informal education?

| Formal education | Informal education |
|---|---|
| Takes place in schools, colleges and universities | Takes place anywhere — home, community, media, workplace |
| Planned, structured curriculum | Unplanned, everyday learning |
| Delivered by trained teachers | Learned from parents, elders, peers, media |
| Assessed by tests and certificates | Not formally assessed |
| Example: studying for O Level examinations | Example: learning to cook, pray or bargain in a market from family members |

## What are the official and hidden curricula?

**The official curriculum** is what schools openly teach: the **subjects**, **syllabus content** and
the **testing** that measures progress (for example national and international examinations).

**The hidden curriculum** is what pupils learn from the way school is organised rather than from
lessons:

- **Norms and values** — respect for authority, competition, working for rewards.
- **Punctuality** — bells, registers and late detentions prepare pupils for the discipline of work.
- **Conformity** — uniforms, rules and assemblies teach pupils to obey.
- **Gender roles** — subject choices, sports, prefect roles and teachers' expectations may reinforce
  ideas of "suitable" activities for boys and girls.

Marxists Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis (*Schooling in Capitalist America*, 1976) argued that the
hidden curriculum prepares obedient workers for capitalism; functionalists see it as socialising
pupils into shared values.

## How effective are different types of school?

| Type | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **State / public** | Free or low-cost; open to all; national standards | Large classes and limited resources in many countries |
| **Private** | Smaller classes, better facilities, strong results | Fees exclude poorer families; may widen inequality |
| **Selective** (entry by test) | Challenge for high attainers | Labels those rejected; often favours advantaged children |
| **Non-selective** (comprehensive) | Mixed intake; equal opportunity | May not stretch the most able |
| **Single-sex** | Some evidence girls choose a wider range of subjects; fewer distractions | Less experience of mixed settings; may reinforce gender stereotypes |
| **Co-educational** | Reflects wider society; social skills with both sexes | Boys may dominate classroom attention |
| **Faith schools** | Strong values and community; often popular with parents | May be socially or religiously segregated |
| **International schools** | Global curricula and qualifications; multilingual | High fees; may distance pupils from local culture |

Judging effectiveness depends on the measure used — examination results, social mobility, values or
equality of opportunity.

## What alternative approaches to education exist?

- **Online learning** — courses delivered over the internet. *Strengths:* flexibility, access to
  expert teaching anywhere. *Limitations:* requires devices and reliable internet; less social
  interaction; harder to keep motivated. School closures during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021)
  showed both the potential and the digital divide.
- **Homeschooling** — parents educate children at home. *Strengths:* tailored pace, safety from
  bullying. *Limitations:* depends on parents' time and knowledge; less socialisation with peers.
- **Unschooling** — children direct their own learning through interests and everyday life, without
  a set curriculum. *Strengths:* curiosity and independence. *Limitations:* gaps in knowledge;
  difficulty gaining qualifications.
- **Vocational learning** — training for specific jobs or trades. *Strengths:* practical skills and
  employability. *Limitations:* often lower status than academic routes; may channel working-class
  pupils into lower-paid work.
- **Progressive schooling** — child-centred schools giving pupils more freedom and say. A.S. Neill's
  Summerhill School in England, founded in 1921, lets pupils choose whether to attend lessons.
  *Strengths:* motivation and democratic values. *Limitations:* may not suit all children; uneven
  academic outcomes.

## How does 5.1 connect to the rest of the Education topic?

The distinctions here are used in later key questions: the hidden curriculum is central to
functionalist and Marxist views of the roles of education (5.2), and private and selective schools
are part of explanations of class differences in achievement (5.3).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the hidden curriculum deliberate?** Not usually. It is learned from school routines and
organisation rather than planned lessons, although some rules are designed to teach discipline.

**Are private schools more effective?** They often achieve higher exam results, but sociologists
point out that their pupils usually start with more advantages, so the school may not be the only
reason.

**What is the difference between homeschooling and unschooling?** Homeschooling usually follows a
curriculum at home; unschooling lets the child decide what and how to learn.
