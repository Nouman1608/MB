**What do these questions practise?** Section 2 of Paper 1, Key Questions 6 to 10. Section B of the
paper uses a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation and a 14-mark evaluation; here they are
grouped by type. Allow about 5, 9 and 16 minutes for each.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the Simla Deputation of 1906. **[4]**

**2.** Describe the Hijrat Movement of 1920. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the Communal Award of 1932. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the Cripps Mission of 1942. **[4]**

**5.** Describe Direct Action Day, 1946. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why the All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why many Muslims rejected the Nehru Report of 1928. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why Congress rule (1937–39) was unpopular with many Muslims. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 failed. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**10.** "The Round Table Conferences (1930–32) achieved nothing for the Muslims of the
subcontinent." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**11.** "Jinnah was more important than Allama Iqbal to the success of the Pakistan Movement." How
far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "The results of the 1945–46 elections were the most important reason for the creation of
Pakistan in 1947." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge parts: 1 mark per relevant point, with a further mark for supporting detail. The 7- and
14-mark parts are marked by levels (tables after the answers). Credit other accurate points.

**1.** In October 1906 a group of Muslim leaders met the Viceroy, Lord Minto, at Simla [1]. It was led
by the Aga Khan [1]. It asked for separate electorates for Muslims [1] and for weightage, more seats
than their share of the population, because of their political importance [1]. Also credit: Minto
was sympathetic, and separate electorates followed in 1909. **[4]**

**2.** Some ulama declared that India was no longer a place where Muslims could freely practise
their faith, so Muslims should migrate [1]. In 1920 many thousands, especially from Sindh and the
North-West Frontier, sold their land and property and moved towards Afghanistan [1]. Afghanistan,
unable to cope with the numbers, closed the border [1], and many migrants returned home having lost
their possessions, and some died on the way [1]. **[4]**

**3.** It was announced by the British Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald, in 1932 [1], after the Round
Table Conferences failed to agree on minority representation [1]. It kept separate electorates for
Muslims and extended them to other minorities [1]. It disappointed Muslims in Punjab and Bengal,
where they did not receive a majority of seats guaranteed by the award [1]. Also credit: Gandhi
opposed separate electorates for the "Depressed Classes", leading to the Poona Pact. **[4]**

**4.** Sir Stafford Cripps was sent to India in March 1942 during the Second World War [1]. He offered
dominion status after the war and an elected body to draw up a constitution [1]. Provinces would be
allowed to opt out of the new union [1]. Both Congress and the League rejected the proposals, and
Congress went on to launch the Quit India Movement [1]. **[4]**

**5.** It was called by the Muslim League for 16 August 1946 [1], after the League withdrew its
acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan [1]. It was meant to show Muslim support for Pakistan through
peaceful protest [1], but severe rioting broke out in Calcutta, where thousands were killed, and
violence spread to other areas [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Congress was seen as a Hindu-dominated party that did not represent Muslim interests.
- The agitation against the partition of Bengal showed Muslims that Hindu pressure could reverse
  decisions that benefited them.
- The Simla Deputation's success with Minto encouraged Muslims to organise to keep up their demands,
  especially for separate electorates.
- Muslims wanted to protect Urdu and their cultural identity after the Hindi–Urdu controversy.
- Educated Muslims, many from Aligarh, wanted a political voice of their own. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- It rejected separate electorates, which Muslims regarded as essential protection.
- It refused one-third of the seats in the central legislature for Muslims, which the Delhi Proposals
  had asked for.
- It proposed a strong central government with residuary powers, where Hindus would be the majority.
- It showed Muslims that Congress would not keep the spirit of the Lucknow Pact, leading Jinnah to
  speak of a "parting of the ways". **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Congress refused to share power with the League, for example in the United Provinces.
- *Bande Mataram*, a song many Muslims found offensive, was sung at official functions.
- The Wardha and Vidya Mandir education schemes were seen as promoting Hindu culture.
- Muslims complained of discrimination in jobs and of the Congress flag flown over public buildings.
- Communal violence increased in some provinces. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The plan's grouping of provinces was accepted by the League as a route to Pakistan, but Congress
  interpreted it differently.
- Nehru's statement in July 1946 that Congress would not be bound by the plan made the League
  withdraw its acceptance.
- Disagreement over forming the interim government, and who could nominate Muslim members, deepened
  distrust.
- The violence after Direct Action Day made co-operation within one federation harder to
  imagine. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Achieved little:* the conferences failed to agree on minority representation; Congress boycotted
  the first and Gandhi, at the second, claimed to speak for all Indians; no constitution was agreed.
- *Achieved something:* Muslim delegates, including Jinnah and the Aga Khan, put their case directly
  to the British; the Communal Award (1932) followed, keeping separate electorates; the discussions
  fed into the Government of India Act (1935), which gave provincial autonomy and kept separate
  electorates; Sindh became a separate province in 1936.
- *Judgement:* for example, the conferences achieved little directly but led to measures that
  protected Muslim representation. **[14]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Jinnah:* led and reorganised the Muslim League; set out the Fourteen Points; presided over the
  adoption of the Lahore Resolution; negotiated with the British and Congress; led the League to
  victory in 1945–46; became Pakistan's first Governor-General.
- *Iqbal:* gave the idea of a separate Muslim nation its philosophical basis; called for a
  north-western Muslim state in the Allahabad address (1930); inspired Muslims through his poetry;
  urged Jinnah to lead; died in 1938.
- *Judgement:* for example, Iqbal provided the vision, but Jinnah's leadership turned it into a
  state, so Jinnah was more important to its *success*. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* the League won every Muslim seat in the central assembly and most Muslim seats in the
  provinces, proving it represented Muslims and strengthening Jinnah's position in negotiations.
- *Disagree:* other factors mattered: Congress rule in 1937–39 had turned Muslims towards the League;
  the Lahore Resolution set the aim; the failure of the Cabinet Mission Plan and the violence after
  Direct Action Day convinced the British that partition was necessary; Britain was weakened by the
  Second World War and wanted to leave quickly.
- *Judgement:* for example, the elections made Pakistan politically impossible to ignore, but the
  failure of the Cabinet Mission made it unavoidable. **[14]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the Cripps Mission (1942) with the Cabinet Mission (1946).
- Writing about the Simla Conference (1945) in a question on the Simla Deputation (1906).
- Describing Congress rule without explaining why each policy upset Muslims.
- In Question 11, writing about Rahmat Ali when the question compares only Jinnah and Iqbal.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need exact election figures for 1945–46?**
No. It is enough to say the League won all the Muslim seats in the central assembly and most Muslim
seats in the provinces.
