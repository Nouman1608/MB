## Budgets

A **budget** is a financial plan for a future period, set out in money terms. It states the revenue the business expects and the spending it will allow. Businesses usually prepare budgets for sales (income budget), for each cost type (expenditure budgets) and for the resulting profit. A budget is a target as well as a forecast: managers are expected to work towards it.

### Why businesses set budgets

- **Planning:** turns objectives into figures.
- **Control:** gives each manager a spending limit.
- **Coordination:** a purchasing budget must fit the sales budget.
- **Motivation:** targets give managers something to aim at.
- **Delegation:** budget holders can spend within limits without asking permission each time.
- **Evaluation:** actual results can be compared with the plan.

### How budgets are set

Most budgets start from last year's actual figures, adjusted for expected changes in sales, prices and costs, and for the objectives of the business. Managers who will carry out the budget usually take part in setting it. A budget agreed with the people responsible is more likely to be met than one imposed on them, but managers may build in slack by asking for more than they need.

## Variances

A **variance** is the difference between a budgeted figure and the actual figure. It is **favourable** when it improves profit compared with the budget, and **adverse** when it reduces profit.

| Item | Favourable when | Adverse when |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (income) | Actual is higher than budget | Actual is lower than budget |
| Cost (expense) | Actual is lower than budget | Actual is higher than budget |
| Profit | Actual is higher than budget | Actual is lower than budget |

Formula for the amount: **variance = actual − budget** (then label it favourable or adverse using the table). The size is often shown as a percentage of the budget: variance ÷ budget × 100.

A common error is to label a cost that is higher than budget as favourable because the number is bigger. Always ask: does it improve profit?

### Worked example 1: calculating variances

Hollin Foods produces a monthly budget and compares it with actual results.

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance | Favourable or adverse | % of budget |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £150,000 | £141,000 | £9,000 | Adverse | 6.0% |
| Materials | £60,000 | £63,600 | £3,600 | Adverse | 6.0% |
| Labour | £35,000 | £33,800 | £1,200 | Favourable | 3.4% |
| Overheads | £25,000 | £25,500 | £500 | Adverse | 2.0% |
| **Profit** | £30,000 | £18,100 | £11,900 | Adverse | 39.7% |

Working. Budgeted profit = 150,000 − 60,000 − 35,000 − 25,000 = £30,000. Actual profit = 141,000 − 63,600 − 33,800 − 25,500 = £18,100. Profit variance = 18,100 − 30,000 = −£11,900, adverse. This equals the sum of the item variances: −9,000 − 3,600 + 1,200 − 500 = −£11,900, which checks the work. Percentages: 9,000 ÷ 150,000 = 6%; 3,600 ÷ 60,000 = 6%; 1,200 ÷ 35,000 = 3.4%; 500 ÷ 25,000 = 2%; 11,900 ÷ 30,000 = 39.7%.

## Analysing variances

Calculating is the first step. **Analysis** asks: how big is it, what caused it, who is responsible and what should be done?

### Worked example 2: looking behind the totals

The profit shortfall is nearly 40% of the budget, much more than any single item variance in percentage terms, because a small fall in revenue and small rises in costs combine on a small profit.

- **Revenue** is 6% below budget. Possible causes: lower demand, a competitor, a price cut, or a lost customer.
- **Materials** are 6% over budget. At first this looks the same size as the revenue shortfall, but think about volume. If lower sales meant 6% less was produced, materials would be expected to fall to £60,000 × 0.94 = £56,400. Actual spending is £63,600, which is £7,200 more than that. So the real problem with materials is larger than the headline £3,600: either prices rose, or wastage increased.
- **Labour** is £1,200 favourable. This could be good (efficiency), or bad (understaffing, lower output, or a loss of skilled staff).
- **Overheads** are only 2% over, which is a small difference and probably not worth investigation.

**Actions.** Check supplier prices and waste in the factory; investigate why sales fell and whether it is temporary; check that the labour saving did not come from overtime cuts that harm quality. Hollin should decide what is controllable (waste, supplier choice) and what is not (a general fall in demand).

A variance is a **signal**, not an answer. The same adverse figure can have an external cause (a rise in commodity prices), an internal cause (poor purchasing) or a flaw in the budget itself (unrealistic targets).

## Impact on the business

**Favourable variances** are not always good news: they might indicate that the budget was too cautious, or that something was left undone, such as maintenance. **Adverse variances** reduce profit and may reduce cash, which can create funding problems. Their impact depends on the size, how long they last and whether they can be reversed.

For Hollin Foods, a 39.7% profit shortfall is serious. If it continued for a year, annual profit would fall by 12 × £11,900 = £142,800. That may affect its ability to repay loans, pay dividends or invest, and could lead to cost cutting that affects staff and suppliers.

| Stakeholder | Impact of budgets and variances |
|---|---|
| Managers | Held accountable; bonuses may depend on meeting budgets |
| Owners | See whether plans are achieved |
| Employees | May face pressure, or job cuts if costs are over budget |
| Lenders | Use budgets and variances to assess risk |
| Suppliers | May face cost pressure if the business squeezes purchasing |

## Evaluating usefulness

**Strengths of budgeting.** Gives direction, control and early warning; coordinates departments; makes managers think ahead; gives a basis for lender discussions; helps motivate.

**Weaknesses.** Time-consuming; based on forecasts that can be wrong; inflexible if conditions change (a budget set in January may not suit July); managers may build in slack or spend unneeded money at the end of the year to protect next year's allowance; short-term focus (cut training to meet the budget); may demotivate if targets are unrealistic or imposed.

**Strengths of variance analysis.** Shows where results differ from the plan; allows corrective action; makes managers accountable; highlights good performance as well as bad.

**Weaknesses.** Based on past events; does not explain causes by itself; a variance on one item may conceal an opposite one on another; too many variances can overload managers; only useful if acted on.

A balanced judgement is that budgeting and variance analysis are very useful for control, if budgets are realistic, agreed with the people responsible and reviewed during the year, and variances are investigated by significance (for example only those over 5% or £2,000), not all treated equally.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling a cost overspend as favourable.
- Giving a variance without saying whether it is favourable or adverse.
- Forgetting that profit variance equals the sum of the item variances.
- Treating a favourable variance as always good.
- Stopping at the calculation instead of analysing causes and actions.

## Exam technique

Show actual − budget, state the amount and label it. In "analyse", give a cause, a consequence and an action for at least two variances. In "evaluate", weigh strengths and weaknesses, name a stakeholder and give a judgement.
