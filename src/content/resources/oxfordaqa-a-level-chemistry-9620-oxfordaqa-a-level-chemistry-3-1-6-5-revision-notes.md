## Core recall

- **Definition (learn it word for word):** a catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount.
- **How it works:** it provides an **alternative reaction route of lower activation energy**.
- It usually **takes part**: used in one step, re-formed in a later one, so it does not appear in the overall equation.
- It does **not** change ΔH, the temperature, the energies of the molecules, or the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve.
- It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions of an equilibrium by lowering both barriers by the same amount, so equilibrium is reached sooner but its **position is unchanged** (3.1.7.1), and Kc is unchanged (3.1.7.2).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Catalyst | Increases the rate without being changed in chemical composition or amount |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy particles need when they collide for a reaction to occur |
| Alternative route | A different reaction pathway, often via an intermediate, whose highest barrier is lower than Ea |
| Intermediate | A species formed in one step and used up in a later step; it sits in a dip on the enthalpy profile |
| Enthalpy profile | Graph of enthalpy against progress of reaction showing reactants, products, ΔH and Ea |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy at least Ea (and suitable orientation) |

## Enthalpy profile checklist

- Reactant and product levels **identical** with and without catalyst, so ΔH is the same.
- Catalysed curve has a **lower peak**; with an intermediate it shows two humps, each lower than the uncatalysed hump.
- Label Ea from the **reactant level** to the **highest** point of each route.
- For an equilibrium: Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH. Lowering the forward barrier by x lowers the reverse barrier by x too.

**Example (invented values):** ΔH = −40 kJ mol⁻¹, Ea(forward) falls from +180 to +120 kJ mol⁻¹. Ea(reverse) falls from +220 to +160 kJ mol⁻¹. Both fall by 60 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch for a catalyst

1. Axes: **energy** (x) and **number of molecules** (y).
2. **One** curve, starting at the origin, single peak, tail approaching but not meeting the x-axis.
3. Ea line in the tail; Ea(cat) line to its **left**.
4. Identify the extra area between the lines: molecules that can react only by the catalysed route.

**Explanation chain:** alternative route → lower Ea → larger proportion of molecules have E ≥ Ea → more successful collisions per unit time → higher rate.

## Catalyst versus temperature

| | Catalyst | Temperature rise |
|---|---|---|
| Curve | Unchanged | Peak lower and further right; same area |
| Ea line | Moves left | Unchanged |
| Proportion with E ≥ Ea | Increases | Increases |
| Collision frequency | Unchanged | Increases slightly |
| Effect on Kc | None | Changes Kc |

## Why catalysts are used

- Faster reaction at a **lower temperature**: less energy for heating, lower fuel costs and less carbon dioxide from burning fuel.
- Equilibrium reached quickly, helping the compromise conditions chosen in industry.
- Not used up, so a small amount lasts.

Heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts are A2 content in 3.2.5.6, in the [transition metals revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals-revision-notes/).

## Exam traps

- "Lowers the activation energy" without "alternative route".
- "Not involved in the reaction": catalysts usually take part.
- Drawing a second curve for a catalyst, or moving the peak.
- Changing ΔH on a catalysed profile.
- Claiming a catalyst increases the **yield** of an equilibrium.
- Measuring Ea from the product level, or to the lower of two humps.
- Forgetting "per unit time" in the final link to rate.

## Quick self-test

1. Write the specification's definition of a catalyst.
2. How does a catalyst increase the rate of a reaction?
3. In an acid-catalysed reaction, H⁺ is used in step 1. Why does it not appear in the overall equation?
4. What happens to ΔH when a catalyst is added?
5. On a Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch, how many curves are drawn to show the effect of a catalyst at fixed temperature?
6. On that sketch, where is Ea(cat) placed relative to Ea?
7. A catalysed profile has two humps. Which one sets the activation energy of the catalysed route?
8. ΔH = −60 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea(forward) = +95 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate Ea(reverse).
9. A catalyst lowers Ea(forward) in question 8 to +70 kJ mol⁻¹. What is the new Ea(reverse)?
10. Explain why a catalyst does not change the position of an equilibrium.
11. Give one economic and one environmental benefit of using a catalyst in industry.
12. State one thing that both a temperature rise and a catalyst increase, and one thing only temperature changes.

### Answers

1. A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount.
2. It provides an **alternative route of lower activation energy**, so a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react.
3. It is **re-formed** in a later step, so it cancels from the overall equation.
4. **No change**.
5. **One**.
6. To the **left** (lower energy), nearer the peak.
7. The **higher** hump, measured from the reactant level.
8. Ea(reverse) = 95 − (−60) = **+155 kJ mol⁻¹**.
9. 70 − (−60) = **+130 kJ mol⁻¹** (both barriers fell by 25 kJ mol⁻¹).
10. It lowers the forward and reverse barriers by the same amount, so it speeds up both reactions equally; equilibrium is reached sooner with the same composition.
11. Economic: lower heating costs (or faster production). Environmental: less fuel burned, so less carbon dioxide released.
12. Both increase the **proportion of molecules with energy at least the activation energy** for the route used (so the rate). Only temperature changes the **Maxwell–Boltzmann curve** (or collision frequency, or Kc).
