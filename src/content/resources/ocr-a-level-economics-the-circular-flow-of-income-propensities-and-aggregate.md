## 1. Measuring national income

National income can be measured in three ways, and in principle all three give the same total because every unit spent is someone's output and someone's income.

- **Output method:** add the value added by each sector. Value added is the value of a firm's output minus the cost of the inputs bought from other firms. Adding final values only, or value added at each stage, avoids double counting.
- **Income method:** add the incomes earned by the factors of production: wages and salaries, rent, interest and profit, including self-employment income.
- **Expenditure method:** add spending on final goods and services: C + I + G + (X − M).

**Worked example (Country A, cu billion, illustrative).**

| Output method | cu bn | Income method | cu bn | Expenditure method | cu bn |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 60 | Wages | 560 | Consumption C | 600 |
| Manufacturing | 280 | Profits | 260 | Investment I | 150 |
| Construction | 90 | Rent | 100 | Government G | 200 |
| Services | 570 | Self-employment | 80 | Exports X | 180 |
| | | | | Imports M | −130 |
| **Total** | **1,000** | **Total** | **1,000** | **Total** | **1,000** |

Expenditure: 600 + 150 + 200 + (180 − 130) = 1,000. Net exports are 50.

**Double counting.** A farm sells wheat for 30 to a mill, the mill sells flour for 50 to a baker, and the baker sells bread for 90. Value added: 30, then 50 − 30 = 20, then 90 − 50 = 40, a total of 90. Adding the sales (30 + 50 + 90 = 170) would count the wheat three times.

## 2. The circular flow of income

In the circular flow model, households supply factor services to firms in return for income, then spend that income on what firms produce.

- In the **simple two-sector model** (households and firms) all income is spent, so the flow is constant.
- Adding the **financial sector, government and overseas sector** gives three types of leakage and three of injection.

**Leakages (withdrawals, W)** take money out of the spending stream:
- saving (S)
- taxation (T)
- spending on imports (M)

**Injections (J)** put money in:
- investment (I)
- government spending (G)
- exports (X)

The flow is in equilibrium when W = J. If J > W, more money enters than leaves, so national income tends to rise. If W > J, it tends to fall.

**Worked example.** Country B has S = 90, T = 210, M = 120, and I = 100, G = 200, X = 110 (cu bn).
- W = 90 + 210 + 120 = 420
- J = 100 + 200 + 110 = 410
- W exceeds J by 10, so income tends to fall until withdrawals match injections again.

Saving is a leakage because it is not passed on as spending; it returns as investment only if the financial sector lends it to firms.

## 3. Physical and monetary flows

**Physical (real) flows** are movements of resources, goods and services. Households supply labour, land and capital services to firms, and firms supply goods and services to households.

**Monetary flows** are the payments that move in the opposite direction: wages, rent, interest and profit paid to households, and consumer spending paid to firms.

A baker employs a worker: labour flows to the bakery and the wage flows to the worker. The worker buys a loaf: bread flows to the household and the payment flows to the bakery. Each physical flow has a monetary flow in the opposite direction.

## 4. Average and marginal propensities

Propensities describe how households divide income. Use disposable income (Yd) when working with households.

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Average propensity to consume (APC) | C / Yd |
| Average propensity to save (APS) | S / Yd |
| Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) | ΔC / ΔYd |
| Marginal propensity to save (MPS) | ΔS / ΔYd |

Since income is either consumed or saved, **APC + APS = 1** and **MPC + MPS = 1**. This holds when the base is disposable income. The average measures relate to the whole income and the marginal measures to the change in income.

**Worked example (a household group in Country C, cu).**

| Yd | C | S = Yd − C | APC | APS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 90 | 10 | 0.90 | 0.10 |
| 200 | 170 | 30 | 0.85 | 0.15 |
| 300 | 250 | 50 | 0.833 | 0.167 |
| 400 | 330 | 70 | 0.825 | 0.175 |

From 100 to 200: MPC = (170 − 90) / (200 − 100) = 80 / 100 = **0.8**, so MPS = 30 − 10 over 100 = **0.2**. Every step gives MPC = 0.8. APC falls as income rises because the household spends only 80% of each extra unit, but spent 90 when Yd was 100.

## 5. The marginal propensity to withdraw

Households can leave the spending stream in three ways, so the **marginal propensity to withdraw (MPW)** is the share of an extra unit of national income that is withdrawn:

MPW = MPS + marginal rate of tax (MRT) + marginal propensity to import (MPM)

- MRT = ΔT / ΔY
- MPM = ΔM / ΔY
- MPS = ΔS / ΔY

The share re-spent on domestically produced goods is 1 − MPW. The **average propensity to withdraw** is W / Y.

**Worked example 1.** National income rises by 100. Saving rises by 10, tax revenue by 20 and imports by 15.
- MPS = 10/100 = 0.10, MRT = 20/100 = 0.20, MPM = 15/100 = 0.15
- MPW = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.15 = **0.45**
- Re-spent on domestic output: 1 − 0.45 = 0.55

**Worked example 2.** Country D's national income rises from 800 to 1,000. Saving moves from 80 to 110, tax from 160 to 210, imports from 120 to 160.
- MPS = 30/200 = 0.15; MRT = 50/200 = 0.25; MPM = 40/200 = 0.20
- MPW = 0.15 + 0.25 + 0.20 = **0.60**
- Average propensity to withdraw: at 800, (80 + 160 + 120)/800 = 0.45; at 1,000, (110 + 210 + 160)/1,000 = 0.48

The average rises because the marginal rate (0.60) exceeds the average (0.45).

## 6. Aggregate demand

**Aggregate demand** is planned spending on the output of an economy during a period, at each possible price level:

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

AD is *planned* spending at each price level; measured national expenditure is what took place.

## 7. Determinants of the components of AD

**Consumption (C):**
- disposable income, which changes with income, taxes and benefits
- interest rates: a rise makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive
- wealth, such as rising house or share prices
- consumer confidence and expectations about jobs, income and prices
- availability and cost of credit

**Investment (I):**
- interest rates, because they are the cost of borrowing and the return given up by using retained profit
- expected returns and business confidence; Keynes called swings in confidence "animal spirits"
- changes in output, which is the accelerator (Section 9)
- technology and corporation tax
- availability of finance

**Government spending (G):** political and policy decisions, tax revenue, the state of the economy and the size of any deficit.

**Net exports (X − M):**
- the exchange rate: a higher rate makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper
- relative price levels and quality
- income abroad, which drives export demand
- trade barriers
- the home country's own income level, which raises imports

**Chain (interest rates).** Lower rate → cheaper borrowing, less reward for saving → higher C and I → higher AD.

## 8. Income and consumption (evaluation)

The simple relationship is a consumption function: C = a + b·Yd, where *a* is autonomous consumption (spending even at zero income) and *b* is the MPC. Higher income raises consumption, but by less than the rise in income when MPC < 1.

**Worked example.** If a = 50 and b = 0.8: at Yd = 400, C = 50 + 0.8 × 400 = 370; at Yd = 500, C = 450. The change in C is 80 for a rise of 100 in Yd, as MPC = 0.8 says.

**Evaluation points:**
- *Income level:* the MPC is usually higher for low-income households, so who receives extra income matters.
- *Expectations:* a rise seen as temporary may be saved, a rise seen as lasting spent.
- *Wealth, credit and interest rates* can change consumption without any income change.
- *Time:* consumption may respond with a lag.

Overall, income is the main determinant, but the link is not fixed.

## 9. The accelerator theory of investment

The accelerator ties **investment to how fast output is changing**. Let *v* be the capital-output ratio, the units of capital needed to produce one unit of output. Net investment = v × ΔY.

**Worked example.** v = 3, replacement investment = 20 each year (illustrative).

| Year | Output Y | ΔY | Capital needed (3Y) | Net investment | Gross investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | | 300 | | |
| 2 | 110 | +10 | 330 | 30 | 50 |
| 3 | 125 | +15 | 375 | 45 | 65 |
| 4 | 135 | +10 | 405 | 30 | 50 |
| 5 | 135 | 0 | 405 | 0 | 20 |
| 6 | 130 | −5 | 390 | −15 | 5 |

Output rises in year 4 but gross investment falls from 65 to 50, because output grew more slowly. Investment depends on how fast output grows, so a small change in output growth causes a larger change in investment.

**Evaluation of the theory:**
- *Strength:* explains why investment is volatile.
- *Spare capacity:* if firms have idle capital, extra output needs no new investment.
- *Expectations:* firms invest only if growth is expected to last.
- *Finance and interest rates* can block investment even when output grows.
- *The capital-output ratio* changes with technology, so *v* is not constant.

## 10. AD and the price level

The AD curve slopes downward. **Axes:** price level on the vertical axis, real output (real GDP) on the horizontal. A higher price level reduces the quantity of real output demanded, for three reasons:

1. **Wealth effect:** higher prices reduce the real value of money wealth, so households spend less.
2. **Interest rate effect:** higher prices increase the demand for money, which pushes interest rates up and reduces C and I.
3. **International trade effect:** higher domestic prices make exports less competitive and imports relatively cheaper, so X − M falls.

A new price level means a **movement along** the AD curve. Anything else that changes planned spending at every price level causes a **shift**.

## 11. Why the AD curve shifts

A change in any component at each price level shifts AD. A rise shifts the curve right; a fall shifts it left.

| Event | Component | Shift |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer confidence falls | C | Left |
| Lower interest rates | C and I | Right |
| Government cuts spending | G | Left |
| Overseas incomes rise | X | Right |
| Exchange rate rises | X down, M up | Left |

**Worked example.** At each price level, C rises by 15, I falls by 10, G rises by 20, X rises by 5 and M rises by 8. The initial change in AD = 15 − 10 + 20 + 5 − 8 = **+22**, a shift to the right. Note that a rise in imports reduces AD, so M enters with a negative sign.

## Common errors

- Writing MPC + MPS = 1 using national income when taxes and imports exist; use disposable income.
- Stating the accelerator as "investment depends on output" instead of its *change*.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three methods of measuring national income?**
The output method adds the value added by each sector, the income method adds incomes earned by the factors of production, and the expenditure method adds spending on final goods and services, C + I + G + (X − M).

**What is the difference between physical and monetary flows in the circular flow?**
Physical flows are movements of resources, goods and services, such as labour supplied to firms and goods supplied to households. Monetary flows are the payments, such as wages and consumer spending, that move in the opposite direction.

**Why does a rise in imports reduce aggregate demand?**
Aggregate demand measures planned spending on domestic output. Spending on imports goes to foreign producers, so imports enter AD with a negative sign: AD = C + I + G + (X − M).

**Is income the main determinant of consumption?**
The OCR A Level Economics (H460) guide concludes that income is the main determinant, but the link is not fixed. Who receives the income, whether a rise is expected to last, wealth, credit, interest rates and time lags all affect how much is spent.

**Does MPC plus MPS always equal 1?**
MPC + MPS = 1 holds when the base is disposable income, because disposable income is either consumed or saved. Using national income as the base when taxes and imports exist is a common error.
