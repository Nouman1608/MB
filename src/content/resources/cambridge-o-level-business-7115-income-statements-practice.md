These questions cover section 5.3 Income statements of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. Constructing an income statement is not assessed, so the questions ask you to read, calculate from and use statements. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Revise first with the [income statements study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-income-statements/) and the [income statements revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-income-statements-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "revenue". **[2]**

**2.** State the difference between gross profit and profit. **[2]**

**3.** A business has revenue of $120,000, cost of sales of $72,000 and expenses of $31,000.

**(a)** Calculate its gross profit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its profit. **[1]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why profit is important to the owner of a private sector business. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a profitable business may be short of cash. **[3]**

**6.** State **two** uses a company can make of its profit after tax. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Juniper Shoes, extracts from income statements ($):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 400,000 | 380,000 |
| Cost of sales | 240,000 | 209,000 |
| Expenses | 110,000 | 118,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit and the profit for each year. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **two** possible reasons why profit rose even though revenue fell. **[4]**

**8.** Lakshmi Lamps sells 5,000 lamps a year at $30 each. Cost of sales is $18 per lamp and expenses are $35,000 a year. The owner has two options.

- Option A: raise the price to $33. Sales are expected to fall to 4,600 lamps.
- Option B: keep the price at $30 and spend an extra $8,000 a year on advertising. Sales are expected to rise to 5,600 lamps.

**(a)** Calculate the current profit. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the expected profit under Option A. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the expected profit under Option B. **[2]**

**(d)** Recommend and justify which option the owner should choose. **[4]**

**9.** Merrow Bakery sells bread and cakes ($ per year):

| | Bread | Cakes |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 90,000 | 40,000 |
| Cost of sales | 45,000 | 22,000 |
| Expenses charged | 30,000 | 21,000 |
| Profit or loss | 15,000 | (3,000) |

Of the expenses charged to cakes, $14,000 are shared costs, such as rent and the oven, that would continue if cakes were dropped.

**(a)** Calculate the bakery's total profit now. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the bakery's total profit if cakes were dropped. **[2]**

**(c)** Consider whether Merrow Bakery should stop selling cakes. Justify your answer. **[5]**

**10.** Hollin Tools made a profit of $80,000. It paid tax of $16,000 and dividends of $40,000.

**(a)** Calculate its retained profit. **[2]**

**(b)** The directors want to spend $60,000 on new machinery. Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of using retained profit rather than a bank loan. **[4]**

**11.** Ardent Engineering made a profit of $45,000 last year, but its bank overdraft increased. During the year it sold most of its output on credit, bought a new machine for $70,000 in cash and built up its inventory. Explain **two** reasons why Ardent's cash fell even though it made a profit. **[4]**

**12.** Corin owns a small furniture shop. She plans to open a second shop and intends to base her decision on last year's income statement. Consider whether an income statement gives Corin enough information to make this decision. Justify your answer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The value of sales made in a period [1], calculated as price × quantity sold [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Money from sales" is close but loses precision; revenue is counted when the sale is made, not when cash arrives.

**2.** Gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales [1], whereas profit is gross profit minus expenses (overheads) [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** 120,000 − 72,000 = **$48,000** [1].

**(b)** 48,000 − 31,000 = **$17,000** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Profit is the reward for taking a risk [1]. The owner has invested savings that could be lost if the business fails [1], so without the chance of profit there would be no reason to start or continue the business (also credit: profit as a source of finance for expansion, explained) [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The business may sell on credit [1], so revenue and profit are recorded when the sale is made [1] but the cash does not arrive until customers pay, which may be weeks later, leaving too little cash to pay suppliers (also credit: buying inventory or assets for cash, repaying loans) [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two: pay dividends to shareholders [1]; keep it as retained profit to reinvest in the business (or pay off loans) [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Year 1: gross profit = 400,000 − 240,000 = **$160,000** [1]; profit = 160,000 − 110,000 = **$50,000** [1]. Year 2: gross profit = 380,000 − 209,000 = **$171,000** [1]; profit = 171,000 − 118,000 = **$53,000** [1].

**(b)** Reason 1: cost of sales fell by $31,000 (about 13%), much more than revenue fell (5%) [1], which suggests Juniper found a cheaper supplier or cut waste, so each pair sold earned more gross profit [1]. Reason 2: Juniper may have stopped giving discounts or raised its prices, selling fewer pairs at a higher margin [1], so revenue fell slightly but the gross profit on each pair rose by more than enough to cover the $8,000 rise in expenses [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers point to the line in the statement where the change happened.

**8. (a)** Revenue = 5,000 × 30 = $150,000; cost of sales = 5,000 × 18 = $90,000 [1]; profit = 150,000 − 90,000 − 35,000 = **$25,000** [1].

**(b)** Revenue = 4,600 × 33 = $151,800; cost of sales = 4,600 × 18 = $82,800 [1]; profit = 151,800 − 82,800 − 35,000 = **$34,000** [1].

**(c)** Revenue = 5,600 × 30 = $168,000; cost of sales = 5,600 × 18 = $100,800 [1]; profit = 168,000 − 100,800 − 43,000 = **$24,200** [1].

**(d)** Recommend Option A [1]: profit rises by $9,000, while Option B lowers profit by $800 because the extra gross profit ($7,200) is less than the $8,000 advertising cost [1]. Option A also sells fewer lamps, so less stock and work are needed [1]. However, A depends on sales falling only to 4,600; if more customers switch to rivals, the gain shrinks, so the owner should test the price rise first [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* The calculations earn their marks only if the recommendation uses them and names the assumption behind them.

**9. (a)** 15,000 − 3,000 = **$12,000** [1].

**(b)** The $14,000 of shared costs would still be paid [1], so total profit = 15,000 − 14,000 = **$1,000** [1].

**(c)** Against dropping: cakes make a gross profit of $18,000 [1], which more than covers the $7,000 of costs that would actually be saved [1], so dropping them would cut total profit by $11,000 [1]. For dropping: cakes take staff time and oven space that might be used for more bread, if extra bread could be sold [1]. Judgement: Merrow should keep cakes unless it can use the freed resources to earn more than $11,000 a year; it could instead try to cut the cost of making cakes [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* The loss shown for cakes is misleading because it includes costs that do not go away.

**10. (a)** 80,000 − 16,000 − 40,000 [1] = **$24,000** [1].

**(b)** Advantage: retained profit has no interest to pay and does not need to be repaid [1], so Hollin's costs and risk do not rise [1]. Disadvantage: retained profit is only $24,000, far less than $60,000, so Hollin would need to cut dividends or delay the purchase [1], which may upset shareholders who expect a return [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Using the figure from part (a) turns a general answer into an applied one.

**11.** Reason 1: sales on credit count as revenue and profit straight away [1], but customers had not yet paid, so cash had not come in [1]. Reason 2: the $70,000 machine and the extra inventory were paid for in cash [1], but neither counts in full as a cost in that year's income statement, so profit stayed high while cash fell and the overdraft grew [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: the income statement shows whether the existing shop is profitable [1], and the size of the gross profit and profit suggests whether the business can cover the expenses of a second shop [1]. Retained profit shows how much internal finance may be available [1]. Against: it shows last year, not the future, and the new shop's location and customers may be different [1]. It does not show cash, so Corin cannot tell whether she can afford the rent and stock before the new shop earns money [1]. Judgement: it is useful but not enough; Corin also needs a cash-flow forecast and market research on the new area before deciding [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "Consider" answers need both what the statement shows and what it cannot show, then a conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "profit" when the question asks for gross profit, or the reverse.
- Leaving out units or labels in calculations.
- Treating a loss-making product as one to drop without checking which costs continue.
- Saying a profitable firm cannot be short of cash.
- Recommending an option without using the profit figures calculated.
- Forgetting that a forecast depends on assumptions about sales.
