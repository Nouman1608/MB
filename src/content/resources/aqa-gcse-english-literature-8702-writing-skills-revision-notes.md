Quick-recall notes on the writing skills in the specification. The full explanation, with a worked
before-and-after paragraph, is in the [Writing study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-8702-writing-skills/).

## What the specification asks for

| Skill | In practice |
|---|---|
| Writing for purposes | Describe, explain, summarise, argue, analyse and evaluate, each in proportion |
| Discussing and maintaining a point of view | A clear thesis sustained across the essay, with other views discussed |
| Selecting and emphasising key points | Three or four strong points in depth, ordered for impact |
| Relevant quotation and detailed reference | Short embedded quotations; precise references to events |
| Accurate Standard English | Correct spelling, punctuation and grammar |

**AO4** is assessed on the Shakespeare question (Paper 1) and the modern text question (Paper 2), 4
marks each, for a range of vocabulary and sentence structures with accurate spelling and punctuation.

## Core recall

- **Thesis first**: arguable, specific, covering the whole text.
- **Topic sentences advance the argument**; they do not just name a new topic.
- **Summary in one clause**, then analysis.
- **Embed quotations** so that sentences read grammatically.
- **Present tense** for the text; past tense only for historical context.
- **Formal register** with precise analytical verbs.
- **Proofread** the two AO4 questions.

## Thesis ladder

| Level | Example for "How does the writer present loyalty?" |
|---|---|
| Topic only | The writer presents loyalty in different ways. |
| A view | The writer presents loyalty as fragile. |
| Arguable, specific, whole text | The writer presents loyalty as fragile because it depends on self-interest, so each betrayal in the text grows from a character protecting themselves. |

Aim for the third level: a reader could disagree with it, and it gives the essay a direction.

## Purposes in proportion

| Purpose | Rough share of a paragraph |
|---|---|
| Summarise or describe | A clause to set up the moment |
| Explain | A sentence on what the evidence means |
| Analyse | The core: how the method creates meaning |
| Evaluate | A sentence judging impact or significance |
| Argue | Topic sentence and closing link to the thesis |

## Linking paragraphs

Each new paragraph should pick up the last one's idea and move it on:

- "This control begins to slip when..."
- "A darker version of this appears in..."
- "By contrast, the ending suggests..."
- "This complicates the earlier view, because..."

## Analytical verbs

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| shows | suggests, conveys, reveals, exposes, emphasises |
| says | asserts, claims, insists, admits, implies |
| makes the reader feel | evokes, provokes, unsettles, invites sympathy |
| is about | explores, examines, questions, challenges |
| uses | employs, deploys, crafts, constructs |

## Quotation conventions

| Convention | Use |
|---|---|
| Quotation marks | Around every quoted word or phrase |
| Ellipsis (...) | Shows words left out |
| Square brackets [ ] | Small changes to fit your grammar |
| Forward slash ( / ) | Marks a line break when quoting verse |
| Underlining (handwriting) | Titles of plays, novels and other whole texts |
| Quotation marks for titles | Poems and short stories |

## Accuracy checklist

| Watch for | Rule |
|---|---|
| its / it's | possession / "it is" |
| their / there / they're | belonging / place / "they are" |
| affect / effect | usually verb / usually noun |
| should of | should have |
| alot | a lot |
| comma splice | join with a full stop, semicolon or connective |
| possessive names | Sheila's (one person); the Birlings' (the family) |
| capital letters | names, titles, the start of sentences, "I" |

## Sentence variety

- **Complex**: "Although X, Y" links contrasting ideas.
- **Short**: a brief sentence for emphasis after a long one.
- **Varied openings**: start with a subordinate clause, an adverb or a quotation, not always "The
  writer".
- **Semicolon**: joins two closely related complete sentences.
- **Colon**: introduces an explanation or example.

## Exam traps

- **No argument**: paragraphs that list features or events.
- **Retelling**: long summaries that crowd out analysis.
- **Dropped quotations** left as separate sentences without comment.
- **Invented quotations** under pressure; use a precise reference instead.
- **Tense drift** from present to past.
- **Misspelled names and terms** repeated throughout an answer.
- **No time to check** the questions carrying AO4.

## Self-test

1. Which questions carry AO4 marks, and how many marks each?
2. What three qualities make a strong thesis?
3. What is the difference between a topic sentence that announces and one that advances?
4. Why should summary be kept to a clause?
5. What does an ellipsis show in a quotation?
6. How do you mark a line break when quoting poetry?
7. Which tense should you use to discuss the events of a text?
8. Correct this: "Its clear that there anger effects them."
9. What is a comma splice, and how do you fix one?
10. Give two analytical verbs to replace "shows".
11. How should you set out the title of a poem in a handwritten essay?
12. What should you do if you cannot remember an exact quotation?

## Answers

1. The Shakespeare question on Paper 1 and the modern text question on Paper 2; 4 marks each.
2. It is arguable, specific and covers the whole text.
3. An announcing sentence names a new topic ("Another theme is guilt"); an advancing sentence links
   to the argument and develops it ("This isolation deepens once guilt replaces ambition").
4. Because summary earns little credit; analysis and argument earn the marks.
5. That words have been left out.
6. With a forward slash.
7. The present tense.
8. "It's clear that their anger affects them."
9. Two complete sentences joined only by a comma; fix it with a full stop, a semicolon or a
   connective.
10. Any two of: suggests, conveys, reveals, exposes, emphasises.
11. In quotation marks.
12. Refer precisely to the event or moment instead; never invent a quotation.
