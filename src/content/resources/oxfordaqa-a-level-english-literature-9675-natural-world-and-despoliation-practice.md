## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain the three parts of the specification's phrase about the natural world and why an essay
should address all three. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between presenting nature as a mirror and presenting it as an
indifferent force. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how pathetic fallacy differs from simple description of weather or landscape. **[2]**

**4.** For one poet in your selection, name one poem that presents nature's beauty and one that
presents its damage, and explain how the contrast could shape an essay. **[4]**

**5.** A student says: "Hardy's poems show the beauty of the countryside." Give two reasons why this
is an incomplete account. **[2]**

## Section B: an original poem and essay skills

**Poem (original).**

> **The Quarry**
>
> My grandfather blasted this hill for forty years.
> He knew its seams the way you know a voice.
> Now water fills the floor he used to clear,
> and in the cuts the ferns have made a choice
> to live where nothing should. The children swim
> in what the engines left. A heron stands
> on the old crane's arm, as if the arm were him,
> and lifts, and shows the rust upon its hands.

**6.** Analyse how the poet presents the quarry as a place of both damage and renewal. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the poem connects the natural world to memory and family. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse how rhyme and line endings contribute to the poem's meaning. **[3]**

**9.** A student wants to compare this poem with a set poem in which nature reclaims human work.
Name a suitable poem from the selections and explain one similarity and one difference. **[3]**

**10.** For your own poet, plan an essay on the question: "In these poems the natural world is always
damaged by human beings." List five elements of the plan. **[5]**

**11.** Read this student paragraph: "Frost lived on a farm in New England. In 'Out, Out –' a boy
dies. The mountains are nice. This shows nature." Explain two weaknesses and write an improved
topic sentence. **[4]**

**12.** Explain how one poem from your selection uses a structural turn to change the significance of
the natural world. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** "Significance" asks what the poem makes nature mean, weighing method, context and
interpretation [1]; "its beauty" asks how and to whom nature appears beautiful, and what beauty does
[1]; "possibly its despoliation" asks whether damage is present, implied or absent, so all three
together let an essay move from description to argument [1]. **[3]**

**2.** As a mirror, the landscape reflects a human mood or condition, so it seems to share the
speaker's feeling [1]; as an indifferent force, nature carries on without regard for human hopes, or
destroys them, so it refuses to share human feeling [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Pathetic fallacy gives the landscape human feeling so that it expresses a speaker's or the
poem's emotional state [1]; simple description reports the scene without making it carry emotion or
meaning [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Two accurate poems [1]; explanation of how the contrast could shape the essay [3], for example
by setting up a debate about whether the poet trusts nature, by showing a change in the poet's
attitude, or by revealing that beauty and damage depend on viewpoint. Example: Wordsworth's
"Tintern Abbey" and "Nutting" [1] could be used to argue that the same landscape can educate or be
harmed [1], that the harm in "Nutting" comes from a child who has not yet learned the lesson
"Tintern Abbey" describes [1], and so the poet links reverence for nature to moral growth [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Any two, [1] each: many of Hardy's landscapes are bleak and wintry rather than beautiful; the
landscape often mirrors loss or a historical moment rather than being admired for itself; nature in
"The Convergence of the Twain" is an indifferent or punishing force; war disturbs the land in
"Channel Firing". **[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- "Blasted this hill for forty years" presents long, violent human use of the land.
- The simile "the way you know a voice" makes the grandfather's knowledge intimate, so damage and
  love coexist.
- Water filling "the floor he used to clear" shows nature returning to the space labour emptied.
- The ferns that "made a choice / to live where nothing should" personify renewal as determination.
- The enjambment in "The children swim / in what the engines left"
  turns a scar into a place of play.
- The heron on "the old crane's arm" places a living creature on a machine, nature occupying
  industry.
- "As if the arm were him" turns the crane into a memorial to the grandfather.
- The final image of "rust upon its hands" suggests both decay and the marks of work, so the poem
  refuses a simple verdict.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the speaker sees the quarry through the grandfather's working life; the
grandfather's intimate knowledge of the hill links family identity to the land; the crane becomes a
substitute for him, so the place holds his memory; the children swimming show the next generation
using the land differently; renewal of the landscape parallels the passing of generations. **[4]**

**8.** Any three, [1] each: alternating rhyme (voice/choice, clear/years as a half-rhyme) gives a
controlled, reflective tone; the rhyme of "choice" with "voice" links the ferns' will to the
grandfather's knowledge; enjambment carries sentences past line ends, imitating water and growth
spreading; the strong rhyme of "hands" with "stands" closes the poem on an image of work. **[3]**

**9.** A suitable poem, such as Frost's "The Wood-Pile" [1]. Similarity [1]: both show abandoned
human work being taken back by nature. Difference [1]: in "The Wood-Pile" the worker is unknown and
the tone is detached, while the original poem is personal and grieving. **[3]**

**10.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on "always"; a poem that supports the view, with
its methods; a poem that challenges it by presenting beauty without damage; a poem where damage is
caused by something other than people (time, weather, indifference); integrated comparison around a
shared question; a relevant context linked to a line; an alternative reading; a conclusion on what the
poet suggests about people and nature. **[5]**

**11.** Weakness one [1]: the biography and plot summary are not linked to how the poem works.
Weakness two [1]: "nice" and "This shows nature" are vague and make no argument about significance.
Improved topic sentence [2] for an argued, precise claim, [1] for a partial improvement. Example:
"Frost sets the boy's death against the calm beauty of the mountain ranges, so the indifference of
the landscape makes the accident with the farm saw more shocking." **[4]**

**12.** Poem and turn identified accurately [1]; explanation of how the turn changes nature's
significance [2]. For example: in "Nutting" the boy's triumph turns to a sense of pain after the
destruction [1], so the bower changes from a prize to a victim [1] and the poem ends by teaching
respect for the spirit of the woods [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing landscapes without arguing what they mean.
- Treating every nature poem as praise of nature.
- Biography or plot summary in place of analysis.
- Writing on poems separately instead of comparing them around a shared idea.
