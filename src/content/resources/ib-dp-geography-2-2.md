## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how waves, tides and currents shape coastal landforms through erosion, transport and deposition.
- Explain how geology, sea-level change and ecosystems influence coastal places.
- Explain how human activity alters coastal processes and the risks people face.

## Tides and tidal range

Tides are produced mainly by the Moon's gravitational pull, with a smaller contribution from the Sun.

- **Spring tides** occur around new and full moon, when Sun, Moon and Earth are aligned; the tidal range is largest.
- **Neap tides** occur around the first and last quarters, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles; the range is smallest.

**Tidal range** varies greatly between coasts because the shape of the sea and coastline can amplify or damp the tide. Funnel-shaped bays and estuaries can produce very large ranges; the Bay of Fundy in Canada is a well-known example. Enclosed seas such as the Mediterranean have small ranges.

Why tidal range matters for coastal places:

- A **large range** spreads wave attack over a wide vertical zone and exposes broad intertidal areas such as mudflats and salt marsh.
- A **small range** concentrates wave energy at one level, so notches and platforms form at a narrow height band.
- Tidal currents in estuaries carry and deposit fine sediment, building mudflats.

## Storm surges

A **storm surge** is a temporary rise in sea level above the predicted tide, caused by a storm. Two processes combine:

1. **Low air pressure.** Lower pressure on the sea surface lets it rise. A rule of thumb is about 1 cm of rise for each hectopascal (hPa) fall in pressure.
2. **Wind set-up.** Strong onshore winds pile water against the coast. In shallow seas and funnel-shaped coasts this effect is far larger than the pressure effect.

**Worked example.** Normal sea-level pressure is about 1,013 hPa. A deep storm has a central pressure of 963 hPa.

```
Pressure fall = 1,013 - 963 = 50 hPa
Rise from low pressure alone = about 50 x 1 cm = 50 cm, or 0.5 m
```

If wind set-up adds 2.5 m on a shallow coast, the surge is about 3 m. Whether that floods the coast depends on **timing**: a surge arriving at high spring tide is far more dangerous than the same surge at low neap tide. This is why the North Sea flood of 1953, which struck the Netherlands and eastern England, was so destructive: a deep storm surge coincided with a high spring tide.

## How wave energy scales

Wave energy depends on the **square of wave height**. A wave twice as high carries about four times the energy; three times as high, about nine times.

```
Energy ratio = (height ratio) squared
2 m wave compared with 1 m wave: (2 / 1) squared = 4 times the energy
3 m wave compared with 1 m wave: (3 / 1) squared = 9 times the energy
```

This explains why most coastal change happens in a few storms. Waves in a storm may be several times the height of normal waves, so they carry many times the energy and can move more sediment in a day than calm conditions move in months. It is also why mean rates of cliff retreat, as the revision notes warn, hide large year-to-year variation.

## Seasonal beach change

Many beaches have two profiles through the year.

| | Summer (or calm) profile | Winter (or storm) profile |
|---|---|---|
| Wave type | Mainly constructive, long-period swell | Mainly destructive, short-period storm waves |
| Sediment movement | Onshore, building a **berm** at the top of the beach | Offshore, forming a **bar** below low tide |
| Beach shape | Wide and steep near the top | Narrow, lower and flatter |

The sediment is not lost: much of it is stored offshore in the bar and returns in calm weather. This matters for management. A beach that looks "eroded" after a winter storm may recover naturally, so measuring a single profile after a storm can mislead.

## Reading a sediment budget

A sediment budget compares inputs with outputs for a stretch of coast over a year.

**Worked example (invented beach, cubic metres per year).**

| Inputs | | Outputs | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cliff erosion updrift | 40,000 | Longshore drift out of the cell | 45,000 |
| River supply | 10,000 | Offshore loss in storms | 10,000 |
| Onshore movement from the seabed | 3,000 | Sand removed by dredging | 3,000 |
| **Total** | **53,000** | **Total** | **58,000** |

Net change = 53,000 - 58,000 = **-5,000 cubic metres a year**, so the beach store is shrinking. Now suppose cliffs updrift are protected by a sea wall and their supply falls by half to 20,000: inputs become 33,000 and the deficit grows to 25,000 cubic metres a year. Protecting one place has starved another, the knock-on effect at the heart of coastal systems thinking.

## Coastal squeeze

Salt marshes and mangroves can respond to sea-level rise by moving inland, provided there is space. Where a sea wall, road or town blocks them, the habitat is caught between the rising sea and the fixed barrier and shrinks. This is **coastal squeeze**. Its consequences:

- Loss of habitats that dissipate wave energy, so the barrier behind them faces bigger waves.
- Loss of nursery areas for fish and of carbon storage.
- Pressure for higher defences, which repeats the problem.

Marshes can also keep pace with rising seas by trapping sediment and building up their surface, but only if sediment supply is enough. Where rivers have been dammed or sediment cells interrupted, the marsh may drown instead.

## Human activity and coastal processes

- **Sand mining** from beaches and rivers removes sediment directly, lowering beaches and increasing wave attack.
- **Coral and mangrove removal** strips away natural wave barriers.
- **Groundwater and gas extraction** under deltas can make land subside, raising relative sea level locally.
- **Ports and breakwaters** interrupt longshore drift.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a storm surge with a tsunami; a surge is caused by weather, a tsunami by sea-floor displacement.
- Treating wave energy as proportional to height rather than to height squared.
- Reading a winter beach profile as permanent erosion.
- Calculating a sediment budget without stating whether the store is gaining or losing.
- Describing coastal squeeze without the fixed barrier that causes it.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** coastal change with the energy input (waves, tides, surge), the process, and the response of the landform.
- **Suggest** reasons for differences along a coast shown in a source: fetch, tidal range, geology, defences.
- **Examine** human influence by showing both direct effects (dredging) and indirect ones (dams inland).
- Quantify where you can: energy ratios, budget totals, pressure falls.
