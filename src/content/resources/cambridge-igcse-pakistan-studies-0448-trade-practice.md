**What do these questions practise?** Section 6 Trade of Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448
Paper 2: exports and imports, changes in trade, GDP and GNP, the balance of trade, trading partners,
and the factors that promote or limit trade. All numerical data below is invented for practice and
does not describe Pakistan. For questions that combine trade with transport, see the
[Trade, Transport and Telecommunications practice questions](/resources/igcse-pakistan-studies-trade-transport-and-telecommunications-practice/).

## Section A: recall and calculation

**1.** Name **two** goods exported from Sialkot. **[2]**

**2.** Name **one** country from which Pakistan imports palm oil. **[1]**

**3.** What is meant by *value added*? **[1]**

**4.** A country's exports are worth $26 bn and its imports $47 bn. Calculate its balance of trade
and state whether it is a surplus or a deficit. **[2]**

**5.** A country has a GDP of $320 bn. Its residents earn $30 bn from abroad, and $8 bn is paid to
foreign owners. Calculate its GNP. **[2]**

**6.** What is a *tariff*? **[1]**

## Section B: explanation

**7.** Describe the changes in the types of goods Pakistan has exported in recent decades. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a fall in the value of the rupee can affect **both** exporters and importers in
Pakistan. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why Pakistan imports large amounts of machinery. **[3]**

**10.** Study the invented data below.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exports ($ bn) | 22 | 24 | 23 | 27 | 31 |

Describe the trend in exports shown. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how membership of trading blocs and agreements can help Pakistan's trade. **[4]**

**12.** Using the example of China, explain how one trading partner can be both a market for
Pakistan's exports and a source of its imports. **[4]**

## Section C: evaluation

**13.** "Pakistan should reduce its dependence on textile exports." Give your views. **[6]**

**14.** Evaluate how far a trade deficit is a problem for Pakistan's economy. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: sports goods, such as footballs [1]; surgical instruments [1]. Also credit: leather
gloves and other leather goods. **[2]**

**2.** Malaysia or Indonesia [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The extra value created when a raw material is processed into a more valuable product, for
example raw cotton made into cloth [1]. **[1]**

**4.** 26 − 47 = −$21 bn [1]; a deficit [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 320 + 30 − 8 [1] = $342 bn [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A tax on imported goods [1]. **[1]**

**7.** Earlier, much cotton was exported raw [1]. Over time more was processed into yarn and cloth
[1], and exports now include more finished goods, such as garments, knitwear, bed linen and towels,
which earn more value added [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Exporters: Pakistani goods become cheaper for foreign buyers, so exporters may win more
orders [1] and earn more rupees for each dollar of sales [1]. Importers: imported goods such as oil
and machinery cost more in rupees [1], which raises costs for businesses and prices for consumers,
and exporters who use imported inputs lose some of their advantage [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Pakistan does not make enough heavy machinery of its own [1]. Industries such as textiles need
modern spinning and weaving machines to compete [1], and power stations, infrastructure projects and
farming also need imported equipment [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Exports rose overall, from $22 bn in Year 1 to $31 bn in Year 5 [1]. There was a small fall
in Year 3, to $23 bn [1], followed by faster growth in Years 4 and 5 [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Members agree to lower or remove tariffs, so Pakistani goods become cheaper in partner
markets [1] and sell in greater quantities [1]. For example, the EU's GSP+ scheme reduces tariffs on
many Pakistani textiles [1], and agreements with neighbours such as through SAARC or ECO can open
markets that are close and cheap to reach [1]. **[4]**

**12.** China buys Pakistani goods such as cotton yarn, rice and seafood, so it is an important
export market [1]. Pakistan buys machinery, electronics, steel and chemicals from China [1], because
China produces these cheaply and in large quantities [1]. Pakistan usually imports far more from
China than it exports to it, so the relationship adds to Pakistan's trade deficit even though a
free-trade agreement links the two countries [1]. **[4]**

**13.** For reducing dependence: falls in world demand or prices for textiles hit Pakistan's
earnings hard [1]; strong competition from other low-cost producers keeps prices low [1]; textiles
need a lot of water and energy, and dyeing causes pollution [1]. Against: textiles use home-grown
cotton and employ many people [1]; Pakistan has experience and established buyers [1]. View: a
supported judgement, for example that Pakistan should keep textiles but move to higher-value
products while developing other exports such as information technology services [1]. **[6]**

**14.** A problem: the deficit must be paid for by borrowing or reserves [1], so foreign debt grows
and debt repayments take money away from development [1]; it puts pressure on the rupee, raising the
cost of imports [1]. Less of a problem: remittances from Pakistanis abroad help cover the gap [1];
some imports, such as machinery, help industry grow and can increase future exports [1]. Judgement:
for example, a deficit that pays for productive machinery is less harmful than one caused by fuel
and consumer goods [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "Punjab" instead of naming Sialkot or Faisalabad.
- Getting the sign wrong in balance-of-trade calculations.
- Describing a data table without quoting figures and years.
- One-sided answers to "give your views" or "evaluate" questions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the exam give me data on Pakistan's trade?**
Questions are built around resources such as graphs and tables, so practise reading trends and
quoting figures accurately.

**How long should a 6-mark evaluation be?**
About two developed paragraphs, one on each side, and a clear judgement.
