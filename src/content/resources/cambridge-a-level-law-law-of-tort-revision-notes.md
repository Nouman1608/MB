A tort is a civil wrong, other than breach of contract, for which the injured person can claim a
remedy, usually damages. Cambridge International A Level Law (9084) tests Topic 4 on Paper 4 Law of
Tort (1 hour 30 minutes, 75 marks): one 25-mark scenario from two in Section A and two 25-mark essays
from three in Section B. Examiners want each element of the tort identified, supported by authority,
applied to the facts and, in essays, evaluated.

These notes condense strands 4.1 to 4.4 of the 2026-2028 syllabus. For fuller explanations read the
[Law of tort study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-law-of-tort-study-guide/); to test
yourself use the [Law of tort practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-law-of-tort-practice/).

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Actionable per se | Actionable without proof of damage (all trespass torts) |
| Vicarious liability | One person (usually an employer) liable for another's tort committed in the course of employment |
| Joint liability | Two or more tortfeasors each liable for the whole damage; they may claim contribution from each other |
| Pure economic loss | Financial loss not consequent on physical damage to the claimant or their property |
| Primary victim | Claimant within the range of foreseeable physical harm |
| Secondary victim | Claimant who suffers psychiatric injury from witnessing harm to another |
| Special damages | Quantifiable losses to the date of trial |
| General damages | Non-pecuniary loss and future losses |

## How do you prove negligence? (4.1)

**Duty (4.1.2)** - neighbour principle (Donoghue v Stevenson, 1932); Caparo v Dickman (1990): foreseeability,
proximity, fair just and reasonable. Established categories apply by precedent; Caparo is for novel
cases (Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire, 2018). Policy can deny a duty (Hill v Chief
Constable of West Yorkshire, 1989), for example to avoid defensive practice or floodgates.

**Breach (4.1.3)** - objective reasonable person (Vaughan v Menlove, 1837).
- Children: a reasonable child of the same age (Mullin v Richards, 1998).
- Learners: the competent standard (Nettleship v Weston, 1971).
- Professionals: Bolam (1957), with the opinion needing a logical basis (Bolitho, 1997).
- Risk factors: likelihood (Bolton v Stone, 1951); seriousness (Paris v Stepney BC, 1951); cost of
  precautions (Latimer v AEC, 1953); social value (Watt v Hertfordshire CC, 1954).

**Causation and remoteness (4.1.4)**
- But for: Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC (1969).
- Multiple causes: Wilsher v Essex AHA (1988), claimant must prove which cause; material contribution
  (Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw, 1956).
- Intervening acts: a new act breaks the chain if it is unforeseeable or reckless (Knightley v Johns,
  1982; Lamb v Camden LBC, 1981).
- Remoteness: type of damage reasonably foreseeable (The Wagon Mound (No 1), 1961); precise manner need
  not be (Hughes v Lord Advocate, 1963); thin skull rule (Smith v Leech Brain, 1962).

**Novel duty situations (4.1.5)**
- Pure economic loss from negligent acts is generally not recoverable (Spartan Steel v Martin, 1973).
- Negligent misstatement: special relationship and reliance (Hedley Byrne v Heller, 1964); auditors owe
  no duty to investors at large (Caparo, 1990).
- Nervous shock: a recognised psychiatric illness is needed. Primary victims recover if physical injury
  was foreseeable (Page v Smith, 1996; Dulieu v White, 1901). Secondary victims must satisfy Alcock v
  Chief Constable of South Yorkshire (1992): close tie of love and affection, proximity in time and
  space including the immediate aftermath (McLoughlin v O'Brian, 1983), perception with their own
  unaided senses. Rescuers and employees are not automatically primary victims (White v Chief
  Constable of South Yorkshire, 1999).
- Possible reform: the Law Commission (Liability for Psychiatric Illness, Law Com No 249, 1998)
  recommended removing the proximity and own-senses requirements for those with a close tie.

## What are the torts affecting land? (4.2)

**Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 - lawful visitors**
- Occupier = control of premises (Wheat v E Lacon, 1966). Common duty of care, s2(2).
- Children, s2(3)(a): occupier must expect children to be less careful; allurements (Glasgow Corporation
  v Taylor, 1922); very young children are primarily their parents' responsibility (Phipps v
  Rochester Corporation, 1955).
- Trade or calling, s2(3)(b): specialists must guard against risks of their trade (Roles v Nathan, 1963).
- Independent contractors, s2(4)(b): occupier not liable if it acted reasonably in choosing and checking
  the contractor (Haseldine v Daw, 1941; contrast Woodward v Mayor of Hastings, 1945).
- Exclusion, s2(1), subject to UCTA 1977 and CRA 2015; warnings, s2(4)(a); volenti, s2(5).

**Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 - trespassers**
- Duty arises under s1(3): occupier aware of the danger, knows or has reasonable grounds to believe a
  trespasser is or may come into its vicinity, and the risk is one it may reasonably be expected to
  protect against. Covers personal injury only.
- Warnings, s1(5); volenti, s1(6) (Ratcliff v McConnell, 1999). No duty for obvious risks from the
  claimant's own choice (Tomlinson v Congleton BC, 2003). Whether the 1984 duty can be excluded is not
  settled by the Act.

**Private nuisance**
- Unlawful, indirect interference with use or enjoyment of land. Claimant needs a proprietary interest
  (Hunter v Canary Wharf, 1997). Defendant: creator, or occupier who adopts or continues it
  (Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan, 1940).
- Unreasonableness factors: locality (Sturges v Bridgman, 1879); duration (Crown River Cruises v
  Kimbolton Fireworks, 1996, short events can count); sensitivity (Robinson v Kilvert, 1889; Network
  Rail v Morris, 2004); malice (Christie v Davey, 1893; Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett, 1936).
- Defences: prescription after 20 years of actionable nuisance (failed in Sturges v Bridgman, because
  the noise was not a nuisance until the consulting room was built); statutory authority (Allen v
  Gulf Oil, 1981). Coming to the nuisance is not a defence (Coventry v Lawrence, 2014).

**Rylands v Fletcher (1868)** - strict liability for escape of a dangerous thing.
- Conditions: bringing on to land; likely to do mischief if it escapes; non-natural use (Rickards v
  Lothian, 1913); escape from land under the defendant's control (Read v J Lyons, 1947); foreseeable
  type of damage (Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather, 1994).
- Relationship: a sub-species of private nuisance, so the claimant needs an interest in land and
  personal injury is not covered (Transco v Stockport MBC, 2003).

**Trespass to land** - direct, intentional entry on land, remaining after permission ends, or placing
objects on it. Intention to enter is enough even if mistaken about ownership (Basely v Clarkson, 1681);
airspace (Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco, 1957, limited by Bernstein v Skyviews, 1978); continuing
trespass while an object stays on the land (Holmes v Wilson, 1839).

## What are the torts affecting the person? (4.3)

| Tort | Elements | Key cases |
|---|---|---|
| Assault | Act causing reasonable apprehension of immediate unlawful force | Read v Coker (1853); words can negate (Tuberville v Savage, 1669); no immediacy (Thomas v NUM, 1985); silence (R v Ireland, 1998) |
| Battery | Direct, intentional application of force | Cole v Turner (1704); everyday contact excluded (Collins v Wilcock, 1984); hostility (Wilson v Pringle, 1987) doubted in F v West Berkshire HA (1990) |
| False imprisonment | Total restraint without lawful authority | Bird v Jones (1845); knowledge not needed (Meering v Grahame-White, 1920; Murray v Ministry of Defence, 1988); reasonable conditions (Robinson v Balmain New Ferry, 1910); detention beyond lawful term (R v Governor of Brockhill Prison, ex p Evans (No 2), 2000) |

Defences to battery: consent (medical consent to the broad nature of the act, Chatterton v Gerson,
1981); self-defence using reasonable force (Lane v Holloway, 1968); necessity (F v West Berkshire HA,
1990; Leigh v Gladstone, 1909).

## Which general defences and remedies apply? (4.4)

**Defences**
- Volenti: full knowledge and free agreement (Smith v Baker, 1891). Employment: rarely succeeds, but did
  in ICI v Shatwell (1965). Medical treatment: consent to treatment. Sport: players accept ordinary risks
  but not reckless fouls (Condon v Basi, 1985); spectators (Wooldridge v Sumner, 1963).
- Contributory negligence: partial defence, Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 s1(1);
  apportionment (Froom v Butcher, 1976; Jones v Livox Quarries, 1952).
- Inevitable accident (Stanley v Powell, 1891); Act of God (Nichols v Marsland, 1876); statutory authority
  (Allen v Gulf Oil, 1981); illegality (Pitts v Hunt, 1991; Gray v Thames Trains, 2009); necessity (Cope v
  Sharpe, 1912; Rigby v Chief Constable of Northamptonshire, 1985).

**Remedies**
- Compensatory damages restore the claimant as far as money can (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal, 1880).
- Special vs general damages; non-compensatory damages: nominal, contemptuous, aggravated and exemplary
  (Rookes v Barnard, 1964).
- Personal injury: loss of earnings and care costs, pain, suffering and loss of amenity; future losses
  as a lump sum or periodical payments (Damages Act 1996, s2).
- Death: claims survive for the estate (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934); dependants and
  a bereavement award (Fatal Accidents Act 1976).
- Injunctions: prohibitory, mandatory, interlocutory (American Cyanamid v Ethicon, 1975). Damages in lieu:
  Shelfer v City of London Electric Lighting (1895), applied more flexibly in Coventry v Lawrence (2014);
  Miller v Jackson (1977); Kennaway v Thompson (1981).

## Worked example: secondary victim

Rana sees on live television that her son's school bus has crashed. She drives to the hospital two
hours later and sees him injured. She develops post-traumatic stress disorder.

1. Recognised psychiatric illness: yes (PTSD).
2. Secondary victim: she was not in physical danger.
3. Close tie: parent and child, presumed.
4. Proximity: the hospital visit two hours later can be the immediate aftermath (McLoughlin v O'Brian),
   though Alcock held that identifying a body some hours later was not.
5. Own senses: live television alone is not enough (Alcock), but seeing her son at hospital may be.
Conclusion: arguable, turning on the aftermath point.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Using Caparo for every scenario instead of relying on established duty categories.
- Saying a trespasser claim under the 1984 Act covers damaged property - it covers injury only.
- Forgetting that Rylands needs an interest in land and does not cover personal injury (Transco).
- Treating contributory negligence as a complete defence.
- Saying false imprisonment needs the victim to know they were detained.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which torts does 9084 Paper 4 cover?**
Negligence (including economic loss and nervous shock),
occupiers' liability, private nuisance, Rylands v Fletcher, trespass to land, assault, battery and
false imprisonment, plus defences and remedies.

**Is vicarious liability examined in depth?**
No. The 9084 syllabus lists vicarious liability "in outline
only" under 4.1.1, alongside personal and joint liability.

**What makes a good Paper 4 essay?**
A clear argument, accurate authority and evaluation of policy, such as
floodgates in nervous shock or the balance between neighbours in nuisance, ending with a reasoned
conclusion.
