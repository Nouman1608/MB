Quick recall for section 2.9 **Market economic system** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the definition, and the advantages and disadvantages "including examples of how it works in a variety of different countries". Full explanations are in the [market economic system study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-economic-system/); test yourself with the [market economic system practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-economic-system-practice/).

## Definition

A **market economic system** allocates resources mainly through the **price mechanism** (demand and supply in markets), with resources mostly **privately owned** and decisions made by households and firms rather than the government.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Price mechanism | The way changes in prices, set by demand and supply, allocate resources |
| Private sector | Households and firms that own resources and make decisions for themselves |
| Profit motive | Firms produce to earn profit, the reward for meeting demand at low cost |
| Consumer sovereignty | Consumers' spending decides what is produced |
| Competition | Rivalry between many sellers for customers |
| Signalling function | Prices show where resources are wanted |
| Incentive function | Profit and loss encourage firms to respond to prices |
| Rationing function | Prices decide who gets scarce goods: those willing and able to pay |
| Mixed economic system | One that combines markets with significant government decisions (section 2.11) |

## The three questions

| Question | Market answer |
|---|---|
| What? | What consumers will buy at a profitable price |
| How? | The lowest-cost method |
| For whom? | Those who can pay |

## Price mechanism in five steps

1. Demand for a product rises.
2. A shortage appears at the old price, so the price rises (signal).
3. Profit rises, so firms expand and new firms enter (incentive).
4. Land, labour and capital move in from products where demand has fallen (reallocation).
5. Until supply catches up, the higher price limits who buys (rationing).

## Advantages, with the reason

| Advantage | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| Wide choice | Firms profit by making what consumers want |
| Quick response to change | Prices adjust and resources follow without waiting for a plan |
| Efficiency | Competition punishes waste, as high-cost firms lose customers |
| Innovation | New products and methods can earn high profit |
| Incentives to work and take risks | People keep the rewards of effort and enterprise |
| No planning body needed | Prices co-ordinate millions of decisions automatically |

## Disadvantages, with the reason

| Disadvantage | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| Public goods missing | Non-payers cannot be excluded, so firms cannot charge |
| Merit goods under-consumed | People undervalue them or cannot afford them |
| Demerit goods and pollution over-produced | Costs to third parties are ignored |
| Inequality | Spending power decides who gets goods |
| Monopoly power | A dominant firm can raise prices and cut output |
| Unemployment and instability | Factors move slowly between industries; output can swing |

## Country examples (use as evidence, then link)

- **Hong Kong**: often described as highly market-oriented, with low taxes and open trade, yet the government provides public housing, schools and hospitals.
- **United States**: relies on private insurance for much healthcare; linked to medical innovation and to unequal access.
- **Poland and other Central and Eastern European economies**: moved from planning to markets after 1989; shortages ended but unemployment rose as state firms closed.
- **China**: market reforms from 1978 brought rapid growth while the state kept ownership of many large firms.

## Evaluation: "it depends on…"

- the product (private goods versus public and merit goods)
- the level of competition
- how unequal incomes are
- the time period (short-run adjustment costs versus long-run gains)

## Exam traps

- Saying there is no government in a market economy.
- Saying the government sets prices.
- Mixing up the market system with market failure.
- Listing points without the reason they happen.
- Dropping in a country name with no link to the argument.
- Concluding "it has advantages and disadvantages" without a judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a market economic system.
2. How does a market system decide how to produce?
3. What is meant by consumer sovereignty?
4. Name the three functions of prices.
5. Demand for solar panels rises. Explain how resources move into making them.
6. Why will a market system not provide street lighting?
7. Give one reason why a market system may lead to inequality.
8. Explain why competition can make firms efficient.
9. What role does the government still play in a market economy?
10. Give one example of a country that moved towards a market system and one result.
11. State two things an evaluation of the market system could depend on.

### Answers

1. A system in which resources are allocated mainly by the price mechanism, with private ownership and decisions made by households and firms.
2. Firms choose the lowest-cost method, because that gives the most profit.
3. The idea that consumers' spending decides what is produced.
4. Signalling, incentive and rationing.
5. A shortage pushes up the price; profit rises; existing firms expand and new firms enter, hiring labour and buying capital that moves from less profitable uses.
6. Non-payers cannot be excluded, so firms cannot charge for it and make a profit.
7. Goods go to those who can pay, so people with low incomes or little wealth go without.
8. Firms with high costs must charge more and lose customers to rivals, so each firm has a reason to cut waste.
9. It enforces property rights and contracts, and keeps law and order (it may also provide defence and some services).
10. Poland after 1989: shortages of consumer goods ended, but unemployment rose as state firms closed (or China from 1978: rapid growth).
11. Any two from: the type of product, the level of competition, the distribution of income, the time period.
