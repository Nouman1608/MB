These questions cover section 3.4.1 Organisational structures of AQA GCSE Business (8132). Revise first with the [Organisational structures study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-organisational-structures-aqa-gcse-business/) and the [Organisational structures revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-organisational-structures-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *span of control*. **[1]**

**2.** Define the term *delayering*. **[1]**

**3.** State **two** reasons why a business has an internal organisational structure. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit to a manager of delegating tasks to staff. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why communication may be less effective in a tall organisational structure. **[3]**

**6.** Hartley Hotels has a general manager who manages 4 department heads. Each department head manages 3 supervisors, and each supervisor manages 9 staff.

**(a)** State the number of levels in the hierarchy. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total number of staff managed by supervisors. **[2]**
**(c)** State the span of control of each department head. **[1]**

### Section B: longer and case-study questions

**7.** Analyse **one** benefit and **one** drawback for a business of having a flat organisational structure. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the difference between centralised and decentralised decision-making. **[4]**

**9.** Dunmore Insurance has removed a level of middle managers, saving £180,000 a year. Each remaining manager's span of control has risen from 6 to 12.

**(a)** Explain **one** benefit to Dunmore Insurance of the delayering. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** problem the wider spans of control might cause. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how the job role of a supervisor differs from that of a director. **[4]**

**11.** Pinecrest Pharmacies has 30 branches. All decisions on stock, staffing and promotions are made at head office. Analyse the possible benefits of decentralising some decisions to branch managers. **[6]**

**12.** Silverline Engineering is a manufacturer with 400 employees and a tall structure with seven levels. It makes safety-critical parts for aircraft, and many staff are newly qualified. A consultant recommends delayering to create a flatter structure. Evaluate whether Silverline Engineering should follow this recommendation. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of employees who report directly to one manager [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Removing one or more levels of management from an organisational structure [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: so that job roles and responsibilities are clear [1]; so staff know who they report to [1]; to avoid tasks being missed or duplicated; to show how authority and communication flow; to show routes to promotion. **[2]**

**4.** Delegating passes routine tasks and decisions to staff [1], which frees the manager's time [1] to concentrate on more important decisions, such as planning for the business's future [1]. (Alternatively: staff gain responsibility, which can motivate them.) **[3]**

**5.** In a tall structure the chain of command is long [1], so a message from senior managers passes through many levels before it reaches the workers who need it [1]. This causes delay, and the message may be changed or misunderstood as it is passed on, leading to mistakes [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** 4 levels: general manager, department heads, supervisors, staff [1].
**(b)** Supervisors: 4 × 3 = 12 [1]. Staff: 12 × 9 = 108 [1].
**(c)** 3 [1]. **[4]**

**7.** **Benefit:** a flat structure has few levels of management [1], so messages and decisions pass through fewer people, making communication faster [1], which lets the business respond quickly to customers and changes in its market [1]. **Drawback:** managers have wide spans of control [1], so each has less time to supervise and support individual staff [1], which may lead to mistakes, especially among less experienced employees, and to managers being overloaded [1]. **[6]**

**8.** In a centralised business, important decisions are made by senior managers, often at head office [1], which keeps decisions consistent across the business [1]. In a decentralised business, authority to make decisions is passed down to managers in branches, regions or departments [1], who can make quicker decisions based on local knowledge [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Dunmore saves £180,000 a year in salaries [1], which lowers its costs [1] and could be used to raise profit or invest elsewhere in the business; communication also passes through one fewer level [1].
**(b)** Each manager now has 12 staff to supervise instead of 6 [1], so they have half as much time for each person [1], which may mean less support and training, more mistakes, and stress for overloaded managers [1]. **[6]**

**10.** A director sets the overall aims and strategy of the business [1] and makes long-term decisions that affect the whole organisation [1]. A supervisor organises the day-to-day work of a team [1], checking that tasks are done and targets are met, and is usually much closer to the operational staff [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Branch managers know their local customers better than head office does [1], so they could stock products that sell well in their area, such as more holiday health products in a tourist town [1], increasing sales and reducing unsold stock [1]. Decisions such as covering a sick member of staff could be made quickly without waiting for head office [1], improving customer service. Having more responsibility could also motivate branch managers [1], making them more committed and more likely to stay with Pinecrest, which reduces recruitment costs [1]. **[6]**

**12.** **For delayering:** seven levels is a long chain of command for 400 employees [1], so decisions and messages may be slow and distorted as they pass through each level [1]. Removing a level would save salary costs [1] and give staff more responsibility, which could motivate them [1]. **Against:** Silverline makes safety-critical parts, so mistakes could be very costly and dangerous [1]. Many staff are newly qualified, so they need close supervision and support [1]. Delayering would widen spans of control, giving managers less time for each worker [1]. **Judgement:** Silverline should not delayer significantly at present [1]. The need for close supervision of inexperienced staff on safety-critical work outweighs the cost savings; it could reconsider once staff are more experienced, or remove a level only where work is less critical [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing span of control with the number of levels.
- Describing delayering as making ordinary workers redundant.
- Assuming flat structures are always better.
- Forgetting that delegation leaves the manager accountable.
- Mixing up delegation and decentralisation.
- Giving a judgement that ignores the case, such as the type of work or staff experience.
