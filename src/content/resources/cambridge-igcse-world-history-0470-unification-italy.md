This guide covers **key question 2 of Core Content Option A** in Cambridge IGCSE History 0470 (2024-2026): "How was
Italy unified?" The focus points ask why Italy was not unified in 1848-49, how important Garibaldi's contribution
was, whether Cavour helped or hindered unification, and how important moves towards Italian unity were for other
European countries. The specified content runs from Austrian influence and Mazzini to the completion of unification
by 1870. **"How was Italy unified?" is the Paper 2 prescribed topic for the November 2026 series**, so candidates
sitting then need it for both Paper 1 and the document paper.

Use this alongside the [Italy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-world-history-0470-unification-italy-revision-notes/)
and the [Italy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-world-history-0470-unification-italy-practice/). For how
Italy compares with Germany, see the [Nineteenth Century 1848-1914 guide](/resources/igcse-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/).

## Italy before 1848: Austrian influence

After 1815 the peninsula was divided into separate states. **Lombardy and Venetia** in the north were ruled directly
by Austria. Tuscany, Modena and Parma were ruled by relatives or allies of the Habsburgs. The **Papal States** across
the centre were ruled by the Pope, and the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies** (Naples and Sicily) by a Bourbon king. Only
**Piedmont-Sardinia**, ruled by the House of Savoy, was independent of Austria. Austrian troops crushed risings in
1821 and 1831, and Metternich dismissed Italy as merely "a geographical expression".

Unity faced deeper obstacles too: different dialects, poor roads, separate currencies and customs barriers, and a
peasant majority with little interest in national politics.

## Mazzini and Italian nationalism

**Giuseppe Mazzini** founded **Young Italy** in 1831. He wanted a united, democratic **republic**, created by the
Italian people rising together rather than by kings or foreign armies. His risings in the 1830s and 1840s all failed,
and he spent most of his life in exile. His importance was as an inspiration: he made unity a cause young Italians
were prepared to die for, and he recruited Garibaldi. Other nationalists had different plans: a federation of states
under the Pope, or an enlarged northern kingdom under Piedmont.

## Why was Italy not unified in 1848-49?

In 1848 Milan drove out the Austrians in the Five Days, Venice declared a republic, and Charles Albert of Piedmont
declared war on Austria with the slogan that Italy would "do it by herself". It failed because:

- **Austria's army recovered.** Radetzky defeated Piedmont at Custoza (1848) and Novara (1849).
- **Leaders and states were divided.** Charles Albert wanted to enlarge Piedmont and distrusted the republicans;
  Mazzini wanted a republic.
- **Pius IX refused** to make war on Catholic Austria (April 1848), ending hopes of a papal lead.
- **Naples withdrew** its troops from the war.
- **France intervened** against the Roman Republic, restoring the Pope in 1849.

The lesson drawn by Piedmont's leaders was that Italy could not "do it by herself": it needed a foreign ally strong
enough to defeat Austria. Piedmont's survival with its **Statuto** constitution and its new king, **Victor Emmanuel
II**, made it the obvious leader.

## Cavour and the war of 1859

**Count Camillo di Cavour** became prime minister of Piedmont in 1852. He strengthened the state through railways,
trade agreements and banking, and reduced the power of the Church. In 1855 he sent troops to the **Crimean War** on
the side of Britain and France, which earned Piedmont a place at the **Congress of Paris (1856)** where the Italian
question was raised.

At **Plombières (July 1858)** Cavour met Napoleon III secretly. France would fight Austria if Austria could be made
to appear the aggressor. Piedmont would gain Lombardy and Venetia to form a kingdom of northern Italy, and France
would receive **Nice and Savoy**. In 1859 Cavour provoked Austria by mobilising; Austria sent an ultimatum demanding
disarmament and then declared war. French and Piedmontese armies won at **Magenta** and **Solferino** (June 1859).

Then Napoleon III made the **armistice of Villafranca** with Austria (July 1859) without consulting Cavour. Piedmont
gained Lombardy but not Venetia. Napoleon feared heavy losses, Prussian mobilisation on the Rhine and French Catholic
anger at any threat to the Pope. Cavour resigned in fury, returning to office in January 1860.

Meanwhile the rulers of **Tuscany, Modena, Parma and the Romagna** had fled. In March 1860 plebiscites there voted to
join Piedmont, and Cavour ceded Nice and Savoy to France in return for Napoleon's agreement.

## Garibaldi and the South

**Giuseppe Garibaldi**, a former follower of Mazzini born in Nice, set out from near Genoa in **May 1860** with about a
thousand volunteers, the **Redshirts**, to support a rising in Sicily. He landed at **Marsala**, won at
**Calatafimi** and took **Palermo**. Peasants joined him, hoping for land. In August he crossed to the mainland and
entered **Naples in September 1860**.

Cavour had not stopped the expedition but did not openly back it. He feared Garibaldi would march on Rome, which was
protected by a French garrison, and provoke war with France, or set up a separate republic in the south. He sent the
Piedmontese army south through the Papal States (avoiding Rome) to reach Naples first. At **Teano (October 1860)**
Garibaldi met Victor Emmanuel and handed over his conquests. Plebiscites in Naples and Sicily approved union. The
**Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed in **March 1861**, with Victor Emmanuel II as king. Cavour died in June 1861.

## Completing unification by 1870

- **Venetia (1866):** Italy allied with Prussia against Austria. Italy was defeated at Custoza and at sea at Lissa,
  but Prussia's victory forced Austria to give up Venetia, handed over through France.
- **Rome (1870):** Garibaldi's attempts to seize Rome failed at Aspromonte (1862) and Mentana (1867). When the
  Franco-Prussian War forced France to withdraw its garrison, Italian troops entered **Rome in September 1870**, and
  it became the capital. The Pope refused to accept the loss of his territory.

## Did Cavour help or hinder?

| Helped | Hindered |
|---|---|
| Won the French alliance that defeated Austria in 1859 | His original aim was an enlarged northern kingdom, not all of Italy |
| Modernised Piedmont's economy and army | Gave away Nice and Savoy |
| Turned the central Italian revolts into annexation by plebiscite | Distrusted Garibaldi and Mazzini and tried to control the expedition |
| Took control of the south in 1860, preventing war with France | Imposed Piedmontese laws, which caused resentment in the south |

## How important was Italy for other European countries?

- **Austria** lost Lombardy (1859) and Venetia (1866) and was pushed out of Italy, which weakened it as Prussia
  challenged it in Germany.
- **France** gained Nice and Savoy, but Napoleon III angered both Italian nationalists and French Catholics.
- **Prussia** gained an ally in 1866, and the Italian example showed how a strong state could unify a nation by war.
- **The Papacy** lost its lands; the "Roman Question" troubled Italian politics for decades.
- **Britain** welcomed Italian unity and refused to support moves against it, as a liberal cause that weakened
  France and Austria.

## Annotated model answer

An original practice question: *"Garibaldi's contribution was more important than Cavour's in unifying Italy." How
far do you agree? [10]*

> Garibaldi's contribution was vital because he conquered the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies with only about a
> thousand volunteers in 1860, doubling the size of the new state. Without him, Cavour would probably have settled
> for a northern kingdom. *[One side explained.]* However, Cavour made Garibaldi's success possible. The war of 1859,
> won with the French alliance agreed at Plombières, removed Austria from Lombardy, and in 1860 Cavour's army took
> the Papal States and Naples, preventing a war with France that could have destroyed everything. *[Other side.]*
> Garibaldi supplied the south, but Cavour supplied the alliance and the state that held it together; by handing
> over his conquests at Teano, Garibaldi accepted that Piedmont, not he, would build Italy. *[Weighted judgement.]*

## Common mistakes

- Saying Italy was unified in 1861. Venetia (1866) and Rome (1870) came later.
- Treating Cavour as a nationalist dreamer. He was a practical politician who widened his aims as events allowed.
- Forgetting the price of French help: Nice and Savoy.
- Describing Garibaldi's march without explaining why Cavour sent troops south.

## Exam technique

For "help or hinder" and "how important" focus points, build a two-column plan before writing, then decide your
judgement. For Paper 2 in November 2026, link each source to its date: a British cartoon of 1860, for example, needs
the context of the Expedition of the Thousand.
