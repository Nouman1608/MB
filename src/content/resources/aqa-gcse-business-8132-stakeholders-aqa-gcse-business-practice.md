These questions cover section 3.1.4, Stakeholders, of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017. All businesses and figures are invented. Short questions come first, then case-based questions. Learn the content in the [stakeholders study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-stakeholders-aqa-gcse-business/) and the [stakeholders revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-stakeholders-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term *stakeholder*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** external stakeholders of a business. **[2]**

**3.** State the main objective the specification gives for each stakeholder.

**(a)** Workers **[1]**
**(b)** Owners **[1]**
**(c)** The local community **[1]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which customers can influence a business. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how local suppliers might be affected if a business switches to a cheaper overseas supplier. **[3]**

**6.** Ridley Bakery employs 9 staff. They ask for a pay rise from £11.50 to £12.10 an hour. Each works 30 hours a week for 50 weeks a year. Last year the bakery made a profit of £40,500.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost of the pay rise per year. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate this extra cost as a percentage of last year's profit. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the conflict between stakeholders that the pay rise creates. **[2]**

**7.** Ridley Bakery sells 3,000 cakes a week at £2.40. The variable cost of each cake is £0.90. It plans to raise the price to £2.70 and expects sales to fall to 2,700 cakes a week.

**(a)** Calculate revenue minus variable costs before and after the price rise, and the change. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify **one** stakeholder who gains and **one** who loses from the price rise, with a reason for each. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** reason why the influence of a local community on a large business may be limited. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how stakeholders can lead a business to change its objectives. **[3]**

**10.** A supermarket plans to open its town-centre store 24 hours a day. Analyse the impact of this decision on **two** stakeholder groups. **[6]**

**11.** Coldwater Quarry Ltd wants to extend its quarry by 40 hectares. The extension would create 25 jobs and keep the quarry open for 20 more years. Residents in a nearby village have started a campaign against it because of dust, noise and up to 60 extra lorry journeys a day. Local building firms rely on Coldwater for stone. The owners expect profit to rise by £1.2 million a year.

Evaluate whether Coldwater Quarry should go ahead with the extension, considering its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An individual or group with an interest in a business [1], because they are affected by its activities or can affect its decisions [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Someone who owns shares" defines a shareholder and earns nothing here.

**2.** Any two: customers; suppliers; the local community [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Owners and employees are internal; government and lenders are also acceptable external stakeholders, but the specification's main list is safest.

**3. (a)** Maximising pay [1]
**(b)** High dividend payments [1]
**(c)** Minimising environmental impact [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Job security or good service are reasonable objectives, but the question asks for the specification's examples.

**4.** Customers decide whether to buy [1]. If they are unhappy with price or quality they can switch to a rival or post poor reviews [1], which reduces the business's sales and may force it to lower prices or improve its products [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the effect on the business, not just the customers' action.

**5.** Local suppliers lose regular orders from the business [1], so their revenue falls [1], which could lead them to cut staff or, for a small supplier that depended on the contract, to close [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Build a chain: lost orders, lower revenue, a further consequence.

**6. (a)** Rise per hour = £12.10 − £11.50 = £0.60 [1]; per worker = £0.60 × 30 × 50 = £900 [1]; for 9 staff = **£8,100** [1]
**(b)** £8,100 ÷ £40,500 × 100 = **20%** [1]
**(c)** Employees gain higher pay, meeting their objective of maximising pay [1], but the owners lose a fifth of last year's profit, reducing money available for dividends or investment [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 20% from (b) in (c); it shows the scale of the conflict.

**7. (a)** Before: 3,000 × (£2.40 − £0.90) = **£4,500** [1]; after: 2,700 × (£2.70 − £0.90) = **£4,860** [1]; change = **+£360** a week [1]
**(b)** Gains: owners, because profit rises by £360 a week as fixed costs are unchanged [1]. Loses: customers, because they pay 30p more per cake, and 300 a week stop buying [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing revenue alone (£7,200 against £7,290) understates the gain, because fewer cakes also means lower variable costs.

**8.** Residents are many separate individuals with little economic power over a large firm [1], and they may not be its customers, so the business may lose few sales by ignoring them unless they organise a campaign or a planning objection [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason must explain why influence is limited, not just state that it is.

**9.** Stakeholders press a business to meet their own objectives [1]. For example, customers and local communities increasingly expect less waste and pollution [1], so a business may add social and environmental objectives, such as cutting packaging, to keep customers and avoid opposition [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Shareholders pushing a plc towards shareholder value is an equally good example.

**10.** Indicative chains for two groups, up to 6.
- **Employees** [1]: more shifts and possible overtime pay raise some workers' incomes, but night work brings unsocial hours, tiredness and safety concerns, so some may leave or ask for higher pay [1] [1].
- **Customers** [1]: shift workers and others can shop at any time, increasing convenience and possibly sales, though prices may not change [1] [1].
- **Local community** [1]: late-night deliveries and visitors bring noise and traffic, but the store may reduce crime by being staffed and lit [1] [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Choose two groups and develop each into a chain with a consequence; three thin points score less than two developed ones.

**11.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9.
- Owners gain a £1.2 million a year rise in profit, supporting dividends and investment [1].
- Employees and the area gain 25 jobs, and the quarry stays open for 20 more years, protecting existing jobs [1].
- Local building firms, as customers, keep a nearby source of stone, avoiding higher transport costs [1].
- Residents face dust, noise and up to 60 extra lorry journeys a day, harming their quality of life [1].
- The residents' campaign may lead to planning objections, delaying the project or causing it to be refused [1].
- Bad publicity could damage the quarry's reputation [1].
- Coldwater could reduce the conflict, for example by limiting lorry hours, spraying to cut dust or funding noise barriers, using part of the £1.2 million [1].
- Consulting residents early may reduce opposition [1].
- Judgement: Coldwater should go ahead, because the benefits to owners, employees and customers are large and long-term, but only if it spends money reducing dust, noise and traffic for residents, since their objections could otherwise block the plan [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong evaluation weighs stakeholders against each other using the case figures and makes a conditional recommendation that deals with the losers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing stakeholders with shareholders.
- Listing stakeholder groups with no impact or no reason.
- Forgetting to multiply by hours, weeks and staff in pay calculations.
- Comparing revenue only when variable costs also change.
- Analysis with many groups but no developed chains.
- Evaluations that never say how to reduce harm to the losing group.
