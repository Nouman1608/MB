## The two systems at a glance

| | Linnaean system | Three-domain system |
|---|---|---|
| Developed by | Carl Linnaeus | Carl Woese |
| Based on | Structure and characteristics (what can be seen) | Evidence from chemical analysis |
| Top level | Kingdom | Domain (above kingdom) |
| Groups | Kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species | Archaea, Bacteria, Eukaryota |

## The seven Linnaean levels

```
kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species
King     Philip   Came    Over    For      Good    Soup
```

- Each group sits inside the one above it.
- Going down, groups get **smaller** and members **share more features**.
- Two organisms that share a **lower** level are **more closely related**.

## Binomial names

- Two parts: **genus** then **species** (for example *Homo sapiens*).
- Genus has a capital letter; species is all lower case.
- Printed in italics; underlined when handwritten.
- One scientific name means one species everywhere, unlike common names.
- The same genus name shows a close relationship (*Panthera leo* and *Panthera pardus*).

## Worked reminder: comparing two classifications

Humans are *Homo sapiens*; chimpanzees are *Pan troglodytes*. Both are in kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata,
class Mammalia, order Primates and family Hominidae, but they are in different genera.

```
shared levels: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family  (5 of 7)
first difference: genus (Homo vs Pan)
conclusion: closely related, but separate genera and separate species
```

Method: count down from kingdom until the names differ. The last shared level is the one to quote.

## Why classification changed

| Development | What it revealed | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Better microscopes | Internal structures, such as whether cells have a nucleus and which organelles | New groupings based on cell structure |
| Understanding of biochemical processes | Similarities and differences in the chemicals inside cells | New models, leading to the three-domain system |

Classification is a **model**; it is revised when new evidence appears.

Answer frame for "describe the impact of developments": name the development → say what it revealed → say how
the groups changed. For example: chemical analysis revealed that archaea differ greatly from bacteria, so they
were moved into a separate domain.

## The three domains

| Domain | Contains |
|---|---|
| Archaea | Primitive bacteria, usually in extreme environments (very hot, salty or high pressure) |
| Bacteria | True bacteria |
| Eukaryota | Protists, fungi, plants and animals |

Archaea and bacteria look alike under a microscope (no nucleus). Chemical analysis showed they are very
different, so they are separate domains.

## Evolutionary trees

- Show how scientists **believe** organisms are related: a hypothesis, not a certainty.
- Use **current classification data** for living organisms and **fossil data** for extinct ones.
- Each **branch point** is a **common ancestor**.
- The more recent the shared branch point, the more closely related the organisms.
- Tips of the tree are separate species; one tip did not evolve from another tip.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Classification | Sorting living things into groups |
| Linnaean system | Classification by structure and characteristics into seven levels |
| Binomial system | Naming each species by its genus and species |
| Genus | The group of closely related species above species level |
| Species | The lowest level; organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring |
| Domain | The highest level in Woese's system |
| Archaea | Domain of primitive bacteria, usually in extreme environments |
| Eukaryota | Domain of organisms whose cells have a nucleus |
| Evolutionary tree | A diagram showing how organisms are believed to be related |
| Common ancestor | An organism from which two or more later groups evolved |

## Exam traps

- **Order before family**, **phylum before class**. Say the memory sentence before writing the list.
- A question that says "use the table" wants a **named shared level** quoted from the table.
- The genus alone is not a species name.
- Woese used **chemical analysis**; microscopes were an earlier development.
- Eukaryota includes **protists and fungi**, not just plants and animals.
- On a tree, distance across the page means nothing; trace back to the branch point.

## Self-test

1. Who developed the traditional system of classification, and what was it based on?
2. List the seven levels from largest to smallest.
3. What two parts make up a binomial name?
4. How should a binomial name be written by hand?
5. Give one advantage of binomial names over common names.
6. *Canis lupus* and *Canis latrans* share which level, and what does this tell you?
7. Name two developments in biology that changed classification systems.
8. Who developed the three-domain system, and on what evidence?
9. Name the three domains.
10. Which domain contains fungi?
11. Why are archaea placed in a different domain from bacteria?
12. What two kinds of data are used to build evolutionary trees?

### Answers

1. Carl Linnaeus; the structure and characteristics of organisms.
2. Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
3. Genus and species.
4. Underlined, with a capital letter for the genus and lower case for the species.
5. The scientific name refers to one species everywhere, whereas common names differ between languages or
   regions and can be confused.
6. The genus (*Canis*); they are closely related species.
7. Improvements in microscopes revealing internal structures; better understanding of biochemical processes.
8. Carl Woese; evidence from chemical analysis.
9. Archaea, bacteria, eukaryota.
10. Eukaryota.
11. Although they look similar, chemical analysis shows they are very different from true bacteria.
12. Current classification data for living organisms and fossil data for extinct organisms.
