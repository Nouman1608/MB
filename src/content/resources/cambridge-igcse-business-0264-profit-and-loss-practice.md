## Questions

### Section A: short answer

**1.** Define the term **profit**. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two reasons why profit is important to a private sector business. **[2]**

**3.** A bakery sells 1,250 wedding cakes a year at $36 each. Calculate its revenue. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the difference between gross profit and profit. **[2]**

**5.** A business has revenue of $84,000, cost of sales of $50,400 and expenses of $26,000. Calculate its gross profit and its profit. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why profit helps a business attract investors. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** The table shows part of the statements of profit or loss for Elmhirst Sports. In Year 2 it sold 12,000 items at an average price of $28.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 300,000 | ? |
| Cost of sales | 180,000 | 210,000 |
| Gross profit | ? | ? |
| Expenses | 96,000 | 112,000 |
| Profit | ? | ? |

**(a)** Calculate the five missing figures. **[5]**

**(b)** Explain why Elmhirst Sports' profit fell even though its revenue rose. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain one way Elmhirst Sports could increase its profit. **[2]**

**8.** Corran Books had revenue of $150,000, expenses of $45,000 and profit of $15,000. Calculate its gross profit and its cost of sales. **[2]**

**9.** Sefton Pizza sells 20,000 pizzas a year at $9 each. Cost of sales is $3.50 per pizza and expenses are $85,000 a year. The owner is thinking of raising the price to $10, and expects sales to fall to 18,500 pizzas.

**(a)** Calculate Sefton Pizza's current profit. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate its expected profit if it raises the price. **[2]**

**(c)** Consider whether Sefton Pizza should raise its price. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why profit is important to a sole trader. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a business that makes a profit could still be short of cash. **[3]**

**12.** Explain why a manager should not make a decision using only one year's statement of profit or loss. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The amount by which revenue is greater than total costs [1] over a period of time [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: reward for risk-taking; source of finance; measure of success; attract investors. One mark for each of two different reasons [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 1,250 x 36 [1] = **$45,000** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales only [1], whereas profit is what remains after expenses such as wages and rent have also been subtracted [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Gross profit = 84,000 - 50,400 = **$33,600** [1]. Profit = 33,600 - 26,000 = **$7,600** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Investors want a return on the money they put in [1], and a profitable business is more likely to pay them a share of profit or increase the value of their investment, so they are more willing to invest [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Year 1 gross profit = 300,000 - 180,000 = **120,000** [1]. Year 1 profit = 120,000 - 96,000 = **24,000** [1]. Year 2 revenue = 12,000 x 28 = **336,000** [1]. Year 2 gross profit = 336,000 - 210,000 = **126,000** [1]. Year 2 profit = 126,000 - 112,000 = **14,000** [1]. **[5]**

**(b)** Revenue rose by $36,000, but cost of sales rose by $30,000, so gross profit rose by only $6,000 [1]. Expenses rose by $16,000 [1], which is more than the rise in gross profit, so profit fell from $24,000 to $14,000 [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Reduce expenses, for example by cutting advertising that does not bring in extra sales [1], since expenses rose by $16,000 and a cut would raise profit directly [1]. Also accept: find a cheaper supplier to reduce cost of sales, or raise prices, with explanation. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* In part (b), quoting the changes in each line, not just "costs went up", is what turns a description into an explanation.

**8.** Gross profit = profit + expenses = 15,000 + 45,000 = **$60,000** [1]. Cost of sales = revenue - gross profit = 150,000 - 60,000 = **$90,000** [1]. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Revenue = 20,000 x 9 = $180,000; cost of sales = 20,000 x 3.50 = $70,000; gross profit = $110,000 [1]. Profit = 110,000 - 85,000 = **$25,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Revenue = 18,500 x 10 = $185,000; cost of sales = 18,500 x 3.50 = $64,750; gross profit = $120,250 [1]. Profit = 120,250 - 85,000 = **$35,250** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** For the rise: expected profit increases by $10,250, from $25,000 to $35,250 [1], because each pizza earns $1 more and fewer pizzas need to be made, so cost of sales falls [1]. Against the rise: the forecast of 18,500 sales is only an estimate; if customers switch to cheaper rivals, sales could fall further and profit could end up lower [1], and 1,500 lost customers may also stop buying drinks or sides, and tell others the price has gone up [1]. Judgement: Sefton should raise the price [1], because profit rises even with a fall in sales, but it should check rivals' prices first and watch sales closely in the first months so it can respond if they fall more than expected [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Profit is the sole trader's reward for risking their own money in the business [1]; without it, they might be better off working for someone else or saving the money [1]. Profit kept in the business is also a source of finance [1], so the sole trader can pay for new equipment without taking a loan and paying interest [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Profit is calculated when sales are made, even if customers have not yet paid [1]. The business may also spend cash on items such as new equipment that do not appear in full as expenses that year [1], so cash leaving the business can be greater than cash coming in, even though revenue is greater than costs [1]. **[3]**

**12.** One year's figures do not show whether profit is rising or falling over time [1]. The year may have been unusual, for example because of a one-off cost or a temporary fall in sales [1], so a decision such as closing a product line could be based on a result that does not last [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking expenses away from revenue to find gross profit.
- Giving revenue as the answer when profit is asked for.
- Forgetting to multiply cost of sales per unit by the new number of units after a price change.
- Saying "costs rose" without stating which line rose and by how much.
- Treating profit and cash as the same thing.
- Ending a "consider" answer without a decision.
