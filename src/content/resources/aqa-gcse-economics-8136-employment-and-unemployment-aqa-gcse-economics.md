Section 3.2.2.3 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification lists three content points: "employment and unemployment and how they are measured", "types, causes and consequences of unemployment" and "government policies to reduce unemployment". The additional guidance names the main types (structural, seasonal, frictional and cyclical) and asks you to explain "the consequences of unemployment for different groups within the economy". ## Who counts as employed, unemployed or inactive?

Economists split the working-age population into three groups.

- **Employed:** people doing paid work, whether full-time or part-time. The UK Labour Force Survey, which uses the International Labour Organization (ILO) definition, counts anyone who did at least one hour of paid work in the survey week as employed.
- **Unemployed:** people without a job who are available to start work and are actively looking for it. The ILO definition requires that the person could start within two weeks and has looked for work in the past four weeks.
- **Economically inactive:** people without a job who are not looking for one or cannot start, such as full-time students, people who have retired early, carers and people with long-term illness.

The **labour force** (or workforce) is the employed plus the unemployed. Inactive people are not part of it. This matters for calculations: the unemployment rate is measured out of the labour force, not out of the whole population.

### Worked example: three rates from one table (invented)

Country P has a working-age population of 40.0 million.

| Group | Millions |
|---|---|
| Employed | 30.4 |
| Unemployed | 1.6 |
| Economically inactive | 8.0 |

- Labour force = 30.4 + 1.6 = **32.0 million**.
- Unemployment rate = 1.6 ÷ 32.0 × 100 = **5.0%**.
- Employment rate = 30.4 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = **76.0%** of the working-age population.
- Inactivity rate = 8.0 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = **20.0%**.

Now suppose 0.4 million inactive students finish their courses and start looking for work, but none finds a job yet. Unemployed = 2.0 million, labour force = 32.4 million, so the unemployment rate rises to 2.0 ÷ 32.4 × 100 = **6.2%** (1 d.p.). Nobody lost a job and employment did not change. A rise in unemployment can come from people joining the labour force as well as from job losses.

## How is unemployment measured in the UK?

| Measure | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labour Force Survey (ILO measure) | The Office for National Statistics asks a large sample of households about each member's work and job search | Uses an international definition, so countries can be compared; counts people who do not claim benefits | Based on a sample, so it has a margin of error; slower and more costly to collect |
| Claimant count | Counts people claiming benefits because they are unemployed (Jobseeker's Allowance and some Universal Credit claimants) | Taken from benefit records, so it is cheap and quick | Leaves out people who are not eligible or choose not to claim; changes to benefit rules change the count even if unemployment has not changed |

The two measures count different people, so they usually give different totals; say which one a figure comes from before you judge it.

## The four types of unemployment and their causes

**Structural unemployment** happens when the pattern of demand or production in the economy changes for the long term, so that the skills or locations of workers no longer match the jobs available. Causes include new technology replacing workers, competition from cheaper imports, and a fall in demand for a product. Structural unemployment lasts because workers are often **occupationally immobile** (they lack the skills for other jobs) or **geographically immobile** (they cannot easily move to areas with jobs, perhaps because housing costs more there or family ties keep them).

**Seasonal unemployment** happens when demand for labour in an industry rises and falls at the same time each year. Agriculture, tourism in seaside towns, and retail around Christmas are typical examples. It is predictable and usually short-term.

**Frictional unemployment** happens when people are between jobs: they have left one job, or have just left education, and are searching for the right next one. It exists because finding and matching takes time and information is imperfect. Some frictional unemployment is healthy, because it lets workers find jobs that suit their skills.

**Cyclical unemployment** (sometimes called demand-deficient unemployment) happens when total spending in the economy falls during a downturn or recession. Firms sell less, so they need fewer workers, and job losses spread across many industries at once. The chain is: lower spending, lower sales, lower output, fewer workers needed.

A useful test in the exam: ask whether the fall in demand for labour is in **one industry for good** (structural), **at the same time every year** (seasonal), **across the whole economy because of a downturn** (cyclical), or whether the worker is **simply between jobs** (frictional).

## Consequences of unemployment for different groups

| Group | Possible consequences |
|---|---|
| The unemployed | Lower income, debt, loss of skills and confidence the longer they are out of work, poorer mental and physical health |
| Their families | Less to spend on essentials; children in workless households may have fewer opportunities |
| Young people | Missing early work experience can lower their earnings for years afterwards |
| Workers still in jobs | Fear of losing their job; weaker bargaining power, so pay rises may be smaller |
| Firms | Lower sales as spending falls; but recruitment is easier and wage pressure is lower |
| The government | Less income tax and VAT received while spending on benefits rises, so borrowing may increase |
| Regions | Areas where a major employer closes can see falling spending in local shops, empty premises and people moving away |
| The economy as a whole | Output is lost for good, because idle labour produces nothing; the economy works inside its production possibility boundary |

Notice that the effects on firms can be mixed. A strong answer says which firms: a discount supermarket may gain customers in a downturn, while a car dealer loses sales.

## Government policies to reduce unemployment

The key skill is to match the policy to the cause.

| Type | Suitable policies | Why they help |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Expansionary fiscal policy (more government spending, lower taxes); lower interest rates | They raise total spending, so firms need more workers |
| Structural | Education, training and apprenticeships; help with moving to areas with jobs; grants for new firms in hard-hit regions | They tackle the mismatch of skills or location |
| Frictional | Better information about vacancies through job centres and online services; help with job search | Workers find suitable jobs faster |
| Seasonal | Encouraging year-round attractions or work; training so workers can switch to winter jobs | Hard to remove completely, because the cause is the season itself |

Every policy has a cost or a trade-off. Raising spending to cut cyclical unemployment may increase government borrowing or cause demand-pull inflation if the economy is close to full capacity. Training takes years to work and costs money, and it only helps if the courses match the skills employers want.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* A large car factory in an invented town closes because it cannot compete with cheaper imported cars. Explain **one** consequence of the closure for the local economy.

*Model answer:* The closure causes structural unemployment, because the workers' skills in car assembly are not needed by other local employers [identifies the type, linked to the case]. With lower incomes, these workers spend less in local shops, cafés and services [develops the chain], so those businesses lose revenue and may cut staff, spreading unemployment further in the town [explains the knock-on consequence for the local economy].

The answer works because every sentence follows from the one before, and it stays with the local economy the question asks about.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the number unemployed by the whole population, or by the working-age population, instead of by the labour force.
- Counting students or retired people as unemployed: they are economically inactive.
- Calling a factory closure in a recession "structural" when the cause is falling demand across the economy (cyclical), or the reverse.
- Recommending lower interest rates for structural unemployment. More spending does not give a miner the skills to become a software developer.
- Saying unemployment is "bad for firms" without saying which firms, or noting that some firms find it easier to recruit.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **Define** questions want a precise, single-sentence answer: for example, the ILO conditions of being without work, available and actively seeking work.
- **Calculate** questions: show the labour force step before the rate, and give units (per cent, millions).
- **Explain** questions: name the type or consequence, then develop it with "because" and "so" links that use the context.
- **Evaluate** questions on policy: compare at least two policies, say which type of unemployment each tackles, weigh time lags and costs, and reach a judgement that depends on the cause in the case.
