This guide goes deeper into **1.2.1 Practical skills** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Section 1.2 sets out the skills you develop through practical work for the Practical Endorsement, and the specification says the same practice also prepares you for the written examinations. Statement 1.2.1 lists ten skills, (a) to (j), under four headings.

The [Practical Endorsement guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-practical-skills-practical-endorsement/) explains how the endorsement is run and assessed, and the [1.2.2 apparatus and techniques guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-2-2/) covers the techniques. This page covers the 1.2.1 skills themselves: how each one maps to the Common Practical Assessment Criteria (CPAC), how to write a risk assessment, what a good lab record contains, using software to process data, and researching and citing sources. Condense it with the [1.2.1 revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-2-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.2.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-2-1-practice/).

The additional guidance adds that (a) includes "how to solve problems in a practical context", that (b) includes "identification of potential hazards" and understanding "how to minimise the risks involved", and that the Practical Skills Handbook gives guidance on methods for citing information.

## How the skills map to CPAC

The specification's Table 2 lists five competencies that you must meet "consistently and routinely" by the end of the course. Each 1.2.1 skill feeds one or more of them.

| CPAC competency | 1.2.1 skills it draws on |
|---|---|
| 1 Follows written procedures | (c) |
| 2 Applies investigative approaches and methods when using instruments and equipment | (a), (j) |
| 3 Safely uses a range of practical equipment and materials | (b) |
| 4 Makes and records observations | (d), (e) |
| 5 Researches, references and reports | (f), (g), (h), (i) |

The specification also says skills (b), (c), (d), (e), (f) and (j) are expected to be developed across all activities, whichever activities your centre chooses.

## 1.2.1(a): investigative approaches

An investigative approach means working out a method for yourself rather than following one. CPAC 2 includes identifying and controlling significant variables, planning for variables that cannot easily be controlled, and choosing equipment and measurement strategies to give suitably accurate results.

**Worked example 1: starting an investigation.** PAG12 (Research skills) gives the example question "How long does it take iron tablets to break down in the stomach?" A sound approach:

1. **Research** what stomach conditions are like (acidity and temperature) and how iron(II) in solution can be measured, using cited sources.
2. **Model the conditions**: a dilute acid solution held at body temperature in a water bath, stirred at a fixed rate.
3. **Choose a measurement**: remove samples at intervals and find [Fe²⁺], for example by titration with acidified potassium manganate(VII), or watch the time for the tablet to disintegrate.
4. **Control variables**: acid concentration and volume, temperature, stirring, same brand of tablet.
5. **Trial and refine**: a short trial shows whether sample times and quantities give readings of a useful size.

The point is not a single correct method but a reasoned one, with each choice justified.

## 1.2.1(b): working safely

A **hazard** is something with the potential to cause harm. A **risk** is the chance of harm happening from the way the work is done. A risk assessment links each hazard to a specific control.

**Worked example 2: a risk assessment for a haloalkane preparation (PAG5).** 2-chloro-2-methylpropane is made by shaking 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel.

| Substance or step | Hazard | Risk | Control measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentrated hydrochloric acid | Corrosive; gives off an irritant vapour | Burns to skin or eyes; breathing in fumes | Eye protection and gloves; use in a fume cupboard |
| 2-methylpropan-2-ol and the product | Flammable liquids | Fire if near a flame | No naked flames nearby |
| Shaking in a separating funnel | Pressure builds up | Stopper forced out, spraying acid | Invert and open the tap often to release pressure, pointing away from people |
| Washing with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution | Carbon dioxide produced | Pressure build-up | Release pressure frequently |

A good control is specific: "use a fume cupboard because the acid gives off an irritant vapour" earns credit; "be careful" does not.

## 1.2.1(c) to (f): instructions, observations, records and presentation

**Following written instructions (c).** Read the whole method before starting, check the quantities and apparatus, and carry out the steps in order. If you have to change something, record what and why.

**Making and recording observations and measurements (d).** CPAC 4 asks for "accurate observations relevant to the experimental or investigative procedure" and "accurate, precise and sufficient data", recorded "methodically using appropriate units and conventions". Record readings straight away to the resolution of the instrument, and describe changes precisely: "pale green solution turned yellow-brown", not "changed colour".

**Keeping records (e).** The CPAC says evidence includes your "contemporaneous record" of each activity, meaning one written at the time. A useful record has:

- the date and the title or aim of the activity;
- the method followed, or a reference to it, with any changes noted;
- raw data in tables with quantity / unit headings;
- observations as they happened;
- processing, a conclusion and a brief evaluation;
- the sources of any information used.

**Presenting information scientifically (f).** Use tables and graphs that follow the usual conventions, SI units, correct chemical names and formulae, and a logical order: aim, method, results, analysis, conclusion, evaluation.

## 1.2.1(g): software and tools

Software turns raw data into processed values and graphs quickly, and data loggers record readings that would be hard to take by hand.

**Worked example 3: a spreadsheet for a rate investigation.** A student times a reaction at several temperatures. The spreadsheet has these columns:

| Column | Heading | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| A | temperature / °C | Typed in |
| B | time / s | Typed in |
| C | T / K | A + 273 |
| D | 1/T / K⁻¹ | 1 ÷ C |
| E | rate / s⁻¹ | 1 ÷ B, as a measure of rate |
| F | ln(rate) | ln of E |

A graph of F against D, with a linear trendline, gives a gradient that is proportional to −Ea/R. Check one row by hand, so a typing error in a formula is caught.

Other tools named in the 1.2.2 techniques include a pH probe on a data logger. A logger can record temperature or pH every few seconds for a continuous-monitoring method.

## 1.2.1(h) and (i): research and citation

**Choosing sources.** Use textbooks, scientific journals and reputable websites, such as those of universities, learned societies and public scientific bodies. For each source ask: who wrote it and with what expertise; when it was published; whether it has been checked by others (peer review is one route); and whether it agrees with other sources.

**Citing sources.** A citation lets a reader find exactly what you used. Follow the method your teacher sets, which may follow the Practical Skills Handbook. Typical elements:

- **Book:** author, year, title, edition, publisher, page.
- **Journal article:** authors, year, article title, journal name, volume, pages.
- **Website:** author or organisation, year, page title, URL, and the date you accessed it.

Cite in the text where you use the information, and list the full references at the end. CPAC 5 asks for citations "demonstrating that research has taken place, supporting planning and conclusions", so link each source to the decision it informed.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "hazard" and "risk" as the same word.
- Control measures that are generic ("wear goggles") rather than linked to the hazard.
- Writing up results later from memory instead of recording them at the time.
- Leaving changes to a method unrecorded.
- Trusting a spreadsheet formula without checking a row by hand.
- Citing a website without a URL or access date, or a book without page numbers.
- Using sources without judging their reliability.
