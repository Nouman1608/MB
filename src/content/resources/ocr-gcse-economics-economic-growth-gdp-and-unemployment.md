## 3.1 Economic growth and how it is measured

**Economic growth** means an economy is producing more over time. It is measured by the percentage change in **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**, the money value of all the final output made inside a country's borders during a set period, usually a year. Growth figures normally use real GDP, which strips out price rises, so you compare output and not just higher prices.

**GDP per capita** is GDP divided by the population. It shows output per person, so it is a better guide to average living standards than total GDP.

Growth rate = (new GDP - old GDP) / old GDP x 100

GDP per capita = GDP / population

### Worked example: Country Z (illustrative data)

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP (bn units) | 600 | 618 | 633 | 611 | 608 | 629 | 654 | 677 |
| Growth (%) | -- | 3.0 | 2.4 | -3.5 | -0.5 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 3.5 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | 4.0 | 3.8 | 4.2 | 4.9 | 5.5 | 4.5 | 4.2 | 3.9 |

Growth from year 2 to year 3: (633 - 618) / 618 x 100 = 15 / 618 x 100 = 2.4%.

Growth from year 3 to year 4: (611 - 633) / 633 x 100 = -22 / 633 x 100 = -3.5%. Negative growth means output fell.

Now add population. In year 1 Country Z has 75 million people. In year 2 it has 78 million.

- Year 1 per capita: 600 billion / 75 million = 8,000 units per person.
- Year 2 per capita: 618 billion / 78 million = 7,923 units per person.
- Change: (7,923 - 8,000) / 8,000 x 100 = -1.0%.

GDP grew by 3.0% and population grew by 4.0%, so output per person fell. A comment that total GDP can rise while GDP per capita falls is a typical top-credit observation.

### Analysing GDP data

When you analyse a table or graph of GDP, work through these steps.

1. Describe the trend with figures: Country Z's real GDP rose from 600 to 677 over the eight years, a rise of 12.8%.
2. Identify turning points: output fell in years 4 and 5, then recovered from year 6. A recession is a period of falling output, usually defined as two or more consecutive quarters of negative growth.
3. Compare recent with historical data: the growth of about 3.5% to 4.0% in years 6 to 8 is faster than the 2.4% to 3.0% of years 2 and 3.
4. Link to other data: unemployment peaked at 5.5% in year 5, when output was lowest. Unemployment often lags behind output, because firms are slow to hire back.
5. State limits: the data gives no information on how income is shared, on the environment, or on unpaid work.

Always check whether the data is real or nominal, whether it is a total or per capita, and what period it covers.

## Determinants of growth

Growth happens when an economy produces more from its resources, or has more resources to use.

| Determinant | How it raises growth |
|---|---|
| Investment | More capital (machines, buildings, infrastructure) lets each worker produce more |
| Technology | Better methods raise output per worker and cut costs |
| Size of workforce | More workers can produce more, if they find jobs |
| Education and training | Skilled workers are more productive and adapt to new technology |
| Natural resources | More oil, minerals, fertile land or fish stocks give more inputs, though they can run out |
| Government policies | Spending on roads and schools, tax incentives for investment, and stable rules support growth; poor policy can hold it back |

A short calculation shows how the determinants combine. If Country Z's workforce rises by 5% and output per worker rises by 2%, total output rises by 1.05 x 1.02 = 1.071, which is 7.1%. It is slightly more than adding 5% and 2% because the extra workers are also more productive.

Chain of reasoning for **investment**: firms buy new machines, so each worker has more capital, so output per worker rises, so potential output rises and growth is higher. Investment also costs something now: money spent on machines is not spent on consumption, which is an opportunity cost. For education and training, the chain is similar: skills rise, productivity rises, wages and tax revenue rise, but training takes years to pay off.

## Costs and benefits of growth

| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Higher incomes, more jobs, higher firm profits, more tax revenue to spend on public services | Rising prices if demand outruns supply; gains may go mostly to the rich; reliance on a few growing sectors |
| Social | Better health care, education and housing can be afforded; more choice | Inequality may widen; long hours and stress; some regions or groups may be left behind; congestion |
| Environmental sustainability | Richer countries can pay for clean technology; new technology can use fewer resources | More pollution and waste; faster use of resources that cannot be replaced; damage to natural habitats |

**Sustainability** means serving the needs of people today while leaving later generations able to meet their own needs. Growth is not automatically unsustainable. It depends on how the output is produced.

### How to evaluate

- Ask who gains: a high GDP per capita figure hides unequal sharing.
- Ask what kind of growth: growth based on renewable energy differs from growth based on burning fossil fuels.
- Ask about time: environmental costs may appear years after the benefits.
- Ask about the starting point: a poor country may gain a great deal from growth, while a rich one may gain little extra wellbeing.
- Reach a judgement: for example, growth is worthwhile if it is shared and sustainable, and the government can use tax revenue and regulation to make sure it is.

## 3.2 Employment and unemployment

A person is **employed** if they have a paid job, including self-employment. A person is **unemployed** if they have no job but are available for work and looking for it. The **labour force** is the employed plus the unemployed. People who are retired, in full-time education or not looking for work are outside the labour force.

### The Claimant Count

The **Claimant Count** measures unemployment by counting the number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits. It is quick and cheap to produce because the government already holds the records, and it can be broken down by area.

Limits: some unemployed people do not claim, for example because of savings or a partner's income, so the count can understate unemployment. Changes in benefit rules change who can claim, which can alter the count even when the real number of jobless people has not changed.

### Calculating the unemployment rate

The rate compares the jobless with everyone in the labour force: unemployed divided by labour force, times 100. The labour force is employed plus unemployed.

Worked example, Country Z year 5: employed 31.2 million, unemployed 1.8 million.

- Labour force = 31.2 + 1.8 = 33.0 million.
- Rate = 1.8 / 33.0 x 100 = 5.5%.

Year 6: employed 31.8 million, unemployed 1.5 million. Labour force = 33.3 million. Rate = 1.5 / 33.3 x 100 = 4.5%. The rate fell by one percentage point, and the number unemployed fell by 0.3 million, which is a 16.7% fall (0.3 / 1.8).

Do not divide by the employed alone or by the whole population. Say percentage points for a change in a rate.

### Regional figures

| | Labour force | Unemployed | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Region North | 9.0 million | 720,000 | 8.0% |
| Region South | 24.0 million | 600,000 | 2.5% |
| Country Z (year 1) | 33.0 million | 1,320,000 | 4.0% |

North has 27% of the labour force but 55% of the unemployed. The national average of 4.0% hides this gap, so regional data shows problems that a single figure conceals.

### Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Fall in total demand during a downturn; firms cut output and jobs | Builders laid off when the economy shrinks |
| Frictional | Time taken to move between jobs | A graduate searching for a first post |
| Seasonal | Jobs available change with the time of year | Fruit pickers out of work after harvest |
| Structural | A long-term mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, often after an industry declines | Mine workers in a region where the mines have closed |

Cyclical unemployment falls as the economy recovers. Frictional unemployment is short and hard to remove. Structural unemployment can last for years and needs retraining or help to move.

## Causes and consequences of unemployment

**Causes** include weak demand in a recession, firms replacing workers with machines, foreign competition that closes factories, a lack of the skills employers want, workers unable to move because of housing costs, and seasonal patterns.

**Consequences:**

- **Individuals:** lost income and a fall in living standards, debt, loss of skills the longer the spell lasts, and worse health and confidence. Young and older workers can find it hardest to return.
- **Regions:** with Region North's 8.0% rate, less spending in local shops, falling property values and fewer new firms. Unemployment becomes self-reinforcing, and young people may leave.
- **Government:** more spending on benefits and lower income tax and national insurance receipts. Suppose each unemployed person in Country Z costs 7,000 units a year in benefits and 4,500 in lost tax. The cost of the 1,320,000 unemployed in year 1 is 1,320,000 x 11,500 = 15,180 million units a year. The opportunity cost is what that money could have funded instead, such as schools or hospitals.

Unemployed resources are also output lost, so unemployment lowers GDP below what the economy could produce.

### Evaluating

The seriousness of unemployment depends on its type, length and location. Short frictional unemployment can help people find better matches. Long structural unemployment in one region does lasting damage. The best answers weigh these cases and then reach a conclusion about who is hurt most.

## Common errors

- Writing that GDP per capita rose because GDP rose, without checking the population.
- Calculating growth from the new year instead of the old year.
- Dividing the unemployed by the employed instead of by the labour force.
- Saying the Claimant Count includes everyone without a job.
- Treating all unemployment as cyclical.
- Listing costs and benefits of growth without a judgement.
