Three words get mixed up all the time:

- **Gas exchange**: oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out, across the alveolar surface.
- **Breathing (ventilation)**: the muscle movements that move air into and out of the lungs.
- **Respiration**: chemical reactions in cells that release energy from glucose (Topic 10). Breathing is not respiration.

## 9.1.4 The structures of the gas exchange system

Air takes one route to the exchange surface:

`nose or mouth → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli`

| Structure | How to recognise it on a diagram or image |
|---|---|
| Larynx | Wider box-like region at the top of the trachea, in the neck (the voice box) |
| Trachea | Single tube down the neck, with rings of cartilage |
| Bronchi (singular bronchus) | The two branches of the trachea, one entering each lung |
| Bronchioles | Many narrow, branching tubes inside each lung |
| Alveoli (singular alveolus) | Tiny air sacs in clusters at the ends of bronchioles |
| Associated capillaries | Fine blood vessels forming a network over the outside of each alveolus |
| Lungs | The two spongy organs filling most of the thorax |

On a photomicrograph of lung tissue, alveoli look like empty spaces with very thin walls, and red blood cells can be seen inside the capillaries in those walls.

## 9.1.1 Features of gas exchange surfaces

The syllabus limits you to four features. For each, know *why* it speeds up diffusion.

| Feature | Why it increases the rate of diffusion |
|---|---|
| Large surface area | More molecules can cross at the same time |
| Thin surface | Short diffusion distance |
| Good blood supply | Blood carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, so a steep concentration gradient is maintained |
| Good air supply | Breathing replaces alveolar air, keeping oxygen high and carbon dioxide low on the air side |

Blood flow keeps the gradient steep on one side; ventilation keeps it steep on the other. Write "maintains a concentration gradient", not just "lots of blood".

## 9.1.5 The alveoli as the exchange surface

**Characteristics** of the alveolar surface:

- **Very many alveoli**, giving a very large total surface area.
- **Thin walls**: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick.
- **Moist lining**: gases dissolve in this film of water before they diffuse across.
- **Dense capillary network** around each alveolus.
- **Well ventilated** by breathing.

**Role.** Air in the alveolus has a higher oxygen concentration than the blood arriving in the capillaries, so oxygen dissolves in the moist lining and **diffuses** into the blood, where red blood cells carry it away. Blood arriving at the lungs has a higher carbon dioxide concentration than alveolar air, so carbon dioxide **diffuses** out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out. Both gases move passively, down their own concentration gradients.

**Worked example 1 (using an image).** A photomicrograph at ×3000 shows the barrier between the air in an alveolus and the blood in a capillary. The barrier measures 3 mm on the image. Calculate its actual thickness in µm.

- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
- Convert first: 3 mm = 3 × 1000 = 3000 µm.
- Actual = 3000 ÷ 3000 = **1 µm**.

Oxygen crosses only two very thin cells.

## 9.1.3 Inspired and expired air

### The differences

| | Inspired air | Expired air |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | about 21% | about 16% |
| Carbon dioxide | about 0.04% | about 4% |
| Nitrogen | about 78% | about 78% (no real change) |
| Water vapour | variable | high (saturated) |
| Temperature | that of the surroundings | warmer, close to body temperature |

### The investigation

**Carbon dioxide.** Two tubes hold equal volumes of **limewater** or **hydrogencarbonate indicator**. A mouthpiece and valves (or a tube arrangement) let inspired air bubble through one tube and expired air through the other. Limewater turns **milky** in carbon dioxide. Hydrogencarbonate indicator turns from **red/orange to yellow** as carbon dioxide rises. Record the time for each tube to change.

**Water vapour.** Breathe onto a cold, dry mirror or tile: condensation shows expired air carries water vapour.

**Temperature.** Breathe gently over the bulb of a thermometer and compare the reading with room temperature.

Control the volume and concentration of indicator, the starting temperature and the breathing pattern. Limewater is a moderate hazard: wear eye protection, and never suck liquid up the tube. Each person uses their own clean mouthpiece.

**Worked example 2.** Hydrogencarbonate indicator turned yellow after **15 s** with expired air and after **240 s** with inspired air. Compare the rates of colour change.

- Rate = 1 ÷ time.
- Expired air: 1 ÷ 15 = **0.067 s⁻¹**.
- Inspired air: 1 ÷ 240 = **0.0042 s⁻¹**.
- Ratio: 240 ÷ 15 = **16**, so the colour changed 16 times faster with expired air, showing it holds much more carbon dioxide.

### The explanation

- **Less oxygen**: oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into the blood and is used by cells in aerobic respiration.
- **More carbon dioxide**: cells produce it in respiration; the blood carries it to the lungs, where it diffuses into the alveoli.
- **More water vapour**: water evaporates from the moist alveolar lining into the air.
- **Warmer**: air is warmed by the body as it passes through the airways and lungs.
- **Nitrogen unchanged**: the body does not use it, so it passes in and out.

Each explanation needs a process (diffusion, respiration, evaporation) and a direction.

## 9.1.6 and 9.1.7 Breathing: volume and pressure

### The structures

- **Ribs**: bones around the thorax, joined to the sternum and backbone.
- **External intercostal muscles**: the outer layer of muscle between the ribs.
- **Internal intercostal muscles**: the inner layer, nearer the lungs.
- **Diaphragm**: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and fibrous tissue under the lungs, separating the thorax from the abdomen.

### The mechanism

Air always moves from higher pressure to lower pressure. Muscles change the **volume** of the thorax; volume changes **pressure**; pressure difference moves air.

**Breathing in (inspiration)**

1. External intercostal muscles **contract**; internal intercostals relax.
2. Ribs move **up and out**.
3. Diaphragm **contracts** and **flattens** (moves down).
4. Thorax volume **increases**, so pressure inside **decreases** below atmospheric pressure.
5. Air moves **into** the lungs.

**Breathing out (expiration)**

1. External intercostals **relax**; ribs move **down and in**.
2. Diaphragm **relaxes** and returns to its **dome** shape (moves up).
3. Thorax volume **decreases**, so pressure inside **increases** above atmospheric pressure.
4. Air moves **out** of the lungs.

In forced breathing out (exercise, coughing), the **internal intercostal muscles contract** to pull the ribs down and in further, pushing more air out. External and internal intercostals are **antagonistic**: when one set contracts, the other relaxes.

**Worked example 3 (a model).** In a bell-jar model, balloons hang from a Y-tube inside a jar, and a rubber sheet closes the base. Explain why the balloons inflate when the sheet is pulled down.

- The sheet represents the diaphragm; the jar represents the thorax; the balloons represent the lungs.
- Pulling the sheet down increases the volume inside the jar.
- Pressure inside the jar falls below atmospheric pressure.
- Air moves in through the Y-tube (the trachea and bronchi), so the balloons inflate.

A limitation: the jar's walls are rigid, so the model cannot show the ribs and intercostal muscles moving.

## 9.1.8 Physical activity and breathing

### Investigating it

- **Rate**: count breaths for a fixed time (for example 30 s) and convert to breaths per minute.
- **Depth**: the volume of air per breath. Measure with a spirometer if you have one, or compare chest expansion with a tape measure.
- Method: rest, record; do a fixed exercise for a fixed time; record straight away and then every minute until values return to resting. Keep the person, exercise and duration the same, and repeat.

**Worked example 4.** At rest, a student counts **9 breaths in 30 s**. After three minutes of step-ups, she counts **13 breaths in 20 s**. Her depth rises from **0.45 dm³** to **1.8 dm³** per breath.

- Resting rate = 9 × (60 ÷ 30) = **18 breaths per minute**.
- Rate after exercise = 13 × (60 ÷ 20) = **39 breaths per minute**.
- Percentage increase = (39 − 18) ÷ 18 × 100 = **116.7%**.
- Volume per minute at rest = 18 × 0.45 = **8.1 dm³**.
- Volume per minute after exercise = 39 × 1.8 = **70.2 dm³**, about **8.7 times** the resting value.

### Explaining it

1. Contracting muscles need more energy, so they respire faster.
2. More oxygen is needed, and more carbon dioxide is produced.
3. Carbon dioxide concentration in the blood rises; the **brain detects** this.
4. The brain makes the breathing muscles contract faster and more strongly, so **rate and depth increase**.
5. More oxygen reaches the alveoli and diffuses into the blood; more carbon dioxide is removed.

Breathing stays fast and deep for a while after exercise to repay the oxygen debt; that belongs to Topic 10 (anaerobic respiration).

## 9.1.9 Protecting the gas exchange system

Inhaled air carries **pathogens** (such as bacteria and viruses) and **particles** (such as dust and pollen). The lining of the trachea and bronchi contains two cell types:

- **Goblet cells** secrete **mucus**.
- **Mucus** is sticky and **traps** pathogens and particles.
- **Ciliated cells** have **cilia** that **beat** in waves, moving the mucus **up** to the throat, where it is swallowed.

This keeps pathogens and particles away from the alveoli, where they could cause infection or damage the thin exchange surface. Ciliated cells are a specialised cell you also meet in [Cell structure and organisation](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/).

## Common errors

- Writing "alveoli are thin" without "so the diffusion distance is short".
- Saying the diaphragm moves up when breathing in. It contracts, flattens and moves down.
- Reversing volume and pressure. Volume up, pressure down, air in.
- Writing that the lungs "suck in" air. Air is pushed in by higher atmospheric pressure.
- Saying inspired air has "no carbon dioxide". It has about 0.04%.
- Explaining faster breathing only as "needing more oxygen" without the carbon dioxide and brain link.
- Saying cilia trap dust. Mucus traps; cilia move the mucus.
- Calling breathing "respiration".
