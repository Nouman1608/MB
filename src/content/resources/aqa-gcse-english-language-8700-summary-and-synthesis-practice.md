## Section A — Short questions

**1.** A text describes a crowded Victorian railway station and suggests that travel exposed how differently rich and poor passengers were treated. State the **topic** and the **theme**. **[2]**

**2.** Compress this sentence into no more than eight words of your own, keeping its meaning: *"He mended the fence, fixed the gate, cleared the gutters and repainted the shed before the end of the week."* **[2]**

**3.** Read this original passage.

> *"The museum is, without doubt, the dullest building in the county. I spent an hour among glass cases of broken pots
> and labels so small I needed my reading glasses. The café, at least, sold decent cake."*

A student summarises it as: *"The writer visited a museum and looked at pots, then went to the café."* Explain **two** ways in which this summary is weak. **[2]**

**4.** Using the passage in Question 3, which **two** statements are true? Write the letters.

A. The writer spent an hour looking at the exhibits.
B. The writer enjoyed the exhibits.
C. The labels were large and easy to read.
D. The writer thought the café cake was good.
E. The writer visited the museum twice. **[2]**

**5.** For each false statement in Question 4, name the kind of trap it uses (for example overstatement or wrong detail). **[3]**

**6.** Turn this paraphrase into a layered inference: *"The servant girl was not allowed to speak at dinner."* **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Topic: a Victorian railway station or travel by train [1]. Theme: that travel revealed social inequality, with rich and poor passengers treated differently [1].

**2.** A concise version that keeps the meaning, for example "He finished all the outdoor repairs within a week" [1], with the four tasks generalised into one category rather than listed [1].

**3.** It loses the writer's attitude: the source calls the museum "the dullest building in the county", but the summary sounds neutral [1]. It omits the key criticism (tiny labels, tedious exhibits) and gives the café equal weight, so the main idea is lost [1].

**4.** A [1] and D [1].

**5.** B reverses the writer's attitude (the writer found the exhibits dull, not enjoyable) [1]; C contradicts a detail (the labels were "so small" the writer needed glasses) [1]; E is an invented detail not stated in the text, sometimes called an addition [1].

**6.** A layered inference with two linked implications [2] joined into a single idea [1]. For example: her silence suggests both that her opinions were thought worthless and that the family wanted to forget she was there, so she was excluded socially even while physically present.

**7.** Shared topic: family homes or domestic life [1]. Theme for Source A, for example: poverty can coexist with joy and closeness [1]. Theme for Source B, for example: comfort and space can bring isolation [1].

**8.** "Obliged" means forced or made to [1]; the smell from the tannery leaves the family no choice but to keep the window shut, even in the heat of August [1].

**9.** Award up to 6 marks:
- an opening synthesised point, for example that Source A's home is cramped and harmful while Source B's is spacious and comfortable [1];
- relevant evidence from Source A, such as "a single room above a tannery" or the "damp wall" [1];
- an inference from Source A, for example that the family must protect their few possessions from the conditions they live in [1];
- a comparative connective linking the sources, such as "whereas" or "in contrast" [1];
- relevant evidence from Source B, such as "the children have their own rooms" or "the heating works" [1];
- an inference from Source B, for example that the family has privacy and security that Source A's family lacks, though "mostly works" hints that comfort is not complete [1].

**10.** Award up to 5 marks:
- a "both" clause naming genuine shared ground, for example that both families spend Sundays together or at leisure [1];
- a "but" turn into the difference [1];
- evidence and inference for Source A: they "walked out to the fields, which cost nothing", suggesting shared, free pleasure and closeness [1];
- evidence and inference for Source B: they "stay in, each of us on a separate screen", suggesting they are together physically but apart in attention [1];
- a concluding inference that synthesises, for example that the poorer family seems more connected than the richer one [1].

**11.** The summary question rewards understanding of content, not analysis of language, so naming word classes and connotations earns little credit [1]; the student also makes no inference about the families [1]. A creditable rewrite, for example: "Source A's father sings in the fields, which suggests real happiness, whereas Source B's family sit on 'separate' screens, implying they are isolated from one another." The rewrite makes inferences about what each detail suggests [1] and links the sources with a comparative connective [1].

**12.** Award up to 4 marks:
- the main point: the council repeatedly fails to repair Mill Lane [1];
- the consequences generalised rather than listed, for example "the road damages vehicles and deters cyclists" [1];
- the writer's attitude kept: frustration or scepticism about the council's promises [1];
- within 40 words and in the student's own words [1].

For example: "The writer is frustrated that the council never keeps its yearly promise to repair Mill Lane, whose dangerous potholes damage vehicles and deter cyclists, and doubts its latest excuse."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the topic with the theme.
- Listing examples instead of generalising them.
- Writing a neutral summary of an opinionated source.
- Analysing word choices in a question that rewards content.
- Giving one source a full treatment and the other a passing mention.
