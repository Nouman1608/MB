These notes condense subtopic **8.2 Relative and expected frequencies** of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus (examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027). The Core (C8.2) and Extended (E8.2) statements are identical, so every line is for both tiers. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [relative and expected frequencies study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-relative-and-expected-frequencies/); for exam-style questions, use the [relative and expected frequencies practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-relative-and-expected-frequencies-practice/).

## What the syllabus says

1. Understand relative frequency as an estimate of probability (for example, from experiments with a spinner).
2. Calculate expected frequencies (for example, using probability to estimate an expected value from a population). This includes understanding what is meant by **fair**, **bias** and **random**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Trial | One go of an experiment |
| Frequency | The number of times an outcome happens |
| Relative frequency | Frequency ÷ number of trials; an estimate of probability |
| Expected frequency | Probability × number of trials; a long-run average |
| Fair | All outcomes equally likely |
| Biased | Some outcomes more likely than others |
| Random | Every item has an equal chance; no pattern or influence |
| Population | The whole group a sample is taken from |

## Formulae

```
relative frequency  = frequency ÷ number of trials
expected frequency  = probability × number of trials
number of trials    = expected frequency ÷ probability
estimate for a population = relative frequency in sample × population size
```

## Reliability of an estimate

| More reliable | Less reliable |
|---|---|
| more trials | few trials |
| combined counts from several experiments | the average of several relative frequencies |
| a random, representative sample | a sample from one place, time or group |

**Combining results:** add all the "successes" and all the trials, then divide. Example: 6 out of 25 and 31 out of 75 give 37/100 = 0.37, not the mean of 0.24 and 0.413…

## Reading a running-total table

Experiments are often recorded as running totals: each column includes all the earlier trials.

| Spins | 10 | 40 | 100 | 250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of 2s so far | 4 | 11 | 23 | 61 |
| Relative frequency | 0.4 | 0.275 | 0.23 | 0.244 |

- The relative frequencies jump about at first, then settle; the best estimate is **0.244**, from all 250 spins.
- Do **not** add the columns: there were 250 spins in total, not 400.
- A sketch of relative frequency against number of trials levels out towards the probability.
- For a fair four-sided spinner the probability of a 2 is 0.25, so 0.244 gives no evidence of bias.

## Testing for bias

```
1. Work out the expected frequency if fair: (1/number of outcomes) × trials.
2. Compare each observed frequency with it.
3. Small differences: no evidence of bias. Large difference: suggests bias.
4. Give a reason using the numbers.
```

Example: a coin tossed 200 times gives 131 heads. Expected if fair: 100. 131 is far above 100 (relative frequency 0.655), so the coin appears biased towards heads.

## Worked reminders

```
P(late) = 0.12,  250 journeys        →  expected late = 0.12 × 250 = 30
Fair spinner with 5 equal sectors, 90 spins  →  expected for one sector = 18
Expected 28 wins, P(win) = 0.35      →  trials = 28 ÷ 0.35 = 80
Sample: 18 of 120 left-handed; school of 950  →  0.15 × 950 = 142.5 ≈ 143 left-handed
```

## Exam traps

- Averaging relative frequencies from samples of different sizes.
- Picking a "nice" estimate rather than the one from the most trials.
- Writing that an expected frequency is exactly what will happen.
- Rounding an expected value when the question did not ask (rounding to a whole number is sensible only when the context needs it).
- Calling something biased from a small difference.
- Giving a reason without numbers, such as "because it is unfair".

## Self-test

1. A coin is tossed 60 times and lands tails 33 times. Find the relative frequency of tails.
2. Why is a relative frequency from 500 trials better than one from 50?
3. P(rain) on a day in a town is 0.15. Estimate the number of rainy days in a 360-day period.
4. A fair dice is rolled 210 times. Find the expected number of scores greater than 4.
5. A spinner has P(red) = 0.4. It is spun 35 times. Find the expected number of reds.
6. Explain the word "random" in "a student is chosen at random".
7. Rolls of a dice: 6 appears 16 times in 96 rolls. Is there evidence of bias towards 6?
8. The relative frequency of a faulty item is 0.025. How many faulty items would you expect in 1600?
9. Results 9 out of 30 and 51 out of 170 are combined. Find the combined relative frequency.
10. Expected number of heads from a fair coin is 85. How many tosses?
11. A sample of 80 people found 12 with blue eyes. Estimate the number with blue eyes in a town of 6000.
12. Define "fair".

### Answers

1. 33/60 = **0.55**
2. More trials make the relative frequency **more reliable** (closer to the true probability).
3. 0.15 × 360 = **54 days**
4. P(5 or 6) = 1/3; 210 × 1/3 = **70**
5. 0.4 × 35 = **14**
6. Every student has the **same chance** of being chosen.
7. Expected if fair = 96 ÷ 6 = 16; observed 16, so **no evidence of bias**.
8. 0.025 × 1600 = **40**
9. 60/200 = **0.3**
10. 85 ÷ 0.5 = **170**
11. 12/80 = 0.15; 0.15 × 6000 = **900**
12. Every outcome is **equally likely**.
