## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a reaction intermediate and a transition state. **[2]**

**2.** State the molecularity of each elementary step.

**(a)** O₃ → O₂ + O **[1]**
**(b)** NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂ **[1]**
**(c)** O + O₂ + N₂ → O₃ + N₂ **[1]**

**3.** Mercury(II) chloride reacts with ethanedioate ions:
2HgCl₂(aq) + C₂O₄²⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2CO₂(g) + Hg₂Cl₂(s)

Starting rates were measured at one temperature.

| Experiment | [HgCl₂] / mol dm⁻³ | [C₂O₄²⁻] / mol dm⁻³ | Starting rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.10 | 0.20 | 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.20 | 0.20 | 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 3 | 0.10 | 0.30 | 7.2 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 4 | 0.050 | 0.40 | ? |

**(a)** Using the data, find the order in HgCl₂ and the order in C₂O₄²⁻. Show which experiments you compare. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the rate equation for this reaction and its overall order. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate k, giving its units. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the starting rate in experiment 4. **[1]**

**4.** Nitryl chloride, NO₂Cl, breaks down in the gas phase: 2NO₂Cl(g) → 2NO₂(g) + Cl₂(g). The experimental rate equation is rate = k[NO₂Cl]. A proposed mechanism is:

Step 1 (slow): NO₂Cl → NO₂ + Cl
Step 2 (fast): NO₂Cl + Cl → NO₂ + Cl₂

**(a)** Show that the mechanism is consistent with the overall equation, and identify the intermediate. **[2]**
**(b)** State the molecularity of each step. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the mechanism is consistent with the rate equation. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why a single-step mechanism in which two NO₂Cl molecules collide can be rejected. **[1]**

**5.** For H₂(g) + I₂(g) → 2HI(g), one proposed mechanism is:

Step 1 (fast, reversible): I₂ ⇌ 2I
Step 2 (slow): H₂ + 2I → 2HI

**(a)** Deduce the rate equation predicted by this mechanism. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** State the molecularity of step 2 and comment on how likely such a step is. **[2]**
**(c)** The overall reaction is slightly exothermic. Sketch an energy profile consistent with this mechanism, labelling the intermediate, the transition states, the activation energy of the rate-determining step and ΔH. **[3]**

**6.** **(a)** Describe the rate–concentration line for a reactant that is (i) zero, (ii) first, (iii) second order. **[3]**
**(b)** The concentration of a reactant falls from 0.60 to 0.30 mol dm⁻³ in the first 40 s and from 0.30 to 0.15 mol dm⁻³ in the next 40 s. Deduce the order with respect to this reactant. **[1]**

**7.** Cyclopropane isomerises to propene in the gas phase. Tangents to a concentration–time graph give these rates.

| [C₃H₆] / mol dm⁻³ | 0.0400 | 0.0250 | 0.0100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ | 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ | 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ | 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ |

**(a)** Deduce the order of reaction, showing your reasoning. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate k, with units. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the rate at [C₃H₆] = 0.0180 mol dm⁻³. **[1]**

**8.** This question links to Reactivity 3.4.

2-iodo-2-methylpropane reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions. rate = k[(CH₃)₃CI]. When [(CH₃)₃CI] = 0.012 mol dm⁻³ and [OH⁻] = 0.050 mol dm⁻³, the rate is 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate k, with units. **[2]**
**(b)** State the new rate if [OH⁻] is doubled and [(CH₃)₃CI] is halved at the same time. **[1]**

1-iodopropane reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions. rate = k[CH₃CH₂CH₂I][OH⁻]. When [CH₃CH₂CH₂I] = 0.020 and [OH⁻] = 0.030 mol dm⁻³, the rate is 2.4 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

**(c)** Calculate k, with units. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the rate when [CH₃CH₂CH₂I] = 0.040 and [OH⁻] = 0.045 mol dm⁻³. **[1]**

**9.** The rate constant of a reaction was measured at four temperatures.

| T / K | 290 | 305 | 320 | 335 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| k / mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ | 0.229 | 0.749 | 2.19 | 5.82 |

**(a)** Calculate 1/T and ln k for the 290 K and 335 K data. **[2]**
**(b)** All four points lie on a straight line. Determine its gradient. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine the activation energy in kJ mol⁻¹. **[2]**
**(d)** Determine the Arrhenius factor, A, with units. **[2]**
**(e)** Deduce the overall order of the reaction. **[1]**

**10.** A first-order reaction has k = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at 298 K and k = 1.50 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 318 K.

**(a)** Describe how the rate constant changes with temperature. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate Ea from the relationship ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate k at 308 K. **[2]**
**(d)** State what the Arrhenius factor, A, takes into account. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** An intermediate is produced by one step and consumed by a later step; it sits in a trough on the energy profile [1]. A transition state is the highest-energy arrangement within one step, bonds part-broken and part-formed; it sits at a peak and cannot be isolated [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a defining point for each species; describing only one earns half the credit at most.

**2. (a)** **Unimolecular** [1]
**(b)** **Bimolecular** [1]
**(c)** **Termolecular**: three particles meet, with N₂ taking away energy [1]
*Examiner insight:* Count only the reacting particles in the step itself.

**3. (a)** Experiments 1 and 2: [HgCl₂] doubles, [C₂O₄²⁻] fixed, rate doubles, so **first order in HgCl₂** [1]. Experiments 1 and 3: [C₂O₄²⁻] rises 1.5-fold and the rate 2.25-fold, which is 1.5², so **second order in C₂O₄²⁻** [1].
**(b)** **rate = k[HgCl₂][C₂O₄²⁻]²**; third order overall [1].
**(c)** k = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.10 × 0.20²) [1] = **8.0 × 10⁻³ mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** 8.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.050 × 0.40² = **6.4 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* State which experiments you compared and which concentration stayed fixed; an order quoted with no comparison shown is hard to credit.

**4. (a)** Adding the steps: 2NO₂Cl + Cl → 2NO₂ + Cl₂ + Cl; Cl cancels to give the overall equation [1]. The intermediate is **Cl** [1].
**(b)** Step 1 is **unimolecular**; step 2 is **bimolecular** [1].
**(c)** The slow step involves only one NO₂Cl, so the predicted rate equation is rate = k[NO₂Cl], which matches experiment [1].
**(d)** Two NO₂Cl in the only step would give rate = k[NO₂Cl]², second order, which does not match the first-order experimental result [1].
*Examiner insight:* Justify "consistent" by linking the slow step's species to the experimental rate equation.

**5. (a)** The slow step uses H₂ and two I atoms; each pair of I atoms comes from one I₂ in the fast step before it [1]. So **rate = k[H₂][I₂]** [1].
**(b)** Step 2 is **termolecular** [1]. Three particles meeting at once with enough energy and the right orientation is rare, so this step is slow [1].
**(c)** Two humps with the intermediate (2I + H₂) in the trough between them [1]; second peak higher than the first, with Ea of the rate-determining step marked from reactants to that peak [1]; products slightly below reactants, ΔH shown as a downward arrow [1].
*Examiner insight:* An intermediate drawn at a peak, or a tallest hump for the fast step, contradicts the mechanism and loses that credit.

**6. (a)** (i) Zero order: a **horizontal line**, as rate does not depend on concentration [1]. (ii) First order: a **straight line through the origin** [1]. (iii) Second order: an **upward curve through the origin** [1].
**(b)** Each 40 s halves the concentration: a constant half-life, so **first order** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the axes you mean: zero order is horizontal on a rate–concentration graph but a sloping straight line on a concentration–time graph.

**7. (a)** From 0.0400 to 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ the concentration is divided by 4 and the rate is divided by 4 (2.4 × 10⁻⁵ to 6.0 × 10⁻⁶) [1]; rate ∝ [C₃H₆], so **first order** [1].
**(b)** k = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.0400 [1] = **6.0 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.0180 = **1.08 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Confirming the order with a third data point catches misreads.

**8. (a)** k = 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.012 [1] = **3.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** [OH⁻] is not in the rate equation, so only the halving counts: **1.8 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** k = 2.4 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ (0.020 × 0.030) [1] = **4.0 × 10⁻³ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** 4.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.040 × 0.045 = **7.2 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The two k values must carry different units because the overall orders differ.

**9. (a)** For 290 K, 1/T is 3.448 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ and ln k is −1.474 [1]. For 335 K, 1/T is 2.985 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ and ln k is 1.761 [1].
**(b)** gradient = (1.761 − (−1.474)) ÷ (2.985 × 10⁻³ − 3.448 × 10⁻³) [1] = 3.235 ÷ (−4.63 × 10⁻⁴) = **−6.99 × 10³ K** [1].
**(c)** Ea = −gradient × R = 6.99 × 10³ × 8.31 [1] = 5.81 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **58.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** ln A = ln k − gradient × (1/T) = 1.761 + 6.99 × 10³ × 2.985 × 10⁻³ = 22.63 [1]; A = e^22.63 ≈ **6.7 × 10⁹ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(e)** k has units mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹, so the reaction is **second order overall** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Graph readings vary slightly, so answers of about 57 to 59 kJ mol⁻¹ are reasonable; a negative Ea shows the gradient's sign was mishandled.

**10. (a)** k increases as temperature increases, roughly exponentially [1].
**(b)** ln(1.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻⁴) = 2.238 [1]; 1/298 − 1/318 = 2.111 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ [1]; Ea = 8.31 × 2.238 ÷ 2.111 × 10⁻⁴ = 8.81 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **88.1 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** ln(k₃₀₈ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻⁴) = (88 100 ÷ 8.31) × (1/298 − 1/308) = 1.155 [1]; k₃₀₈ = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ × e^1.155 = **5.1 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** The frequency of collisions with the proper orientation [1].
*Examiner insight:* R = 8.31 is in joules, so turn Ea into J mol⁻¹ first; working in kJ throws the answer out by a factor of 1000.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking orders from the coefficients in the overall equation (question 3 has 2HgCl₂ but is first order in HgCl₂).
- Missing or wrong units on k.
- Drawing the intermediate at a peak.
- Using °C instead of K, or mixing kJ and J, in the Arrhenius equation.
- Dropping the minus sign from the gradient, so Ea comes out negative.
