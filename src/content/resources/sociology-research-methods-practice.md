## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between a research method and a research design. **[2]**

**2.** State **four** factors a sociologist must consider when choosing a method. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **two** strengths and **two** limitations of participant observation. **[8]**

**4.** Explain **two** strengths and **two** limitations of using official statistics as a source of data. **[8]**

**5.** Explain the difference between **reliability** and **validity**, and explain why a method that is high in one is often low in the other. **[6]**

**6.** Explain **three** ethical principles a sociologist must observe, and explain the ethical difficulty of covert participant observation. **[9]**

**7.** A researcher wishes to study a group of homeless people.

**(a)** Explain why random sampling would be impractical. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a suitable sampling method and justify it. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A **method** is the **specific technique used to collect data**, such as a questionnaire or an interview [1]. A **design** is the **overall structure of the study** — who will be studied, in what conditions, and in what sequence [1].

**2.** Any four: **practical factors** — time, cost, access and the researcher's skills [1]; **ethical factors** — consent, harm and confidentiality [1]; **theoretical position** — positivist or interpretivist [1]; **the nature of the topic and the group being studied**, particularly whether it is sensitive or hard to reach [1].

**3. Strengths:** it produces **highly valid, in-depth qualitative data**, because the researcher sees what people actually do rather than what they say they do [1] [1]. It allows the researcher to achieve **verstehen — an understanding of the meanings the group attaches to its own behaviour** — which no questionnaire could capture [1] [1]. It is also useful for **studying groups who would not agree to be interviewed** [1].
**Limitations:** it is **very time-consuming and expensive**, and the sample is necessarily **small and unrepresentative**, so findings cannot be generalised [1] [1]. It is **unreliable** — the study depends on the personal relationships and interpretations of one researcher, so it **cannot be replicated** and there is a risk of **"going native"**, losing objectivity by over-identifying with the group [1] [1].

**4. Strengths:** they are **readily available, free and cover very large populations**, often nationally and over many years, so trends and comparisons are possible at no cost to the researcher [1] [1]. They are usually **collected in a standardised way**, making them **reliable and quantitative**, which suits positivist research [1] [1].
**Limitations:** they are **collected by the state for its own administrative purposes**, not for the sociologist's research question, so the definitions and categories may not fit — the definition of unemployment, for example, has been changed many times [1] [1]. They may **lack validity**: crime statistics record only offences reported to and recorded by the police, so the "dark figure" of unreported crime is missing entirely [1] [1].

**5. Reliability** means a method is **consistent — repeating it would produce the same results** [1]. **Validity** means the data gives a **true and authentic picture of what is being studied** [1].
They often pull in opposite directions because **standardisation creates reliability but restricts what respondents can say** [1] — a structured questionnaire can be repeated exactly, but forces answers into pre-set boxes that may not reflect people's real views [1]. Conversely, **unstructured methods let people express themselves freely, giving validity**, but are shaped by the individual researcher and cannot be repeated identically [1] [1].

**6.** Any three, 2 marks each: **Informed consent** — participants must know what the research involves and agree to take part, with the right to withdraw [1] [1]. **Confidentiality and anonymity** — identities and data must be protected, using pseudonyms and secure storage [1] [1]. **Protection from harm** — participants should not be exposed to physical or psychological harm, or to legal risk, greater than in everyday life [1] [1].
**Covert participant observation** is ethically difficult because **participants cannot give informed consent, since the whole method depends on deceiving them** [1]; the researcher may also **witness or be drawn into illegal or harmful acts** without being able to intervene or report them, and the group's trust is exploited [1] [1].

**7. (a)** Random sampling requires a **complete sampling frame — a list of everyone in the population** [1]; no such list of homeless people exists, since by definition they have no fixed address to be registered at [1].
**(b)** **Snowball sampling** [1] — the researcher contacts one or two individuals, perhaps through a shelter or outreach worker, and asks them to introduce others [1]. This works because it **uses existing trust networks to reach a hard-to-access group**, though the sample will not be representative and members of the network may be similar to one another [1].

**8. Strength:** an experiment is **controlled**, so variables other than the one being tested can be held constant, allowing the researcher to identify **cause and effect** with more confidence than other methods allow [2]. **Limitation:** the controlled, artificial setting means behaviour may not reflect how people act in real life, giving the study **low ecological validity** [2].

**9.** A **pilot study** tests the research method on a small scale before the main study is carried out [1]. It allows the sociologist to **identify and fix problems with wording, timing or procedure** — such as a confusing question or a questionnaire that takes too long — before committing the time and cost of the full study [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Giving strengths of observation generally rather than of participant observation.
- Treating official statistics as objective fact.
- Suggesting random sampling for a group with no sampling frame.
