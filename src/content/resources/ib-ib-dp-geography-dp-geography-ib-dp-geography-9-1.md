Because the unit is part of the core, it is examined in Paper 2 for every student. Test yourself afterwards with the [9.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-9-1-practice/); the [Global Climate study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-global-climate/) gives the overview of all three sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the atmospheric system as an energy budget with inputs, outputs and stores.
- Explain the natural greenhouse effect and how human activity enhances it.
- Explain how changes in the energy balance arise from named factors, and how feedback loops amplify or dampen them.
- Relate greenhouse gas sources and emissions to globalisation and development, at more than one scale.

## Why greenhouse gases trap outgoing energy

The Sun is very hot, so it emits most of its energy at short wavelengths: visible light, with some ultraviolet and near-infrared. The Earth's surface is far cooler, so it emits at long wavelengths, as infrared. This difference is the key to the greenhouse effect.

Gases such as carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapour are almost transparent to incoming shortwave radiation, so sunlight passes through and warms the surface. The same molecules absorb strongly at some infrared wavelengths. They take in part of the longwave radiation rising from the surface and re-emit it in all directions, including back down. The lower atmosphere and surface therefore warm until they radiate enough energy to restore balance at the top of the atmosphere.

Two refinements lift an answer:

- **The atmospheric window.** Some infrared wavelengths pass through the atmosphere with little absorption. Adding more of a gas that absorbs inside this window has a stronger effect than adding more of a gas whose wavelengths are already almost fully absorbed. This is one reason a molecule of methane has a far larger warming effect than a molecule of carbon dioxide.
- **Lifetime matters as well as strength.** Methane breaks down in the atmosphere within about a decade, while a large share of added carbon dioxide stays for centuries. Cutting methane slows warming quickly; cutting carbon dioxide decides how much warming is eventually locked in.

## Natural forcings over different timescales

Not every change in the energy balance is human. Sorting natural causes by timescale helps you explain why scientists attribute recent warming to people.

| Factor | Timescale | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital changes | Tens of thousands of years | Variations in the Earth's orbit and tilt change how solar energy is distributed by season and latitude; they pace the glacial and interglacial cycles |
| Solar output | Years to centuries | The roughly 11-year sunspot cycle and longer quiet periods slightly change energy received |
| Volcanic aerosols | One to two years | Sulphate particles in the stratosphere reflect sunlight, causing global dimming |
| Greenhouse gas changes | Decades to centuries | Human emissions have raised concentrations far faster than natural processes did in the recent geological past |

Orbital changes are far too slow to explain warming over a century, solar output has not risen since the mid-20th century, and volcanic cooling fades within a couple of years. Only the rise in greenhouse gases fits the timing and scale of recent warming.

## Methane release and the permafrost store

Permafrost is ground that stays frozen for at least two consecutive years. Arctic permafrost holds very large amounts of organic carbon: plant and animal remains that froze before they could decompose. When it thaws, microbes break down this material. Where oxygen is present, they release carbon dioxide; in waterlogged, oxygen-poor soils they release methane.

This makes permafrost a **carbon store that can become a source**. The thaw is driven by warming, and the gases it releases cause more warming, so it is a positive feedback. Two points show depth:

- The release is gradual and hard to reverse. Once organic matter decomposes, the carbon cannot easily be put back.
- Methane is also released by wetlands, which emit more as they warm, and from human sources such as livestock, rice paddies, landfill and leaks from fossil fuel extraction. Rising methane concentrations reflect both natural and human sources, so answers should not attribute all of it to permafrost.

## Oceans: the planet's heat store and conveyor

The oceans have absorbed the great majority of the extra heat trapped by the enhanced greenhouse effect, because water has a high heat capacity. This delays some surface warming, but stored heat raises sea levels by thermal expansion and will keep influencing climate for centuries.

Ocean currents also move energy. Surface currents such as the Gulf Stream carry warm water towards the poles. In the North Atlantic, cold, salty water is dense enough to sink and flow south at depth. This sinking helps drive the **thermohaline circulation** (thermo for temperature, haline for salt), sometimes called the global ocean conveyor, which links all the ocean basins and redistributes heat from the tropics towards higher latitudes.

Climate change could weaken this circulation. Meltwater from Greenland and higher rainfall add fresh water to the North Atlantic, making surface water less salty and so less dense, which reduces sinking. A weaker circulation would change the regional energy balance: less heat carried north, with knock-on effects on rainfall patterns and sea levels along some coasts. This is a good example of a change in energy transfer rather than in total energy received.

## Emissions, development and globalisation

International variations in emissions are linked to stage of development, energy mix and trade. Strong answers specify which measure is being used.

- **Total annual emissions** show which countries matter most for global concentrations now.
- **Per-capita emissions** show lifestyle and fairness: a large country can have high totals but low emissions per person.
- **Cumulative emissions** show historical responsibility, because carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere for a long time.
- **Consumption-based emissions** reassign the emissions released in making traded goods to the country that consumes them.

Globalisation moved much manufacturing to emerging economies, so their territorial emissions rose while some high-income importers' territorial emissions fell. Development also changes the source of emissions: early industrialisation tends to rely on coal and heavy industry, while high-income economies emit more from transport, buildings and services.

## Worked example: two ways of counting

All figures are invented for practice. Mt means million tonnes of carbon dioxide.

```
Country P (manufacturing exporter), population 100 million
  Territorial emissions                 500 Mt
  Emissions embodied in its exports    -150 Mt
  Emissions embodied in its imports     +30 Mt
  Consumption-based emissions           500 - 150 + 30 = 380 Mt
  Per capita: territorial 5.0 t; consumption 3.8 t

Country R (high-income importer), population 60 million
  Territorial emissions                 300 Mt
  Emissions embodied in its exports     -40 Mt
  Emissions embodied in its imports    +160 Mt
  Consumption-based emissions           300 - 40 + 160 = 420 Mt
  Per capita: territorial 5.0 t; consumption 7.0 t
```

Both countries emit 5.0 tonnes per person on a territorial basis, so they look equally responsible. Once trade is included, each person in R is responsible for almost twice the emissions of each person in P (7.0 against 3.8). The interpretation to write: territorial figures understate the footprint of high-income consumers and overstate that of manufacturing exporters, which matters when countries argue about who should cut most.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how the melting of sea ice can act as a positive feedback on global temperature.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Sea ice has a high albedo, reflecting much of the incoming shortwave radiation that reaches it **[property stated precisely]**. As global temperature rises, sea ice melts and is replaced by open ocean, which is dark and absorbs most of the sunlight falling on it **[change in surface linked to warming]**. The surface therefore absorbs more energy, the ocean and the air above it warm further, and more ice melts **[the loop is closed: the effect becomes a cause]**. Because the response amplifies the original warming, it is a positive feedback; this helps explain why the Arctic is warming faster than the global average **[term defined by its effect, with a consequence]**."

## Common mistakes

- Saying greenhouse gases "trap sunlight". They let most incoming shortwave radiation through and absorb outgoing longwave radiation.
- Treating the ozone hole as a cause of global warming. Ozone loss and the enhanced greenhouse effect are separate problems.
- Describing the thermohaline circulation as stopping suddenly, rather than weakening, and giving no reason why fresh water slows sinking.
- Calling permafrost thaw a "methane explosion" without explaining the store, the microbes and the feedback.
- Comparing two countries' emissions without saying whether the figures are total, per-capita, cumulative or consumption-based.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an energy budget diagram by naming the flows (incoming shortwave, reflected, absorbed, outgoing longwave) and quoting values with units if they are given.
- **Explain** a feedback by writing it as a closed loop of linked steps, ending where it began.
- **Distinguish** needs both items and the difference made explicit, for example natural greenhouse effect against enhanced greenhouse effect.
- **Examine** questions on causes reward weighing natural against human factors by timescale and scale, with evidence such as the timing of solar output and temperature trends.
- When you use emissions data, state the year and the measure, and avoid figures you cannot recall precisely.
