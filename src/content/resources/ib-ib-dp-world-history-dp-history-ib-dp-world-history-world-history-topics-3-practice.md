Section A checks precise knowledge of rulers and their methods. Section B builds analysis and ends with two full essays, each to be written in about 45 minutes and marked with the five 15-mark markbands.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** sources of legitimacy available to a medieval ruler. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the significance of Charlemagne's coronation in 800. **[3]**

**3.** Outline how Song Taizu came to power in 960. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **three** methods William I used to control England after 1066. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the Mandate of Heaven could both strengthen and threaten a Chinese emperor. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **two** ways in which Mansa Musa used Islam to strengthen his rule. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why the Abbasid caliphs lost political power by the mid-tenth century. **[3]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which the Hongwu Emperor centralised power in Ming China. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why the Investiture Contest threatened the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor. **[4]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the role of religion in the rule of **two** rulers, each chosen from a different region. **[6]**

**11.** "Succession was the greatest threat to medieval dynasties." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view, using examples from at least **two** dynasties. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the success of **one** ruler in consolidating power after taking the throne by force. **[15]**

**13.** Examine the reasons for the decline of **one** dynasty between 750 and 1500. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: divine sanction, such as the Mandate of Heaven or anointing (1); hereditary descent (1); designation by a predecessor (1); election or acclamation (1); conquest (1); coronation by a religious leader (1). **[2]**

**2.** It revived the title of emperor in the West and linked Frankish power to the Roman imperial tradition (1). It tied Charlemagne's rule to the papacy and the Church, giving it religious sanction (1). It raised questions about the relationship between pope and emperor, since the pope had placed the crown, and it angered the Byzantine emperors, who claimed the Roman imperial title (1). **[3]**

**3.** Zhao Kuangyin was a leading general of the Later Zhou dynasty (1). In 960 his troops proclaimed him emperor in a military coup, and he founded the Song dynasty as Taizu (1). **[2]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: building castles (1); suppressing rebellions, as in the Harrying of the North (1); replacing the English nobility with Norman followers (1); the Domesday survey (1); the oath of Salisbury (1); appointing Normans to senior church posts, such as Lanfranc (1). **[3]**

**5.** It strengthened an emperor because it presented his rule as approved by Heaven, demanding obedience (1). It threatened him because disasters, rebellions or misrule could be read as signs that he had lost the mandate (1). Successful rebels, such as dynastic founders, could claim the mandate had passed to them, so it justified overthrowing a failing ruler (1). **[3]**

**6.** 2 marks for each developed way: his pilgrimage to Mecca (1324-1325) displayed his wealth and piety, raising his prestige among Muslim rulers and merchants (2); he built mosques and supported scholars in Timbuktu, linking Mali to the wider Islamic world and drawing learned men to his court (2); Islam provided a shared faith with the merchants who carried Mali's trade, strengthening the economic base of his power (2). **[4]**

**7.** The caliphs came to depend on Turkish slave soldiers, whose commanders gained control of the court (1). Provinces broke away under local dynasties, reducing the caliphs' revenue (1). In 945 the Buyids took control of Baghdad, and the caliph kept religious authority but lost real political power (1). **[3]**

**8.** 2 marks for each developed way: he abolished the chancellorship in 1380 after purging Hu Weiyong, so that ministries reported directly to the emperor (2); he used large-scale purges and a secret police (the Embroidered Uniform Guard) to intimidate officials (2); he created systems of household registration and local organisation to control taxation and labour (2). **[4]**

**9.** 2 marks for each developed reason: bishops were great landholders and royal officials, so losing the right to appoint them weakened the emperor's control of land, revenue and administration (2); Pope Gregory VII excommunicated Henry IV, freeing his subjects from their oaths and encouraging German princes to rebel (2); the dispute challenged the religious basis of imperial authority, as at Canossa in 1077 the emperor had to do penance before the pope (2). **[4]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example Charlemagne (Europe) and Mansa Musa (Africa and the Middle East). Similarities (up to 2): both used religion to raise their prestige and legitimacy (1); both patronised religious buildings and learning (1). Differences (up to 4): Charlemagne received his imperial title from the pope, while Mansa Musa's authority did not depend on a religious leader's grant (1); Charlemagne used force to convert the Saxons, while Islam in Mali was mainly the faith of the elite and merchants, alongside older beliefs (1); Charlemagne's alliance with the papacy shaped European politics for centuries, while Mansa Musa's religion linked him outward to Mecca and Cairo (1); Charlemagne directed church reform, while Mansa Musa supported scholars without directing doctrine (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: the Carolingian empire was divided and weakened by civil war among Louis the Pious's sons, ending in the Treaty of Verdun (843) (2); after Henry I of England died in 1135 without a surviving son, the rival claims of Stephen and Matilda led to years of civil war (2). Against: the Abbasids declined mainly because of military dependence and loss of provinces, not succession rules (2); the Song survived many successions but lost north China to the Jin in 1127 because of military weakness (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *For example, William I.* Methods: castles; suppression of revolts (1067-1071), including the Harrying of the North; a new aristocracy of tenants-in-chief; church appointments; the Domesday survey; legitimacy through coronation and claims to be Edward's heir. Evidence of success: no successful English rebellion after 1071; a stable succession to William II in England. Limits: the violence and devastation; rebellion by his son Robert and some Norman lords; the division of England and Normandy between his sons. A strong answer defines success, weighs methods and reaches a judgement. *Alternative example:* Song Taizu or the Hongwu Emperor. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, the Abbasids.* Factors: reliance on Turkish slave soldiers and the move to Samarra; succession disputes, including the civil war between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun; the loss of provinces to local dynasties such as the Fatimids in Egypt; fiscal strain; Buyid control from 945 and Seljuk dominance from 1055; the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258). A strong answer distinguishes political decline from the survival of religious and cultural prestige, ranks factors and decides whether decline was gradual or sudden. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a dynasty and a ruler both be used in a comparison?**
Yes, if the question allows it, but compare like with like: methods with methods, reasons with reasons.

**Is Mali in the same region as the Abbasids?**
Yes. The IB region "Africa and the Middle East" covers both, so do not pair them in a two-region question.
