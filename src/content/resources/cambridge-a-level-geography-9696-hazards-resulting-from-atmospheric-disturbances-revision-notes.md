Condensed recall for **9.3 Hazards resulting from atmospheric disturbances** (Cambridge 9696, Paper 3, Hazardous environments option). Full explanations are in the [9.3 atmospheric hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-hazards-resulting-from-atmospheric-disturbances/); test yourself with the [9.3 atmospheric hazards practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-hazards-resulting-from-atmospheric-disturbances-practice/). Haiyan, Idai and Joplin are summarised in the [Topic 9 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-hazardous-environments-revision-notes/).

## What the syllabus lists

- Distribution of areas most at risk from tropical cyclones (cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons) and tornadoes.
- Processes of formation and development.
- Large-scale hazards: storm surge, coastal flooding, intense rainfall (river floods, mass movement), high winds.
- Small-scale hazards: intense rain and hail, high winds, pressure imbalances.
- Primary and secondary impacts; prediction, preparedness, monitoring; perception of risk.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tropical cyclone | Intense low-pressure system over warm tropical seas; called hurricane, typhoon or cyclone by region |
| Eye / eyewall | Calm sinking centre / ring of strongest winds and heaviest rain |
| Latent heat | Heat released by condensation; powers the storm |
| Wind shear | Change of wind speed or direction with height |
| Saffir-Simpson scale | Hurricane categories 1-5 by sustained wind speed |
| Supercell | Long-lived thunderstorm with a rotating updraught |
| Mesocyclone | Rotating updraught in a supercell |
| Enhanced Fujita scale | Tornado rating EF0-EF5 from damage |
| Storm surge | Rise in sea level from low pressure and onshore winds |
| Tornado watch | Conditions favour tornadoes over a region for some hours |
| Tornado warning | Rotation detected or tornado seen; minutes ahead |
| Warning fatigue | Reduced response after repeated warnings with no event |

## Distribution recall

- **Cyclones:** form about 5°-20° latitude; move west then poleward; hit Caribbean and US Gulf and Atlantic coasts, Pacific Mexico, Philippines, south China, Taiwan, Japan, Vietnam, Bay of Bengal, Madagascar, Mozambique, northern Australia. Rare in South Atlantic and south-east Pacific (cool water, wind shear).
- **Tornadoes:** USA has more than 1,000 a year (Great Plains, south-east); Bangladesh and eastern India in the pre-monsoon season.

## Formation recall

**Cyclone:** warm sea, evaporation, convergence, rising air, condensation releases latent heat, core warms, pressure falls, more inflow, Coriolis spin, eye forms. Decays over land, cool water or in strong wind shear.

| Stage | Sustained wind |
|---|---|
| Depression | Below 63 km/h |
| Tropical storm | 63-118 km/h |
| Hurricane / typhoon / cyclone | 119 km/h and above |
| Category 5 | 252 km/h and above |

**Tornado:** instability + wind shear + lift; horizontal rolling tilted upright by updraught into a mesocyclone; rear downdraught helps bring rotation to the ground.

## Examples at a glance

| Event | Main hazard | Point to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclone Nargis, Myanmar, 2 May 2008 | Storm surge | About 138,000 deaths in the Irrawaddy delta |
| Hurricane Katrina, USA, 29 August 2005 | Coastal flooding, levee failures | About 1,800 deaths; New Orleans flooded |
| Hurricane Harvey, USA, 2017 | Intense rainfall | Over 1,500 mm in places; Houston flooding |
| Moore, Oklahoma, 20 May 2013 | EF5 tornado winds | 24 deaths |
| Odisha 1999 and Cyclone Phailin 2013 | Preparedness | About 10,000 deaths in 1999; under 50 in 2013 after about a million were evacuated |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Large-scale** disturbances (cyclones) last days and affect hundreds of kilometres; **small-scale** ones (tornadoes) last minutes and affect a narrow path.
- **Track** forecasts are more reliable than **intensity** forecasts.
- **Storm surge** is a rise in sea level; **coastal flooding** is the land being inundated by surge, waves and rain together.

## Mini essay plan

*"Storm surge is the most dangerous hazard of a tropical cyclone." How far do you agree?*

1. **Define** the four large-scale hazards in the syllabus.
2. **For:** surge caused most deaths in Nargis (2008); low, densely populated deltas and funnel bays are most exposed.
3. **Against:** Harvey (2017) did most damage through rainfall; inland areas face river floods and landslides; winds destroy weak housing.
4. **Vulnerability:** preparedness changes which hazard kills (Odisha 1999 against Phailin 2013).
5. **Judgement:** surge is usually deadliest on low coasts; rainfall is often costliest inland.

## Exam traps

- Saying cyclones form at the equator (no Coriolis effect).
- Surge explained by low pressure alone; wind does most of it.
- Watch and warning confused.
- Saffir-Simpson (wind) and Enhanced Fujita (damage) confused.
- Impacts listed without primary and secondary classification.
- Preparedness praised without evidence such as death tolls compared.

## Self-test

1. Why do tropical cyclones not form within about 5° of the equator?
2. What is the role of latent heat in cyclone development?
3. Describe the eye of a tropical cyclone.
4. At what sustained wind speed does a tropical storm become a hurricane?
5. Why do cyclones weaken over land?
6. State the three main conditions for tornado formation.
7. About how much does sea level rise for each 1 hPa fall in pressure?
8. Why are funnel-shaped bays at risk from storm surge?
9. Give two hazards of a supercell other than the tornado itself.
10. Distinguish a tornado watch from a tornado warning.
11. What does the Odisha comparison show?
12. Give two reasons why people may not evacuate.

### Answers

1. The Coriolis effect is too weak to start rotation.
2. Condensation releases latent heat, warming the core so air rises faster and surface pressure falls, drawing in more air.
3. Calm, often clear, with sinking air and the lowest pressure, surrounded by the eyewall.
4. 119 km per hour.
5. They lose their supply of warm, moist air and friction slows the winds.
6. Instability, wind shear and a lifting mechanism.
7. About 1 cm.
8. Water piled up by onshore winds is concentrated into a narrowing area, raising its height.
9. Large hail and intense rain causing flash floods (also damaging straight-line winds).
10. A watch means conditions favour tornadoes over hours; a warning means rotation or a tornado has been detected, minutes ahead.
11. Evacuation and preparedness cut deaths from about 10,000 in 1999 to under 50 in 2013 for storms of similar strength.
12. Any two: warning fatigue, past survival of weaker storms, fear of looting, lack of transport, crowded or distant shelters.
