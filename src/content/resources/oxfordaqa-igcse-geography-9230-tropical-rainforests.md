This guide covers section 3.1.2.2, Tropical rainforests, in Section B (The living world) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. It has three key ideas: "Tropical rainforest ecosystems have a range of distinctive characteristics"; "Deforestation has economic and environmental impacts"; and "Tropical rainforests need to be managed to be sustainable." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). For the general ecosystem ideas that this theme builds on, see the [ecosystems study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-ecosystems/).

## What the specification lists

- The physical characteristics of a tropical rainforest.
- The interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people.
- How plants and animals adapt to the physical conditions; issues related to biodiversity.
- Changing rates of deforestation.
- **A case study of a tropical rainforest** to illustrate the causes of deforestation (subsistence and commercial farming, logging, road building, mineral extraction, energy development, settlement, population growth) and its impacts (economic development, soil erosion, loss of biodiversity, contribution to climate change).
- The value of rainforests to people and the environment.
- Sustainable management: selective logging and replanting, conservation and education, ecotourism, international agreements about tropical hardwoods, and debt reduction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biodiversity | The variety of living things in an area |
| Canopy | The layer of treetops that forms a roof over the forest |
| Emergent | A very tall tree that rises above the canopy |
| Interdependence | Parts of a system relying on each other |
| Deforestation | The permanent removal of forest |
| Leaching | The washing of nutrients down through the soil by rain |
| Sustainable | Meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs |
| Selective logging | Cutting only some mature trees, leaving the rest |
| Ecotourism | Tourism that is low-impact, benefits local people and protects the environment |

## Physical characteristics

**Location.** Tropical rainforests lie within about 10° of the equator. The main areas are the Amazon Basin in South America, the Congo Basin in Africa and South-East Asia, especially Indonesia and Malaysia.

**Climate.** It is hot and wet all year. Temperatures are about 26 to 28°C every month, with very little seasonal change. Rainfall is high, usually over 2 000 mm a year, falling in daily convectional storms. Rising air at the equator, part of the atmospheric circulation, explains the heavy rain.

**Structure.** The forest has layers.

- **Emergent layer:** the tallest trees, perhaps 40 to 60 m, with their crowns above the roof.
- **Canopy:** a dense layer at about 25 to 40 m that captures most of the sunlight and is home to most animals.
- **Understorey:** shade-loving trees and young plants.
- **Forest floor:** dark and bare, because very little light arrives.

**Soils.** They are often deep but infertile. Heat and moisture break down dead leaves very quickly, so the nutrients are taken up at once by shallow roots. They are stored in the plants, not the soil. Heavy rain leaches nutrients out of the soil that remains.

## The interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people

The parts of the rainforest depend on each other.

- Heat and rain provide ideal conditions for plants to grow all year.
- Plants recycle water. Roots take it up, leaves release it (transpiration), and this adds moisture to the air, so that more rain falls.
- Dead plants and animals decay quickly, releasing nutrients that plants absorb straight away. This is a fast nutrient cycle, with most nutrients held in the biomass.
- Trees protect soil from heavy rain with their canopy and bind it with their roots.
- Animals depend on plants for food and shelter, and plants depend on animals to pollinate flowers and spread seeds.
- People, including indigenous communities, have lived in the forest for thousands of years, using it for food, shelter and medicine, usually at a low impact.

Remove the trees and the chain breaks: soil is washed away, local rainfall can fall, and animals lose their habitat.

## Adaptations

| Feature | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Drip tips and waxy leaves | Shed heavy rain quickly, which prevents fungus growing |
| Buttress roots | Support tall trees in shallow soil |
| Shallow roots | Catch nutrients near the surface |
| Lianas and epiphytes | Climb or perch on trees to reach light |
| Very tall emergents | Reach sunlight |
| Camouflage (for example sloths and insects) | Avoid predators |
| Bright warning colours (for example poison dart frogs) | Warn predators of poison |
| Prehensile tails and strong limbs (for example monkeys) | Move through the canopy |
| Large beaks (for example toucans) | Reach fruit on thin branches |

## Biodiversity issues

Rainforests cover a small proportion of the Earth's land but hold a very large share of its species, many of which are found nowhere else. Biodiversity matters because:

- many medicines originate from rainforest plants;
- species provide food crops and genetic resources;
- a diverse system is more resilient to change;
- many people place value on the existence of species.

The threats are habitat loss through deforestation, fragmentation of habitat, hunting and the pet trade, and climate change. Once a species is lost, it cannot be replaced.

## Changing rates of deforestation

Rainforest is being cleared across the tropics. Rates change over time and between places. In the Brazilian Amazon, the area cleared each year was very high in the early 2000s (around 27 000 km² in 2004), then fell sharply to under 5 000 km² by 2012 after tighter enforcement and market pressure, and then rose again in later years (about 13 000 km² in 2021). Use this as an example of why rates are not constant and depend on government policy, prices and enforcement. Your case study may have different figures; use your own.

## Case study: the Amazon rainforest, Brazil

The Amazon basin is the largest rainforest on Earth, covering several million square kilometres, with the largest share in Brazil. Check these details against your own notes.

### Causes of deforestation

- **Commercial farming:** cattle ranching is the biggest cause in Brazil, and soya bean farms have also expanded, often for export.
- **Subsistence farming:** poor farmers clear small plots by slash and burn.
- **Logging:** valuable hardwoods such as mahogany are cut for export. Logging tracks open the forest to farmers.
- **Road building:** highways such as the Trans-Amazonian highway, begun in the 1970s, opened the interior to settlers, ranchers and loggers.
- **Mineral extraction:** mines such as the Carajás iron ore mine, and illegal gold mining, clear land and pollute rivers.
- **Energy development:** hydroelectric dams, for example Belo Monte on the Xingu River, flood forest and displace people.
- **Settlement and population growth:** government programmes encouraged people to move into the Amazon, and a growing population needs land, food and fuel.

### Impacts of deforestation

- **Economic development:** jobs, exports and income from beef, soya, timber and minerals; but short-term gains may be followed by long-term losses when the soil is exhausted.
- **Soil erosion:** without tree cover, rain washes soil into rivers, which silts them up. Cleared land loses fertility quickly because nutrients were in the trees.
- **Loss of biodiversity:** habitats are destroyed and species are lost.
- **Climate change:** trees store carbon. Burning or rotting releases carbon dioxide. Loss of trees also reduces the amount of water returned to the air.
- **People:** indigenous groups lose land, resources and culture.

## Value of tropical rainforests

- **To people:** timber, food, medicines, jobs, tourism, and homes for indigenous people.
- **To the environment:** habitats for biodiversity; carbon stores; recycling water; protecting soils; affecting regional rainfall.

## Sustainable management

| Strategy | How it works | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Selective logging and replanting | Only mature trees are cut and new ones planted, so the forest survives | Needs enforcement; heavy machines still damage the forest |
| Conservation and education | Protected areas and national parks; teaching local people about the value of the forest | Hard to police huge areas; protection costs money |
| Ecotourism | Visitors pay to see the forest; income goes to local people | Too many tourists can cause damage; money may not reach communities |
| International agreements about tropical hardwoods | Agreements and certification, such as the Forest Stewardship Council, encourage legal and sustainable timber | Illegal logging continues; voluntary schemes |
| Debt reduction | Countries reduce debt in exchange for protecting forest ("debt-for-nature swaps") | Small in scale; needs agreement from creditors |

In the Amazon, examples include protected areas and indigenous territories, the Amazon Fund (set up in 2008 with donations from other countries to support forest protection) and an agreement by major soya traders not to buy soya grown on land cleared after 2008 (the soy moratorium, first agreed in 2006).

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain the causes and impacts of deforestation in a tropical rainforest that you have studied. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "In the Amazon in Brazil, cattle ranching is the main cause because large areas are cleared for pasture to produce beef for export. Roads such as the Trans-Amazonian highway made the forest accessible, so loggers and settlers moved in. Mining and dams also clear forest. The economic benefits include jobs and export earnings, but the soil is poor because nutrients are held in the trees, so farms are abandoned after a few years. Without tree cover, heavy rain erodes the soil, which washes into rivers. Biodiversity is lost as habitats are destroyed. Burning and clearing trees releases carbon dioxide, adding to climate change."

This answer names the place, gives causes with specific detail, and links impacts to the physical system.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing about rainforests in general when a case study is asked for.** Name the place and give specific detail.
- **Saying that rainforest soil is very fertile.** It is nutrient-poor; the fertility is in the plants.
- **Listing causes without saying how they lead to clearance.** Show the mechanism.
- **Confusing selective logging with clear-felling.**
- **Ignoring trade-offs.** Management strategies have limits as well as benefits.
- **Quoting figures without a date or source.** Use dates and approximate values.
