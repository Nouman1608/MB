These questions cover **section 3.2.2.4 Inflation and price stability** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: what inflation is, how it is measured with the CPI, its causes and consequences, and policies to manage it. There are no tiers. All figures are invented apart from the UK inflation target. For explanations, see the [inflation and price stability study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-inflation-and-price-stability-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [inflation and price stability revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-inflation-and-price-stability-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** The rate of inflation falls from 4% to 1.5%. Which **one** of the following statements is correct? **[1]**

A: The general price level has fallen

B: The general price level is rising more slowly

C: The general price level is unchanged

D: The economy is experiencing deflation

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to cause demand-pull inflation? **[1]**

A: A rise in world oil prices

B: A cut in the Bank Rate

C: A rise in VAT

D: A rise in the National Minimum Wage

**3.** State the inflation target the UK government has set for the Bank of England. **[1]**

**4.** An invented country's CPI was 140.0 in Year 1, 145.6 in Year 2 and 148.5 in Year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of inflation between Year 1 and Year 2. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the rate of inflation between Year 2 and Year 3, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(c)** State what happened to the general price level between Year 2 and Year 3. **[1]**

**5.** A price index has three categories: housing (weight 0.40, index 108), food (weight 0.35, index 102) and leisure (weight 0.25, index 96). Calculate the weighted price index. **[2]**

**6.** A rail season ticket costs £840 after a year in which its price rose by 5%. Calculate its price a year earlier. **[2]**

**7.** A saver holds £2,000 in an account paying 2% interest a year. Inflation is 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the amount in the account after one year. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the saver is worse off despite having more money. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain **two** causes of cost-push inflation. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a rise in the Bank Rate could reduce demand-pull inflation. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why UK exporters may be harmed if UK inflation is higher than inflation in other countries. **[3]**

**11.** Inflation in an invented country rises to 8% and stays there for several years. Mrs Ahmed is retired and lives on a fixed private pension. Mr Clarke has a large mortgage on a fixed interest rate.

Analyse how the inflation is likely to affect Mrs Ahmed and Mr Clarke. **[6]**

**12.** UK inflation is 7%, mainly because world energy prices have risen sharply. Some people argue that the Bank of England should raise the Bank Rate quickly.

Evaluate whether raising the Bank Rate is the best way to bring inflation back to target. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A lower but positive rate means prices are still rising. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Cheaper borrowing raises spending; the other options raise firms' costs (cost-push). **[1]**

**3.** 2% inflation, measured by the CPI [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** (145.6 − 140.0) ÷ 140.0 × 100 [1] = **4.0%** [1].

**(b)** (148.5 − 145.6) ÷ 145.6 × 100 [1] = **2.0%** (1.99%) [1].

**(c)** It continued to rise, but more slowly than before [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Always divide by the earlier year's index.

**5.** (0.40 × 108) + (0.35 × 102) + (0.25 × 96) = 43.2 + 35.7 + 24.0 [1] = **102.9** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** 840 ÷ 1.05 [1] = **£800** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Taking 5% off £840 gives £798, which is wrong, because the 5% was calculated on the earlier price.

**7. (a)** 2,000 × 1.02 = **£2,040** [1].

**(b)** Prices have risen by 6%, faster than the 2% interest [1], so the £2,040 buys less than the £2,000 did a year earlier; the real value of the savings has fallen (to about £1,925 in last year's prices) [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Any two developed: higher wages not matched by higher productivity [1] raise firms' labour costs, which are passed on in prices [1]; dearer raw materials or energy [1] increase production costs across many industries, so firms raise prices to protect profit [1]; a fall in the exchange rate [1] makes imported components dearer, raising costs and prices [1]; a rise in indirect taxes [1] adds to the price of goods [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A higher Bank Rate makes loans and mortgages more expensive [1], so households and firms borrow and spend less [1]. Saving becomes more rewarding, so consumers save more of their income [1]. Total demand grows more slowly, so firms face less pressure to raise prices and inflation falls [1]. **[4]**

**10.** UK goods become relatively more expensive than foreign rivals' goods [1]. Buyers abroad switch to cheaper alternatives [1], so UK exporters' sales and profits fall, which may cost jobs [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Mrs Ahmed:* her pension stays the same in pounds while prices rise 8% a year [1], so its purchasing power falls each year [1]; over several years she can afford noticeably fewer goods and services, so her living standards fall [1].

*Mr Clarke:* his monthly repayments are fixed in pounds [1], but prices and probably incomes rise, so the real value of his debt and repayments falls [1]; he gains, at least until his fixed rate ends, when lenders may charge a higher interest rate [1]. **[6]**

**12.** *For a higher Bank Rate:*

- It reduces spending, which lowers the pressure on firms to raise prices [1].
- It may strengthen the pound, making imported energy slightly cheaper in pounds [1].
- It signals that the Bank is serious, which may stop workers and firms expecting high inflation and so limit a wage-price spiral [1].

*Against:*

- The main cause is cost-push: a higher Bank Rate cannot lower world energy prices [1].
- Cutting demand when households already face high energy bills may cause a recession and rising unemployment [1] [1].
- Interest-rate changes take time to affect spending, and energy prices may fall back on their own [1].

*Judgement:* a moderate rise is justified to stop high inflation becoming built into wages and prices, but rapid large rises are not the best approach because they tackle demand rather than the cost-push cause [1]; the answer depends on whether pay growth is also rising strongly [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Name the cause of the inflation in the case before judging the policy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering that prices fall when the inflation rate falls.
- Dividing by the later index number.
- Finding an earlier price by subtracting a percentage from the new price.
- Saying savers lose without comparing the interest rate with inflation.
- Writing about "people" in general when the question names particular groups.
