## Definitions

- **Electric potential energy, Ep:** the work done by an external agent to assemble a system of charges from infinite separation.
- **Electric potential, Ve:** the work done per unit charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to the point. Unit: volt, V = J C⁻¹.
- **Scalar:** Ve has size and sign only, no direction. Zero potential is defined at infinity.
- **Potential gradient:** the rate of change of Ve with distance. Field strength is minus this gradient.
- **Equipotential surface:** a surface on which Ve is the same at every point.
- **Electronvolt, eV:** the work done on charge e through 1 V. 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

## Formulas

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ep of two point charges | Ep = k q₁q₂ / r | Keep signs; unlike charges give negative Ep |
| Potential of a point charge | Ve = kQ / r | Sign of Q kept; also outside a spherical charge |
| Field from potential | E = –ΔVe / Δr | Field points towards falling Ve |
| Uniform field | E = V / d | Constant gradient between plates |
| Work done | W = qΔVe | Joules; divide by e for eV |
| Conducting sphere surface | Ve = kQ / R | Inside: same constant value |

## Method boxes

**Ep of a group of charges**

1. List every pair once. Three charges have three pairs, four charges have six.
2. Work out k q q / r for each pair, with signs.
3. Add them. The result does not depend on the order of assembly.

**Potential at a point from several charges**

1. Find the distance from the point to each charge.
2. Work out kQ/r for each, with the sign of Q.
3. Add the numbers. No vectors, no angles.

**Finding where Ve = 0 between two opposite charges**

1. Let x be the distance from the first charge and (d – x) the distance from the second.
2. Set kQ₁/x + kQ₂/(d – x) = 0 with the signs included.
3. Solve for x. Look for a second solution outside the pair, nearer the smaller charge.

**Speed after acceleration through a potential difference**

1. Kinetic energy gained = qΔV (convert to joules).
2. Set this equal to ½mv² and solve.
3. If the answer is needed in eV, the energy is the charge in units of e multiplied by the voltage, so an electron through 200 V gains 200 eV and a doubly charged ion gains 400 eV.

## Small worked reminders

**Reminder 1.** A +1.5 nC charge and a +3.0 nC charge are 0.090 m apart. Ep = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.090 = 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ J. Positive, since like charges repel.

**Reminder 2.** Ve at 0.25 m from –2.0 nC: 8.99 × 10⁹ × (–2.0 × 10⁻⁹) / 0.25 = –72 V.

**Reminder 3.** A uniform field region has Ve falling from 150 V to 90 V over 0.020 m. E = 60 / 0.020 = 3.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹, directed towards the lower potential.

**Reminder 4.** A proton moves from a point at 40 V to a point at 240 V. ΔVe = 200 V, so the agent does 200 eV = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ J of work on it. If it were released from the higher point, it would gain 200 eV of kinetic energy.

**Reminder 5.** Three +1.0 nC charges lie in a line with 0.20 m between neighbours. Pairs: 0.20 m, 0.20 m and 0.40 m. Ep = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.0 × 10⁻⁹)² × (1/0.20 + 1/0.20 + 1/0.40) = 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ J.

**Reminder 6.** A conducting sphere of radius 0.050 m carries +2.0 nC. Surface Ve = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.050 = 3.6 × 10² V. At 0.15 m from the centre, Ve = 360 / 3 = 1.2 × 10² V. At 0.030 m from the centre (inside), Ve is still 3.6 × 10² V and E = 0.

## Equipotentials: what to recognise

| Source | Equipotentials | Field |
|---|---|---|
| Single point charge | Concentric circles (spheres), further apart as r grows | Radial, perpendicular to the circles |
| Two equal and opposite charges | Ve = 0 on the line (plane) halfway between; closed loops around each charge | Lines run from + to – |
| Four charges | Small circles near each charge; use symmetry and the net charge far away | Zero at the centre for symmetric arrangements |
| Charged conducting sphere, solid or hollow, outside | Concentric spheres as for a point charge at the centre | As for a point charge |
| Charged conducting sphere, solid or hollow, inside | One equipotential, equal to the surface value | Zero |
| Parallel plates | Equally spaced planes parallel to the plates, bending outward at the edges | Uniform in the middle |

Rules to state in answers:

- No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential, because ΔVe = 0.
- Field lines cross equipotentials at 90°.
- Close equipotentials (at equal steps in V) mean a strong field.
- Equipotentials never cross.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Ep and Ve:** Ep belongs to a system and is in joules. Ve belongs to a point and is in joules per coulomb. Ep = qVe for a charge q placed at a point of potential Ve.
- **Potential and field:** Ve is a scalar and E is a vector. E is the gradient of Ve, not its value.
- **1/r and 1/r²:** Ve and Ep fall as 1/r. E falls as 1/r².
- **Zero potential and zero field:** neither implies the other. Midway between two equal opposite charges Ve = 0 but E is not 0. Inside a charged conducting sphere E = 0 but Ve is not 0.
- **Joule and electronvolt:** both measure energy. Convert before using mass and speed.
- **Work by the agent and work by the field:** these have opposite signs. The agent raises Ep when it moves a charge against the field; the field lowers Ep when it accelerates a free charge.

## Quick self-test

1. Find Ep for charges of +1.0 μC and +2.0 μC separated by 0.50 m.
2. Find Ve at 0.25 m from a point charge of +3.0 nC.
3. Ve falls uniformly from 300 V to 180 V over 0.040 m. Find the field strength.
4. How much work is needed to move +4.0 μC through a potential difference of 75 V?
5. An electron is accelerated through 1000 V. State its energy gain in J and in eV.
6. State the field and potential inside a charged hollow conducting sphere of surface potential 600 V.
7. At what distance from a +4.0 nC point charge is the potential 90 V?
8. A sphere has a surface potential of 600 V and radius R. What is the potential at 2R from its centre?
9. Explain why Ep is negative for a proton and an electron.
10. A charge is moved along an equipotential surface. State the work done and why.

### Answers

1. Ep = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ / 0.50 = **3.6 × 10⁻² J**.
2. Ve = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.25 = **1.1 × 10² V** (108 V).
3. E = 120 / 0.040 = **3.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹**, towards the 180 V end.
4. W = qΔV = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 75 = **3.0 × 10⁻⁴ J**.
5. Energy = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1000 = **1.60 × 10⁻¹⁶ J = 1000 eV**.
6. The field is **zero** and the potential is **600 V** everywhere inside.
7. r = kQ/Ve = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 90 = **0.40 m**.
8. Ve = kQ/(2R) = half the surface value = **300 V**.
9. They attract, so work must be done to separate them to infinity; the system has less energy than at infinite separation. Mathematically q₁q₂ is negative.
10. **Zero**, because ΔVe = 0 and W = qΔVe.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the minus sign of a negative charge in Ep or Ve, so an attractive pair appears to store positive energy.
- Using the distance in centimetres or nanometres without converting to metres.
- Forgetting that the second charge in a pair is not counted twice: each pair is counted once.
- Adding potentials with vector diagrams, which wastes time and invites errors.
- Giving E = V/d for a point charge or any non-uniform field.
- Writing that zero field means zero potential inside a conducting sphere.
- Drawing equipotentials that cross each other, or field lines that meet them at a slant.
- Mixing eV with J in kinetic energy equations.
- Leaving out the direction of E, which is towards falling Ve.
- Treating Ve inside a conducting sphere as kQ/r with a small r. The interior value stays at the surface value kQ/R.
- Writing W = qΔVe with the wrong sign convention and not saying who does the work. State whether it is the external agent or the field.
- Giving a speed without checking that energy was converted to joules first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is electric potential examined at IB DP Physics SL?**
No. This is the additional higher level part of sub-topic D.2, so all of it is HL only.

**Does zero electric potential mean zero field?**
No, neither implies the other. Midway between two equal and opposite charges Ve = 0 but E is not zero, and inside a charged conducting sphere E = 0 but Ve is not zero.

**How do potential and field strength change with distance from a point charge?**
Ve and Ep fall as 1/r, while the field strength E falls as 1/r².

**Can E = V/d be used for a point charge?**
No. E = V/d applies only to a uniform field, such as between parallel plates. For a point charge or any non-uniform field, use E = −ΔVe/Δr.
