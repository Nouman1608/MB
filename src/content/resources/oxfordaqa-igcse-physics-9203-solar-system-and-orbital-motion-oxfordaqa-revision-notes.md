These notes condense section 3.8.2 (Solar system and orbital motion) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The equation for centripetal force is not required. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [solar system and orbits study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-solar-system-and-orbital-motion-oxfordaqa/), then test yourself with the [solar system and orbits practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-solar-system-and-orbital-motion-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solar system | The Sun, the eight planets, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, and the moons orbiting several planets |
| Planet | A large body orbiting the Sun |
| Moon | A natural satellite of a planet |
| Satellite | Any body that orbits a larger body; natural (a moon) or artificial |
| Galaxy | A collection of thousands of millions of stars; ours is the Milky Way |
| Geostationary orbit | An orbit above the equator with a period of 24 hours, so the satellite stays above one point |
| Low polar orbit | A low orbit passing over both poles, with a short period |
| Centripetal force | The resultant force towards the centre that keeps a body moving in a circle; for orbits it is gravity |
| Velocity | Speed in a given direction |

## Sizes and motions to recall

- **Sun:** a medium-sized star, about 109 times Earth's diameter; the planets orbit it.
- **Planets:** eight; the four inner ones small and rocky, the four outer ones much larger. Jupiter is about 11 times Earth's diameter.
- **Moons:** smaller than the planet they orbit; they orbit the planet and travel round the Sun with it.
- **Dwarf planets:** smaller than planets (Pluto is about a fifth of Earth's diameter); they orbit the Sun.
- **Asteroids:** small rocky bodies, mostly between Mars and Jupiter, orbiting the Sun.
- **Comets:** small bodies of ice and dust on long, stretched orbits round the Sun; they come close to the Sun only briefly.

**Answer pattern:** one size point and one motion point for each object, with what it orbits.

## The universe in one nested list

Moon → planet → star (our Sun) → galaxy (the Milky Way, thousands of millions of stars) → universe (thousands of millions of galaxies).

## Orbit reasoning chain (3.8.2 d, e)

1. Gravity provides the centripetal force.
2. It acts towards the centre, at right angles to the motion.
3. It changes the direction of motion but not the speed.
4. Changing direction means changing velocity.
5. Changing velocity means acceleration towards the centre, caused by the unbalanced force.

Without gravity the body would move off in a straight line at constant speed.

## Distance, force and speed (3.8.2 f, g)

| Further from the centre | Closer to the centre |
|---|---|
| Weaker gravitational force | Stronger gravitational force |
| Lower orbital speed | Higher orbital speed |
| Longer path and longer period | Shorter path and shorter period |

- At each radius there is one speed for a stable orbit.
- A change in orbital speed results in a change in orbital radius.
- Satellite placing: choose the radius needed, then give the speed for that radius.

## Calculation toolkit

Speed = distance ÷ time, with distance for one orbit = 2πr.

1. Convert the period to seconds (days × 86 400; hours × 3600).
2. Use r from the centre of the body being orbited. If a height above the surface is given, add the body's radius.
3. Divide 2πr by the period.

**Worked reminder.** A satellite orbits 600 km above the Earth's surface; Earth's radius is 6370 km. Radius = 6970 km = 6.97 × 10⁶ m. If the period is 5790 s, speed = 2π × 6.97 × 10⁶ ÷ 5790 = 7.56 × 10³ m/s.

**Worked reminder.** A geostationary satellite at 4.22 × 10⁷ m from Earth's centre: 2π × 4.22 × 10⁷ ÷ 86 400 = 3.07 × 10³ m/s.

## Comparing the two named satellite orbits

| | Geostationary | Low polar |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Above the equator, high | Over the poles, low |
| Period | 24 hours | Short, around an hour and a half |
| Speed | Lower | Higher |
| Why it suits its use | Stays over one point, so a fixed dish can point at it | Earth turns beneath it, so it passes over the whole surface in time |

## Exam traps

- "Constant speed so no acceleration." Wrong: the velocity changes, so it accelerates.
- "Gravity pushes the satellite forward." Gravity acts towards the centre only.
- "A geostationary satellite is not moving." It orbits once in 24 hours.
- "Further planets move faster because they have further to go." The opposite: they move more slowly.
- Using height instead of radius in 2πr.
- Leaving the period in hours or days when speed is wanted in m/s.
- Calling the Milky Way the universe, or the solar system a galaxy.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five kinds of natural body in the solar system listed in the specification, besides the Sun.
2. What is a moon?
3. Give one size difference and one motion difference between a comet and a planet.
4. How many stars, roughly, are in the Milky Way?
5. What provides the centripetal force for the Moon's orbit round the Earth?
6. In which direction does that force act?
7. Explain why a satellite in a circular orbit is accelerating even though its speed is constant.
8. What happens to orbital speed as orbital radius increases? Why?
9. A satellite's speed is reduced. What happens to its orbit?
10. A planet orbits at 1.08 × 10¹¹ m with a period of 1.94 × 10⁷ s. Calculate its orbital speed.
11. Why must a geostationary satellite be at one particular height?
12. Why is a low polar orbit useful for mapping the whole Earth?

### Answers

1. Planets, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and moons.
2. A natural satellite of a planet.
3. Size: a comet is much smaller than a planet. Motion: a comet follows a long, stretched orbit, coming close to the Sun only briefly, whereas a planet's orbit is near-circular.
4. Thousands of millions.
5. The gravitational force of the Earth on the Moon.
6. Towards the centre of the orbit (towards the Earth).
7. Gravity changes its direction continuously, so its velocity changes, and a change in velocity is an acceleration.
8. It decreases, because the gravitational force is weaker at greater separation, so a lower speed is needed to stay in orbit.
9. Its orbital radius changes; at the new speed it cannot stay in the same orbit.
10. 2π × 1.08 × 10¹¹ = 6.79 × 10¹¹ m; ÷ 1.94 × 10⁷ = **3.50 × 10⁴ m/s** (35.0 km/s).
11. It must have a 24-hour period; only one radius gives that period, because each radius has one stable orbital speed.
12. It passes over both poles, and the Earth turns beneath it, so each orbit covers a different strip until the whole surface is seen.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a moon always smaller than a dwarf planet?**
No. A moon is smaller than the planet it orbits, but some moons are larger than dwarf planets. What makes it a moon is that it orbits a planet.

**What changes and what stays constant in a circular orbit?**
The speed stays constant. The direction of motion, and therefore the velocity, changes all the time.
