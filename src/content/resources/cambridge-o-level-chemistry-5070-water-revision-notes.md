These notes condense section 10.1, Water, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). All seven outcomes are required for every candidate. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [water study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-water/); then try the [water practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-water-practice/).

## The seven outcomes in one line each

1. **Tests for water**: anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns white to blue; anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride turns blue to pink.
2. **Purity**: pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C; impurities change these values.
3. **Distilled water**: used in practical work because it contains fewer chemical impurities than tap water.
4. **Natural water may contain**: dissolved oxygen, metal compounds, plastics, sewage, harmful microbes, nitrates from fertilisers, phosphates from fertilisers and detergents.
5. **Beneficial**: dissolved oxygen for aquatic life; some metal compounds provide essential minerals.
6. **Potentially harmful**: some metal compounds are toxic; some plastics harm aquatic life; sewage contains disease-causing microbes; nitrates and phosphates cause deoxygenation and damage aquatic life.
7. **Treatment**: sedimentation and filtration remove solids; carbon removes tastes and odours; chlorination kills microbes.

## Presence versus purity

| | Test for presence of water | Test for purity of water |
|---|---|---|
| Method | add an anhydrous reagent | measure melting point or boiling point |
| Result for water | colour change | 0 °C and 100 °C exactly |
| Result for salt water | same colour change | freezes below 0 °C, boils above 100 °C |
| Tells you | water is there | whether anything else is there |

Memory hook: **"Copper goes blue, cobalt goes pink."** The copper compound starts white; the cobalt compound starts blue.

## Effect of dissolved impurities

| Property | Pure water | Water with dissolved impurity |
|---|---|---|
| Melting / freezing point | 0 °C, sharp | below 0 °C |
| Boiling point | 100 °C, sharp | above 100 °C |
| Behaviour on heating | boils at one temperature | may boil over a range |

Values apply at atmospheric pressure.

## Tap water compared with distilled water

- Tap water is treated to be safe to drink, not to be pure.
- It still contains dissolved substances, such as metal compounds from rocks and chlorine compounds from treatment.
- These could react with reagents, give false positive results in ion tests, or contaminate crystals.
- Distilled water has been boiled and condensed, leaving most dissolved impurities behind.

## Natural water: sorting the substances

| Substance | Source | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | air, aquatic plants | beneficial for aquatic life |
| Metal compounds | rocks and soil | some essential minerals; some toxic |
| Plastics | waste | some harm aquatic life |
| Sewage | human and animal waste | contains harmful microbes |
| Harmful microbes | sewage | cause disease |
| Nitrates | fertilisers | deoxygenation, damage to aquatic life |
| Phosphates | fertilisers, detergents | deoxygenation, damage to aquatic life |

Details of the eutrophication process are not required by the syllabus.

## Water treatment in order

```
river or reservoir
   │
sedimentation ── large insoluble solids settle out
   │
filtration (sand and gravel) ── smaller insoluble particles removed
   │
carbon ── tastes and odours removed
   │
chlorination ── microbes killed
   │
domestic supply
```

Filtration cannot remove dissolved substances. Chlorine kills microbes but does not remove them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Anhydrous | containing no water of crystallisation |
| Hydrated | containing water of crystallisation, such as blue copper(II) sulfate |
| Pure substance | a single substance with nothing mixed with it; it has a sharp melting point and boiling point |
| Distilled water | water purified by boiling and condensing |
| Sedimentation | settling of insoluble particles to the bottom of still water |
| Filtration | separating insoluble solids from a liquid by passing it through a filter |
| Chlorination | adding chlorine to water to kill microbes |
| Deoxygenation | loss of dissolved oxygen from water, damaging aquatic life |
| Microbes | microscopic organisms; harmful ones cause disease |

## Exam traps

- A colour change with anhydrous copper(II) sulfate does **not** show the water is pure.
- Do not say "turns blue" for cobalt(II) chloride: it starts blue and turns **pink**.
- "Dirty" is not an explanation for avoiding tap water. Say "contains dissolved chemical impurities that could interfere with the results".
- Impurities **lower** the melting point and **raise** the boiling point.
- Filtration removes insoluble solids only.
- Carbon does not kill microbes; chlorine does not remove tastes and odours.
- "Metal compounds" cannot be your answer for both a beneficial and a harmful substance in the same question.
- Dissolved oxygen is beneficial, not a pollutant.

## Self-test

1. Give the colour change when water is added to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate.
2. Give the colour change when water is added to anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride.
3. A colourless liquid turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue. A student says this proves it is pure water. Why is the student wrong?
4. State the melting point and boiling point of pure water at atmospheric pressure.
5. A water sample boils at 101.5 °C. What does this show?
6. Explain why distilled water is used to make up solutions in practical chemistry.
7. Name **two** substances in river water that come from fertilisers.
8. State **one** beneficial substance found in natural water and why it is beneficial.
9. Why is sewage in drinking water harmful?
10. Which stage of water treatment removes tastes and odours?
11. Why does filtration not remove dissolved metal compounds?
12. State the purpose of chlorination.

## Answers

1. White to blue.
2. Blue to pink.
3. The test shows only that water is present. The liquid could be a solution containing dissolved substances; purity must be tested by measuring the melting point or boiling point.
4. Melting point 0 °C; boiling point 100 °C.
5. The water is impure: a dissolved substance has raised its boiling point above 100 °C.
6. Tap water contains dissolved chemical impurities which could react with or interfere with the substances being used; distilled water contains fewer impurities.
7. Nitrates and phosphates.
8. Dissolved oxygen, needed by aquatic life (or some metal compounds, which provide essential minerals).
9. It contains harmful microbes which cause disease.
10. Treatment with carbon.
11. Dissolved substances are spread through the water as particles small enough to pass through the filter; filtration removes only insoluble solids.
12. To kill harmful microbes.
