Condensed notes on section **3.6.2 Analysing human resource performance** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and the Fairlight Stores worked example are in the [HR performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance/); exam-style questions are in the [HR performance practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance-practice/).

## Specification content

- Calculating and interpreting HR data: labour turnover; labour productivity; employee costs as a percentage of turnover; labour cost per unit.
- The use of data for HR decision making and planning.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover | Percentage of the workforce leaving in a period |
| Labour retention | Proportion of staff who stay |
| Labour productivity | Output per employee |
| Turnover (revenue) | Sales income |
| Employee costs | Wages, salaries, employer's national insurance, pensions, benefits |
| Labour cost per unit | Total labour cost divided by output |
| Exit interview | Interview with a leaver to find their reasons |

## Formulas

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover (%) | leavers in period ÷ average number of employees × 100 |
| Labour productivity | total output ÷ number of employees |
| Employee costs as % of turnover | employee costs ÷ turnover × 100 |
| Labour cost per unit | total labour cost ÷ total output |
| Cost of turnover | leavers × cost of replacing one employee |

State units: %, units per employee, £ per unit.

## Worked example to remember

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Average employees | 200 | 208 |
| Leavers | 46 | 31 |
| Output | 480,000 | 520,000 |
| Employee costs | £4.8m | £5.2m |
| Turnover | £24m | £28.6m |

- Labour turnover: 46 ÷ 200 = **23%**; 31 ÷ 208 = **14.9%**.
- Cost of replacing at £3,500 each: £161,000 and £108,500; a saving of **£52,500**.
- Productivity: 480,000 ÷ 200 = **2,400**; 520,000 ÷ 208 = **2,500** (up 4.2%).
- Employee costs % of turnover: **20%** and **18.2%**.
- Labour cost per unit: **£10.00** in both years.
- Reading: turnover and productivity improved, but pay per employee rose 4.2% too (£24,000 to £25,000), so labour cost per unit did not fall. Employee costs fell as a share of turnover because revenue grew faster than costs (19% against 8.3%).

## Interpreting each measure

| Measure | High or rising may mean | Low or falling may mean |
|---|---|---|
| Labour turnover | Low pay, poor management, weak engagement, rivals paying more; costly | Stable staff, good morale; but may mean stagnation if too low |
| Labour productivity | Skilled, motivated staff; good technology | Low morale, poor training, old equipment, inexperienced staff |
| Employee costs % of turnover | Over-staffing, high wages, low prices, falling sales | Efficiency, higher prices, strong sales |
| Labour cost per unit | Wages rising faster than productivity, overtime | Productivity rising faster than pay |

Some turnover is healthy: new ideas and promotion opportunities. What is "high" depends on the industry (retail and hospitality are higher).

## Using the data

| Question | Possible decision |
|---|---|
| Is the workforce stable? | Review pay, engagement, management, induction; use exit interviews |
| Right number of staff? | Recruit, train, redeploy or automate |
| Can we afford a pay rise? | Link to productivity gains |
| Is training working? | Compare productivity before and after |
| Which unit is struggling? | Compare turnover and productivity between sites |
| Where to locate? | Compare labour cost per unit |

## Limits of HR data

Averages hide differences between groups; turnover does not separate good from bad leavers; productivity ignores quality; employee-cost ratio is affected by prices and sales; data shows what, not why; small samples mislead; past data may not predict the future.

## Exam traps

- Labour turnover uses the **average** number of employees.
- Employee costs % of turnover and labour cost per unit are different measures.
- Not all turnover is bad.
- Pay rises can cancel productivity gains.
- Always interpret the figure.

## Self-test

1. A business has an average of 80 employees and 12 leave. Calculate labour turnover.
2. Replacing each leaver costs £2,500. Calculate the cost of turnover.
3. Output is 96,000 units with 80 employees. Calculate labour productivity.
4. Employee costs are £1.2 million and turnover is £6 million. Calculate employee costs as a percentage of turnover.
5. Labour costs are £1.2 million for 96,000 units. Calculate labour cost per unit.
6. Productivity rises 10% and pay per employee rises 10%. What happens to labour cost per unit?
7. Give two possible causes of high labour turnover.
8. Give one reason why some turnover can be good.
9. Give two limitations of HR data.
10. Employee costs as a percentage of turnover rises from 25% to 30%. Give two possible explanations.
11. Give one way a business could use labour turnover data.
12. Why might a business collect exit interview information as well as turnover data?

### Answers

1. 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = **15%**.
2. 12 × £2,500 = **£30,000**.
3. 96,000 ÷ 80 = **1,200 units per employee**.
4. 1.2 ÷ 6 × 100 = **20%**.
5. £1,200,000 ÷ 96,000 = **£12.50**.
6. It stays the same, because output per employee and cost per employee both rise by 10%.
7. Any two: low pay; poor management; limited promotion; stress; poor induction; competitors paying more.
8. It brings in new ideas and skills and creates promotion opportunities.
9. Any two: averages hide differences; ignores quality; shows what but not why; small samples mislead; past data may not predict the future.
10. Any two: over-staffing; wage rises; lower prices; falling sales.
11. Any one: compare departments; spot trends; justify pay or engagement spending; plan recruitment.
12. Turnover data shows how many leave, not why; exit interviews give the reasons.
