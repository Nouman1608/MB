These questions cover part of section 7.6.4 of the Cambridge International AS and A Level Economics 9708 syllabus (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): contestable markets, price and non-price competition, collusion and the Prisoner's Dilemma, and efficiency in oligopoly. Topics 1 to 6 are AS Level content and topics 7 to 11 are A Level content; this unit is A Level only. All figures are invented. Read the [study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-oligopoly-contestable-markets-price-and-non-price/) and [revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-oligopoly-contestable-markets-price-and-non-price-revision-notes/) first, and see the [Cambridge Economics hub](/boards/cambridge/a-level/economics/) and [checklist](/checklists/cambridge/a-level/economics/).

## Questions

**1.** State **three** features of a contestable market. **[3]**

**2.** Distinguish between price competition and non-price competition, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Two firms, A and B, each choose to Restrict or Expand output. Pay-offs are annual profit in millions (A, B).

| | B: Restrict | B: Expand |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Restrict** | 18, 18 | 8, 24 |
| **A: Expand** | 24, 8 | 12, 12 |

**(a)** Show that Expand is A's dominant strategy. **[2]**
**(b)** State the likely outcome and compare joint profit with the cooperative outcome. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the firms do not both Restrict. **[2]**

**4.** A firm sells 500 units at 80 with a unit cost of 40. It cuts price by 10% to 72.

**(a)** Calculate its profit before the cut. **[1]**
**(b)** If rivals do not respond, sales rise to 680. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and the new profit. **[2]**
**(c)** If rivals match and the firm's sales rise only to 530, calculate the profit and comment. **[2]**

**5.** A single bus operator faces demand P = 18 − 0.5Q, marginal cost 6 and a recoverable fixed cost of 40 per day. The market is contestable.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising price and the profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the highest price that deters hit-and-run entry. **[3]**

**6.** An oligopoly faces market demand P = 90 − Q (Q in thousands) and constant marginal cost 30.

**(a)** State the output that would be allocatively efficient. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the price and output if the firms act together to maximise joint profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the deadweight loss. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the deadweight loss if tacit collusion holds price at 50. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why collusion between oligopolists tends to break down. **[4]**

**8.** Use the pay-off matrix in question 3. Suppose firm A cheats in year one by choosing Expand while B Restricts, and thereafter both choose Expand in every year. Without discounting:

**(a)** Compare A's total profit over two years with the profit from both Restricting. **[3]**
**(b)** Repeat for three years and state what this suggests about collusion. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Define X-inefficiency. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why an oligopolist protected by high barriers to entry may be X-inefficient. **[4]**

**10.** Discuss whether a market with only two firms must be uncompetitive. **[8]**

**11.** Assess whether non-price competition benefits consumers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three of: low or no barriers to entry and exit [1]; no or low sunk costs [1]; entrants can use technology as good as the incumbents' [1]; good information for entrants; hit-and-run entry possible. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A list of features with no wording about entry or exit tends to earn little; name the feature precisely.

**2.** Price competition is rivalry through price [1], for example a discount or price war [1]. Non-price competition is rivalry through factors other than price [1], for example advertising or after-sales service [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The distinction needs both definitions; two examples with no contrast do not show the difference.

**3. (a)** If B Restricts, A earns 18 by Restricting and 24 by Expanding, so Expand is better [1]. If B Expands, A earns 8 or 12, so Expand is better again [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Both Expand and earn (12, 12), joint profit 24 [1], against 36 if both Restricted, a loss of 12 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Each firm gains by Expanding whatever the rival does [1], so a promise to Restrict is not credible because each is tempted to cheat [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Checking both of the rival's options is what makes a strategy dominant; one comparison is not enough.

**4. (a)** (80 − 40) × 500 = **20,000** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** Sales rise by 36%, price falls by 10%, so elasticity = 36 ÷ −10 = **−3.6** [1]. Profit = (72 − 40) × 680 = **21,760** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Profit = 32 × 530 = **16,960** [1], which is 3,040 lower than before. A matched cut lowers profit, so price cuts are risky in oligopoly [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A comment on the calculation tends to gain credit only if it uses the figures, such as the fall of 3,040.

**5. (a)** MR = 18 − Q = 6, so Q = 12 [1], P = 18 − 6 = **12** [1]. Profit = 12 × 12 − 6 × 12 − 40 = **32** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Set P = AC: (18 − 0.5Q)Q = 6Q + 40 [1], so Q² − 24Q + 80 = 0, giving Q = 20 or 4. Take Q = 20 [1]. P = 18 − 10 = **8** [1], and average cost = 6 + 40/20 = 8 **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show why the larger root is used; a bare price with no check that profit is zero is easy to get wrong.

**6. (a)** P = MC: 90 − Q = 30, so **Q = 60** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** MR = 90 − 2Q = 30, so Q = 30 [1] and P = **60** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** DWL = ½ × (60 − 30) × (60 − 30) = **450** [1] on the correct triangle [1] **[2]**
**(d)** At P = 50, Q = 40, DWL = ½ × 20 × 20 = **200** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The triangle is drawn between the actual output and the efficient output, so the base is 60 − 30 and not 60.

**7.** Each firm earns more by cheating on the agreed price or output [1] while others keep to it [1]. Breakdown is more likely with many firms or differentiated products, which make monitoring hard [1], and with falling demand or new entry [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the incentive to cheat to the conditions that make it worse tends to be stronger than listing conditions.

**8. (a)** Both Restrict: 18 × 2 = 36 [1]. Cheat: 24 + 12 = 36 [1]. They are equal, so A gains nothing by cheating over two years [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Three years: Restrict 54, cheat 24 + 12 + 12 = 48 [1]. Collusion pays when the horizon is long enough that punishment outweighs the one-off gain [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the assumption of no discounting and permanent punishment; the conclusion depends on it.

**9. (a)** Costs above the lowest possible level [1] because of weak cost control or lack of pressure to be efficient [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** High barriers mean little threat of entry [1]. Without pressure on profit, managers may tolerate waste, such as high pay or poor contracts [1]. Supernormal profit can mask higher costs [1], so the firm is above the lowest average cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain the mechanism that links weak competition to higher costs.

**10.** Reasons it can be competitive: if the market is contestable, entry threat forces price near average cost [1]; two firms may compete on price [1]; consumers may switch easily [1]. Reasons it may not be: high sunk costs deter entrants [1]; two firms can collude tacitly [1]; products may be differentiated, giving market power [1]. Judgement: it depends on barriers and sunk costs [1], and on whether collusion is stable or checked by competition law [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers tend to reach a reasoned conclusion with a stated condition, not just two lists.

**11.** For: better quality and choice [1]; innovation can become dynamic efficiency [1]; avoids damaging price wars [1]. Against: advertising costs raise prices [1]; spending may be matched and wasted, as in the Prisoner's Dilemma [1]; brand loyalty can act as a barrier to entry [1]. Judgement: consumers gain if the spending creates real value [1], and lose if it only moves market share [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish informative advertising from persuasive advertising; this tends to give the judgement something to rest on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a feature of a contestable market without linking it to entry or exit.
- Assuming a contestable market must have many firms.
- Checking only one of the rival's options when finding a dominant strategy.
- Calculating deadweight loss with the wrong base.
- Giving a limit price with no check that profit is zero.
- Treating collusion as stable without the incentive to cheat.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no final judgement.
- Failing to use the figures given when asked to comment.
