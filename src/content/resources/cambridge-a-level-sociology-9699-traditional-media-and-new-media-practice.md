This set covers section 9.1 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: trends in
media organisation, debates about control, differences between traditional and new media, and
whether the traditional media has been undermined. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for
developed explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *media conglomerate*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between horizontal and vertical integration in the media. **[4]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by *synergy* in a media conglomerate. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how digitalisation has made media convergence possible. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **three** differences between the traditional media and the new media. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why platform companies can be seen as gatekeepers even though they produce little
content themselves. **[3]**

**7.** Explain what Bruns meant by *gatewatching*. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** way in which the state may control the media. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why concentration of media ownership might reduce the diversity of
media content. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which traditional media organisations have adapted to the growth of
the new media. **[8]**

**11.** "The traditional media has been undermined by the growth of the new media." Evaluate this
view. **[35]**

**12.** "In the age of the new media, audiences rather than owners control the media." Evaluate
this view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** A very large corporation that owns many media companies across different media [1], often
with non-media interests as well [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Horizontal integration is buying companies at the same stage of the same industry [1], for
example one newspaper group buying another [1]. Vertical integration is owning several stages of
production and distribution [1], for example a film studio that also owns a streaming service [1].
**[4]**

**3.** Synergy is when one part of a conglomerate promotes another part's products [1], such as a
film marketed through the same company's television channels, soundtrack and merchandise [1].
**[2]**

**4.** Digitalisation converts text, sound and images into the same digital code [1], so a single
device or network can carry them all [1]; for example, a smartphone delivers news, film, music and
messaging, and firms from once separate industries now compete [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any three: one-to-many versus many-to-many communication [1]; professional versus
user-generated production [1]; editors versus algorithms and users as gatekeepers [1]; scheduled
versus on-demand content [1]; national versus global reach [1]; easier versus harder regulation [1].
**[3]**

**6.** Gatekeeping means deciding what reaches the audience [1]. Platforms use algorithms to rank
and recommend content [1], so they decide what billions of users see first or at all, a role once
held by editors [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Users select and share content from many sources [1], taking on part of the gatekeeping role
that editors used to control [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Identify [1], explain [1], example [1]: for example, licensing [1] — broadcasters need
permission to operate and must meet standards set by a regulator [1], such as Ofcom in the United
Kingdom [1]. Also creditable: censorship such as China's internet controls; laws on defamation,
privacy or national security. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a theory or concept [1],
example [1].
- **Owners' influence** [1]: owners of many outlets can shape the political line across all of
  them [1]; instrumental Marxists see this as spreading ruling-class ideology [1]; scrutiny after
  the *News of the World* closure (2011) focused on one owner's links with politicians [1].
- **Synergy and commercial pressure** [1]: conglomerates prefer content that can be sold across
  formats [1], favouring safe, familiar products such as franchises over risky or minority
  content [1]; the result is many outlets carrying similar material [1].
- Also creditable: cost-cutting that reduces investigative journalism; fewer independent local
  outlets. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], consequence [1].
- **Moving online with paywalls or subscriptions** [1]: newspapers publish digitally and charge
  readers [1], replacing lost print and advertising income [1]; this shows the brand surviving in a
  new form [1].
- **Using social media to distribute content** [1]: broadcasters and newspapers post clips and
  stories on platforms [1] to reach audiences who no longer buy papers or watch schedules [1];
  this supports Chadwick's hybrid media system (2013) [1].
- Also creditable: launching streaming services and podcasts; using user-generated footage in
  professional reporting. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs economic, audience and agenda-setting evidence on
both sides and reaches a supported judgement. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** advertising has moved to search and social platforms; print circulation and
  scheduled TV audiences have fallen in many countries; news is increasingly reached through
  feeds; citizen journalism and smartphone footage break stories; editors have lost their
  gatekeeping monopoly.
- **Against the view:** Curran, Fenton and Freedman (2012) argue the internet revolution was
  overstated; traditional brands run many of the most-used news sites; their stories set the
  agenda for social media discussion; they have adapted with paywalls, podcasts and streaming;
  concentration has moved to platform conglomerates rather than disappeared.
- **Concepts:** convergence; hybrid media system (Chadwick, 2013); gatewatching (Bruns, 2005).
- **Evaluation:** distinguish economic weakening from loss of influence; differences between
  countries and between newspapers and television.
- **Judgement:** for example, weakened as a business but not undermined as a source of news and
  agenda-setting power. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level contrasts pluralist and Marxist positions, uses new media
evidence and judges where control actually lies. Indicative content:
- **For the view (pluralist and digital optimist):** audiences choose among vast numbers of
  sources; user-generated content and gatewatching (Bruns, 2005) give users some gatekeeping
  power; online campaigns can force companies and broadcasters to change course.
- **Against the view (Marxist and critical):** ownership has concentrated in platform
  conglomerates such as Alphabet and Meta; algorithms, designed for advertising revenue, rank
  what users see; owners and states still control licensing, laws and censorship; attention
  remains concentrated on a few sources.
- **Other actors:** journalists and editors; regulators such as Ofcom; states such as China
  controlling the internet.
- **Evaluation:** audiences have more choice but not equal power; control has shifted from
  publishers to platforms rather than to users; differs by country.
- **Judgement:** for example, audiences control what they click, but owners and platforms control
  the menu they choose from. **[35]**
