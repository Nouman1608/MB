Subtopic 2.1 sets out the principles of fault that underpin every offence on Paper 2 Criminal Law. The syllabus has two strands: **actus reus** (2.1.1), covering conduct, omissions and causation, and **mens rea** (2.1.2), covering direct and oblique intention and recklessness. The [Topic 2 overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-law-criminal-law/) gives the definitions; this guide adds the cases, the tests and the reasoning you need to apply them to a scenario. Many leading cases on causation and intention are homicide cases, but the principles apply across the criminal law, including the property offences in 2.2.

## The basic principle

*Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*: an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. Liability normally requires a prohibited act, omission or state of affairs, and a blameworthy state of mind. This reflects the idea that the criminal law should punish those who chose to do wrong, not those who caused harm by pure accident.

## 2.1.1 Actus reus

### Actus reus as the conduct element

The actus reus is every element of the offence other than the mental element. It may be:

- **conduct** – the act itself, such as appropriating property in theft;
- **a result** – a consequence that must follow, such as damage in criminal damage;
- **circumstances** – surrounding facts, such as property "belonging to another".

The conduct must be **voluntary**. In *Hill v Baxter* (1958) the court gave the example of a driver attacked by a swarm of bees, whose movements would not be voluntary acts. Exceptionally, some offences are committed by being in a **state of affairs**: in *R v Larsonneur* (1933) a woman brought into the UK against her will was convicted of "being found" in the UK as an alien without permission, and in *Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent* (1983) a drunk man removed from a hospital by police and placed on the highway was convicted of being found drunk on a highway. These cases are often criticised as imposing liability without real fault.

### Actus reus by omission

The general rule is that there is **no liability for failing to act**, even where a moral duty exists. Liability arises only where the defendant was under a legal duty to act:

| Duty | Case | Facts |
|---|---|---|
| Statute | Road Traffic Act 1988 | Failing to provide a breath specimen |
| Contract | *R v Pittwood* (1902) | Level-crossing keeper left the gate open; a cart was hit by a train |
| Public office | *R v Dytham* (1979) | Police officer watched a man being beaten and did nothing |
| Voluntary assumption of care | *R v Stone and Dobinson* (1977) | Couple took in a sick relative and failed to get help |
| Special relationship | *R v Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) | Father and partner starved his child |
| Creating a dangerous situation | *R v Miller* (1983) | Squatter started a fire accidentally and left without dealing with it |

*R v Evans* (2009) extended the *Miller* principle: a woman who supplied heroin to her half-sister, then failed to get help when she saw she had overdosed, was under a duty because she had contributed to the danger. In *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993) withdrawing treatment from a patient in a persistent vegetative state was treated as an omission, lawful because continued treatment was no longer in the patient's best interests.

Evaluation: the narrow omissions rule protects individual autonomy and avoids vague duties, but it can let morally blameworthy bystanders escape liability.

### Causation

Where the offence requires a result, the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct caused it, both factually and legally.

**Factual causation – the "but for" test.** In *R v White* (1910) the defendant poisoned his mother's drink, but she died of a heart attack before the poison took effect. But for his act she would still have died, so he was not guilty of murder (he was guilty of attempt).

**Legal causation.** The defendant's act must be an *operating and substantial* cause, though not necessarily the only one (*R v Smith* (1959), where a stabbed soldier was dropped twice and given poor treatment, but the wound was still operating). The contribution must be more than minimal.

**Intervening acts (*novus actus interveniens*).**

- **Third party acts** – in *R v Pagett* (1983) police returning fire killed a girl used as a human shield; their reasonable response did not break the chain.
- **Medical treatment** – rarely breaks the chain. In *R v Cheshire* (1991) negligent treatment did not break it unless so independent of the defendant's act and so potent that the original wound became insignificant. Contrast *R v Jordan* (1956), where "palpably wrong" treatment of an almost healed wound did break the chain.
- **The victim's own act** – in *R v Roberts* (1971) a woman jumped from a moving car to escape advances; the chain was not broken because her reaction was reasonably foreseeable, not "so daft" as to be unexpected. But in *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007) the victim's free, deliberate and informed decision to inject himself with drugs supplied by the defendant broke the chain.

**The thin skull rule.** The defendant must take the victim as they find them, including physical and other characteristics. In *R v Blaue* (1975) a stabbed Jehovah's Witness refused a blood transfusion on religious grounds and died; the refusal did not break the chain.

## 2.1.2 Mens rea

### Mens rea as the mental element

Mens rea is the state of mind the definition of the offence requires. It is assessed **subjectively** – what this defendant intended or foresaw – rather than what a reasonable person would have intended. The main levels are intention and recklessness.

### Intention

**Direct intention** is where the consequence is the defendant's aim or purpose. *R v Mohan* (1975) described it as a decision to bring about the consequence, no matter whether the defendant desired it.

**Indirect (oblique) intention** covers consequences that were not the defendant's aim but were a by-product of it. The law developed through several cases:

1. *R v Moloney* (1985) – foresight of consequences is only evidence of intention, not intention itself.
2. *R v Hancock and Shankland* (1986) – the greater the probability of a consequence, the more likely it was foreseen and intended.
3. *R v Nedrick* (1986) – the "virtual certainty" test.
4. *R v Woollin* (1998) – the jury is not entitled to find the necessary intention unless the consequence was a **virtual certainty** (barring some unforeseen intervention) as a result of the defendant's actions, **and** the defendant **appreciated** that this was the case.

In *R v Matthews and Alleyne* (2003) the Court of Appeal confirmed that this is a rule of evidence: foresight of virtual certainty allows the jury to find intention but does not oblige it to.

Evaluation: the *Woollin* test keeps murder for the most culpable, but the word "entitled" leaves the jury a discretion that can make outcomes inconsistent.

### Recklessness

Recklessness is the unjustified taking of a risk. English law now uses a **subjective** test.

- *R v Cunningham* (1957): the defendant tore a gas meter from a wall to steal money, and gas seeped into the next house. He was reckless only if he foresaw the risk of harm and went on to take it.
- *Metropolitan Police Commissioner v Caldwell* (1982) introduced an objective test – a risk obvious to a reasonable person – which convicted defendants who were incapable of seeing the risk.
- *R v G* (2003) overruled *Caldwell*. Two young boys set fire to newspapers behind a shop, causing extensive damage; they had not foreseen the risk. A person is reckless as to a result when they are **aware of a risk** that it will occur and it is, in the circumstances known to them, **unreasonable to take** that risk.

Intention is the higher level of fault; recklessness is lower but still requires awareness of risk. Many property offences, such as criminal damage under the Criminal Damage Act 1971, can be committed intentionally or recklessly.

## Worked example

*Jade throws a brick at Omar's car to frighten him, knowing that Omar is sitting inside with the window down. The brick hits Omar on the head. In hospital a doctor misreads Omar's chart and gives him the wrong drug, which delays his recovery. Discuss the elements of liability for Omar's injury and the damage to the car.*

1. **Actus reus – conduct and result:** Jade voluntarily threw the brick; the injury and damage are results.
2. **Factual causation:** but for the throw, Omar would not have been injured – satisfied (*White*).
3. **Legal causation:** the head wound remains an operating and substantial cause (*Smith*). The doctor's error delayed recovery but, applying *Cheshire*, it was not so independent and potent as to make Jade's act insignificant. The chain is unbroken.
4. **Mens rea – intention:** her aim was to frighten, not injure, so there is no direct intention. Oblique intention requires injury to be a virtual certainty and Jade to appreciate this (*Woollin*); throwing at an open window makes harm likely, but perhaps not virtually certain.
5. **Mens rea – recklessness:** Jade knew Omar was inside, so she was aware of a risk of injury and damage, and taking that risk was unreasonable (*R v G*). At least recklessness is proved.
6. **Conclusion:** actus reus and mens rea coincide at the moment of the throw; Jade caused both harms and was at least reckless as to each.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the "but for" test as enough for causation without considering legal causation.
- Saying any medical negligence breaks the chain of causation.
- Writing that foresight of virtual certainty *is* intention, rather than evidence of it.
- Applying the overruled *Caldwell* test.
- Stating that there is never liability for omissions, without the duty situations.

## Exam technique

In Section A, take each element in turn: identify the actus reus, establish causation where a result is required, then deal with mens rea, each with an authority. In a Section B essay, *evaluate* or *discuss* needs the development of the law and a judgement, for example on whether the omissions rule or the *Woollin* test is satisfactory.
