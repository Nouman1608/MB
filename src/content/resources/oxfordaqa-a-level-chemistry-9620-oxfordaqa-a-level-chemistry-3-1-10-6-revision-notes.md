## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Buffer solution | Maintains an approximately constant pH despite dilution or addition of small amounts of acid or base |
| Acidic buffer | A weak acid and the salt of that weak acid |
| Basic buffer | A weak base and the salt of that weak base |
| Partial neutralisation | Adding less strong base than the weak acid present, leaving both HA and A⁻ |
| Conjugate base | The species left when an acid loses H⁺ (A⁻ from HA) |

## Must-know equations

**Acidic buffer (ethanoic acid / ethanoate)**

```
equilibrium:     CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
acid added:      CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH          equilibrium shifts left
alkali added:    H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, then CH₃COOH dissociates   equilibrium shifts right
```

**Basic buffer (ammonia / ammonium)**

```
acid added:      NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
alkali added:    NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O
```

**Why the pH hardly changes:** both members of the pair are present in large amounts, so a small addition changes their ratio very little.

**Why dilution has no effect:** both concentrations fall by the same factor, so the ratio is unchanged.

## Acidic buffer pH: the method

1. Find moles of weak acid, n(HA), and of salt anion, n(A⁻).
2. If acid or base has been added, react it first:
   - added H⁺: n(HA) goes **up**, n(A⁻) goes **down** by the same amount
   - added OH⁻: n(HA) goes **down**, n(A⁻) goes **up** by the same amount
3. [H⁺] = Ka × n(HA) / n(A⁻)
4. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]

**Shortcuts**

- n(HA) = n(A⁻) → pH = pKa.
- Half-neutralised weak acid → pH = pKa.
- More salt than acid → pH above pKa; more acid than salt → pH below pKa.
- Designing a buffer: n(A⁻) / n(HA) = Ka / [H⁺]target.

## Making an acidic buffer

| Method | Mixture | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Mix directly | weak acid + its sodium or potassium salt | amounts set the ratio |
| Partial neutralisation | weak acid + **less** than one equivalent of NaOH | n(A⁻) = n(OH⁻) added; n(HA) = start − n(OH⁻) |

If NaOH is in excess, no HA is left and the mixture is **not** a buffer.

## Applications

- Blood pH is kept close to 7.4, mainly by a carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate buffer.
- Buffer solutions of known pH calibrate pH meters.
- Foods, drinks and cosmetics are buffered to keep pH stable during storage.
- Enzyme-controlled and industrial processes that need a steady pH.

## Exam traps

- Using √(Ka × [HA]) instead of Ka × [HA] / [A⁻].
- Inverting the ratio.
- Forgetting to react added acid or alkali before substituting.
- Using volumes in cm³ in a concentration expression; moles avoid this.
- Describing HCl + NaCl or NaOH + NaCl as a buffer.
- Saying a buffer "keeps pH constant" without "approximately" or "small amounts".
- Explaining the basic buffer with ethanoate equations, or the acidic buffer with ammonia equations.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a buffer solution.
2. State what an acidic buffer contains.
3. Explain why a mixture of hydrochloric acid and sodium chloride is not a buffer.
4. Write an equation for how an ammonia–ammonium chloride buffer removes added H⁺.
5. A buffer contains 0.0400 mol propanoic acid and 0.0250 mol sodium propanoate. Ka = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Calculate the pH.
6. A buffer contains equal amounts of a weak acid of pKa 4.20 and its sodium salt. State its pH.
7. 0.0020 mol NaOH is added to a buffer containing 0.0200 mol ethanoic acid and 0.0200 mol sodium ethanoate (Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³). Calculate the new pH.
8. 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid (pKa 4.76). State the pH of the mixture and explain.
9. Methanoic acid has pKa 3.74. Calculate the ratio n(HCOO⁻) / n(HCOOH) needed for a buffer of pH 4.00.
10. Explain why diluting an acidic buffer with water does not change its pH.
11. Give one application of buffer solutions.
12. Explain why a buffer stops working if a large amount of acid is added.

### Answers

1. A solution that maintains an approximately constant pH despite dilution or the addition of small amounts of acid or base.
2. A weak acid and a salt of that weak acid.
3. HCl is a strong acid and fully dissociated, so there is no reservoir of undissociated acid to replace H⁺ removed by alkali; Cl⁻ does not remove added H⁺.
4. **NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺**
5. [H⁺] = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0400 / 0.0250 = 2.16 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; pH = **4.67**.
6. pH = pKa = **4.20**.
7. n(HA) = 0.0180, n(A⁻) = 0.0220; [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0180 / 0.0220 = 1.42 × 10⁻⁵; pH = **4.85**.
8. Half the acid is neutralised (0.00250 mol of each remain), so n(HA) = n(A⁻) and pH = pKa = **4.76**.
9. Ka = 1.82 × 10⁻⁴; ratio = Ka / [H⁺] = 1.82 × 10⁻⁴ / 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ = **1.82**.
10. [HA] and [A⁻] fall by the same factor, so their ratio, and therefore [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻], is unchanged.
11. Any one: controlling blood pH; calibrating pH meters; keeping the pH of foods or drinks steady.
12. The added H⁺ uses up nearly all the A⁻, so there is no longer a large reservoir to remove more H⁺, and the ratio [HA]/[A⁻] changes greatly.
