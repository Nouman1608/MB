## Questions

**1.** A small laundry (illustrative) cleans 4,200 garments in a week. It has 7 staff, each working 40 hours. Calculate labour productivity as (a) garments per worker and (b) garments per worker-hour. **[2]**

**2.** The laundry in question 1 hires 3 more staff, each working 40 hours, and cleans 5,500 garments. Calculate the percentage change in (a) output and (b) output per worker, and comment on what has happened to productivity. **[3]**

**3.** Country Q (illustrative) has real output of 2,000, 2,100 and 2,288 (billions of units) in years 1, 2 and 3, with hours worked of 50, 50 and 52 (billions). Calculate output per hour for each year, express it as an index with year 1 = 100, and find the percentage change in output per hour from year 2 to year 3. **[4]**

**4.** In another country, output per hour at current prices is 36.0 in year 1 (price index 100) and 39.9 in year 2 (price index 105). Calculate the percentage rise in real output per hour. **[3]**

**5.** A forest (illustrative) holds 800 thousand cubic metres of timber. Loggers take 60 a year and regrowth adds 45 a year. Calculate the stock after 8 years and the percentage fall, then explain how this shows a link between production and the natural environment. **[4]**

**6.** Output per worker-hour in five firms is 12, 15, 16, 19 and 48. Calculate the mean and the median, and explain which better represents a typical firm. **[5]**

**7.** A mug workshop (illustrative) has four workers, each working 40 hours. A mug needs three tasks: shaping (1 hour), glazing (2 hours) and decorating (1 hour). When each worker does all three, 1 hour is lost switching tasks per mug. After division of labour (one shaper, two glazers, one decorator), each task is 20% faster. Calculate weekly output in each case and the percentage rise in output per worker-hour. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two costs of division of labour for a firm or its workers. **[4]**

**9.** A tailor wants eggs, the egg seller wants shoes, and the shoemaker wants a coat. Explain why exchange between them is difficult under barter, and how money helps. **[4]**

**10.** Calculate the number of exchange ratios needed under barter for 15 goods, and compare it with the number of money prices. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that division of labour always raises a firm's productivity. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) 4,200 ÷ 7 = **600 garments per worker** [1]. (b) Hours = 7 × 40 = 280; 4,200 ÷ 280 = **15 garments per hour** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the unit, and use total hours (workers × hours each) when finding a per-hour figure; a bare number is harder to credit.

**2.** (a) (5,500 − 4,200) ÷ 4,200 × 100 = **+31.0%** [1]. (b) Output per worker = 5,500 ÷ 10 = 550, and (550 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = **−8.3%** [1]. Output rose but productivity fell, because staff rose by 43% (3 ÷ 7) and output by less; hiring explains the gain in output, not better use of workers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing the growth rate of output with the growth rate of the input is the clearest way to show you understand the gap between production and productivity.

**3.** Output per hour: 2,000 ÷ 50 = 40; 2,100 ÷ 50 = 42; 2,288 ÷ 52 = 44 [1]. Index (year 1 = 100): 100, 105, 110 [1]. Percentage change year 2 to 3: (44 − 42) ÷ 42 × 100 [1] = **+4.8%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Take the earlier year as the starting value for a percentage change, and do not confuse a rise of 5 index points with a rise of 5%.

**4.** Real output per hour in year 2 = 39.9 ÷ 105 × 100 = 38.0 [1]. Year 1 real value = 36.0 because the base price index is 100 [1]. Rise = (38.0 − 36.0) ÷ 36.0 × 100 = **5.6%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Deflate before calculating growth; using the nominal rise of 10.8% would overstate the real gain.

**5.** Net fall = 60 − 45 = 15 a year [1]. After 8 years: 800 − 120 = **680** [1]. Percentage fall = 120 ÷ 800 × 100 = **15%** [1]. The forest supplies timber as an input to production, but extraction faster than regrowth reduces the stock, so future output is threatened [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the calculation to the idea in the question; a correct number with no economic comment leaves the explanation incomplete.

**6.** Mean = (12 + 15 + 16 + 19 + 48) ÷ 5 = 110 ÷ 5 = **22** [1]. Median = middle value of 12, 15, 16, 19, 48 = **16** [1]. One firm at 48 is far above the rest [1]. It pulls the mean above four of the five firms [1]. The median therefore better represents a typical firm [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the outlier and saying how it affects each average is usually stronger than stating that the median is "less affected".

**7.** Everyone does everything: time per mug = 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 (switching) = 5 hours [1]. Team hours = 160, so output = 160 ÷ 5 = **32 mugs** [1]. Division of labour: shaping 0.8, glazing 1.6, decorating 0.8 hours [1]. Two glazers give 1.6 ÷ 2 = 0.8 hours per mug, so every stage takes 0.8 hours and the team makes 1.25 mugs an hour [1]. Weekly output = 1.25 × 40 = **50 mugs** [1]. Output per worker-hour rises from 32 ÷ 160 = 0.2 to 50 ÷ 160 = 0.3125, a rise of **56.25%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the time each stage takes. The balance of workers across stages is why the output figure is what it is.

**8.** First cost, such as monotony: repetitive tasks lower motivation [1], which can raise absence and staff turnover and lower quality [1]. Second cost, such as interdependence: if one stage stops or is slow, the whole process is held up [1], so output falls even if other workers are fully able to work [1]. (Narrow skills or training costs are also acceptable.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each cost needs a reason and a consequence. Naming a cost alone does not complete the explanation.

**9.** The tailor wants eggs but the egg seller wants shoes, not coats [1]. There is no double coincidence of wants between any pair, so a direct swap fails [1]. With money, the tailor sells a coat to anyone for money [1], then uses the money to buy eggs, so no matching of wants is needed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the stated goods to show the missing match, not just the definition of barter.

**10.** Barter ratios = 15 × 14 ÷ 2 = **105** [1]. Money prices = **15**, one for each good [1]. Money cuts the number of prices to compare from 105 to 15, saving time and information [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State the formula or the pairing logic as well as the answer, and make the comparison explicit.

**11.** Benefit: repetition raises skill and speed, and less time is lost switching tasks, so output per worker-hour rises [1]. The mug case shows this: output per worker-hour can rise sharply when stages are balanced [1]. Capital can be kept in constant use [1]. Against this: monotony can lower motivation and quality, offsetting gains [1]. Interdependence means one weak stage limits the process [1]. Narrow skills reduce flexibility if demand or technology changes [1]. Judgement: the effect depends on how well stages are balanced, the size of the market, and whether management uses rotation or training [1]. Conclusion: the claim is too strong; division of labour usually raises productivity but not always, and the gains are less secure if the costs are ignored [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned conclusion that answers "always" directly, with conditions, is stronger than a list of points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not stating units, or giving per-worker figures when per-hour was asked for.
- Treating a rise in output as proof of a rise in productivity.
- Dividing a change by the later year's value instead of the earlier one.
- Confusing index points with percentage points.
- Forgetting to deflate nominal values before comparing real output.
- Quoting the mean without checking for an outlier.
- Naming costs of division of labour without a consequence.
- Describing barter without applying it to the goods in the question.
- Ending an evaluation without a conclusion that answers the question.
