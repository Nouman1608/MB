## Questions

**1.** A school survey asks pupils how they travel to school.

**(a)** Write a closed question on this topic, with four response options. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an open question on the same topic. **[1]**

**2.** **(a)** State two advantages of closed questions compared with open questions. **[2]**
**(b)** State one advantage of an open question compared with a closed question. **[1]**

**3.** A questionnaire records the age, in whole years, of cinema customers using these options: "Under 18", "18–30", "30–45", "50 or over".

**(a)** State two faults in these response options. **[2]**
**(b)** Write a corrected set of options. **[2]**

**4.** Each question below is badly worded. Name the fault and rewrite the question.

**(i)** "How often do you take exercise? Often, Sometimes, Rarely." **[2]**
**(ii)** "Do you think the library should open for longer and have more computers?" **[2]**
**(iii)** "Do you agree that the terrible food in the canteen needs to be improved?" **[2]**

**5.** Thirty residents answer the open question "What annoys you most about living on your street?" The answers are coded as: parking 11, noise 8, litter 6, other 5.

**(a)** Find the percentage of residents who mentioned litter. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the angle for each category in a pie chart. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the answers had to be coded before the pie chart was drawn. **[1]**
**(d)** State one piece of information lost when answers are coded. **[1]**

**6.** A researcher tries a questionnaire on 12 pupils before the main survey. She finds that 5 pupils left question 4 blank because no option matched their answer, 3 asked what "extracurricular" means, and the form took 25 minutes on average.

**(a)** Give one reason for carrying out a pilot survey. **[1]**
**(b)** For each of the three findings, suggest a change to the questionnaire. **[3]**

**7.** A club hands out 250 questionnaires. 135 are returned, and 5 of these are incomplete and cannot be used.

**(a)** Calculate the response rate, using usable forms only. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why non-response can make the results biased. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two ways to increase the response rate. **[2]**

**8.** Fifty shoppers answer "How would you rate the new car park?" The results are: very good 12, good 21, neither 9, poor 5, very poor not shown.

**(a)** Find the number who chose "very poor". **[1]**
**(b)** Find the percentage who chose "good" or "very good". **[2]**
**(c)** The researcher adds an open question after this one. Give a reason for doing so. **[1]**

**9.** A gym manager wants to know why members do or do not use the swimming pool.

**(a)** Write three questions for the questionnaire: one closed question with options, one rating-scale question, and one open question. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the manager might use both open and closed questions. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the manager should pilot the questionnaire. **[3]**

**10.** A council asks residents: "Do you think the council should waste money on cycle lanes? Yes / No".

**(a)** State two faults in this question. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite it as a closed question with a five-point rating scale. **[2]**
**(c)** The council adds the open question "Why?". Give one benefit and one drawback of this for the analysis. **[2]**
**(d)** The form is available online only. State one way this could bias the results. **[1]**

**11.** A school asks 80 pupils the open question "What one change would improve lunch breaks?" The coded answers are: longer break 26, more clubs 18, better seating 14, shorter queues 12, other 10.

**(a)** Show that "longer break" was the most common answer and find its percentage of the 80 pupils. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the pie chart angle for each category. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the size of the "other" category. **[2]**
**(d)** One pupil writes "the queues are long and the seating is dirty". Explain how a coding rule should deal with an answer like this. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) A clear question about the journey, for example "How do you usually travel to school?" [1] with four distinct options such as walk, cycle, bus, car (adding "other" gives an exhaustive list) [1]
(b) For example "Why do you travel to school in this way?" [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A closed question gains credit only when the options are shown. Check that no two options could both be true for one pupil.

**2.** (a) Any two from: quicker to answer; quicker and cheaper to tally and analyse; answers are in the same categories, so easy to compare; more likely to be completed [2]
(b) Respondents can give reasons or answers the researcher did not think of [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate points are needed in part (a). Restating "quick" and "fast" is one point written twice.

**3.** (a) 30 appears in two options (18–30 and 30–45), so options are not mutually exclusive [1]; ages 46 to 49 appear in no option, so the options are not exhaustive [1]
(b) For example: Under 18, 18–30, 31–45, 46 or over. No overlaps [1], no gaps [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A corrected list that still has a gap or an overlap is unlikely to earn full credit. Test every whole number between the bands.

**4.** (i) Vague: "often" and "sometimes" mean different things to different people [1]. Rewrite: "On how many days in the last week did you take exercise? 0, 1–2, 3–4, 5–7" [1]
(ii) Double-barrelled: asks about opening hours and computers at once [1]. Rewrite as two separate questions, one on opening hours and one on computers [1]
(iii) Leading and loaded: "terrible" and "needs to be improved" suggest the answer [1]. Rewrite: "How would you rate the food in the canteen? Very good, good, neither, poor, very poor" [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the fault is not enough for the rewrite mark. The new question must remove the fault, so a rewrite that keeps "terrible" is not a fix.

**5.** (a) 6/30 × 100 = **20%** [1]
(b) Angle = frequency/30 × 360°, so each person is worth 12° [1]. Parking 132°, noise 96°, litter 72°, other 60° (total 360°) [1]
(c) The answers were written in many different words, so they had to be sorted into categories before they could be counted [1]
(d) For example: a resident's second complaint, the strength of feeling, or the reasons given [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the total of the angles. Adding to 360° is a quick check on arithmetic.

**6.** (a) To find and fix problems in the questionnaire before the main survey [1]
(b) Question 4: add an option such as "Other (please state)" or check that the options cover every answer [1]. "Extracurricular": replace it with plain words such as "school clubs" [1]. Time: shorten the form by removing questions that are not needed for the aim [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each change to its finding. A general reply such as "make it better" is unlikely to be credited.

**7.** (a) Usable forms = 135 − 5 = 130 [1]. Response rate = 130/250 × 100 = **52%** [1]
(b) The people who did not reply may differ from those who did [1], so the replies may not represent the whole group, and results are pushed in one direction [1]
(c) Any two from: send a reminder; shorten the form; give a prepaid reply envelope; explain the purpose clearly [1] [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Read "usable forms only" carefully. Using 135 gives 54%, which does not answer the question asked.

**8.** (a) 50 − (12 + 21 + 9 + 5) = 50 − 47 = **3** [1]
(b) 12 + 21 = 33 [1]. 33/50 × 100 = **66%** [1]
(c) It lets respondents explain their rating, which the scale alone cannot show [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason specific to the rating. "To get more information" is too general.

**9.** (a) A closed question with options, for example "Do you use the pool? Yes / No" [1]; a rating question such as "How satisfied are you with pool opening times? Very satisfied, satisfied, neither, dissatisfied, very dissatisfied" [1]; an open question such as "What would make you use the pool more?" [1]
(b) Closed questions give counts that are easy to tally and compare [1]. Open questions give reasons and ideas that the manager had not listed [1]
(c) Try the questionnaire on a small group of members, like the real respondents [1]. Look for unclear questions, blank answers, options that do not fit, and the time taken [1]. Change the questionnaire to fix these before the main survey [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In a design question, wording quality is judged. A leading or double-barrelled question can lose the mark even if it is the right type.

**10.** (a) "Waste" is a loaded word that suggests the answer [1]. Only Yes/No is offered, so there is no neutral or "don't know" option [1]
(b) "How far do you agree that the council should spend money on cycle lanes?" [1] with a balanced scale: strongly agree, agree, neither, disagree, strongly disagree [1]
(c) Benefit: it gives reasons behind the answer [1]. Drawback: the answers must be read and coded before they can be counted, which takes longer [1]
(d) Residents without internet access cannot reply, so they are under-represented [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* For part (c) give one benefit and one drawback that both refer to analysis. An answer about the respondent's time does not address the question.

**11.** (a) 26 is greater than 18, 14, 12 and 10, so it is the largest [1]. 26/80 × 100 = **32.5%** [1]
(b) Each pupil is worth 360°/80 = 4.5° [1]. Longer break 117°, clubs 81°, seating 63°, queues 54°, other 45° (total 360°) [1]
(c) "Other" is 10 of 80, which is 12.5% [1]. This is smaller than the main categories so it is acceptable, but the 10 answers should be read in case several share a theme that deserves its own category [1]
(d) The rule must be decided before counting [1]. For example, code only the first point made (shorter queues), or count the answer in both categories and then say that percentages are of answers, not of pupils [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) give a judgement and a reason, not just the percentage. In part (d) state the rule you chose and apply it to every answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing response options that overlap (such as 20–30 and 30–40) or leave gaps between bands.
- Naming a fault in a question but not rewriting it, or keeping the loaded word in the rewrite.
- Giving the same advantage twice in different words when two separate points are asked for.
- Describing open and closed questions in general terms without linking the point to the survey in the question.
- Treating the pilot survey as a small copy of the main survey instead of a test of the questionnaire.
- Using the number returned instead of the number usable when finding a response rate.
- Leaving out working or units for percentages and pie chart angles.
