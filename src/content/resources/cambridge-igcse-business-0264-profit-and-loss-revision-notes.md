Condensed notes for section 5.3 (Profit and loss) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029: 5.3.1 What is profit and why it is important, and 5.3.2 Statement of profit or loss. Worked examples on missing figures, a supplier price rise and a loss-making branch are in the [profit and loss study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss/). Test yourself with the [profit and loss practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-practice/). The combined [profit and loss and statement of financial position notes](/resources/igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-and-statement-of-financial-position-revision-notes/) also cover section 5.4.

All examples are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What profit is

- **Profit:** the amount by which revenue is greater than total costs in a period.
- **Loss:** when total costs are greater than revenue.
- Profit is **not** revenue and **not** cash.

## Why profit matters to private sector businesses

The four reasons named in the syllabus:

| Reason | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| Reward for risk-taking | Owners risk their money and expect a return |
| Source of finance | Retained profit can fund growth without borrowing or interest |
| Measure of success | Shows whether the business earns more than it spends; compare years or rivals |
| Attract investors | A profitable business finds it easier to sell shares or bring in partners |

## The statement of profit or loss

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Income from sales in the period |
| Cost of sales | Cost of the goods sold (materials, bought-in stock) |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales |
| Expenses | Other running costs: wages, rent, advertising, utilities |
| Profit | Gross profit minus expenses |

Note: you will not be asked to construct a statement, but you must read one, calculate from it and make decisions with it.

## Formulae (from the syllabus)

```
Revenue      = selling price per unit x number of units sold
Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales
Profit       = total revenue - total costs
     or      = gross profit - expenses
```

**Working backwards:**

```
Cost of sales = revenue - gross profit
Gross profit  = profit + expenses
Expenses      = gross profit - profit
```

### Worked reminder

Ashgrove Stationery: revenue $90,000; gross profit $36,000; profit $8,000.

```
Cost of sales = 90,000 - 36,000 = 54,000
Expenses      = 36,000 - 8,000  = 28,000
```

## Using a statement to decide

| What changed | Effect | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of sales rose | Gross profit and profit fall | Find a cheaper supplier; raise price; cut waste |
| Expenses rose | Gross profit unchanged; profit falls | Cut expenses such as rent or advertising |
| Price raised | Revenue up if sales hold; down if sales fall a lot | Check how customers and rivals react |
| One product or branch makes a loss | Drags total profit down | Look for the cause, cut its costs, or close it |

### Worked reminder: a rent rise

Brockley Bikes has gross profit of $70,000 and expenses of $58,000, so profit is $12,000. Its landlord raises the rent by $5,000.

```
New expenses = 58,000 + 5,000 = 63,000
New profit   = 70,000 - 63,000 = 7,000
```

Gross profit is unchanged, because rent is an expense, not part of cost of sales. Profit falls by the full $5,000.

Questions to ask before deciding:

- Is the change in one year only, or a trend?
- What does the statement *not* show: customer reaction, quality, competitors?
- Will sales stay the same after the change? State your assumption.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Profit | Revenue minus total costs |
| Loss | Total costs greater than revenue |
| Revenue | Money from sales in a period |
| Cost of sales | Cost of the goods actually sold in the period |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales |
| Expenses | Running costs other than cost of sales |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business rather than paid to owners |
| Statement of profit or loss | A financial statement showing revenue, costs and profit for a period |

## Exam traps

- Gross profit uses cost of sales only; do not subtract expenses yet.
- Show each line of working; a slip early on can still earn method marks later.
- Put a dollar sign and the period ("a year") on answers.
- A rise in revenue does not mean a rise in profit; check costs.
- The four reasons are for **private sector** businesses.

## Quick self-test

1. Define profit.
2. State the four reasons why profit is important to private sector businesses, as named in the syllabus.
3. How is gross profit calculated?
4. Give two examples of expenses.
5. A business sells 3,000 items at $15. Calculate revenue.
6. Revenue is $120,000 and cost of sales is $70,000. Calculate gross profit.
7. Using Question 6, expenses are $38,000. Calculate profit.
8. Revenue is $250,000 and gross profit is $95,000. Calculate cost of sales.
9. Profit is $12,000 and expenses are $41,000. Calculate gross profit.
10. Explain why profit can be a source of finance.
11. Cost of sales rises but revenue and expenses stay the same. What happens to profit?
12. Why is profit not the same as cash?

### Answers

1. The amount by which revenue is greater than total costs in a period.
2. Reward for risk-taking; source of finance; measure of success; attract investors.
3. Revenue minus cost of sales.
4. Any two: wages, rent, advertising, utilities, insurance.
5. 15 x 3,000 = $45,000.
6. 120,000 - 70,000 = $50,000.
7. 50,000 - 38,000 = $12,000.
8. 250,000 - 95,000 = $155,000.
9. 12,000 + 41,000 = $53,000.
10. Profit kept in the business (retained profit) can pay for new equipment or growth without borrowing, so no interest is paid.
11. Gross profit falls, so profit falls by the same amount.
12. Revenue may be earned before customers pay, and cash may be spent on things that are not expenses in that period, such as new equipment, so a profitable business can still be short of cash.
