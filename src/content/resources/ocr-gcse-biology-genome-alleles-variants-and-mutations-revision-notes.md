## Definitions to learn word for word (B5.1a)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Gamete | A sex cell that fuses with another gamete at fertilisation |
| Chromosome | A long, coiled molecule of DNA in the nucleus carrying many genes |
| Gene | A section of DNA that codes for a protein |
| Allele (variant) | A different version of the same gene |
| Genotype | The alleles an organism has for a gene |
| Phenotype | The observable characteristic produced |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles of a gene |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles of a gene |
| Dominant | An allele whose characteristic shows with one copy present |
| Recessive | An allele whose characteristic shows only with two copies |

Use capital and lower-case letters as a pair, such as T and t. Genotype is letters; phenotype is words.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Genotype or phenotype**: Gg is a genotype; green is a phenotype.
- **Homozygous or heterozygous**: GG and gg are homozygous; Gg is heterozygous.
- **Dominant does not mean common or stronger**. It describes which characteristic shows when both alleles are present. Dominant alleles do not block recessive ones.
- **Gene or allele**: a gene is the instruction; an allele is one version of it.
- **Allele or variant**: variant is the wider word. It includes differences in non-coding DNA.
- **Genome or gene**: the genome is all of the genetic material; genes are only part of it.

### Method: working out a phenotype from a genotype

1. Identify the dominant allele (the capital letter).
2. If at least one dominant allele is present, the dominant phenotype shows.
3. If both alleles are recessive, the recessive phenotype shows.
4. For a dominant phenotype, say the genotype could be homozygous or heterozygous. For a recessive phenotype, the genotype is certain.

## The genome (B5.1b)

- The genome is **all the genetic material** held by an organism.
- Order of size: genome, then chromosome, then gene, then DNA base sequence.
- Includes non-coding DNA, which is most of the human genome.
- Human genome: nuclear DNA plus mitochondrial DNA. Bacterial genome: main chromosome plus plasmids.

Worked reminder. A genome has 1 000 000 000 base pairs, and 2% is coding. Coding DNA = 1 000 000 000 × 0.02 = 20 000 000 base pairs. The rest, 980 000 000, is non-coding.

## Genome and environment (B5.1c)

| Feature | Discontinuous | Continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Values | Separate categories | Any value in a range |
| Examples | Eye colour, ABO blood group | Height, weight |
| Cause | Mostly genes, one or a few | Many genes plus environment |
| Graph | Bar chart with gaps | Histogram or bell-shaped curve |

- Phenotype = genome + environment working together.
- Identical genomes (clones, identical twins) that differ must differ because of the environment.
- Examples of environmental effects: nutrition on height, light on plant growth, soil acidity on hydrangea flower colour.

### Method: reading variation data

1. Check the total frequency adds up.
2. Find the modal class: the tallest bar or largest frequency.
3. Describe the shape: bell-shaped with no gaps is continuous; separate bars is discontinuous.
4. Percentage in a class = class frequency ÷ total × 100.
5. For clones in different conditions, compare the means and attribute the difference to the environment. Mention the small sample if there is one.

Worked reminder. Ten cuttings from one plant: five in warm conditions have a mean height of 30 cm, five in cool conditions have a mean of 24 cm. Difference = 6 cm; 6 ÷ 30 × 100 = 20% lower in the cool conditions. Same genome, so the cause is the environment.

## Mutations (B5.1d)

- A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence.
- All variants first arose as mutations.
- **Most** have no effect on phenotype, **some** influence it, a **very few** determine it.
- Random events. More likely with ionising radiation, ultraviolet light and some chemicals.
- Not all are harmful.

Why a mutation may have no effect: it falls in non-coding DNA, or it changes a base without changing the amino acid the codon specifies.

## How variants change phenotype (B5.1e, Higher Tier only)

| Where | What changes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Coding DNA | Order of amino acids | Different protein shape, so different activity |
| Coding DNA of an enzyme | Active site shape | Substrate fits less well, so slower or no reaction |
| Non-coding DNA near a gene | How the gene is expressed | Transcription to mRNA is switched on or off differently, so more or less protein, or at a different time |

Chain to write in full: base change, then amino acid sequence, then protein folding, then shape (active site), then activity, then phenotype.

For non-coding variants: protein sequence is unchanged; the amount or timing of the protein changes. A variant can stop a protein attaching to DNA that normally stops transcription, so the gene stays on. You do not need the terms promoter or transcription factor.

Examples: phenylketonuria (enzyme with lower activity), lactase persistence (a variant in non-coding DNA near the gene keeps it active in adults).

Worked reminder. Normal mRNA codons AUG GAA CCU UAA code Met-Glu-Pro then stop. If GAA changes to GAG, Glu is still coded and the protein is unchanged. If GAA changes to UAA, a stop codon appears and the protein is only Met.

### Method: answering a data question on a variant enzyme (Higher Tier only)

1. Compare the two sets of figures at the same conditions, using numbers.
2. Work out the variant's activity as a percentage of the normal enzyme's: variant ÷ normal × 100.
3. Name the cause as a different amino acid sequence, giving a different protein shape.
4. Link the shape to the active site and say what happens to substrate binding.
5. State the effect on the reaction rate and, if asked, on the phenotype.

Worked reminder. Normal enzyme makes product at 3.0 units per minute; the variant makes 0.6 units per minute. 0.6 ÷ 3.0 × 100 = 20%. The variant works at one fifth of the normal rate, which fits an altered active site that binds the substrate poorly.

### Three levels of effect at a glance

| Level | Meaning | Example idea |
|---|---|---|
| No effect | Phenotype is the same | Change in non-coding DNA, or a codon that still gives the same amino acid |
| Influences phenotype | Phenotype is altered a little | One amino acid swapped, enzyme works more slowly |
| Determines phenotype | The characteristic is set by the variant | Enzyme made so short that it cannot work |

## Quick self-test

1. Define a gene.
2. A plant has genotype Rr and red petals, with R dominant. State whether its genotype is homozygous or heterozygous, and give the genotype of a plant with white (recessive) petals.
3. What is the genome?
4. Is blood group A, B, AB or O discontinuous or continuous variation?
5. Two clones differ in mass because one received more water. What does this show?
6. In a set of 40 seedlings, 14 are in the modal class. What percentage is that?
7. State what a mutation is.
8. Fill in: most mutations have ___ effect on phenotype, some ___ it and a very few ___ it.
9. (Higher Tier only) How can a mutation in the gene for an enzyme stop the enzyme working?
10. (Higher Tier only) A variant lies in non-coding DNA beside a gene, and the protein's amino acid sequence is normal. How can it still change the phenotype?

### Answers

1. A section of DNA that codes for a protein.
2. Heterozygous; rr (homozygous recessive).
3. The entire genetic material of an organism.
4. Discontinuous: it falls into separate categories.
5. The environment (water) influences phenotype, because the genomes are identical.
6. 14 ÷ 40 × 100 = 35%.
7. A change in the base sequence of DNA.
8. No; influence; determine.
9. The base change alters the amino acid sequence, so the protein folds into a different shape. If the active site changes, the substrate no longer fits, so the reaction slows or stops.
10. It alters how the gene is expressed, such as whether transcription to mRNA happens. More or less protein, or protein at a different time, changes the phenotype.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a loose definition of gene, such as "a piece of chromosome" or "what you inherit", rather than a section of DNA coding for a protein.
- Using genotype and phenotype the wrong way round.
- Claiming a dominant phenotype tells you the genotype exactly.
- Writing that the dominant allele "takes over" or "kills" the recessive one.
- Saying the genome is "the genes".
- Describing a graph without naming the shape, the modal class or the gaps.
- Calculating a percentage difference using the wrong starting value.
- Writing that all mutations are harmful, or that all of them change the phenotype.
- Writing "the mutation damages the enzyme" without the chain from amino acid sequence to shape to active site.
- Mixing up coding and non-coding effects: non-coding variants change expression, not the amino acid sequence.
