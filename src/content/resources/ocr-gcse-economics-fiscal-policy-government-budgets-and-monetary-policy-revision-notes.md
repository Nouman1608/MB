## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Deliberate changes to what the government spends and taxes to steer the economy |
| Monetary policy | Use of interest rates (and sometimes the money supply) by the central bank to influence the economy |
| Budget deficit | Government spending is greater than revenue in a year |
| Budget surplus | Government revenue is greater than spending in a year |
| Balanced budget | Revenue equals spending |
| Direct tax | Paid directly from income or profit (income tax, national insurance, corporation tax) |
| Indirect tax | Added to the price of goods and services (VAT, excise duties) |
| Progressive tax | The share of income paid in tax grows as income grows |
| Regressive tax | The share of income paid in tax shrinks as income grows |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |
| Expansionary policy | Raises aggregate demand (more spending, lower taxes, lower interest rates) |
| Contractionary policy | Reduces aggregate demand (less spending, higher taxes, higher interest rates) |

## Formulas and methods

| Task | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Budget balance | Revenue − spending | 325 − 340 = −15 (deficit) |
| Share of revenue | Part ÷ total × 100 | 105 ÷ 325 = 32.3% |
| Tax in a band | (Income in band) × rate | 18,000 × 0.2 = 3,600 |
| Average tax rate | Total tax ÷ income × 100 | 11,600 ÷ 55,000 = 21.1% |
| Tax revenue (per-unit tax) | Tax × new quantity | 3 × 400 = 1,200 |
| Subsidy cost | Subsidy × new quantity | 1.5 × 550 = 825 |
| Knock-on effect | Spending × 1 ÷ (1 − share re-spent) | 200 × 1 ÷ 0.4 = 500 |
| Interest cost | Loan × rate | 150,000 × 0.04 = 6,000 |
| Real interest rate | Nominal rate − inflation (approx.) | 3 − 4 = −1% |

## Method in steps: a per-unit tax or subsidy

1. Locate the starting equilibrium: where demand meets supply.
2. Move the supply curve vertically by the per-unit amount: up for a tax, down for a subsidy.
3. Find where demand now meets the shifted supply: that gives the price buyers pay and the quantity bought.
4. Seller's price: buyer's price − tax (or + subsidy).
5. Government revenue or cost = amount per unit × new quantity.
6. Burden: buyers' share = price rise × quantity. Sellers' share = the rest.

## Government spending and revenue

- **Spending purposes:** public and merit goods, transfer payments, demand management, subsidies, debt interest.
- **Revenue sources:** direct taxes, indirect taxes, borrowing, sales of assets and charges.
- A recession often widens the deficit because tax receipts fall and benefit payments rise.
- A deficit is a one-year gap. The national debt is the accumulated total owed.

## Fiscal policy chains

**Expansionary:** lower tax or higher spending → higher disposable income or demand → higher consumption → higher aggregate demand → more output and jobs → growth, lower unemployment → risk of higher prices if capacity is full.

**Contractionary:** higher tax or lower spending → lower demand → lower inflation pressure → but slower growth and possible job losses.

**Knock-on effect:** an injection is re-spent in rounds. Saving, taxes and imports reduce each round.

## Evaluating fiscal policy

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Direct and targeted | Time lags |
| Funds public services | Borrowing and interest payments |
| Can tackle recession | Opportunity cost of alternative uses |
| Reduces inequality | Disincentives, avoidance |

**Judgement words:** depends on the size of the change, the state of the economy, how quickly it works and who is affected.

## Progressive, proportional, regressive

| Tax type | Average rate as income rises | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive | Rises | Bands of 0%, 20%, 40% |
| Proportional | Same | A flat 10% on all income |
| Regressive | Falls | Sales tax on a necessity, as a share of income |

**Redistribution evaluation.** Gains: lower inequality, funding for health and education, higher spending from low-income households. Costs: possible reduced effort, avoidance, high cost for taxpayers, reliance on benefits.

## Monetary policy chains

**Rate cut:** cheaper borrowing, lower return on saving → more consumption and investment → higher aggregate demand → growth, more jobs → inflation pressure rises.

**Rate rise:** dearer borrowing, better return on saving → less consumption and investment → lower demand → slower growth, less inflation.

| Group | Rate rise | Rate cut |
|---|---|---|
| Borrowers | Worse off | Better off |
| Savers | Better off | Worse off |
| Firms | Less investment | More investment |
| Consumers | Less spending | More spending |

**Limits:** time lags, weak confidence, banks may not pass on changes, heavy household debt, savers hurt by low rates.

## Matching policies to objectives

| Problem | Fiscal response | Monetary response | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak growth, high unemployment | Cut taxes or raise spending | Cut interest rates | Inflation if there is little spare capacity |
| Inflation too high | Raise taxes or cut spending | Raise interest rates | Slower growth, job losses |
| Unequal incomes | Progressive taxes, benefits | Little direct effect | Disincentives, avoidance |

## Worked reminders

**Budget position.** Country B takes in 410 cu and spends 385 cu. Balance = 410 − 385 = +25, a surplus of 25 cu. A surplus can be used to reduce debt or fund tax cuts.

**Where the burden falls.** A 2 cu tax pushes the price buyers pay from 10 to 11.50, and sellers keep 9.50. Buyers bear 1.50 of the 2 and sellers bear 0.50. The more inelastic the demand, the more of the tax falls on buyers.

**Investment decision.** A café plans a new coffee machine costing 12,000 cu that adds 840 cu a year of profit before interest. Return = 840 ÷ 12,000 = 7%. If it borrows at 5%, interest is 600 cu and it gains 240 cu. If rates rise to 9%, interest is 1,080 cu and it loses 240 cu. Higher rates therefore lower investment.

**Spending power.** If a worker's pay rises 3% while prices rise 5%, real pay falls by about 2%. Higher prices cut the real value of both wages and savings.

## Command words for evaluation answers

- **Explain:** make a point, then give a reason or a chain.
- **Analyse:** break the effect into steps, each leading to the next.
- **Evaluate:** weigh both sides and reach a supported judgement.
- **Calculate:** show the working and include units.

## Fiscal versus monetary: must-know distinctions

| | Fiscal | Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Government | Central bank (Bank of England in the UK) |
| Tools | Taxes, spending | Interest rates |
| Direct effect on | Disposable income, public services | Cost of borrowing and reward for saving |
| Main side effect | Debt, opportunity cost | Effects on savers and borrowers |

## Quick self-test

1. What is fiscal policy?
2. Government spending is 260 and revenue is 275. State the budget position and its size.
3. Is VAT a direct or indirect tax?
4. Using bands of 0% to 12,000, 20% from 12,000 to 40,000, find the tax and average rate on 25,000 cu.
5. Government spends 50 cu and people re-spend 50% of extra income. Find the total rise in income.
6. A 4 cu per-unit subsidy is paid on 300 units. What is the cost?
7. An interest-only loan of 80,000 cu rises from 5% to 7%. Find the extra yearly cost.
8. Nominal rate 3%, inflation 4%. What is the approximate real rate?
9. State two effects of a fall in interest rates on inflation and growth.
10. Give one opportunity cost of higher government spending on defence.
11. Why is a progressive tax likely to reduce inequality?
12. Give two reasons why a rate cut may fail to increase spending.

### Answers

1. Changing government spending and taxes to steer the economy.
2. Surplus of 15 (275 − 260).
3. Indirect.
4. Income in band = 13,000. Tax = 13,000 × 0.2 = 2,600 cu. Average rate = 2,600 ÷ 25,000 = 10.4%.
5. 50 × 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 50 × 2 = 100 cu.
6. 4 × 300 = 1,200 cu.
7. 80,000 × 0.07 = 5,600; 80,000 × 0.05 = 4,000; extra 1,600 cu a year.
8. About −1%.
9. Growth rises as borrowing and spending increase; inflation pressure rises as demand grows faster than supply.
10. The money cannot be used for schools or hospitals.
11. High earners pay a larger share of income, which leaves a smaller gap after tax.
12. Low confidence, heavy household debt, or banks not passing on the cut.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a deficit "debt", or the other way round.
- Listing national insurance as an indirect tax.
- Applying the top rate to every pound earned, rather than only the slice above the threshold.
- Forgetting that government revenue or cost uses the new quantity, not the old.
- Describing a progressive tax by the total paid instead of the average rate.
- Chains that stop at "spending rises" with no link to growth, jobs or prices.
- Evaluation that lists points without a judgement or a condition such as "it depends on spare capacity".
- Mixing up the effects on savers and borrowers when rates change.
- Giving figures without units, or real figures when the question asks for nominal ones.
