> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for practice on 1123 Paper 2 Section A,
> Directed Writing, where candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s)". The
> two source texts are original and shorter than the real 400–450 words. Question 10 is a full task at
> the syllabus tariff of 25 marks (15 writing, 10 reading); the other questions practise its separate
> skills, with practice marks.

Related: [Directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/),
[revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/)
and the [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/).

## Section A

**1.** State whether each sentence is fact or opinion: (a) "The canteen sold 3,000 plastic bottles last
term." (b) "Plastic bottles are the worst thing ever invented." (c) "Reusable bottles are obviously
cheaper in the long run." **[3]**

**2.** Identify two loaded words in this sentence and explain the bias they show: "The reckless plan to
scrap the bus service will leave our helpless pensioners stranded." **[2]**

**3.** Develop this source point for an audience of fifteen-year-old students: "A longer lunch break
would allow time for clubs." **[2]**

**4.** Write one sentence evaluating this claim: "Studies prove that homework is useless." **[2]**

**5.** Rewrite for a speech to classmates: "It is respectfully submitted that the proposal merits
consideration." **[2]**

**6.** Name three features a report should have. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Read the two texts (original, written for this practice set).

> **Text 1: from a message by the President of the Student Council**
>
> Every lunchtime, our canteen hands out hundreds of plastic bottles and wrappers, and most of them end
> up in the bins within ten minutes. We are proposing that, from next term, the canteen stops selling
> drinks in single-use plastic. Instead, water fountains would be installed and every student would be
> given a reusable bottle. Other schools in our area have already done this, and their students say the
> playground is noticeably cleaner. We know change is hard, but if we care about the environment, we
> have to start somewhere, and the canteen is the obvious place.
>
> **Text 2: from a reply by the canteen manager**
>
> I support the idea of reducing plastic, but the proposal is not as simple as it sounds. Water
> fountains cost money to install and must be cleaned several times a day. Reusable bottles get lost,
> and students may forget to bring them, which on a hot day is a health risk. Drinks in bottles are also
> a large part of the canteen's income, and that income keeps food prices low for everyone. No one from
> the council has asked how we would cover the loss.

From the texts, identify one point you would **use**, one you could **develop** and one you would
**evaluate**, and say briefly how. **[3]**

**8.** Write the opening (about 60 words) of a speech to your year group at assembly, persuading them to
support the proposal. **[3]**

**9.** Write one body paragraph (about 80 words) for the same speech that evaluates the canteen
manager's concerns. **[4]**

**10.** Write the full speech to your year group, persuading them to support or oppose the proposal.
Use and evaluate ideas from both texts. Write about 250–350 words. **[25]**

**11.** The task is changed: *write a report for the head teacher on the proposal, with a
recommendation*. List the headings or sections you would use and say what would go under each.
**[3]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) fact [1]; (b) opinion [1]; (c) opinion: "obviously" presents a judgement as if it were
certain, with no figures [1].

**2.** "reckless" and "helpless" [1]; they present the planners as careless and the pensioners as
victims, so the writer is clearly against the plan and wants the reader to be outraged rather than to
weigh evidence [1].

**3.** For example [2]: "A longer lunch break would mean we could join the coding club or the football
squad without gulping our lunch, and students who travel home by bus would not miss out on clubs after
school." One mark for applying the point to students, one for a concrete development.

**4.** For example [2]: "The claim that studies 'prove' homework is useless is unconvincing: no study is
named, and 'useless' ignores the practice it gives before examinations." One mark for identifying the
weak evidence, one for a reason.

**5.** For example [2]: "I think this idea is worth a serious look." One mark for natural spoken
register, one for keeping the meaning.

**6.** Any three [3]: a title; a statement of purpose; headings or clear sections; findings or
information drawn from evidence; a conclusion or recommendation; an objective, impersonal tone.

**7.** One mark each [3]. Use: playgrounds in other schools are cleaner (Text 1). Develop: the
canteen's income keeps food prices low (Text 2), applied to students' own pockets. Evaluate: "other
schools say the playground is cleaner" is only students' opinion, with no figures; or the manager's
point that no one asked about the loss is fair and shows the proposal is incomplete.

**8.** Model [3]:

> Good morning, everyone. Look around this hall. Every one of us will buy at least one plastic bottle
> this week, and most of those bottles will be in the bin before the bell rings. Today I want to
> persuade you that we can stop that waste, and that we, not the adults, can lead the way.

Marking: speech conventions (direct address, rhetorical device) [1]; clear persuasive position [1];
source material used, not copied [1].

**9.** Model [4]:

> Our canteen manager raises fair points, and we should listen to them. Fountains do need cleaning, and
> a forgotten bottle on a hot day is a real risk. But these are problems to solve, not reasons to give
> up. A rota of student volunteers could check the fountains, and the canteen could keep a supply of
> paper cups for emergencies. The loss of income is more serious, so let us ask the council to fund the
> fountains in the first year.

Marking: concedes fairly [1]; evaluates rather than dismisses [1]; develops practical solutions [1];
persuasive register sustained [1].

**10.** Marked out of 25 [25]: 15 writing (W1–W5) and 10 reading (R3, R5). High-level responses:

- **Reading (10)**: use points from both texts; develop them (consequences for students, practical
  solutions); evaluate them (weak evidence in Text 1, fair concerns in Text 2, the missing question of
  funding).
- **Writing (15)**: speech conventions throughout (address, signposts, rhetorical questions, a call to
  action at the end); a clear persuasive structure; register suited to peers; varied vocabulary and
  sentences; accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar.

Lower-level responses retell the texts in order, use only one text, or write an essay without any
features of a speech. Either side (support or oppose) can score fully if it is argued from the
sources.

**11.** For example, three marks for sensible sections with content [3]:
- *Title and purpose*: report on the proposal to stop selling single-use plastic drinks.
- *Findings*: benefits (less litter, environmental gain, other schools' experience); concerns (cost
  and cleaning of fountains, lost bottles and health, loss of income).
- *Recommendation*: for example, a one-term trial with council funding for fountains and a review of
  canteen income.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7**: giving three "use" points; the skill is in developing and evaluating.
- **Question 9**: dismissing the manager ("she just wants money"), which is not evaluation.
- **Question 10**: forgetting the audience is your year group, so the speech sounds like a letter to
  the council.
- **Question 11**: writing paragraphs of argument with no headings or recommendation.
