These questions cover section 3.7.2 Ionizing radiation from the nucleus, statements a to j, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Questions 10 to 12 use statements h to j, which are assessed in the full Physics award only. ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by saying that radioactive decay is a random process. **[1]**

**2.** Which radiation is electromagnetic?

A: alpha

B: beta

C: gamma

D: neutron

**[1]**

**3.** A student puts a radioactive source in a freezer and says it will decay more slowly. Explain whether she is correct. **[2]**

**4.** State the change in mass number and in atomic number when a nucleus emits a beta particle. **[2]**

**5.** A detector records 180 counts in 10 minutes with no source present. With a source present it records 2856 counts in 4 minutes. Calculate the corrected count rate. **[3]**

**6.** State which radiation is the most strongly ionising and explain why it has the shortest range in air. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Complete the mass number A and atomic number Z in each equation.

**(a)** ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ᴬ_Z X + ⁴₂He **[2]**

**(b)** ¹⁴₆C → ᴬ_Z X + ⁰₋₁e **[2]**

**(c)** ᴬ_Z X → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He **[2]**

**(d)** ᴬ_Z X → ⁴⁰₂₀Ca + ⁰₋₁e **[2]**

**8.** A source is tested with absorbers. The background count rate is 20 counts per minute.

| Absorber | Count rate (counts per minute) |
|---|---|
| None | 820 |
| Paper | 815 |
| 3 mm aluminium | 260 |
| Thick lead | 30 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count rate with the aluminium in place. **[1]**

**(b)** State, with a reason, whether the source emits alpha particles. **[2]**

**(c)** Use the data to show that the source emits both beta and gamma radiation. **[3]**

**9.** Describe what each of alpha, beta and gamma radiation is, and state what a beta particle's production does to the nucleus. **[4]**

**10.** The corrected count rate of a sample is measured every 2 hours.

| Time (h) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count rate (per minute) | 800 | 566 | 400 | 283 | 200 |

**(a)** Use the data to find the half-life of the isotope. **[2]**

**(b)** Predict the count rate at 12 hours. **[2]**

**(c)** Give the definition of half-life in terms of the number of nuclei. **[1]**

**11.** A hospital worker gets a small amount of an alpha-emitting substance on his hands. Another object is irradiated by a gamma source to sterilise it.

**(a)** Explain the difference between contamination and irradiation. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the alpha-emitting substance is especially dangerous if it is swallowed. **[2]**

**(c)** State whether the sterilised object becomes radioactive. **[1]**

**(d)** Give two precautions for people working near the gamma source. **[2]**

**12.** A doctor must choose a radioactive isotope to inject into a patient so that its radiation can be detected outside the body.

| Isotope | Radiation | Half-life |
|---|---|---|
| P | Alpha | 6 hours |
| R | Gamma | 6 hours |
| S | Gamma | 30 years |

Evaluate which isotope is most suitable. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** It is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** She is wrong [1]. Decay depends on the nucleus and is not affected by physical processes such as cooling [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Mass number: no change [1]. Atomic number: increases by 1 [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Background = 180 ÷ 10 = 18 per minute [1]. With source = 2856 ÷ 4 = 714 per minute [1]. Corrected = 714 − 18 = **696 counts per minute** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Alpha [1]. It causes many ionisations, and each ionisation transfers energy from the alpha particle [1], so it loses all its energy in a short distance [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** A = 222 [1]; Z = 86 [1].

**(b)** A = 14 [1]; Z = 7 [1].

**(c)** A = 234 + 4 = 238 [1]; Z = 90 + 2 = 92 [1].

**(d)** A = 40 [1]; Z = 20 − 1 = 19 [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* In parts (c) and (d) the unknown is the parent. Add the products' numbers: for beta, 20 + (−1) = 19, so the parent has one fewer proton than the product.

**8. (a)** 260 − 20 = **240 counts per minute** [1].

**(b)** No [1]: the paper reduces the count rate only from 820 to 815, a change within random variation [1].

**(c)** The aluminium reduces the corrected rate greatly, from 795 to 240, so beta is present [1]. Radiation still passes through the aluminium [1], and thick lead reduces it to 10 per minute, so it is gamma [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Alpha: two protons and two neutrons (a helium nucleus) [1]. Beta: a high-speed electron ejected from the nucleus [1]. Gamma: electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus [1]. A neutron turns into a proton, so the atomic number increases by one [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** The count rate falls from 800 to 400 [1] in **4 hours**, so the half-life is 4 hours (check: 400 to 200 also takes 4 hours) [1].

**(b)** 12 hours is 3 half-lives [1]: 800 → 400 → 200 → **100 counts per minute** [1].

**(c)** The average time taken for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve [1]. **[5]**

**11. (a)** Contamination is the unwanted presence of radioactive atoms on or in a material, and the hazard continues while they decay [1]. Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source outside the object; it stops when the source is removed [1].

**(b)** Inside the body, the alpha particles are not stopped by skin [1], and because alpha is strongly ionising it damages nearby cells [1].

**(c)** No [1].

**(d)** Any two: use shielding such as lead; keep as far away as possible; limit the time spent near the source; handle with remote tools or tongs [1] [1]. **[7]**

**12.** P is unsuitable, because alpha cannot pass through the body to be detected outside, and it would strongly ionise cells inside [1]. S is unsuitable, because a 30-year half-life means the patient stays radioactive for a very long time [1]. R is gamma, which passes out of the body to the detector and ionises weakly [1], and its 6-hour half-life is long enough to take measurements but short enough that activity soon falls, so **R** is most suitable [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not converting counts to rates before subtracting background.
- Making the atomic number fall in beta decay.
- Treating small changes of a few counts as absorption.
- Saying that an irradiated object becomes radioactive.
- Choosing a source by type alone without considering half-life.
- Describing a beta particle as "an electron from the atom" instead of from the nucleus.
