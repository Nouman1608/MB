Condensed recall for specification section **3.2.2** of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Language
(9670). For explanations, a full plan and a model paragraph, use the
[Language and Social Groups – Writing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-section-b-language-and-social-groups-writing/).

## The task in brief

- One compulsory writing task, 25 of Unit 2's 50 marks.
- Focus: **the skills of academic argument in written language**.
- The essay discusses **an aspect of language and social group behaviour** that **connects with the Section A data**.
- Unit 2 weights AO1 most heavily, then AO2, then AO3: concepts and terminology, data analysis, controlled writing.
- Read the Section B question before writing Section A.

## Academic argument: the components

1. **Thesis**: a one-sentence answer, in the introduction.
2. **Claims**: topic sentences that each advance the thesis.
3. **Evidence**: quoted Section A data and accurate research findings.
4. **Analysis**: how the evidence supports the claim.
5. **Counter-argument**: limitation, alternative explanation, contrary study.
6. **Evaluation**: how far the claim holds.
7. **Conclusion**: a refined, direct answer.

## Not academic argument

- A summary of the Section A analysis.
- An opinion piece without linguistic evidence.
- A list of theories not applied to the question.

## Paragraph template

**Claim** (answers the question) → **data** (short quotation, named feature) → **concept or study** → **analysis** →
**qualification** (hedge or counter-point) → **link** to thesis.

## Academic register toolkit

| Need | Language |
|---|---|
| Hedge | suggests, may indicate, appears to, to some extent, tends to |
| Report a finding | found, showed, observed, recorded |
| Report a contested view | claims, argues, proposes, asserts |
| Contrast | however, by contrast, whereas, nevertheless |
| Consequence | therefore, consequently, as a result |
| Concession | although, admittedly, while it is true that |
| Evaluation | this complicates, this only partly supports, this challenges |

Use **precise terms**: asymmetrical address, covert prestige, convergence, positive face, in-group lexis.

## Studies to cite accurately

| Study | Finding to state |
|---|---|
| Labov (1966), New York | Post-vocalic /r/ linked to the status of the department store |
| Trudgill (1974), Norwich | Some men over-reported non-standard forms: covert prestige |
| Milroy (1980), Belfast | Dense, close-knit networks maintained vernacular forms |
| Eckert (1989), Detroit-area high school | "Jocks" and "burnouts" marked group identity through variation |
| Giles | Convergence and divergence in accommodation theory |
| Brown and Levinson (1987) | Positive and negative face; politeness strategies |
| Lakoff (1975) / Tannen (1990) / Cameron (2007) | Deficit, difference, and critique of gendered-language claims |
| Wareing | Instrumental and influential power |

Only cite what you can state correctly. A misremembered study weakens AO1.

## Turning Section A observations into evidence

- **Observation**: a feature in the text ("the coach signs off 'Regards, Mr Patel'").
- **Evidence**: what it shows about social group behaviour ("formal sign-off maintains role distance within a group
  that otherwise uses first names").
- Select data that **supports**, **complicates** and **contradicts** the thesis.

## Handling the question's key word

Words like **mainly, always, only, entirely, simply** are the evaluation point. Plan to test them: "mainly" invites
you to show other functions; "always" invites a counter-example.

## Introductions and conclusions

**Introduction** (three moves): define the key term in the question; state the thesis; signal the route.

> *Group membership is signalled through shared ways of speaking. This essay argues that language often marks
> belonging, but that the data also show speakers using it to claim status within the group, so the view is only
> partly persuasive.*

**Conclusion** (three moves): answer the question directly; name the strongest evidence; state the limit of the claim.

> *Language does mark membership, as the in-group lexis shows; yet the captain's imperatives reveal hierarchy inside
> the same group. Belonging is one function among several, not the main one.*

## Five-minute plan

- Underline the key claim and the qualifying word.
- Write the thesis.
- Note three claims with one piece of Section A data and one concept or study each.
- Note one counter-argument.
- Note the final judgement.

## Exam traps

- No thesis; the essay drifts.
- Section A analysis repeated rather than used.
- Research named without findings, or findings invented.
- No counter-argument.
- Informal or vague register.
- The conclusion repeats the introduction.
- The key qualifying word in the question ignored.

## Self-test

1. What does the specification say Section B focuses on?
2. What must the essay connect with?
3. How does Section B differ from Unit 1's Section B?
4. What is a thesis, and where should it appear?
5. Give three hedging expressions.
6. Why choose "claims" rather than "found" for some research?
7. What did Milroy's Belfast study suggest?
8. What is the difference between an observation and evidence?
9. Why is a counter-argument essential?
10. How should you treat the word "mainly" in a question?
11. Why read the Section B question before writing Section A?

**Answers:** 1. The skills of academic argument in written language. 2. The data analysed in Section A. 3. Unit 1's
Section B is directed writing that transforms source material into a new text; Unit 2's is an academic essay
arguing about language and social groups. 4. A one-sentence answer to the question, given in the introduction.
5. Any three: suggests, may indicate, appears to, to some extent, tends to. 6. "Claims" signals that the view is
contested, while "found" presents a result as evidence. 7. That speakers in dense, close-knit networks maintained
local vernacular forms more. 8. An observation identifies a feature in the text; evidence explains what that feature
shows about the question. 9. Evaluation depends on weighing the claim against alternatives; without a
counter-argument, there is no judgement. 10. As the point to evaluate: show what else language does besides the
function claimed. 11. So that your Section A analysis gathers the evidence your essay needs.
