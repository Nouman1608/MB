## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a merit good and why its classification depends on a value judgement. **[4]**

**2.** In Country C, adult evening courses are supplied by private colleges. Q is in thousands of places and values are in currency units (cu). Marginal private benefit is P = 54 − 3Q, marginal social benefit is 66 − 3Q and marginal cost is 6 + Q.

**(a)** Find market output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the optimum output and the marginal cost there. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the welfare gain from moving to the optimum. **[3]**

**3.** A householder keeps a well-kept front garden that neighbours enjoy. Explain why this product creates a positive externality in consumption but is not normally called a merit good. **[4]**

**4.** In Country D, online betting has marginal private benefit P = 70 − 5Q, marginal social benefit 58 − 5Q and marginal cost 10 + Q (Q in thousands of bets).

**(a)** Find the output and price the market produces. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the output that maximises social welfare. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the deadweight loss. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how imperfect information can lead to over-provision of a demerit good such as an energy drink. **[5]**

**6.** In a market for pre-owned bicycles in Country E, 80 of 200 are good and 120 are poor. Buyers value a good one at 500 and a poor one at 250. Sellers of good ones will accept at least 420; sellers of poor ones at least 200. Buyers cannot tell them apart.

**(a)** Calculate a buyer's expected value of a bicycle. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what happens to the market. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the gains from trade lost. **[2]**

**7.** A single firm in Country A faces demand P = 84 − 3Q with constant marginal cost 24 and no fixed cost (Q in thousands).

**(a)** Find the output and price if the market were competitive. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the profit-maximising output and price of the monopolist. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate its supernormal profit. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the deadweight loss. **[2]**

**8.** Suppose instead that, in the market in question 7, a fragmented industry of small firms would have constant marginal cost 60, whereas the single large firm has marginal cost 24. Assess whether monopoly is the worse outcome. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the immobility of factors of production can cause market failure. **[4]**

**10.** In Region P of Country G a factory closes and 450 workers become unemployed. Average annual output per worker is 28,000 cu. Region Q has 300 vacancies needing different skills. A scheme retrains 120 of the workers at 6,500 cu each.

**(a)** Calculate the annual output lost while all 450 are idle. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many months it takes the extra output of the retrained workers to cover the scheme's cost. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the vacancies still unfilled. **[1]**

**11.** A government classifies online betting as a demerit good. Evaluate whether this classification is justified. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A merit good is one judged to be good for consumers [1], which would be under-consumed in a free market because consumers underestimate its benefit or because it creates external benefits [1]. Whether it is good for people is an opinion about what is better, not a measurable fact [1]. People and governments disagree about this, and views change over time [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition on its own is usually not enough here; the second half of the question asks for the reason a judgement is involved, so say who is judging and what they are judging.

**2. (a)** 54 − 3Q = 6 + Q gives 4Q = 48, so Q = 12 [1] and P = 6 + 12 = 18 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 66 − 3Q = 6 + Q gives 4Q = 60, so Q = 15 [1] and marginal cost is 21 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At Q = 12, MSB = 66 − 36 = 30 and MC = 18, a gap of 12 [1]. Gain = ½ × (15 − 12) × 12 [1] = **18 thousand cu** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing each equation you set equal makes it clear which curves you used; a correct triangle height can only be found if both curves are evaluated at the market quantity.

**3.** Neighbours gain benefit without paying, so there is an external benefit in consumption [1]. A merit good requires a judgement that consumers themselves would be better off consuming more [1]. The householder knows the garden's value and benefits from it directly, and nobody argues that they under-consume it for their own good [1]. Vaccination differs: it creates an external benefit and people are thought to undervalue it, so it is both [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A clear contrast with a genuine merit good helps to show you understand the specification's point that an externality alone does not make a product a merit good.

**4. (a)** 70 − 5Q = 10 + Q gives 6Q = 60, so Q = 10 [1] and P = 20 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 58 − 5Q = 10 + Q gives 6Q = 48, so Q = 8 [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** At Q = 10, MC = 20 and MSB = 8, a gap of 12 [1]. Loss = ½ × (10 − 8) × 12 [1] = **12 thousand cu** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For a demerit good the triangle lies between the optimum and market output; if your answer is negative or larger than the whole surplus, check you subtracted in the right order.

**5.** Consumers may underestimate the long-term harm of the drink, perhaps because the effects are delayed or advertising plays them down [1]. Their perceived private benefit is therefore higher than the true private benefit [1]. Demand is higher than it would be with full information [1]. The market produces where perceived marginal private benefit equals marginal cost, which is more than the output where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost [1]. Resources are over-allocated to the product and there is a welfare loss [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name the curve that is wrong (perceived demand) and the consequence for output, rather than only saying that consumers are not well informed.

**6. (a)** 0.4 × 500 = 200 [1] and 0.6 × 250 = 150, so the expected value is **350** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 350 is below 420, so sellers of good bicycles withdraw [1]. Only poor bicycles are traded, since buyers' value of 250 exceeds the 200 reserve [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 80 × (500 − 420) [1] = **6,400** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain the market's response in the sequence the information creates; a bare statement that the market fails earns less than showing that good sellers leave.

**7. (a)** 84 − 3Q = 24 gives Q = 20 [1] and P = 24 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MR = 84 − 6Q [1]. 84 − 6Q = 24 gives Q = 10 [1]. P = 84 − 30 = 54 [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** (54 − 24) × 10 = **300** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** ½ × (20 − 10) × (54 − 24) [1] = **150** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is to set MR equal to MC but then read price from the MR curve; take the quantity up to the demand curve.

**8.** Fragmented competition gives 84 − 3Q = 60, so Q = 8 at P = 60 [1] [1]. The monopoly gives Q = 10 at P = 54 [1]. Consumers pay less and receive more under the monopoly, so the failure is smaller than in a simple comparison [1]. The conclusion depends on the cost saving being real and lasting, and on entry being a threat; a protected monopolist would not necessarily stay at 54, and the advantage disappears if rivals' cost were below 54 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that states the condition under which it holds (the cost difference and the lack of entry threat) is usually more convincing than a general statement that monopolies are good or bad.

**9.** Occupational immobility means workers' skills do not transfer easily to expanding sectors [1]. Geographical immobility means housing costs, family ties or information costs prevent moving to where jobs are [1]. As a result unemployment persists in some areas or occupations while vacancies remain unfilled elsewhere [1]. Prices (wages) signal where factors are needed but resources do not respond, so output is lost and resources are misallocated [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give four distinct ideas; repeating the two types in different words does not add the consequence.

**10. (a)** 450 × 28,000 [1] = **12,600,000 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cost = 120 × 6,500 = 780,000 [1]. Extra output = 120 × 28,000 = 3,360,000 a year [1]. 780,000 / 3,360,000 × 12 = **about 2.8 months** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 300 − 120 = **180** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the payback to months at the end rather than rounding earlier, and include units, because a bare number can be read as years or months.

**11.** A demerit good is judged harmful and over-consumed [1]. Betting may impose costs on families, a negative externality in consumption [1]. Gamblers may underestimate their losses or the risk of addiction, an information problem [1]. On a diagram, marginal social benefit lies below marginal private benefit, so market output exceeds the optimum and there is a deadweight loss [1]. Yet the classification is a value judgement, and others value individual freedom [1]. Many adults bet with full knowledge for enjoyment, so some of the output may be efficient [1]. The size of the harm is uncertain and differs between people [1]. Better information might correct the problem without restricting the good [1]. Judgement: the label is justified where there is evidence of external cost or systematic misjudgement, but the extent of harm should decide the response, not the label itself [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation scores better when it ends in a reasoned judgement about the case given, rather than listing arguments for and against.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating every externality good as a merit or demerit good.
- Giving a classification as fact, with no mention of a value judgement.
- Reading price from the marginal revenue curve instead of the demand curve.
- Using price instead of marginal cost for the height of a monopoly deadweight loss.
- Calculating the welfare triangle from the wrong output levels.
- Explaining information failure without naming what quantity is misallocated.
- Treating immobility as a labour-only issue with no stated consequence.
- Evaluating with a list, without a final judgement and a reason for it.
