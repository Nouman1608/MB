## 10A General principles

### Classifying reactions (10.1)

- **Addition:** two molecules join to give one product.
- **Elimination:** a small molecule (HBr, H₂O) is removed and a C=C forms.
- **Substitution:** one atom or group is swapped for another.
- **Oxidation / reduction:** the organic compound gains oxygen or loses hydrogen / gains hydrogen or loses oxygen. Combustion is oxidation.
- **Hydrolysis:** a bond is broken by reaction with water (or hydroxide ions).
- **Polymerisation:** many monomers join into a long chain.

Some reactions fit two labels: hydrogenating an alkene is addition and reduction.

**Worked example 1.** Classify each reaction.

1. Cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane (nickel catalyst, heat). This is **addition** (and **reduction**).
2. CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + KOH(aq) → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + KBr: **substitution**, also called **hydrolysis**.
3. CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH (in ethanol) → CH₃CH=CH₂ + KBr + H₂O: **elimination**.
4. n CH₂=CHCl → poly(chloroethene): **polymerisation**.
5. C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl (UV light): **substitution** (free-radical, from Unit 1).

### Mechanisms and bond breaking (10.2, 10.3)

A **reaction mechanism** is the sequence of steps by which reactants become products: which bonds break, which form, and where the electrons go. A curly arrow shows an electron pair moving. It starts at a lone pair or a bond.

- **Homolytic fission:** each atom keeps one electron, giving two **radicals**.
- **Heterolytic fission:** one atom takes both electrons, giving a positive and a negative species.

Heterolytic fission gives the species of ionic mechanisms. An **electrophile** accepts an electron pair to form a covalent bond (H⁺, or the δ+ H of HBr). A **nucleophile** is its electron-rich partner.

### Nucleophiles (10.4)

**Definition:** a nucleophile is a species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond with an electron-deficient atom.

Examples here: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃ and H₂O. Ammonia and water show that a nucleophile need not be charged.

### Bond polarity and mechanism type (10.5)

| Bond in the organic molecule | Electron distribution | Attacked by | Typical mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–H, C–C in alkanes | Non-polar | Radicals | Free-radical substitution |
| C=C in alkenes | Electron-rich π bond | Electrophiles | Electrophilic addition |
| C–X in halogenoalkanes | Polar, C is δ+ | Nucleophiles | Nucleophilic substitution |

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon of C–X is δ+ and attracts the nucleophile's lone pair.

## 10B Halogenoalkanes

### Names and formulae (10.6)

Use the longest chain, the prefixes fluoro, chloro, bromo and iodo, the lowest locants, and alphabetical order.

**Worked example 2.**

- CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂I: four-carbon chain numbered from the iodine end, **1-iodo-2-methylbutane**. Skeletal: a four-carbon zigzag, a branch at carbon 2, "I" at the end of carbon 1.
- CH₃CHBrCH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃: locants 2,3 beat 3,4, so **2-bromo-3-methylpentane**.
- (CH₃)₂CClCH₂CH₂CH₃: **2-chloro-2-methylpentane**.

A displayed formula shows every atom and bond. In a skeletal formula, lines stand for the carbon chain and only the halogen is labelled.

### Primary, secondary and tertiary (10.7)

Count the carbons bonded to the C–X carbon: one (or none) is **primary**, two is **secondary**, three is **tertiary**. In worked example 2 the three compounds are primary, secondary and tertiary in that order.

### The five reactions (10.8)

For 1-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br:

| Reagent and conditions | Role of the reagent | Organic product | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| KOH(aq), heat under reflux | OH⁻ is a nucleophile | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH, 2-methylpropan-1-ol | Substitution (hydrolysis) |
| KOH dissolved in ethanol, heat under reflux | OH⁻ is a base | (CH₃)₂C=CH₂, 2-methylpropene | Elimination |
| Aqueous AgNO₃ in ethanol, warm | H₂O is a nucleophile | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH, plus a cream AgBr precipitate | Substitution (hydrolysis) |
| Ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube under pressure | NH₃ is a nucleophile | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂NH₂, 2-methylpropylamine (2-methylpropan-1-amine) | Substitution |
| KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux | CN⁻ is a nucleophile | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CN, 3-methylbutanenitrile | Substitution |

Two equations to learn in full: RBr + 2NH₃ → RNH₂ + NH₄Br, and RBr + H₂O → ROH + H⁺ + Br⁻ followed by Ag⁺ + Br⁻ → AgBr.

KOH gives two products. Water as solvent favours substitution; ethanol favours elimination, where OH⁻ pulls off an H⁺ from a carbon adjacent to the one carrying bromine. A secondary halogenoalkane can give more than one alkene.

**The nitrile reaction lengthens the carbon chain by one.** Count the CN carbon when naming: CH₃CH₂CN is propanenitrile.

**Worked example 3.** 5.48 g of 1-bromo-2-methylpropane is heated with excess KCN in ethanol. The yield is 75%. Find the mass of nitrile. Use H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, Br = 79.9.

1. M(C₄H₉Br) = 136.9 g mol⁻¹.
2. n = 5.48 ÷ 136.9 = 0.0400 mol.
3. The ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.0400 mol of C₅H₉N, M = 83.0 g mol⁻¹.
4. Theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 83.0 = 3.32 g.
5. Actual mass = 3.32 × 0.75 = **2.49 g**.

### Mechanisms with primary halogenoalkanes (10.9)

**Hydroxide ions with 1-bromopentane.**

1. Show the dipole Cδ+–Brδ−.
2. One curly arrow starts at an oxygen lone pair of OH⁻ and ends at the δ+ carbon; another moves the C–Br bonding pair onto Br.
3. Products: CH₃(CH₂)₄OH and Br⁻.

**Ammonia with iodoethane.**

1. Dipole Cδ+–Iδ−. The nitrogen lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon while the C–I bonding pair moves onto iodine, giving CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ and I⁻.
2. A second NH₃ removes H⁺: arrow from its lone pair to an H on the NH₃⁺ group, and from that N–H bond to N.
3. Products: ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂, and NH₄⁺.

Hence two moles of ammonia. Excess ammonia reduces further substitution of the amine. The specification says SN1 and SN2 are tested in Unit 4.

### Comparing rates of hydrolysis (10.10, 10.12, 10.13)

Ethanol is a common solvent, because halogenoalkanes do not mix with water. As water substitutes the halogen, halide ions meet Ag⁺ and precipitate: AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow. Rate is taken as proportional to 1/time.

**Trend with the halogen (10.13).** Rate: iodoalkane > bromoalkane > chloroalkane. Use these mean bond enthalpies in kJ mol⁻¹: C–Cl 346, C–Br 290, C–I 228. The C–I bond is the weakest, so it breaks most easily. Bond polarity predicts the opposite order (C–Cl is the most polar), so bond enthalpy, not polarity, controls the rate.

**Trend with structure (10.12).** Rate: tertiary > secondary > primary. Know the order and show it from data; the reasons come in Unit 4.

**Worked example 4.** Equal amounts of three isomers, C₅H₁₁Br, were each added to silver nitrate in ethanol at 50 °C.

| Isomer | Class | Time for cream precipitate / s | 1/t / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-bromopentane | 1° | 210 | 4.76 × 10⁻³ |
| 2-bromopentane | 2° | 95 | 1.05 × 10⁻² |
| 2-bromo-2-methylbutane | 3° | 8 | 1.25 × 10⁻¹ |

The tertiary isomer reacts about 26 times faster than the primary (210 ÷ 8 = 26.25). In a second run 1-iodopentane took 35 s, so it reacts 6.0 times faster than 1-bromopentane.

### Core Practical 5 (10.11)

1. Warm tubes of ethanol plus aqueous silver nitrate, and tubes of each halogenoalkane, in a water bath at a fixed temperature such as 50 °C.
2. Mix, start the timer and record the time for a precipitate to appear.

Keep temperature, volumes, silver nitrate concentration and amount of halogenoalkane the same. Ethanol is flammable, so use a water bath, not a flame.

### Core Practical 6 (10.14)

(CH₃)₃COH + HCl → (CH₃)₃CCl + H₂O

The tertiary alcohol reacts at room temperature.

1. Shake the alcohol with excess concentrated hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel. Release the pressure often.
2. Allow the layers to separate. 2-chloro-2-methylpropane is less dense than water (about 0.85 g cm⁻³), so it floats on top. Drain away the aqueous layer below it.
3. Shake with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to neutralise leftover acid, venting the CO₂, then shake with water.
4. Dry with anhydrous sodium sulfate until clear.
5. Distil, collecting the fraction that boils near 51 °C.

**Worked example 5.** 12.0 cm³ of 2-methylpropan-2-ol (density 0.78 g cm⁻³) gives 8.10 g of 2-chloro-2-methylpropane. Find the percentage yield. Use H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5.

1. Mass of alcohol = 12.0 × 0.78 = 9.36 g.
2. M(C₄H₁₀O) = 74.0 g mol⁻¹, so n = 9.36 ÷ 74.0 = 0.1265 mol.
3. M(C₄H₉Cl) = 92.5 g mol⁻¹. Theoretical mass = 0.1265 × 92.5 = 11.70 g.
4. Percentage yield = 8.10 ÷ 11.70 × 100 = **69.2%**.

### Further suggested practicals

To identify the halogen, warm the halogenoalkane with NaOH(aq) to release the halide ion. Acidify with dilute nitric acid to remove OH⁻, which would otherwise give brown silver oxide. Add silver nitrate and note the colour. Ammonia confirms: AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia, AgBr dissolves in concentrated ammonia, and AgI does not dissolve.

1-bromobutane is made by heating butan-1-ol with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid, which form HBr in the flask.

## Common errors

- Calling OH⁻ a nucleophile in the ethanolic KOH reaction, or writing "KOH" with no solvent.
- Starting the first curly arrow from the minus sign instead of a lone pair.
- Leaving out the second ammonia molecule and NH₄⁺ in the amine mechanism.
- Explaining the chloro, bromo, iodo trend by polarity. The specification links it to bond enthalpy.
- Naming CH₃CH₂CN as ethanenitrile. Count the CN carbon: propanenitrile.
- Placing 2-chloro-2-methylpropane in the lower layer of the separating funnel.
