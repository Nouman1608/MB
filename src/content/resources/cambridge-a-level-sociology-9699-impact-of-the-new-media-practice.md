This set covers section 9.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: the new
media and globalisation, challenges to power, digital optimism and pessimism, and effects on
identities and relationships. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations
and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *digital divide*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the **three** digital divides described by Norris (2001). **[3]**

**3.** Explain what Appadurai meant by *mediascapes*. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which the new media contributes to globalisation. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by *slacktivism*. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what Fuchs meant by *digital labour*. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the concept of *context collapse* helps us understand online identity. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what Madianou and Miller meant by *polymedia*. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which the new media may challenge existing power structures. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which the new media may affect interpersonal relationships. **[8]**

**11.** "The new media has brought more benefits than harms to society." Evaluate this view.
**[35]**

**12.** "The new media allows individuals to create their own identities freely." Evaluate this
view. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** Unequal access to, and use of, digital technologies [1] between countries or between social
groups within a country [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The global divide (between richer and poorer countries) [1]; the social divide (within
countries) [1]; the democratic divide (between those who use digital resources to participate in
public life and those who do not) [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Global flows of media images and stories [1] that people draw on to imagine lives and
possibilities elsewhere [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Identify [1], explain [1], example [1]: instant communication across borders [1] lets
migrants keep daily contact with families and follow homeland news [1], sustaining transnational
identities and diaspora communities [1]. Also creditable: global platforms spreading brands and
popular culture; local content reaching global audiences (glocalisation); cross-border campaigns.
**[3]**

**5.** Low-effort online support for a cause, such as liking or sharing [1], which may replace
rather than lead to sustained political action (Morozov) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Users' online activity, such as posting, liking and sharing, is unpaid [1] but generates the
data and attention that platforms sell to advertisers [1]; from a Marxist view this is a form of
exploitation that benefits platform owners [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Context collapse is the merging online of audiences normally kept separate, such as family,
friends and employers [1]. A post is seen by all of them at once [1], so users must manage one
identity for many audiences, which can lead to self-censorship or carefully neutral
self-presentation [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Polymedia is the environment in which people can choose between many media, such as calls,
messages and video [1]; the choice of medium itself communicates feeling [1]. In Madianou and
Miller's study of Filipino migrant mothers it helped sustain transnational family relationships but
could also increase conflict and monitoring [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a study or concept [1],
example or evaluation [1].
- **Networked protest** [1]: social media allow people to share grievances and coordinate action
  without traditional organisations [1]; Castells (2012) analysed the Arab Spring and Occupy in
  these terms [1]; however, many such movements struggled to build lasting organisations [1].
- **Exposure through leaks and citizen journalism** [1]: individuals can publish evidence that
  editors or states might suppress [1]; WikiLeaks (2010) and the Snowden disclosures (2013) spread
  worldwide through new media [1]; states responded with prosecutions, showing the limits of the
  challenge [1].
- Also creditable: hashtag campaigns such as #MeToo (2017); bypassing traditional media
  gatekeepers. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], example or evaluation [1].
- **Sustaining long-distance ties** [1]: video calls and messaging let families and friends keep
  daily contact across borders [1]; Madianou and Miller's polymedia concept (2012) [1]; but the
  same tools can increase monitoring and conflict [1].
- **Networked individualism** [1]: people belong to loose personal networks rather than tightly
  bound groups [1]; Rainie and Wellman (2012) [1]; this gives choice but may weaken local
  community ties [1].
- Also creditable: cyberbullying extending conflict beyond school; constant connection weakening
  face-to-face intimacy. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level weighs optimist and pessimist arguments across several
areas (democracy, information, economy, relationships) and considers who benefits. Indicative
content:
- **Benefits (digital optimists):** Shirky (2008) on easy collective action; wider access to
  information; marginalised voices; networked protest (Castells, 2012); transnational family
  ties (Madianou and Miller, 2012); low-cost global markets for small producers.
- **Harms (digital pessimists):** Sunstein's echo chambers (2001); misinformation; state and
  corporate surveillance; digital labour (Fuchs, 2014); slacktivism (Morozov); cyberbullying;
  digital divide (Norris, 2001).
- **Evaluation:** benefits and harms are unevenly distributed by class, gender, age and country;
  the same tools serve activists and governments; "society" is not one group.
- **Judgement:** for example, the new media is a contested space whose benefits go mainly to
  those with access, skills and power. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level contrasts postmodern views of free identity choice with
interactionist, structural and comparative evidence. Indicative content:
- **For the view:** postmodernists see online spaces as allowing people to try out and combine
  identities; communities of interest for sexuality, fandom, faith or politics; control over
  self-presentation through editing and curation.
- **Against the view:** Goffman (1959) — self-presentation is a performance shaped by audiences'
  expectations; context collapse (Marwick and boyd, 2011) limits freedom; Nakamura's identity
  tourism reproduces stereotypes; platform design and algorithms reward particular kinds of
  self-presentation; Miller et al. (2016) show local culture shapes how social media are used;
  the digital divide limits who can participate.
- **Evaluation:** identity online is chosen within social and technical constraints; offline
  inequalities follow people online.
- **Judgement:** for example, new media widen the range of identity resources but do not make
  identity free from social structure. **[35]**
