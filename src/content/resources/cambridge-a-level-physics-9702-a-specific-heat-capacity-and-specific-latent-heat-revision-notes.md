Subtopic 14.3 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers the definition and use of specific heat capacity and specific latent heat, and the distinction between latent heat of fusion and of vaporisation. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [study guide for this subtopic](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-specific-heat-capacity-and-specific-latent-heat/); to apply the ideas, try the [practice questions for this subtopic](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-specific-heat-capacity-and-specific-latent-heat-practice/).

## Definitions

- **Specific heat capacity c**: the energy required per unit mass to raise the temperature by one degree (1 K).
- **Specific latent heat L**: the energy required per unit mass to change the state of a substance without a change in temperature.
- **Specific latent heat of fusion L_f**: for solid ↔ liquid.
- **Specific latent heat of vaporisation L_v**: for liquid ↔ gas.

```
Q = mcΔθ          c in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
Q = mL            L in J kg⁻¹
electrical energy: Q = VIt = Pt
```

## Fusion versus vaporisation

| | Fusion | Vaporisation |
|---|---|---|
| Change | Melting / freezing | Boiling / condensing |
| Molecular separation | Little change; structure breaks down | Large increase; nearly all attractions overcome |
| Volume change | Small | Very large; work done against the atmosphere |
| Water | 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ | 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ (about 7 × L_f) |

During a change of state the temperature is constant: the energy increases molecular **potential** energy, not mean kinetic energy.

## Heating curve at constant power P

```
sloping section:  gradient Δθ/Δt = P / (mc)   →   c = P / (m × gradient)
flat section:     L = PΔt / m
```

- Steeper slope ⇒ smaller c.
- Longer plateau ⇒ larger L.
- For water, the boiling plateau is about 7 times as long as the melting plateau.

## Multi-stage method

1. List the stages (warm solid, melt, warm liquid, boil, warm vapour).
2. Use Q = mcΔθ with the right c for each warming stage.
3. Use Q = mL with L_f or L_v for each change of state.
4. Add the energies; divide by power for time.

Example: 0.50 kg ice at –15 °C to water at 40 °C: 15 750 + 167 000 + 84 000 = 2.7 × 10⁵ J.

## Energy losses

- With losses, the energy supplied is more than the sample absorbs, so calculated c or L is **too large**.
- **Two-power method** (boiling liquid): run at P₁ and P₂ for equal times t; losses h cancel:

```
L = (P₁ – P₂) t / (m₁ – m₂)
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specific heat capacity | Energy per unit mass per unit temperature rise |
| Specific latent heat | Energy per unit mass to change state at constant temperature |
| Fusion | Melting (solid to liquid) |
| Vaporisation | Boiling (liquid to gas) |
| Heating curve | Graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at constant power |
| Plateau | Flat section of a heating curve, during a change of state |

## Exam traps

- Leaving "per unit mass" out of either definition.
- Leaving "without a change in temperature" out of the latent heat definition.
- One value of c for both ice and water.
- Applying Q = mcΔθ during a change of state.
- Using L_f in a boiling question.
- Grams not converted to kilograms.
- Claiming losses make the calculated value too small.
- Saying no energy is supplied during the plateau.

## Self-test

1. Define specific heat capacity.
2. Define specific latent heat of vaporisation.
3. State the unit of specific latent heat.
4. How much energy raises the temperature of 2.0 kg of aluminium (c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) by 15 K?
5. How much energy melts 0.25 kg of ice at 0 °C?
6. Give two reasons why L_v is greater than L_f for water.
7. Why does temperature stay constant while a substance boils?
8. A 2.0 kW kettle boils water steadily. How much water boils away in 60 s, ignoring losses?
9. On a heating curve at constant power, the liquid section is less steep than the solid section. Which state has the larger c?
10. 0.40 kg of a solid melts in 200 s with a 150 W heater. Find L_f.
11. In an experiment to measure c, energy is lost to the surroundings. Is the calculated value too large or too small?
12. Write the two-power equation for L and explain why the losses cancel.

The liquid (gradient = P/mc, so a smaller gradient means a larger c). 10. L_f = Pt/m = 150 × 200 ÷ 0.40 = 7.5 × 10⁴ J kg⁻¹. 11. Too large: more energy is supplied than the sample absorbs. 12.
