**This guide covers one of the two question types in Paper 2 Drama.** The syllabus states: "There
is a choice of two questions on each text: either (a) a passage-based question or (b) an essay
question. Candidates must answer one passage-based question and one essay question", and "Relevant
passages are printed on the question paper." The
[Paper 2 Drama study guide](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-2-drama/) explains the paper's
format and the one-of-each rule; this page teaches the method for the (a) question itself: how to
read a printed extract from a play and turn it into a 25-mark answer. It uses an original scene,
because set plays change by year.

## What the passage-based question asks

The question prints an extract of a page or two from your set play and asks about it with a focus:
how the playwright makes a moment dramatic, tense, revealing, amusing or moving, or how a character
or relationship is presented at that point. The question is about **this** extract. Knowledge of the
whole play still matters, but only to explain why the moment matters: what the audience already
knows, what has just happened, and what is about to follow.

The four assessment objectives apply exactly as on every 0475 paper. In a passage answer they tend to
show up like this:

- **AO1:** precise, short quotations from across the extract, including its final lines.
- **AO2:** understanding of what the moment reveals about characters, relationships and themes.
- **AO3:** the playwright's methods: dialogue, stage directions, entrances and exits, structure of the
  extract, and, in verse drama, shifts between verse and prose.
- **AO4:** your response to the question's key word, sustained across the answer.

## Step 1: place the extract

Begin with two sentences that place the moment. Ask:

- What has the audience just seen?
- What does the audience know that a character on stage does not (dramatic irony), or the reverse?
- Is this moment a turning point, a climax, a calm before a crisis, or a consequence?

This is not a summary of the plot. It is a lens: it tells the examiner why the details you analyse
are significant.

## Step 2: read the extract as a performance

A play is written to be watched and heard. As you annotate, picture the stage.

- **Who is on stage, and who is not?** An absent character can dominate a scene if everyone is
  talking about them.
- **Entrances and exits.** An interruption by a new arrival often changes the balance of power or
  breaks the tension at its height.
- **Stage directions.** Pauses, silences, movements, props, lighting and sound are the playwright's
  instructions to the audience's eye and ear. Treat them as language to analyse, not as background.
- **Who speaks most?** Long speeches can show control or desperation; short lines can show tension,
  evasion or anger.
- **Questions and answers.** Unanswered questions, changes of subject and repeated words reveal what
  characters are avoiding.
- **Asides and soliloquies.** They let the audience into a character's mind and often create dramatic
  irony.
- **Verse and prose.** In plays that use both, a character's move from verse into prose, or a broken
  verse line, can mark a loss of control or a change of status.

## Step 3: track the shape of the extract

Extracts are chosen because something changes within them. Divide the extract into two or three
stages and note where the tension rises or falls. Strong answers follow this movement, so that the
reader of your essay sees the scene develop. Always reach the end: the final lines frequently contain
the reversal, the exit or the image that the question is pointing towards.

## Worked example: an original scene

The scene below was written for this guide. It is not from a set play. Imagine it comes midway through
a play in which the audience already knows that Ruth has spent the family's savings on a ticket to
leave the country, which her father has not yet discovered.

> **From *The Long Field*** *(original scene)*
>
> *A farm kitchen at night. One lamp. ELIAS sits at the table with the accounts book. RUTH enters
> quietly, her coat already on.*
>
> **ELIAS:** You're up late.
>
> **RUTH:** I couldn't sleep.
>
> **ELIAS:** *(not looking up)* Nor me. The figures won't add. Thirty years I've kept this book and
> they've always added.
>
> **RUTH:** Perhaps you've made a mistake.
>
> **ELIAS:** I don't make mistakes with money. *(He turns a page.)* Someone's taken from the tin.
>
> *Pause. RUTH does not move.*
>
> **RUTH:** Who would do that?
>
> **ELIAS:** That's what I'm asking. *(He looks up for the first time and sees her coat.)* Where are
> you going?
>
> **RUTH:** Nowhere. It's cold.
>
> *ELIAS closes the book slowly. Outside, a car horn sounds twice. Neither of them moves.*

**Question:** How does the playwright make this such a tense moment in the play?

**What a strong candidate annotates:**
- *Opening stage direction:* "One lamp" and night create a small pool of light in which secrets
  feel exposed; Ruth's coat is "already on", so the audience sees the clue before Elias does.
- *Dramatic irony:* the audience knows Ruth took the money, so every innocent line from Elias tightens
  the tension.
- *Short exchanges:* the clipped dialogue ("You're up late." / "I couldn't sleep.") keeps both
  characters guarded.
- *"(not looking up)":* Elias's attention on the book delays the moment of discovery; when he
  "looks up for the first time", the stage direction marks the turning point of the extract.
- *Ruth's question:* "Who would do that?" answers a question with a question, a form of evasion.
- *The lie:* "Nowhere. It's cold." Two short sentences that the coat contradicts; the audience sees
  her lie.
- *Ending:* the car horn "twice" signals that someone is waiting for Ruth; "Neither of them moves"
  freezes the scene at maximum tension.

**Model paragraph:**

> The playwright builds tension by letting the audience see what Elias does not. Ruth enters with her
> coat "already on", so from the first stage direction the audience knows she is leaving, while Elias
> stays "not looking up", absorbed in the accounts. His certainty that "I don't make mistakes with
> money" becomes painful to watch, because we know the mistake is not his. When he finally "looks up
> for the first time", the stage direction turns the scene: the coat that the audience has watched
> all along now becomes evidence. Ruth's reply, "Nowhere. It's cold.", is so brief that it sounds
> rehearsed, and I find it the most tense line of the extract because the lie is visible on stage
> as she speaks it.

*Why it works:* it analyses stage directions as carefully as dialogue; it uses dramatic irony to
explain the key word "tense"; it follows the extract's movement to its turning point; and the
personal response grows from the analysis.

## Planning the answer

A clear plan for a passage answer:

1. **Introduction:** place the extract and answer the question in one sentence.
2. **Stage one of the extract:** how the situation and atmosphere are set up.
3. **Stage two:** how tension (or humour, or sympathy) builds through dialogue and action.
4. **Stage three:** the turning point and the ending of the extract.
5. **Conclusion:** your judgement on the key word, and why the moment matters in the play.

On a 90-minute paper with two equal questions, keep to time and leave the essay question its share.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating stage directions as background.** They are the playwright's method; quote and analyse
  them.
- **Writing about "the reader".** In drama, write about the audience and what it sees and hears.
- **Drifting into the whole play.** Wider references should explain the extract, not replace it.
- **Stopping halfway.** Cover the extract to its final line.
- **Listing features.** "There is a pause" earns little; say what the pause does to the audience.
- **Paraphrasing dialogue.** Quote briefly and analyse word choice and rhythm.

## Command words and question focuses

"Explore" means "write in detail about particular aspects", so choose the most revealing moments of
the extract rather than commenting on every line. When a question asks "How does the playwright make
this moment so…?", the key word ("dramatic", "revealing", "disturbing") is the test of relevance for
every paragraph.
