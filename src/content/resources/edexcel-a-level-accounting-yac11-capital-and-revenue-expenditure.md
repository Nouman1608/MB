## The two definitions

**Capital expenditure** is spending to acquire a non-current asset, or to improve one so that it gives greater benefits than before. It is debited to a non-current asset account, shown in the statement of financial position and written off over the asset's useful life by depreciation.

**Revenue expenditure** is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, including keeping existing non-current assets in their present condition. It is debited to an expense account and charged in full in the statement of profit or loss for the period.

The question to ask is always about **purpose**: does the spending create or improve a long-term resource, or does it use up a benefit in the current period?

## What forms part of the cost of a non-current asset

The cost of an asset includes everything needed to bring it to the location and condition for its intended use. IAS 16 Property, Plant and Equipment applies this idea.

| Included in cost (capital) | Not included (revenue) |
|---|---|
| Purchase price, after deducting trade discount | Training staff to use the asset |
| Import duties on the asset | Advertising a new product made with it |
| Delivery to the business | Running costs once in use: fuel, power, insurance |
| Site preparation, such as a concrete base | Routine servicing and repairs |
| Installation and assembly | General administration costs |
| Testing that the asset works properly | Costs of a period when the asset is ready but idle |
| Legal and professional fees for buying property | |

Two items catch candidates out. Training is revenue even though it relates to the new asset, because it benefits the staff rather than making the asset ready. Trade discount reduces the cost; the asset is recorded at the price actually payable.

### Worked example 1: analysing a purchase

Larch Bakery buys a new oven. The payments are:

| Item | $ | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Oven list price 30,000 less 10% trade discount | 27,000 | Capital |
| Delivery | 600 | Capital |
| Installation and gas connection | 1,400 | Capital |
| Test bake to check temperatures | 250 | Capital |
| Training two bakers to use it | 750 | Revenue |
| Advertising the new bread range | 1,200 | Revenue |

The oven is recorded at **$29,250** (27,000 + 600 + 1,400 + 250). The $1,950 of training and advertising is charged to profit this year.

## Improvement or repair?

Spending on an existing asset is capital only if it improves the asset beyond its original condition. Tests that indicate capital expenditure:

- the asset's **useful life is extended** beyond the original estimate;
- its **capacity** increases (it can produce more);
- the **quality** of its output improves;
- its **running costs** fall significantly.

Restoring an asset to how it was, however expensive, is revenue expenditure. A $9,000 engine overhaul that only returns a lorry to its normal condition is a repair; fitting a larger engine that lets it carry heavier loads is an improvement.

When one invoice covers both, **split it**. A builder's bill of $12,000 for repairing a damaged roof ($4,500) and adding a storage loft ($7,500) gives $4,500 revenue and $7,500 capital.

## Own work on non-current assets

If a business uses its own staff and materials to build or improve a non-current asset, those costs become capital expenditure. The wages and materials, first recorded as expenses, are transferred to the asset:

```
Dr Non-current asset (e.g. premises)
    Cr Wages
    Cr Purchases (materials)
```

This reduces expenses and so increases profit for the year, while the asset is depreciated over its life.

## The concepts behind the treatment

- **Accruals (matching)**: a capital item benefits several periods, so its cost is spread over them by depreciation; revenue items benefit only the current period.
- **Prudence**: if it is doubtful that spending will bring future benefit, it is treated as revenue so that assets and profit are not overstated.
- **Materiality**: small long-lasting items are expensed because the information is not worth the cost of tracking.
- **Consistency**: the same capitalisation policy, such as a minimum value, is applied each year.
- **Going concern**: spreading cost over future years assumes the business will be there to use the asset.

## Worked example 2: errors across several years

Larch Bakery depreciates equipment at 20% a year on cost, straight line, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase.

**Error A: a repair capitalised.** A $6,000 repair is debited to equipment in Year 1.

| | Year 1 | Years 2 to 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Expense that should have been charged | 6,000 | 0 |
| Depreciation actually charged on it | 1,200 | 1,200 each year |
| Effect on profit | Overstated by 4,800 | Understated by 1,200 each year |

Over the five years the errors cancel (4,800 − 4 × 1,200 = 0), but every individual year is wrong, and non-current assets are overstated until the "asset" is fully depreciated.

**Error B: capital expenditure expensed.** A $15,000 machine is charged to repairs in Year 1.

| | Year 1 | Years 2 to 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Expense charged | 15,000 | 0 |
| Depreciation that should have been charged | 3,000 | 3,000 each year |
| Effect on profit | Understated by 12,000 | Overstated by 3,000 each year |

Questions often ask for the effect on profit **and** on the statement of financial position. Error B also understates non-current assets by $12,000 at the end of Year 1.

## Correcting the error in Year 1

If Error B is found before the Year 1 statements are finalised:

```
Dr Machinery                         15,000
    Cr Repairs                                 15,000
Being machine wrongly charged to repairs

Dr Depreciation expense               3,000
    Cr Provision for depreciation              3,000
Being depreciation for Year 1 on the machine
```

Profit rises by 15,000 − 3,000 = $12,000, matching the understatement found above. Journal entries to correct errors are developed further in the [correction of errors study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-correction-of-errors/).

## Common mistakes

- Classifying by size rather than purpose.
- Recording an asset at list price, ignoring trade discount.
- Capitalising training, advertising or running costs.
- Treating an expensive repair as capital.
- Forgetting the depreciation effect when stating the effect of an error after Year 1.
- Giving the effect on profit only, not on the statement of financial position.
- Leaving own wages and materials in expenses when they built an asset.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify** or **State**: classify each item as capital or revenue in a table.
- **Explain** the treatment: classification + reason linked to the item + the concept that justifies it.
- **Calculate** the cost of an asset: list every capital element and total.
- **Prepare** journal entries: debit and credit with narratives.
- **Comment** or **Evaluate** a policy, such as capitalising all items over a set value: weigh simplicity and materiality against accuracy, and conclude.
