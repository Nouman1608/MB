**This guide covers content area 3.1.2.5 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiat (9237), Zakah: "The role, importance and wisdom of giving Zakah and Ushr, including how why Zakah and Ushr are given" and "The criteria for giving (Nisaab) and receiving Zakah."** It goes further than the Five Pillars overview in the course guides. It explains what each term means, who must pay and how much, who may receive, and why Muslims believe the system is wise.

## What the words mean

**Zakah** comes from an Arabic root meaning both *purification* and *growth*. Muslims give it to purify what is left of their wealth and to help the whole community grow in fairness. It is the third Pillar of Islam. The Qur'an often pairs it with prayer: "establish prayer and give Zakah" (2:43). Prayer is worship of Allah directly; Zakah is worship through care for people.

**Ushr** means "a tenth". It is a form of Zakah paid on farm produce, because a harvest is wealth that arrives in one season rather than being saved over a year.

**Sadaqah** is voluntary giving at any time, in any amount. Zakah is a fixed duty. A common mistake is to treat the two as one thing.

**Nisaab** is the minimum amount of wealth a Muslim must hold before Zakah becomes due. Someone below it does not pay.

## Why Zakah is given: role and importance

The first reason is obedience. Zakah is a command from Allah, so paying it is an act of worship. The prescribed passage, Qur'an 9:58-60, ends its list of recipients with the words "an obligation [imposed] by Allah. And Allah is Knowing and Wise." The word "obligation" shows that Zakah is not a favour from the rich but a duty. The last phrase says the system comes from knowledge and wisdom.

The second reason is that wealth belongs to Allah. Muslims believe they hold money as a trust (*amanah*). They earned it by the ability Allah gave them, so part of it must be returned to those in need. This idea protects against pride, because a wealthy person is a manager, not an owner.

The third reason is that Zakah supports the community. In the early Muslim state it was collected by officials and spent on public needs. After the death of the Prophet (pbuh), Hazrat Abu Bakr (RA) insisted that tribes who had stopped paying must continue, because Zakah is a Pillar and not an optional gift. This shows how seriously the early community took it.

## The wisdom of Zakah

Teachers of Islam usually describe the wisdom under four headings. Learn one example for each so that you can build an "explain" answer.

| Wisdom | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spiritual | Purifies the giver's heart from greed and miserliness; shows gratitude to Allah | A Muslim who pays feels less attached to money and more aware of Allah |
| Moral | Builds generosity, compassion and humility | The giver learns that money is not a measure of worth |
| Social | Reduces the gap between rich and poor; prevents wealth circling only among the rich | A family struggling with rent receives support without having to beg |
| Economic | Encourages wealth to be used, not hoarded | A person with idle savings knows a share is lost each year if left unused, so is encouraged to invest it |

The Qur'an says that wealth should not "be a perpetual distribution among the rich among you" (59:7). Zakah is one of the main ways Islam puts this principle into practice. Another Qur'anic idea is purification: Allah tells the Prophet (pbuh) to "take from their wealth a charity by which you purify them" (9:103).

## The Nisaab: the criteria for giving

A Muslim must pay Zakah when all of the following are true.

1. **The person is a Muslim** who is an adult and of sound mind. (Scholars discuss the position of children's wealth, so keep to the main rule in an exam answer.)
2. **The wealth reaches the Nisaab.** This is the value of a set weight of gold or silver. The weights commonly given are about 87.5 grams of gold or 612 grams of silver. Many Muslims today calculate using the silver figure or the current price of gold, so the cash value changes over time and between scholars.
3. **The wealth is held for one full lunar year (*hawl*).** The date is often chosen by a Muslim and kept the same each year.
4. **It is surplus.** Food, a family home, clothes and tools needed for daily life are not counted. Debts that are due are taken away first.

The rate on savings, gold, silver and trade goods is **2.5 per cent** (one-fortieth).

Wealth that counts includes: money in the bank and at home, gold and silver, business stock and profit, and some investments. Wealth that does not count includes: the house you live in, personal clothes and furniture, and a car used for daily needs.

### Ushr on crops

Ushr applies to the harvest, not to a yearly balance. The Prophet (pbuh) taught that crops watered by rain or natural springs are subject to a tenth (10 per cent), while crops that need effort and cost to irrigate, such as by carrying water or using a pump, are subject to half of that (5 per cent). The reason is fairness. The more work and expense the farmer put in, the smaller the share due. A farmer pays at harvest. Scholars differ over the minimum amount of produce and the types of crop that count, so remember the rates and the principle.

## The criteria for receiving: the eight groups

The recipients are listed in the prescribed passage, Qur'an 9:60. Each phrase in the passage names one group.

| Group | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The poor (*fuqara*) | People with almost nothing | A widow with no income |
| The needy (*masakin*) | People who earn something but not enough | A worker whose pay does not cover food and rent |
| Those employed to collect | Honest officials who gather and distribute Zakah | A trustworthy collector in a charity fund |
| Bringing hearts together | New Muslims or those whose goodwill helps the community | A recent convert who has lost family support |
| Freeing captives or slaves | Helping to free people from bondage | Paying a ransom for a prisoner |
| Those in debt | People who cannot repay a lawful debt | A family overwhelmed by medical bills |
| The cause of Allah | Work for the benefit of the faith and the community | Support for a worthy cause serving Muslims |
| The stranded traveller | A traveller cut off from money | A student who has lost belongings abroad |

Most scholars say Zakah may **not** be given to: a person who is rich enough to pay Zakah, a person's own dependent parents, spouse or children (their upkeep is already a duty), or members of the Prophet's family. Zakah should go to those who truly need it, and the giver should intend it for Allah, not for show.

## Worked example: calculating Zakah

**Scenario.** Yusuf holds 8,400 in savings and 1,100 in the form of stock for his small shop. He owes 500 to a supplier that is due now. A local scholar tells him the Nisaab is 5,000 in his currency and that he has held these amounts for a full lunar year.

1. Add what counts as wealth: 8,400 + 1,100 = 9,500.
2. Take away debts due now: 9,500 − 500 = 9,000.
3. Check the Nisaab: 9,000 is above 5,000, so Zakah is due.
4. Apply the rate: 9,000 × 2.5% = 9,000 × 0.025 = **225**.

Yusuf must give 225 to the groups named in 9:60. If his total had been 4,800 he would not owe Zakah, but he could still give sadaqah.

**Ushr example.** A farmer harvests 3,000 kg of wheat from a field watered by rain. Ushr is 10 per cent, so 3,000 × 0.10 = 300 kg. If the same harvest came from land irrigated by a costly pump, the rate would be 5 per cent, so 3,000 × 0.05 = 150 kg.

## A model 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain **two** ways that Zakah benefits the person who gives it. **[4]**

> Zakah purifies the giver's wealth and heart [1]; paying a part of what they own reminds a Muslim that all wealth belongs to Allah and loosens the grip of greed [1]. It also builds gratitude and generosity [1], because a person who sees the needs of others learns to value what Allah has given them [1].

Each point names the benefit and then explains it. Spending time on a story or on how Zakah helps the receiver would not answer this question, which asks about the **giver**.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "Zakah is 2.5 per cent of everything you own".** It applies only to qualifying wealth above the Nisaab, held for a year.
- **Mixing up Zakah and sadaqah.** Zakah is compulsory and measured; sadaqah is voluntary.
- **Giving Ushr the wrong rate.** Rain-fed crops are 10 per cent; effort-irrigated crops are 5 per cent.
- **Ignoring the receiving side.** The specification names the criteria for **receiving** too. Be ready to list the groups in 9:60.
- **Describing Zakah only as charity.** Include purification, obedience and the idea of wealth as a trust.

## Exam technique

**Give** and **name** questions want a single fact: the rate, a term or a recipient. **Explain** questions need a reason followed by development. In the 6-mark question, add a source of authority, for example 2:43 ("establish prayer and give Zakah") or 9:60 (the recipients). In the 12-mark question on the prescribed passage, quote 9:60 directly: "for the poor and for the needy" for social justice, "an obligation [imposed] by Allah" for worship and duty, and "Knowing and Wise" for the wisdom behind the system. Then weigh the argument: Zakah is a duty that also solves real social problems, but it works only if Muslims pay sincerely.
