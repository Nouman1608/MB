This guide covers subtopic **28.4, Stereoisomerism in transition element complexes**, from Topic 28,
Chemistry of transition elements, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series.
It is A Level content. The topic overview in
[Transition Elements: Colour, Stereoisomerism and Stability Constants](/resources/a-transition-elements-colour-stereoisomerism-and-stability-constants/)
names the syllabus examples; this page gives you a method for deciding, for any complex you are shown,
whether it has geometrical isomers, optical isomers, both or neither, and whether each isomer is polar.
Afterwards, use the [stereoisomerism revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-stereoisomerism-in-transition-element-complexes-revision-notes/)
and the [stereoisomerism practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-stereoisomerism-in-transition-element-complexes-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

Two learning outcomes:

1. describe the types of stereoisomerism shown by complexes, including those associated with **bidentate**
   ligands:
   - (a) **geometrical (cis/trans)** isomerism, for example square planar [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] and octahedral
     [Co(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ and [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺;
   - (b) **optical** isomerism, for example [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺ and [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺;
2. **deduce the overall polarity** of complexes such as these.

Throughout, H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ (1,2-diaminoethane) is abbreviated to **en**. It is bidentate: both nitrogen atoms
donate a lone pair to the metal.

## Stereoisomers: the key idea

Stereoisomers have the **same formula and the same bonds** (each ligand bonded to the metal through the same
atom) but a **different arrangement of the ligands in space**. Converting one into the other would mean
breaking and re-forming metal–ligand bonds. In complexes, the "arrangement in space" is simply the set of
positions around the metal that each ligand occupies.

Position language you need:

- In a **square planar** or **octahedral** complex, two positions are **cis** if they are at **90°** to each
  other (adjacent) and **trans** if they are at **180°** (opposite).
- In a **tetrahedral** complex every pair of positions is at about 109.5°, so all pairs are equivalent.
  **Tetrahedral complexes of the type [MA₂B₂] have no cis/trans isomers.**

## Geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism

**Square planar [MA₂B₂].** Put the first B ligand at any corner. The second B is either next to it (cis) or
across from it (trans). There are exactly two isomers. [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is the syllabus example; the cis isomer
is the anti-cancer drug cisplatin.

**Octahedral [MA₄B₂].** Put the first B at the top. Of the five remaining positions, four are at 90° to it
and one is at 180°. So the second B is cis or trans: two isomers. [Co(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ is the syllabus example.

**Octahedral with two bidentate ligands, [M(en)₂B₂].** A bidentate ligand such as en has a short "bite": its
two donor atoms can only span **two cis positions**, never two trans positions. Two en ligands therefore
occupy four positions, and the two remaining positions, which hold the B ligands, can be cis or trans to each
other. [Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺ is the syllabus example.

A quick count for [MA₂B₂] in each shape:

| Shape | Number of geometrical isomers |
|---|---|
| tetrahedral | none |
| square planar | 2 (cis, trans) |
| octahedral [MA₄B₂] | 2 (cis, trans) |

## Optical isomerism

A complex shows optical isomerism when it is **chiral**: it is **not superimposable on its mirror image**.
The two non-superimposable forms are **enantiomers**; they rotate plane-polarised light in opposite
directions. At this level the practical test is: **does the complex have a plane of symmetry?** If it has
one, it is not chiral. If it has none, it is chiral and exists as a pair of optical isomers.

**[Ni(en)₃]²⁺.** Three en ligands wrap around the octahedron like the blades of a propeller. The propeller
can twist one way or the other, and the two forms are mirror images that cannot be superimposed. No plane
of symmetry exists, so there is **one pair of optical isomers**.

**[Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺.**

- **trans form:** the two H₂O ligands are opposite each other and both en ligands lie in the plane between
  them. That plane is a plane of symmetry, so the trans isomer is **not optically active**.
- **cis form:** the two H₂O ligands are adjacent and the en ligands twist round the remaining positions.
  There is no plane of symmetry, so the cis form exists as **a pair of optical isomers**.

So [Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺ has **three stereoisomers** in total: trans, and two optical isomers of cis.

**[Co(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺** shows only geometrical isomerism. Monodentate ligands cannot create the propeller
twist, and both the cis and trans forms contain a plane of symmetry.

## Worked example 1: applying the method to a new complex

*The ethanedioate ion, C₂O₄²⁻, is a bidentate ligand. Deduce the stereoisomers of [Cr(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻.*

**Step 1: shape and coordination number.** Three bidentate ligands supply six donor atoms, so the complex is
octahedral with coordination number 6.

**Step 2: geometrical isomers?** All three ligands are identical, and each must span two cis positions. There
is only one way to arrange them geometrically, so there are **no cis/trans isomers**.

**Step 3: plane of symmetry?** The arrangement is the same propeller as [Ni(en)₃]²⁺, with no plane of
symmetry. The complex is chiral.

**Answer:** one pair of **optical isomers**, drawn as mirror images, with **no** geometrical isomers.

This is exactly the reasoning the syllabus means by "including those associated with bidentate ligands":
you are expected to transfer it from the en examples to other bidentate ligands named in 28.2.

## Deducing overall polarity

A complex has an overall dipole when its individual metal–ligand bond dipoles **do not cancel**. Use the
bond-dipole model:

- **trans isomers** of [MA₂B₂] and [MA₄B₂]: identical bonds are opposite each other, so their dipoles cancel
  in pairs. The complex is **non-polar** overall.
- **cis isomers**: the two different bonds are at 90°, so their dipoles add to a resultant. The complex is
  **polar** overall.
- **[M(en)₃]ⁿ⁺** or other complexes with three identical bidentate ligands: the arrangement is symmetrical,
  so the dipoles cancel and the complex is **non-polar**.
- **cis-[Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺** (either enantiomer) is **polar**; **trans-[Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺** is **non-polar**.

**Worked example 2: an annotated model answer.**

*Question: [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] exists as two isomers. Draw them, name the type of isomerism and deduce which isomer
is polar.*

> Two square planar structures drawn: one with the two Cl atoms adjacent, labelled *cis*, and one with the
> two Cl atoms opposite, labelled *trans* **[both structures, square planar, correct labels]**. This is
> geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism **[type named]**. In the trans isomer each Pt–Cl bond dipole is
> cancelled by the opposite Pt–Cl bond, and each Pt–N by the opposite Pt–N, so it has no overall dipole.
> In the cis isomer the bond dipoles do not cancel, so the **cis** isomer is polar **[polarity deduced with
> reason]**.

Polarity has a practical consequence you may be asked to suggest: the polar cis isomer is expected to be
more soluble in water than the non-polar trans isomer.

## Drawing octahedral isomers clearly

- Draw the metal in the centre with two bonds vertical (up and down) and four in a square around the
  "equator", using wedges for the two bonds towards you and dashes for the two going away.
- Draw each **en** ligand as a curved line joining two adjacent positions, with N at each end.
- When drawing a pair of **optical isomers**, draw the first complex, then a dashed mirror line, then its exact
  reflection. Examiners look for genuine mirror images.
- When showing **cis and trans**, keep the same orientation for both so the difference is obvious.

## Common mistakes

- **Claiming cis/trans isomers for a tetrahedral complex.** All tetrahedral positions are adjacent.
- **Drawing en across trans positions.** It can only span adjacent (cis) positions.
- **Saying the trans form of [Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺ is optically active.** It has a plane of symmetry.
- **Counting two stereoisomers for [Ni(en)₂(H₂O)₂]²⁺.** The cis form is itself a pair, so there are three.
- **Deciding polarity from the words alone.** Look at whether the bond dipoles cancel by symmetry in the
  structure you have drawn.
- **Drawing "mirror images" that are actually rotations** of the same structure.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** the stereoisomerism: name the type(s) and draw the isomers with correct geometry.
- **Deduce** the number of stereoisomers or the polarity: show the step that justifies the answer (a plane
  of symmetry, or dipoles cancelling).
- **Explain** why a complex is chiral: "non-superimposable mirror images; no plane of symmetry".
- **Draw**: three-dimensional octahedral drawings with wedges and dashes; square planar drawings flat.
