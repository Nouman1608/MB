This page practises **3.3.2 Unseen poetry**, Section B of Route B Paper 2b: one 30-mark question on
a previously unseen poem, in about 45 minutes. Section A trains the four features the specification
names (content, theme, structure and use of language) on one short poem. Section B gives two full
questions.

## Section A: skills questions

Read Poem A, then answer Questions 1 to 8.

> **Poem A: Power Cut**
>
> At nine the city switches itself off.
> The fridge stops humming its one long note.
> Somebody laughs on a balcony.
> We find the candles where we always find them,
> behind the rice, and the room grows smaller
> and kinder, the way rooms used to be
> before we filled them with light.
> My brother tells the story about the snake.
> Nobody checks a phone. Nobody wants
> the fan to start again, though it is hot,
> though it is so hot, and the light comes back
> at ten, like a teacher into a noisy class.

**1.** Summarise what happens in the poem in one sentence. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the effect of the opening line, "At nine the city switches itself off". **[2]**

**3.** How does the poet use the line break in "the room grows smaller / and kinder" to present the
change in the room? **[3]**

**4.** Explain the effect of the repetition in "though it is hot, / though it is so hot". **[3]**

**5.** How does the final simile, "like a teacher into a noisy class", present the return of the
light? **[4]**

**6.** Identify where the tone of the poem shifts, and explain the shift. **[3]**

**7.** The poem is written as a single stanza. Explain **one** effect of this structure. **[2]**

**8.** How do the details "We find the candles where we always find them" and "My brother tells the
story about the snake" present the family's relationship? **[3]**

## Section B: exam-style questions

Read Poem B, then answer Question 9.

> **Poem B: The Repair Shop**
>
> My uncle mends what other people drop:
> a clock that lost its nerve, a kettle's spout,
> a radio that only spoke in static.
> He works with the door open, so the street
> can see him at it, lamp pulled close,
> a screw held on his tongue like a word
> he is not ready to say.
>
> Now they buy new. The window holds
> three televisions no one came back for,
> their screens like ponds with the fish gone.
>
> Still, mornings, he lifts the shutter,
> lays out his tools in their exact order,
> and waits, the way a doctor waits
> in a village where nobody is ill.

**9.** How does the poet present the uncle in *The Repair Shop*? **[30]**

Read Poem C, then answer Question 10.

> **Poem C: Kite**
>
> All afternoon my daughter argues with the wind.
> It gives her a little, then takes it back,
> then gives her more than she can hold,
> and she runs, laughing, to keep the string
> from leaving her hands.
>
> I want to tell her: this is what it's like.
> Instead I hold the spool and let it turn,
> and watch the kite climb into a sky
> so wide it makes her small,
> so blue it makes her brave.

**10.** Explore how the poet presents the parent's feelings in *Kite*. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** During a power cut one evening, a family sits by candlelight, enjoying the quiet together
until the electricity returns [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Personifying the city as something that "switches itself off" makes the power cut sound
sudden and total [1]; the plain, end-stopped line creates a calm, almost matter-of-fact tone that
suggests power cuts are familiar to the speaker [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The break after "smaller" makes the reader expect a loss [1], but the next line turns it into
something positive, "and kinder" [1], so the structure enacts the speaker's discovery that a
smaller, darker room brings the family closer [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The repetition admits the discomfort of the heat [1], and the intensifier "so" makes it
stronger [1], which emphasises how much the family values the moment: they would rather be hot than
lose it [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The simile presents the light as an authority figure interrupting fun [1]. A teacher entering
a noisy class brings order and silence [1], suggesting that electricity restores routine and ends the
family's freedom [1]. Placing the image at the very end gives the poem a slightly comic but regretful
close [1]. **[4]**

**6.** The tone shifts at "and the light comes back" near the end [1], from relaxed, warm
enjoyment of the candlelit room [1] to a mild disappointment as normal life resumes [1]. **[3]**

**7.** A single unbroken stanza mirrors the single hour of the power cut as one continuous
experience [1], so the reader moves through it without a pause, just as the family does not want it
to be interrupted [1]. **[2]**

**8.** "Where we always find them" shows the power cut is a familiar routine [1], and the shared "we"
presents the family acting together without fuss [1]. The brother's well-known story, "the story",
suggests a tradition they enjoy repeating, so the darkness brings out closeness and shared
memories [1]. **[3]**

### Section B

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors, not by adding up points. The
indicative content shows what a high-level response might include; other valid readings should be
credited.

**9. *The Repair Shop*.**

- **AO1 (ideas):** the uncle is presented with affection and respect, as a skilled, patient man whose
  trade is being made unnecessary by a throwaway culture; the poem moves from pride to quiet loss,
  but ends on dignity.
- **AO2 (methods):**
  - the list of broken objects is personified ("a clock that lost its nerve", a radio that "spoke in
    static"), so mending becomes almost like healing people;
  - the open door shows his pride in his work and his place in the street's community;
  - the simile of the screw "held on his tongue like a word / he is not ready to say" suggests
    concentration, but perhaps also feelings he keeps to himself; the line break delays "he is not
    ready to say";
  - the short, blunt sentence "Now they buy new." marks the turn into the second stanza;
  - the simile "screens like ponds with the fish gone" creates an image of emptiness and lost life;
  - "Still" opens the final stanza, signalling persistence; "exact order" shows discipline and
    self-respect;
  - the closing simile of a doctor in a village "where nobody is ill" is gently ironic: his skill is
    intact, but no one needs it.
- **Structure:** three stanzas move from the past (busy), to the present (decline), to his daily
  response (endurance).
- **AO3:** a clear argument about the uncle's dignity in decline, precise terminology and tentative
  readings of the final image. **[30]**

**10. *Kite*.**

- **AO1 (ideas):** the parent feels love, pride and a restrained anxiety, recognising that the child
  is learning to cope with forces she cannot control; the kite becomes a metaphor for growing up and
  letting go.
- **AO2 (methods):**
  - the personified wind that "gives her a little, then takes it back" presents life as unpredictable;
  - the child's laughter and running suggest delight in the struggle;
  - "I want to tell her: this is what it's like." The colon and short clause suggest the parent's
    urge to explain life, which is then held back by "Instead";
  - holding "the spool" while letting it "turn" captures the balance between protection and freedom;
  - the parallel final lines "so wide it makes her small, / so blue it makes her brave" move from
    vulnerability to courage, ending on a hopeful note; the alliteration of "blue" and "brave" links
    the open sky to the child's confidence.
- **Structure:** the first stanza observes the child; the second turns inward to the parent's
  thoughts, so the poem's shape mirrors the shift from watching to reflecting.
- **AO3:** an interpretation of the poem as more than a description of a kite, with tentative
  readings of the ending and accurate terminology. **[30]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are the short questions on a different poem from the 30-mark tasks?**
To practise each skill separately before applying them all under timed conditions.

**Is it acceptable to disagree with the indicative content?**
Yes. Any reading supported by the poem is creditable.
