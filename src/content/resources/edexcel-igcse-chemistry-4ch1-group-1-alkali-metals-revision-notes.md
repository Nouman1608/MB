These notes condense sub-topic 2(a) Group 1 (alkali metals) – lithium, sodium and potassium (points 2.1 to 2.4C) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). Points 2.1 to 2.3 can appear on Paper 1 and Paper 2; point 2.4C is **Paper 2 only**. Model answers and a reacting-mass example are in the [Group 1 study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-group-1-alkali-metals/); test yourself with the [Group 1 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-group-1-alkali-metals-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alkali metals | The Group 1 elements; they react with water to form alkaline hydroxide solutions |
| Family of elements | Elements that react in the same way and form the same types of product |
| Tarnish | A shiny surface becoming dull as a layer of oxide forms |
| Reactivity | How readily an element reacts |
| Shielding | Inner shells of electrons reducing the attraction of the nucleus for the outer electron |

## Reaction with water (2.1)

```
alkali metal + water -> metal hydroxide + hydrogen
2Li(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2LiOH(aq) + H2(g)
2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)
2K(s)  + 2H2O(l) -> 2KOH(aq)  + H2(g)
```

Shared observations: floats; fizzes; moves about; gets smaller and disappears; solution becomes alkaline.

Evidence of a family: same reaction, same type of products, same equation shape, 1+ ions in every compound.

## Observation → explanation

| Observation | Because |
|---|---|
| Floats | Less dense than water |
| Fizzes | Hydrogen produced |
| Moves | Pushed by the hydrogen |
| Melts (Na, K) | Reaction is exothermic; low melting point |
| Lilac flame (K) | Hydrogen ignites; potassium colours the flame |
| Indicator purple | Hydroxide ions; strongly alkaline solution |

Product tests: lighted splint → squeaky pop (hydrogen); red litmus → blue (alkali).

## The trend (2.2)

| | Lithium | Sodium | Potassium |
|---|---|---|---|
| With water | Fizzes steadily | Fizzes rapidly, melts into a ball | Violent; lilac flame; may spark |
| With air | Tarnishes slowly | Tarnishes quickly | Tarnishes almost at once |

Reactivity increases **down** the group. Oxides formed in air: Li₂O, Na₂O, K₂O (for example 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O). All are stored under oil.

## Predicting (2.3)

For rubidium and caesium, below potassium:

- even more reactive; react explosively with water;
- form RbOH / CsOH and hydrogen, and strongly alkaline solutions;
- 1+ ions: Rb₂O, Cs₂O, RbCl, CsCl;
- lower melting points than potassium;
- tarnish almost instantly in air.

Method: state the trend's direction, then extend it. An estimated value should follow the pattern of the differences.

## Explaining the trend (2.4C) — Paper 2 only

```
Li 2,1      Na 2,8,1      K 2,8,8,1
down the group:
  more occupied shells -> outer electron further from nucleus
  more shielding by inner shells
  weaker attraction between nucleus and outer electron
  outer electron lost more easily -> more reactive
```

Each line is a separate marking point. Include "outer electron" and "nucleus" explicitly.

## Calculation reminders

- Moles of metal = moles of hydroxide (2 : 2).
- Moles of hydrogen = half the moles of metal (2 : 1).
- Paper 2 only: volume of H₂ at rtp = moles × 24 000 cm³.

Reminder: 0.78 g of potassium (Ar 39) is 0.020 mol, giving 0.020 mol KOH (1.12 g, Mr 56) and 0.010 mol H₂ (240 cm³ at rtp).

## Answer frames

- **Same family (2.1):** "All three react with water in the same way, forming a metal hydroxide and hydrogen; all form 1+ ions, so the equations have the same form."
- **Trend from evidence (2.2):** "Potassium reacts more vigorously with water than sodium, and its cut surface tarnishes faster in air, so it is more reactive."
- **Prediction (2.3):** "Reactivity increases down the group, so [element] will react more vigorously than [element above it]."

## Exam traps

- "It dissolves" for the metal: it reacts.
- Hydroxide formulae such as NaOH₂: Group 1 hydroxides are MOH.
- Only one difference given when the question asks for air **and** water.
- "Larger atoms" with no link to the outer electron and nuclear attraction.
- Saying the solution is acidic or neutral.
- Forgetting state symbols when asked: hydroxide is (aq), hydrogen (g).
- Predicting that caesium reacts less vigorously than potassium.

## Self-test

1. Write the word equation for potassium reacting with water.
2. Give three observations seen when lithium is added to water.
3. Explain why sodium floats on water.
4. Name the gas produced and describe a test for it.
5. Give one piece of evidence from reactions with air that potassium is more reactive than sodium.
6. Write a balanced equation, with state symbols, for lithium reacting with water.
7. Give the formula of rubidium oxide.
8. Predict the pH of the solution formed when caesium reacts with water.
9. Explain why alkali metals are stored under oil.
10. (Paper 2 only) Explain why potassium is more reactive than sodium.
11. Calculate the mass of lithium hydroxide formed from 0.35 g of lithium. (Ar: Li = 7, O = 16, H = 1)
12. Why must pieces of equal size be used to compare the reactivity of the metals?

### Answers

1. Potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen.
2. Any three: floats; fizzes; moves slowly; gets smaller and disappears; solution becomes alkaline.
3. It is less dense than water.
4. Hydrogen; a lighted splint gives a squeaky pop.
5. A freshly cut surface of potassium tarnishes faster than one of sodium.
6. 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g).
7. Rb₂O.
8. About 14 (strongly alkaline).
9. To stop them reacting with oxygen and water vapour in the air.
10. Potassium's outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus, with more shielding, so it is less strongly attracted and lost more easily.
11. 0.35 ÷ 7 = 0.050 mol Li → 0.050 mol LiOH; Mr 24; 0.050 × 24 = 1.2 g.
12. Size affects the amount of metal and surface area, which change the rate; equal sizes make the comparison fair.
