Condensed notes for sub-topic **4.1 Capital and revenue expenditure and receipts**. Worked classifications and four incorrect treatments are in the [4.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-and-receipts/); test yourself with the [4.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-and-receipts-practice/).

## What 4.1 asks for

- The distinction between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, and how to account for each.
- The distinction between capital receipts and revenue receipts, and how to account for each.
- How to identify and calculate the effect of incorrect treatment on profit and on asset valuations.

## Expenditure

| | Capital expenditure | Revenue expenditure |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Buying or improving non-current assets | Running the business day to day |
| Benefit | Several accounting periods | The current period |
| Examples | Purchase price, delivery, installation, testing, legal fees on premises, extensions | Repairs, maintenance, fuel, insurance, wages, training, redecoration, goods for resale |
| Recorded in | Non-current asset account | Expense account |
| Financial statement | Statement of financial position (then depreciation in the statement of profit or loss) | Statement of profit or loss in full |

**The improvement test:** does it make the asset bigger, better or longer-lasting (capital), or keep it as it was (revenue)?

## Receipts

| | Capital receipts | Revenue receipts |
|---|---|---|
| What they are | Receipts not from normal trading | Income from normal activities |
| Examples | Capital introduced, loans received, proceeds of sale of non-current assets | Sales, rent received, commission received, interest received, discount received |
| Recorded in | Capital, loan or disposal account | Income account |
| Financial statement | Statement of financial position (profit or loss on disposal only reaches the statement of profit or loss) | Statement of profit or loss |

## Effects of incorrect treatment

| Error | Profit for the year | Non-current assets |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue | Understated | Understated |
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital | Overstated | Overstated |
| Capital receipt treated as revenue | Overstated | Loan: liabilities understated. Sale proceeds: asset still shown, so overstated |
| Revenue receipt treated as capital | Understated | No effect on assets |

**With depreciation (straight-line, full year in year of purchase):**

- Capital item of 5,000 expensed, depreciation rate 20%: profit understated by 5,000 - 1,000 = 4,000; assets understated by 4,000.
- Repair of 900 capitalised and depreciated at 10%: profit overstated by 900 - 90 = 810; assets overstated by 810.

**Quick check:** the profit effect and the asset effect are the same amount when only expenditure and non-current assets are involved.

## Why the distinction matters

- **Matching:** the cost of an asset is spread over the periods it helps to earn revenue.
- **Accurate profit:** wrong treatment misleads the owner about drawings, lenders about lending and the tax authority about tax.
- **Asset values:** the statement of financial position should show the business's resources fairly.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Spending on acquiring or improving non-current assets |
| Revenue expenditure | Spending on the running costs of the business |
| Capital receipt | Money received that is not from normal trading, such as a loan or asset sale |
| Revenue receipt | Income from normal business activities |
| Non-current asset | An asset kept for use in the business for more than one year |
| Depreciation | The part of the cost of a non-current asset used up in a period |
| Overstated | Shown at more than the correct figure |
| Understated | Shown at less than the correct figure |

## Exam traps

- **Delivery and installation** of a new asset are capital, even though they are services.
- **Repairs** are revenue, even when they are expensive.
- **Training staff** to use a new machine is revenue.
- **Sale proceeds** of a non-current asset are a capital receipt; only the profit or loss on disposal is income or expense.
- When a depreciation rate is given, the effect of an error is **cost less depreciation**, not the full cost.
- A loan wrongly treated as sales overstates profit but does **not** overstate assets.

## Self-test

**1.** Define capital expenditure.

**2.** Classify: fuel for the delivery van; a new delivery van; signwriting the business name on the new van before use.

**3.** Classify: commission received; a bank loan; proceeds from selling old shop fittings.

**4.** Where is revenue expenditure recorded?

**5.** Legal fees of 1,800 for buying premises were debited to general expenses. Premises are not depreciated. State the effect on profit and on non-current assets.

**6.** A machine repair of 600 was debited to machinery, depreciated at 25% of cost. State the effect on profit.

**7.** A new computer of 2,000 was charged to office expenses. Computers are depreciated at 30% of cost. Calculate the effect on profit.

**8.** Rent received of 400 was credited to the loan account. State the effect on profit and on liabilities.

**9.** Why is the sale of an old van not revenue?

**10.** Explain how the matching concept applies to capital expenditure.

**11.** Owner's capital introduced of 5,000 was credited to sales. State the effect on profit.

**12.** Is the extension of a warehouse capital or revenue? Why?

## Self-test answers

**1.** Spending on acquiring or improving non-current assets, including the costs of getting them ready for use.

**2.** Fuel: revenue. Van: capital. Signwriting before use: capital.

**3.** Commission received: revenue. Loan: capital. Sale proceeds: capital.

**4.** In an expense account, transferred to the statement of profit or loss.

**5.** Profit understated by 1,800; non-current assets understated by 1,800.

**6.** Overstated by 600 - 150 = 450.

**7.** Understated by 2,000 - 600 = 1,400.

**8.** Profit understated by 400; liabilities overstated by 400.

**9.** It does not come from normal trading; it converts an existing asset into cash.

**10.** The asset benefits several periods, so its cost is spread over them through depreciation rather than charged in one year.

**11.** Profit overstated by 5,000.

**12.** Capital: it improves the asset by increasing its capacity.
