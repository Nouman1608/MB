These questions cover section 1.4 (production possibility curve diagrams) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [PPC diagrams study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-production-possibility-curve-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [PPC diagrams revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-production-possibility-curve-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

Several questions use this schedule for Country Talmor (invented), which produces computers and rice.

| Point | Computers (thousands) | Rice (million tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 60 |
| B | 20 | 56 |
| C | 40 | 46 |
| D | 60 | 28 |
| E | 80 | 0 |

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** An economy is producing at a point inside its PPC. What does this show? **[1]**

A: Resources are fully employed

B: Some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently

C: The economy has experienced economic growth

D: The combination is unattainable

**2.** Which event would cause a country's PPC to shift inward? **[1]**

A: A fall in unemployment

B: A rise in the school-leaving age that improves skills

C: Large-scale emigration of skilled workers

D: The discovery of new oil reserves

**3.** Define a production possibility curve. **[2]**

**4.** Using Talmor's schedule, calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand computers when moving from C to D. **[2]**

**5.** Talmor produces 40 thousand computers and 38 million tonnes of rice.

**(a)** Identify whether this point is under, on or beyond the PPC, using the schedule. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the rice output lost. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why Talmor's PPC is bowed outward rather than a straight line. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Using Talmor's schedule:

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand computers for each move: A to B, B to C, D to E. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain what your answers show about opportunity cost. **[2]**

**8.** Talmor's government invests in irrigation, which raises maximum rice output from 60 to 72 million tonnes. Maximum computer output is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in maximum rice output. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the PPC diagram would change. **[2]**

**9.** Explain the difference between a movement along a PPC and a shift of a PPC, giving one cause of each. **[4]**

**10.** Talmor's unemployment rate falls sharply after a rise in exports. Explain, with reference to a PPC diagram, how this affects the economy. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** causes of an outward shift of the whole of a country's PPC. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether an outward shift of a country's PPC will always raise its people's living standards. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A describes a point on the curve, C a shift and D a point beyond the curve. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Emigration reduces the quantity of labour, so capacity falls; A is a movement towards the curve, and B and D shift it outward. **[1]**

**3.** The maximum combinations of two goods (or groups of goods) an economy can produce [1] when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with given technology [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Rice lost = 46 − 28 = 18 million tonnes; computers gained = 20 thousand [1]. Opportunity cost = 18 ÷ 20 = **0.9 million tonnes of rice per thousand computers** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Under the curve, because point C shows 46 million tonnes is possible with 40 thousand computers [1].

**(b)** 46 − 38 = **8 million tonnes** of rice [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Resources are not equally suited to both goods [1]. As more computers are made, resources better at growing rice, such as farmland and farm workers, must be switched, so ever more rice is given up for each extra computer: increasing opportunity cost [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** A to B: 4 ÷ 20 = **0.2 million tonnes per thousand** [1]; B to C: 10 ÷ 20 = **0.5** [1]; D to E: 28 ÷ 20 = **1.4** [1].

**(b)** Each extra thousand computers costs more rice [1], which shows increasing opportunity cost as resources less suited to computer production are moved across [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** (72 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 [1] = **20%** [1].

**(b)** The curve pivots outward on the rice axis, with its intercept rising from 60 to 72 [1], while the computer intercept stays at 80 thousand, so more rice is possible at every output of computers below the maximum [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A movement along a PPC is a reallocation of fully used resources from one good to the other, with capacity unchanged [1]; for example, the government decides to produce more defence equipment and fewer consumer goods [1]. A shift is a change in the economy's capacity to produce, so the whole curve or one intercept moves [1]; for example, new technology shifts it outward or a war shifts it inward [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Before the change, unemployed workers mean Talmor is producing at a point under its PPC [1]. As workers find jobs, output of computers, rice or both rises [1], so the economy moves from inside the curve towards it [1]. The curve itself does not shift, because the quantity and quality of resources and technology are unchanged [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Cause 1: education and training [1] raise the quality of labour, so the same workers produce more of both goods and maximum output rises [1]. Cause 2: net investment in capital [1], such as new machinery beyond what replaces worn-out capital, increases the quantity of capital, so more of both goods can be made [1]. Other acceptable causes: a larger labour force, discovery of resources, better technology. **[4]**

**12.** For: an outward shift means more goods and services can be produced [1], so incomes and consumption per person can rise [1]; higher output brings more tax revenue, which can fund health and education [1]. Against: the shift raises potential output only; if resources stay unemployed, actual output need not rise [1]; if the population grows faster than output, output per person may not rise [1]; growth may bring pollution, congestion and depletion of resources, which lower the quality of life [1]; the extra output may go mainly to a small group, so many people gain little [1]. Conclusion: an outward shift makes higher living standards possible, but whether they rise depends on resources being fully used and on how the extra output is shared and produced [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Separate "potential" from "actual" output. The PPC shows what can be produced; living standards depend on what is produced and who receives it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reading the wrong row of the schedule when comparing a point with the curve.
- Giving the opportunity cost as the good gained divided by the good lost.
- Describing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Drawing a full outward shift when only one good's capacity has changed.
- Writing that growth "always" improves living standards without weighing distribution, population or environmental costs.
