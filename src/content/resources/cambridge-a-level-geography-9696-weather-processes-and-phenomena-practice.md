## Section A

**1.** Define the term "dew point". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between deposition and sublimation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how condensation affects the temperature of rising air. **[2]**

**4.** Air at 25°C can hold a maximum of 23 g/m³ of water vapour. A parcel at this temperature contains 11.5 g/m³. Calculate its relative humidity. Show your working. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why radiation cooling rarely produces rain. **[2]**

**6.** Outline the conditions needed for radiation fog to form. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Air at sea level with a temperature of 18°C is forced over a mountain range 2600 m high. Its condensation level is 600 m. Use a dry adiabatic lapse rate of 10°C per km and a saturated adiabatic lapse rate of 5°C per km, and assume the air loses its moisture as precipitation on the windward side.

**(a)** Calculate the temperature of the air at the condensation level. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its temperature at the summit. Show your working. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate its temperature when it returns to sea level on the leeward side. Show your working. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the leeward side is warmer and drier than the windward side. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows observations at a fictional weather station as a mid-latitude depression passed.

| Time | Cloud | Precipitation | Temperature (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 06:00 | Cirrus | None | 8 |
| 09:00 | Altostratus | Light rain | 8 |
| 12:00 | Nimbostratus | Steady, moderate rain | 9 |
| 15:00 | Stratus | Drizzle | 13 |
| 18:00 | Cumulonimbus | Heavy showers and hail | 12 |
| 21:00 | Scattered cumulus | None | 7 |

**(a)** Using evidence from the table, identify when the warm front and the cold front passed the station. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the differences in the precipitation brought by the two fronts. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the formation of hail. **[5]**

**10.** Compare the formation of radiation fog and advection fog. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the release of latent heat affects the development of clouds and precipitation. **[5]**

**12.** Assess the relative importance of convectional, frontal and orographic uplift as causes of precipitation in different parts of the world. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The temperature at which air becomes saturated [1] when it is cooled without any change in its water vapour content [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Deposition is the change of water vapour directly into ice, as in frost [1]. Sublimation is the change of ice directly into water vapour without melting [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Condensation releases latent heat into the air [1], so saturated rising air cools more slowly than unsaturated air (at the SALR rather than the DALR) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 11.5 / 23 x 100 [1] = 50% [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Radiation cooling chills only a shallow layer of air in contact with the ground [1], so it produces dew, frost or fog rather than the deep cloud needed for raindrops to grow and fall [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Clear skies, so the ground loses heat rapidly by longwave radiation [1]. Moist air near the ground, so the dew point is reached as it cools [1]. Light winds, which spread the cooling through a shallow layer without mixing in warmer, drier air [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 18 - (0.6 x 10) = 12°C [1].

**(b)** Rise from 600 m to 2600 m = 2 km at the SALR [1]; 12 - (2 x 5) = 2°C [1].

**(c)** Descent of 2.6 km at the DALR = 26°C of warming [1]; 2 + 26 = 28°C [1].

**(d)** On the windward side, the air cooled more slowly above cloud base because condensation released latent heat [1]. The moisture fell as precipitation, so the descending air is unsaturated and warms at the faster DALR all the way down [1]. Warming raises its capacity to hold vapour while its vapour content stays low, so relative humidity falls and a rain shadow forms [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** The warm front passed between 12:00 and 15:00, when steady rain gave way to drizzle and temperature rose from 9°C to 13°C [1]. The cold front passed between 18:00 and 21:00, after the cumulonimbus and hail, when temperature fell from 12°C to 7°C [1].

**(b)** At the warm front, warm air rises gently over the shallow slope of the cold air [1], so cloud thickens gradually and precipitation is steady and moderate over several hours [1]. At the cold front, cold air undercuts the warm air along a steep slope, forcing it up rapidly [1], producing cumulonimbus clouds with short, heavy showers and hail [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Hail forms in tall cumulonimbus clouds with very strong updrafts [1]. Small ice particles or frozen droplets are carried upwards above the freezing level [1]. They collide with supercooled water droplets, which freeze onto them, adding a layer of ice [1]. Updrafts and downdrafts cycle the pellets through the cloud many times, so they gain several layers [1]. They fall as hail when they become too heavy for the updrafts to support [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Both form when moist air cools to its dew point near the ground, so condensation occurs at ground level [1]. Radiation fog forms when the ground loses heat at night by longwave radiation under clear skies, cooling the air above it [1]. Advection fog forms when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a colder surface, such as a cold sea current or snow-covered ground [1]. Radiation fog forms over land on calm nights and clears as the sun warms the ground, whereas advection fog can occur at any time of day and often persists along coasts [1]. **[4]**

**11.** As rising air reaches its dew point, condensation releases latent heat [1]. This warms the parcel, so it cools more slowly as it rises [1]. The parcel may stay warmer and less dense than the surrounding air, making it unstable [1], so it continues to rise and builds tall cumulonimbus clouds [1]. Deep clouds with strong updrafts allow droplets and ice to grow large, producing heavy rain, hail and thunderstorms [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Convectional uplift is dominant in equatorial regions, where intense heating and moist air give daily afternoon storms [1], and it brings summer showers in continental interiors [1]. Frontal uplift is the main cause of precipitation in the mid-latitudes, where warm and cold air masses meet in depressions [1], giving rain throughout the year in places such as north-west Europe [1]. Orographic uplift is most important on windward slopes of mountain ranges facing moist prevailing winds [1], and it intensifies the other types, since mountains can trigger convection and enhance frontal rain [1]. Areas in rain shadows receive little precipitation of any type [1]. Overall, the importance of each depends on latitude and relief, and in many places two causes combine [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the SALR for air below its condensation level, or for descending air.
- Converting metres to kilometres incorrectly in lapse-rate calculations.
- Identifying fronts from cloud type alone without using the temperature evidence.
- Writing that condensation cools the air.
- Explaining hail without mentioning updrafts and supercooled water.
