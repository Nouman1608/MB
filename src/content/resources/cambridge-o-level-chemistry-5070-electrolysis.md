Subtopic 4.1 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 defines electrolysis as "the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current". Its eleven outcomes ask you to name the parts of a cell, explain how charge moves, predict and observe the products for four named electrolytes, write half-equations, and describe electroplating.

This guide concentrates on the reasoning that lets you handle any electrolyte, including ones you have not met. The broader [Electrolysis and Fuel Cells study guide](/resources/electrolysis-and-fuel-cells/) covers the whole of Topic 4. For quick recall use the [electrolysis revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-electrolysis-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [electrolysis practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-electrolysis-practice/).

## The cell and its vocabulary

- **Anode:** the **positive** electrode, connected to the positive terminal of the power supply.
- **Cathode:** the **negative** electrode.
- **Electrolyte:** the molten or aqueous ionic substance that undergoes electrolysis.
- **Inert electrodes:** platinum or carbon (graphite), which conduct but do not react.

A solid ionic compound cannot be electrolysed because its ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice. Melting or dissolving frees the ions to move, and that movement is what carries the current through the electrolyte.

## How charge moves: three parts to every answer

Outcome 3 asks you to describe the transfer of charge in three places. A full answer mentions all three:

1. **In the external circuit (wires):** electrons flow from the anode, through the power supply, to the cathode.
2. **At the electrodes:** at the cathode, positive ions **gain** electrons (reduction); at the anode, negative ions **lose** electrons (oxidation).
3. **In the electrolyte:** **ions** move. Positive ions (cations) move towards the cathode; negative ions (anions) move towards the anode.

The most common error is saying that electrons flow through the electrolyte. They do not: in the electrolyte, charge is carried only by moving ions.

## Predicting products: the rules

Outcome 6 gives the general pattern: **metals or hydrogen form at the cathode; non-metals (other than hydrogen) form at the anode.**

**Molten binary compounds (outcome 7).** Only two ions are present, so the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode. Molten zinc chloride gives zinc at the cathode and chlorine at the anode; molten potassium iodide gives potassium and iodine.

**Aqueous solutions.** Water provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions as well, so each electrode has a choice.

- At the **cathode**: if the metal is **less reactive than hydrogen** (such as copper or silver), the metal is deposited. If it is more reactive (such as sodium, potassium, magnesium or calcium), **hydrogen** is given off instead.
- At the **anode** (inert electrodes): a **concentrated** halide solution gives the **halogen**. A **dilute** halide solution, or a solution with no halide at all (such as a sulfate or nitrate), gives **oxygen**.

Outcome 8 is specifically about halides "in dilute or concentrated aqueous solution". Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride gives chlorine at the anode, but very dilute aqueous sodium chloride gives mainly oxygen, because chloride ions are then too scarce to be discharged in preference to hydroxide ions. In both cases hydrogen forms at the cathode.

## The four named electrolytes and what you see

Outcomes 4 and 5 ask for **observations** as well as products. Examiners reward what you would actually see.

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes unless stated) | Cathode | Anode | Other observations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molten lead(II) bromide | Grey, shiny lead collects | Red-brown bromine vapour | Must be kept molten; done in a fume cupboard |
| Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride | Bubbles of hydrogen | Bubbles of pale yellow-green chlorine | Solution left becomes alkaline (sodium hydroxide) |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | Bubbles of hydrogen | Bubbles of oxygen | Hydrogen volume is twice the oxygen volume |
| Aqueous copper(II) sulfate, carbon electrodes | Pink-brown copper coats the electrode | Bubbles of oxygen | Blue colour fades; solution becomes acidic |
| Aqueous copper(II) sulfate, copper electrodes | Copper deposited; cathode gains mass | No gas; anode dissolves and loses mass | Blue colour stays the same |

The two copper(II) sulfate rows are a favourite comparison. With **carbon** electrodes, copper ions are removed from solution at the cathode and nothing replaces them, so the blue fades. With **copper** electrodes, the anode itself is oxidised, Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻, putting copper ions back into solution as fast as the cathode removes them. The concentration, and so the colour, stays constant, and the mass gained by the cathode equals the mass lost by the anode.

## Writing half-equations

Outcome 9: reduction at the cathode, oxidation at the anode. Electrons appear on the **left** of a cathode equation and the **right** of an anode equation.

```
Cathode (reduction)              Anode (oxidation)
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn                  2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu                  2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂                   4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag                    Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻   (copper anode)
```

Check every half-equation two ways: the atoms balance, and the total charge is the same on both sides. In 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻, the left has a charge of 4−, and the right has 4 electrons, also 4−.

## Worked example: an unfamiliar electrolyte

**Question.** Concentrated aqueous copper(II) chloride is electrolysed using carbon electrodes. Predict the product at each electrode, write the half-equations and describe what would be seen. **[5]**

**Model answer.**

- Ions present: Cu²⁺ and Cl⁻ from the salt; H⁺ and OH⁻ from water.
- Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so **copper** is deposited: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. A pink-brown coating forms on the cathode.
- Anode: the solution is a concentrated chloride, so **chlorine** is formed: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Bubbles of pale yellow-green gas are seen; the gas bleaches damp litmus paper.
- The blue-green colour of the solution fades as copper(II) ions are removed.

Each bullet is a separate marking point: product, half-equation and observation at each electrode.

## Electroplating

Outcome 10 asks why metal objects are electroplated: to **improve their appearance** and their **resistance to corrosion**. Outcome 11 asks how. The set-up for plating a steel key with nickel:

- **Cathode:** the object to be plated (the steel key).
- **Anode:** a bar of the plating metal (nickel).
- **Electrolyte:** an aqueous solution containing ions of the plating metal (a nickel salt solution such as nickel sulfate).

Nickel ions are reduced at the key, Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni, building up a thin, even layer. The nickel anode dissolves, Ni → Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻, keeping the concentration of nickel ions constant. This is the same principle as copper(II) sulfate with copper electrodes. To get an even coat, the object is cleaned first and the current kept low.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up anode and cathode.** Anode is positive, cathode is negative. Positive ions are attracted to the cathode, so they are called cations.
- **Writing that electrons move through the solution.** Ions carry the charge in the electrolyte.
- **Forgetting that hydrogen forms at the cathode** when a reactive metal such as sodium is in solution. Sodium is never deposited from water.
- **Forgetting observations.** "Chlorine forms" scores the product; "bubbles of yellow-green gas" scores the observation.
- **Plating the wrong electrode.** The object being plated must be the cathode, because metal ions gain electrons there.
- **Unbalanced charge** in half-equations, especially the oxygen equation.

## Command words in 5070 electrolysis questions

- **Identify:** name the product or electrode, with no explanation required.
- **Describe** (observations): what you would see, hear or detect, such as bubbles, colours, a coating or a change in mass.
- **Predict:** apply the rules to an electrolyte you may not have studied; a reason linked to reactivity or concentration strengthens the answer.
- **Construct:** a balanced half-equation with electrons shown.
