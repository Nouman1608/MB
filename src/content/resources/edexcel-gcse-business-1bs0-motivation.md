This guide covers content item 2.5.4, Motivation, in Theme 2 Topic 2.5 Making human resource decisions of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0), Issue 2, July 2022. The page on [training and motivation](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-training-and-motivation/) defines each method. This page practises the decisions: which method suits which employees, how much it costs, and how to show its effect with figures. Every business and figure is invented.

Companion pages: the [motivation revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-motivation-revision-notes/) and the [motivation practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-motivation-practice/).

## What 2.5.4 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| The importance of motivation in the workplace | attracting employees, retaining employees, productivity |
| How businesses motivate employees | financial methods: remuneration, bonus, commission, promotion, fringe benefits; non-financial methods: job rotation, job enrichment, autonomy |

The specification does not include named motivation theories in this item, so answers should be built from the methods above and the facts of the business.

## Why motivation matters: three routes to the business's objectives

Each of the three reasons connects motivation to something the business can measure.

| Reason | What it means | Evidence to look for in a case |
|---|---|---|
| Attracting employees | A business known as a good place to work gets more, and better, applicants | Number of applicants per vacancy; difficulty filling posts |
| Retaining employees | Motivated staff stay longer, so recruitment and training costs fall and experience is kept | Number or percentage of staff leaving; reasons given in leaving interviews |
| Productivity | Motivated staff produce more output per worker or per hour, at a higher standard | Output per worker; errors; customer complaints |

Productivity matters for costs. If each worker produces more for the same wage, the labour cost of each unit falls, so the business can cut prices or earn a higher profit margin.

### Worked example: productivity at Hollins Print

Hollins Print has 12 machine workers, each paid £480 a week. They produce 9,600 printed boxes a week. After the manager introduced job rotation between cutting, folding and packing, weekly output rose to 10,080 boxes with the same workers. Productivity here is output ÷ number of workers, the standard measure.

- Before: 9,600 ÷ 12 = **800 boxes per worker**.
- After: 10,080 ÷ 12 = **840 boxes per worker**, an increase of (840 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = **5%**.
- Labour cost per box before: £480 ÷ 800 = **£0.60**. After: £480 ÷ 840 = **£0.57** (to the nearest penny).

Using the figures: the method cost nothing extra in pay but lowered the labour cost of each box by about 3p. Over a year that is a significant saving for a business making thousands of boxes a week.

## Financial methods: choosing the right one

The five financial methods suit different jobs and different problems.

| Method | Best suited to | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Remuneration (pay) | Attracting staff when pay at rivals is higher | A permanent rise in costs; boredom is not solved by pay |
| Bonus | Rewarding a target reached, by a person or a team | Staff may chase the target at the expense of quality |
| Commission | Sales jobs where sales can be traced to one person | Pushy selling; unstable pay can lower motivation in a bad month |
| Promotion | Ambitious staff; keeping talent in a growing business | Limited number of posts; those passed over may be demotivated |
| Fringe benefits | Retaining staff without raising basic pay | Not every benefit is valued equally by every employee |

### Worked example: commission or salary at Calder Kitchens

Calder Kitchens sells fitted kitchens. A salesperson is offered two pay schemes:

- **Scheme A:** £1,900 a month basic pay plus 3% commission on sales.
- **Scheme B:** £2,300 a month basic pay and no commission.

In a month with £30,000 of sales:

- Scheme A pay = £1,900 + (0.03 × £30,000) = £1,900 + £900 = **£2,800**.
- Scheme B pay = **£2,300**.

The schemes give the same pay when commission equals the £400 difference in basic pay: £400 ÷ 0.03 = sales of about **£13,333** a month. Above that, Scheme A pays more.

Using the figures: Scheme A rewards strong sellers and costs Calder less in a poor month, so the business shares the risk with the salesperson. A new salesperson may prefer Scheme B's security. A judgement could recommend Scheme A for experienced staff and Scheme B during a new employee's first months.

## Non-financial methods: changing the job itself

- **Job rotation** moves employees between tasks. It suits repetitive work where boredom is the problem, as at Hollins Print, and gives cover for absences. It does not make any single task more challenging.
- **Job enrichment** adds more demanding, responsible tasks to a job. It suits capable staff who feel under-used, such as a shop assistant asked to plan window displays and order stock.
- **Autonomy** gives employees freedom to decide how to do their work. It suits skilled, experienced staff, such as designers or engineers, but is risky for new staff who need guidance.

Telling rotation and enrichment apart: rotation changes **which** tasks a person does, at the same level; enrichment changes **how demanding** the job is.

Non-financial methods are cheap compared with pay rises, but they may require training, and some employees prefer more money to more responsibility.

## Matching method to problem

| Problem in the case | Methods that fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hard to recruit; rivals pay more | Remuneration, fringe benefits | Attract applicants |
| Experienced staff leaving for promotion elsewhere | Promotion, job enrichment | Retain talent with a career path |
| Bored staff on repetitive tasks; low output | Job rotation, team bonus | Variety and a reason to raise productivity |
| Sales team performance uneven | Commission or bonus | Reward is linked to results |
| Skilled staff frustrated by close supervision | Autonomy | Trust raises motivation |

A strong answer links three things: the problem, the method chosen, and the reason in the specification (attracting, retaining or productivity) that the method improves.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (no context): "A bonus is an extra payment for meeting a target. This gives employees a clear reason to work harder, so productivity can rise."
- **Outline**: two linked points about the business, such as the cost of Scheme A at Calder.
- **Analyse**: from method, to motivation, to one of the three outcomes, using the case.
- **Justify**: choose between two methods, with evidence on both and a cost comparison where figures are given.
- **Evaluate**: decide whether a method will work and what it depends on, such as whether staff value money or responsibility more.

### Annotated model answer

*Question (Justify, extract):* Should Hollins Print introduce a team bonus or continue job rotation?

*Strong answer extract:* "Job rotation has already raised output per worker by 5% at no extra cost in pay, and it reduces boredom on repetitive work. A team bonus could raise output further but would add to labour costs, cancelling some of the 3p saving per box. I recommend continuing job rotation, unless output stops rising, when a modest team bonus linked to quality could be added."

*Why it works:* it uses the case figures for one option, compares the cost of the other, and states the condition that would change the decision.

## Common mistakes

- Listing methods without linking them to attracting, retaining or productivity.
- Treating promotion as non-financial: the specification lists it among financial methods.
- Confusing a bonus (for a target or results) with commission (a share of sales).
- Calling any change to a job "enrichment" when it is rotation.
- Recommending a pay rise without mentioning its permanent cost.
- Saying autonomy suits everyone; new or inexperienced staff may need direction.
