## Section A

**1.** State the difference between population density and population distribution. **[2]**

**2.** A country has a population of 2.8 million and an area of 56,000 km². Calculate its
population density. Give the unit. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** large areas of the world that are sparsely populated because they are too cold.
**[2]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why river floodplains are often densely populated. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** The table shows data for the four regions of a country.

| Region | Main landscape | Population | Area (km²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Coastal lowland with a port | 8,400,000 | 12,000 |
| B | Forested hills | 950,000 | 38,000 |
| C | Irrigated river delta | 5,250,000 | 3,500 |
| D | Dry, rocky plateau | 60,000 | 30,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the population density of each region. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the population density of the whole country, to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage of the population living in Regions A and C combined. **[1]**
**(d)** Describe the distribution of population in this country. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest reasons for the high density in Region C and the low density in Region D. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how **three** human factors can lead to a high population density. **[6]**

**7.** Explain why some hot desert areas have dense pockets of population. **[3]**

**8.** For a named country, describe and explain the location of **one** densely populated area and
**one** sparsely populated area. **[7]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Population density is the **average number of people per km²** [1]. Population distribution
is the **pattern of where people live** across an area, showing where they are concentrated or
scarce [1].

**2.** 2,800,000 ÷ 56,000 = **50** [1] **people per km²** [1].

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: Siberia (northern Russia); northern Canada; Greenland; Antarctica;
Alaska; the Tibetan Plateau.

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: flat land that is easy to farm and build on; fertile alluvial soil;
water supply for drinking and irrigation; the river as a transport route; fishing.

**5. (a)** A: 700; B: 25; C: 1,500; D: 2 people per km². All four correct [2]; two or three correct
[1].
**(b)** 14,660,000 ÷ 83,500 [1] = **176 people per km²** [1].
**(c)** (8,400,000 + 5,250,000) ÷ 14,660,000 × 100 = **93%** [1].
**(d)** Up to 3 marks: very **uneven** distribution [1]; population **concentrated in the coastal
lowland and the delta** (A and C), which hold 93% of the people on under a fifth of the land [1];
**sparse** in the hills and especially the plateau, which has only 2 people per km² [1]. Credit
use of figures.
**(e)** Region C, up to 2 marks: fertile **alluvial soils** and **irrigation water** allow
intensive farming that supports many people [1]; flat land is easy to build on and to cross with
roads [1]. Region D, up to 2 marks: **little water** and thin, rocky soils make farming difficult
[1]; rough land and remoteness mean few jobs and poor transport links [1].

**6.** Three factors, 2 marks each (1 for the factor, 1 for the explanation):
- **Employment** in industry and services [1] attracts migrants, so cities grow [1].
- **Good transport links**, such as a port or rail junction [1], make an area accessible for trade
  and commuting [1].
- **Government policy** [1], for example a capital city or planned development scheme, such as
  Brasília in Brazil, brings jobs and investment [1].
- **Political stability** [1] encourages investment and settlement, while conflict drives people
  away [1].
- **Long history** of settlement and farming [1] means large cities and infrastructure already
  exist [1].

**7.** Up to 3 marks: where **water** is available, such as at an oasis, a river (like the Nile) or
from groundwater [1]; where there are valuable **resources** such as oil or minerals that create
jobs [1]; where **technology** such as irrigation, desalination and air conditioning makes life
possible, as in cities of the Gulf states [1].

**8.** Mark in levels.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** general factors with no named areas, or one area only.
- **Level 2 (4–5):** named country with a named dense and a named sparse area, with some
  explanation.
- **Level 3 (6–7):** specific named areas with detailed, linked explanation of physical and human
  factors for both.

Indicative content (Pakistan): **dense** — the Punjab plains and Indus valley, with flat land,
fertile alluvial soil and a large irrigation system, supporting intensive farming and cities such
as Lahore; Punjab is the most populous province. **Sparse** — Balochistan, the largest province by
area but with well under a tenth of the people: dry, rugged plateau and mountains with little
water, poor soils, few jobs and long distances to markets. Equivalent credit for another named
country, such as Egypt (Nile valley and delta versus the Western Desert) or Brazil (south-east
coast versus the Amazon interior).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering question 5(d) with reasons instead of a description.
- Leaving out the unit in density calculations.
- Case studies naming only the country, not the areas within it.
