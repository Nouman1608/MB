## 8.1 Diet

### Nutrients, sources and why you need them (8.1.1)

| Nutrient | Principal sources | Dietary importance |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | Rice, bread, potatoes, pasta, sugar | Main source of energy, released in respiration |
| Lipids (fats and oils) | Butter, cooking oil, cheese, nuts | Energy store; insulation; part of cell membranes |
| Proteins | Meat, fish, eggs, beans, lentils | Growth and repair; making enzymes, antibodies and haemoglobin |
| Vitamin C | Citrus fruits, guava, peppers, broccoli | Needed to make collagen, so keeps skin, gums and blood vessels healthy and helps wounds heal |
| Vitamin D | Oily fish, egg yolk, butter; made in the skin in sunlight | Helps the body absorb calcium, so bones and teeth form properly |
| Calcium | Milk, cheese, yoghurt | Hardens bones and teeth; needed for blood clotting and muscle contraction |
| Iron | Red meat, liver, beans, green leafy vegetables | Needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells |
| Fibre (roughage) | Wholemeal bread, vegetables, fruit, cereals | Adds bulk, so the gut muscles can push food along by peristalsis; prevents constipation |
| Water | Drinks and most foods | Solvent for chemical reactions and transport; makes up most of blood plasma; lost in sweat and urine |

Fibre is mostly cellulose. You cannot digest it, so it stays in the gut as bulk.

### Deficiency diseases (8.1.2)

| Lack of | Disease | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin C | **Scurvy** | Bleeding gums, bleeding under the skin and easy bruising, slow wound healing, tiredness |
| Vitamin D | **Rickets** | Soft, weak bones that bend, giving bowed legs; bone pain; poor growth |
| Calcium | **Rickets** | Same as above: bones are not hardened properly |
| Iron | **Anaemia** | Tiredness, weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath |

Calcium hardens bone and vitamin D is needed to absorb it, so a shortage of either causes rickets.

Less iron means less haemoglobin, so blood carries less oxygen to respiring cells: the person feels tired and breathless.

### A balanced diet (8.1.3)

A **balanced diet** contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions, with enough energy for the person's needs. It is not "equal amounts of everything". The right amounts change with age, sex, activity level and pregnancy. A manual worker, for example, needs more energy than an office worker.

A diet can supply plenty of energy and still lack iron or vitamin C.

## 8.2 Human digestive system

### The regions (8.2.1)

Food passes through the **mouth**, **oesophagus**, **stomach**, **small intestine** (first the **duodenum**, then the **ileum**) and **large intestine** (**colon**, **rectum** and **anus**). Food does not pass through the **salivary glands**, **liver**, **gall bladder** or **pancreas**. These are associated organs that pour secretions into the gut through ducts.

### Why digest food at all? (8.2.2–8.2.5)

Most food is made of large, insoluble molecules such as starch, proteins and lipids. These are too big to pass through the lining of the gut into the blood. **Digestion breaks them into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed.**

- **Physical digestion** is the breakdown of food into smaller pieces **without chemical change** to the food molecules. Chewing, churning in the stomach and emulsification by bile are all physical.
- **Chemical digestion** is the breakdown of **large molecules into small molecules**, catalysed by enzymes.
- Physical digestion **increases the surface area** of food for enzymes to act on in chemical digestion.

**Worked example: why chewing helps**

A piece of food is modelled as a 2 cm cube. It is chewed into 1 cm cubes, then into 0.5 cm cubes. Calculate the total surface area at each stage.

```
2 cm cube:     6 faces × (2 × 2) = 24 cm²
1 cm cubes:    8 cubes × 6 × (1 × 1) = 48 cm²
0.5 cm cubes:  64 cubes × 6 × (0.5 × 0.5) = 96 cm²
```

The volume of food is still 8 cm³ at every stage. Each time the side length halves, the surface area doubles. More surface means more enzyme molecules can reach their substrate at once, so chemical digestion is faster.

### Teeth (8.2.6–8.2.8)

| Type | Position | Function in physical digestion |
|---|---|---|
| Incisors | Front, chisel-shaped | Biting and cutting off pieces of food |
| Canines | Beside the incisors, pointed | Gripping and tearing food |
| Premolars | Behind the canines, with ridges (cusps) | Grinding and crushing |
| Molars | At the back, broad with cusps | Grinding and crushing |

Structure of a tooth, from outside in:

- **Enamel** covers the crown. It is the hardest substance in the body and resists wear from chewing.
- **Dentine** lies under the enamel. It is hard but softer than enamel.
- The **pulp** in the centre contains **nerves** and blood vessels.
- **Cement** covers the root and helps anchor the tooth.
- The tooth is **embedded in the gum**, which surrounds its base, with the root held in the jaw.

### What each region does (8.2.9)

| Region | Function |
|---|---|
| Mouth | Ingestion; physical digestion by teeth; chemical digestion of starch by amylase |
| Salivary glands | Secrete saliva containing amylase |
| Stomach | Physical digestion (churning by muscle); chemical digestion of protein by protease; hydrochloric acid in gastric secretions |
| Small intestine (duodenum and ileum) | Chemical digestion of starch by amylase, maltose by maltase, protein by protease and lipids by lipase |
| Liver | Produces bile; stores glycogen |
| Gall bladder | Stores bile |
| Pancreas | Alkaline secretion containing amylase, protease and lipase |
| Ileum and colon | Absorption |
| Rectum and anus | Egestion |

**Egestion** is the removal of undigested food (faeces) through the anus.

### The four enzymes (8.2.10)

| Enzyme | Substrate | End-products | Where it acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amylase | Starch | Maltose | Mouth; small intestine |
| Maltase | Maltose | Glucose | Small intestine |
| Protease (pepsin, trypsin) | Protein | Amino acids | Stomach (pepsin); small intestine (trypsin) |
| Lipase | Lipids | Fatty acids and glycerol | Small intestine |

Starch digestion takes two enzymes: amylase stops at maltose, and maltase finishes the job. Only glucose is small enough to be absorbed.

### Acid and pH (8.2.11–8.2.12)

**Hydrochloric acid** in the stomach **kills ingested bacteria**. That is its function in the syllabus, so lead with it.

The stomach is acidic and the duodenum is not, because the pancreas adds an alkaline secretion. The two proteases are adapted to these places: **pepsin** in the stomach works best at an acidic pH, and **trypsin** in the duodenum works best at a slightly alkaline pH. The [enzymes guide](/resources/enzymes/) explains why the wrong pH slows an enzyme down.

### Bile (8.2.13)

Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder and released into the duodenum. It **emulsifies** fats: it breaks large fat droplets into many small droplets. This is physical digestion, because no bonds in the fat molecules are broken. The small droplets have a much larger total **surface area**, so **lipase** can digest the fat to **fatty acids and glycerol** faster.

**Worked example: explaining a result**

A student mixes milk, lipase and an indicator in two tubes. Tube A also has bile; tube B has water instead. Tube A changes colour first. Explain.

1. Lipase breaks lipids in the milk into fatty acids and glycerol, and the fatty acids lower the pH, so the indicator changes colour.
2. Bile emulsifies the fat in tube A into smaller droplets.
3. Smaller droplets give a larger surface area for lipase to act on.
4. So fatty acids are produced faster in tube A and the pH falls sooner.

### Peristalsis (8.2.14)

The gut wall has two muscle layers: **circular** muscle running round the gut, and **longitudinal** muscle running along it. **Peristalsis** is waves of contraction of these muscles that move food through the digestive system. When circular muscle contracts behind a lump of food, the gut narrows there and squeezes the food forward; contraction of longitudinal muscle shortens and widens the section ahead.

## 8.3 Absorption and assimilation

### Absorption and assimilation (8.3.1–8.3.3)

The **small intestine** is the region where nutrients are absorbed.

**Absorption** is the movement of nutrients from the intestines into the cells lining the digestive system and then into the blood. It happens by **diffusion**, **osmosis** (for water) and **active transport**. Active transport lets the gut take up glucose and amino acids even when their concentration in the lumen is lower than in the blood.

**Assimilation** is the uptake and use by cells of nutrients from the blood. Two examples: body cells take up amino acids and build them into new proteins; liver cells take up glucose and store it as glycogen.

### Villi and microvilli (8.3.4–8.3.5)

The inner wall of the ileum is covered in millions of finger-like **villi**. Each villus has:

- an **epithelium one cell thick**, so nutrients cross a short distance;
- **microvilli** on the epithelial cells, which fold the cell surface membrane;
- a dense network of **capillaries**, which absorb glucose, amino acids, water and ions and carry them away in the blood;
- a central **lacteal**, a lymph vessel that absorbs fatty acids and glycerol.

Villi increase the internal surface area of the ileum, and microvilli increase it again on every cell. A larger surface area means more nutrients are absorbed per second. The blood flow in the capillaries keeps carrying nutrients away, which maintains a concentration gradient for diffusion.

### Water (8.3.6)

Water is absorbed from the lumen of both the **small intestine** and the **colon**, but **most water absorption happens in the small intestine**.

### The hepatic portal vein (8.3.7)

Most molecules and ions absorbed from the ileum travel to the **liver** in the **hepatic portal vein**. The liver can process them, for example storing excess glucose as glycogen, before they reach the rest of the body.

## Common errors

- Writing that bile is an enzyme or that it "breaks down fat". It emulsifies fat; lipase digests it.
- Saying amylase makes glucose. Amylase makes maltose; maltase makes glucose.
- Giving "provides an acidic pH" as the main function of hydrochloric acid. The syllabus function is killing ingested bacteria.
- Listing the liver or pancreas as places food passes through.
- Confusing egestion (undigested food) with excretion (metabolic waste).
- Naming only vitamin D for rickets: calcium deficiency also causes it.
