## 4.3 Achieving quality production

### What quality means

Quality means how well a product or service meets the needs and expectations of the customer. It is not the same as luxury. A cheap notebook is high quality if it holds together and does its job for the price. A hotel room with a broken lock is poor quality whatever the price.

### Why quality matters for all businesses

The syllabus says "all businesses", so be ready to apply it to a manufacturer, a shop, a clinic, a bus company or a charity.

- Customers repeat purchases and recommend the business, which protects sales revenue.
- A good reputation can support a higher price or make it easier to win new customers.
- Faults cost money: scrapped materials, reworking, replacements, refunds and staff time dealing with complaints.
- Poor quality can lead to legal claims if a product is unsafe or a service causes harm.
- Business customers who buy components will drop a supplier whose parts are late or faulty.

Take care with the direction of cause. Quality does not automatically raise profit. Getting quality right costs money too, so the question is whether the benefits outweigh the costs for that business.

### Quality control

Quality control means checking products or services, usually after a stage of production or at the end, to find faults and stop faulty items reaching the customer. It is about finding mistakes.

Typical ways businesses implement it:

- an inspector examines every item, or a random sample, at one or more stages
- testing, such as stress-testing a chair or running a sample of devices for several hours
- checking against a standard, such as a template, a weight or a size tolerance
- removing faulty items for scrapping or reworking

Sampling is common when checking every item would be too slow, too costly or would destroy the product (a business cannot taste-test every tin of food and still sell it).

**Strengths:** it is easy to understand, catches faults before delivery and protects the brand's name. **Weaknesses:** it finds faults rather than preventing them, materials and labour are already wasted when a fault is found, inspectors cost money, and sampling can miss a faulty item.

#### Worked example 1: sample inspection

Narvik Chutneys fills 6 000 jars a day. An inspector takes a sample of 300 jars from each day's output and finds 12 with loose lids.

Step 1, the proportion faulty in the sample: 12 ÷ 300 = 0.04, which is 4%.

Step 2, assume the sample represents the whole day: 4% of 6 000 = 240 faulty jars.

Step 3, cost of waste: each faulty jar wastes 0.65 of materials and labour, so 240 × 0.65 = 156 per day, or 780 over a five-day week.

Step 4, after adjusting the lid-fitting machine the defect rate falls to 1%. That is 60 faulty jars a day, so the saving is (240 − 60) × 0.65 = 117 per day, or 585 per week.

The figures are estimates because they come from a sample, and they count only wasted production costs, not lost goodwill if faulty jars reach shops.

### Quality assurance

Quality assurance means organising the whole business so that faults are prevented at every stage, rather than found at the end. It is about preventing mistakes. Responsibility spreads to every worker, not only to inspectors.

Typical ways a business can implement it:

- agreed quality standards for every stage, written down and known by all workers
- checking and approving suppliers so that materials arrive to a set standard
- training workers in the skills and standards their job needs
- giving workers responsibility to check their own work and to stop a process if something is wrong
- maintaining machines on a plan so that they do not drift out of tolerance
- collecting customer feedback and using it to improve the process
- recording faults, finding the cause and changing the process

**Strengths:** fewer faults, less waste and rework, and often better worker involvement. **Weaknesses:** training, supplier checks and new systems cost money and time, it can take months to show results, and it needs commitment from managers as well as workers.

#### Worked example 2: comparing outcomes

Umberlee Bakery sold 6 000 loaves a week and had 90 returned as stale or misshapen. It then introduced a written standard for each stage, supplier checks on flour and training for the night shift.

Returns before: 90 ÷ 6 000 = 0.015, which is 1.5%. Returns after, with 30 returns: 30 ÷ 6 000 = 0.005, which is 0.5%. Returns fell by one percentage point, and by two thirds in relative terms.

This shows faults fell for this bakery in this period. A sensible answer would also mention the cost of the training and checks.

### Control versus assurance

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Main idea | Find faults | Prevent faults |
| When | After production or at checkpoints | Throughout, including before production starts |
| Who | Inspectors mainly | Everyone in the business |
| Waste | Faulty items already made | Less waste in the first place |
| Cost pattern | Cost of inspection and of faulty items | Cost of training, systems and supplier checks |

Many businesses use both. If asked to recommend one, fit it to the business: a workshop making one-off items may rely on its owner checking each piece, while a large producer of identical items gains more from preventing faults.

## 4.4 Location decisions

### Factors for manufacturing businesses

Location is the place a business chooses for its operations. Common factors for manufacturers:

- **Cost of land and buildings:** rent, purchase price and local taxes.
- **Labour:** number of workers, their skills, and wage rates.
- **Raw materials and suppliers:** the cost of moving bulky or perishable inputs.
- **Customers:** how far finished goods must travel, especially if heavy or fragile.
- **Transport links:** roads, rail, ports and airports.
- **Utilities and room to expand:** reliable power and water, and space on the site.
- **Government help:** grants, tax breaks or support in some areas.

The importance of each factor depends on the product. A producer of bulky, low-value goods may need to be near its materials, while a maker of compact, high-value products can locate where skilled labour is available.

### Factors for service businesses

Service businesses often depend on customers coming to them, or on workers being able to reach customers:

- **Closeness to customers:** footfall, local population, passing trade.
- **Competitors:** rivals nearby can reduce sales, though a cluster (like a food street) can also attract more shoppers.
- **Visibility and access:** parking, public transport, safety, easy entry.
- **Rent and rates:** often high in busy central locations.
- **Staff:** whether suitably skilled employees live nearby.
- **Digital delivery:** for an online service, customers' location matters little, so cost and staff matter more.

#### Worked example 3: comparing locations

Pindar Dental can lease either a high-street unit at 3 500 a month or an out-of-town unit at 1 800 a month. It expects 420 patients a month on the high street and 300 out of town, paying an average of 55 each.

Revenue on the high street: 420 × 55 = 23 100. Out of town: 300 × 55 = 16 500. Revenue after rent: 23 100 − 3 500 = 19 600 against 16 500 − 1 800 = 14 700. The high street is 4 900 a month better on this comparison.

This ignores other costs, such as extra staff. A full answer treats the figures as one input, then considers parking, access for older patients and lease length.

### Choosing which country

When a business considers a site abroad, the factors include:

- labour costs, skills and availability
- the size and growth of the market it hopes to serve
- transport and shipping costs and the quality of infrastructure
- taxes on imports (tariffs), trade barriers and trade agreements
- exchange rates, which affect costs and revenue when currencies change
- political stability and the risk of disruption
- language, culture and local customs
- government incentives for foreign firms
- legal controls, covered below

#### Worked example 4: unit costs in two countries

Ovelle Candles can make a candle in Country X or Country Y and sell it in a third market. Labour, materials and shipping per candle:

| Cost per candle | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| Labour | 1.80 | 1.10 |
| Materials | 2.10 | 2.10 |
| Shipping | 0.90 | 1.60 |
| Import tariff | none | 10% of labour plus materials |

Country X: 1.80 + 2.10 + 0.90 = 4.80. Country Y: tariff = 10% × (1.10 + 2.10) = 0.32, so the total is 1.10 + 2.10 + 1.60 + 0.32 = 5.12. Country X is 0.32 cheaper per candle, which is 64 000 on 200 000 candles a year. Cheaper labour in Y is outweighed by shipping and the tariff.

A recommendation would still weigh other points, such as political stability and skills.

### The role of legal controls

Governments use laws to influence where businesses locate. In general terms, legal controls can:

- restrict the use of land, so that a factory may not be built in a residential or protected area
- require permission before building or changing the use of a site
- set environmental rules, such as limits on pollution or waste, which can rule out some sites or raise costs
- set health and safety, employment and building standards
- offer incentives, for example to locate in areas needing jobs

Describe controls generally and show the effect: a law may block a location, add cost or delay, or make an area more attractive.

### Relocation

A business may relocate when its premises become too small, when rent or other costs rise, when its customers or suppliers move, when a lease ends or when a new location offers better access to labour or markets. Costs and risks include moving expenses, disruption to production, losing staff who will not move, and the risk that customers cannot find the new site.

### Recommending and justifying a location

For "recommend and justify" questions, use this pattern:

1. Choose one location clearly.
2. Give two or three reasons that use facts from the case (costs, distance, labour, laws).
3. Explain the effect on the business (cost, sales, quality, delivery).
4. Show you considered the other option and why it is weaker, or what risk remains.

## Common errors

- Describing quality as expensive or luxurious instead of meeting customer needs.
- Mixing up quality control with quality assurance, as though the two meant one idea.
- Listing location factors without linking them to the type of business in the question.
- Treating the cheapest site as automatically the best, ignoring labour skills, customers or laws.
- Naming specific laws or tariffs you cannot be sure of, rather than describing the control.
