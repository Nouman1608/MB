## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define productivity. **[2]**

**2.** Which is an example of capital-intensive production?

A: A hand-weaving workshop making carpets to order

B: A tea estate where leaves are picked by hand

C: An automated plant bottling soft drinks

D: A hairdressing salon

**[1]**

**3.** Which change would most directly raise labour productivity in a factory?

A: Hiring 20 more workers on the same machines

B: Training workers to use faster equipment

C: Raising the price of the product

D: Increasing the number of shifts

**[1]**

**4.** Explain why the demand for factors of production is a derived demand. **[2]**

**5.** Two factories make the same product in a week.

| | Workers | Hours per worker | Output (units) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Factory A | 20 | 40 | 12,000 |
| Factory B | 15 | 45 | 10,800 |

**(a)** Calculate output per hour worked in each factory. **[2]**

**(b)** State which factory has the higher labour productivity. **[1]**

**6.** A worker earns $12 an hour and produces 30 units an hour. Wages then rise by 10% and output per hour rises by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate labour cost per unit before and after the changes. **[2]**

**(b)** State what this shows about wage rises and costs. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A firm can make 1,000 units a week by two methods.

- Method L: 10 workers at $200 a week each, plus equipment costing $300 a week.
- Method C: 2 workers at $200 a week each, plus a machine costing $1,400 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per unit of each method at 1,000 units a week. **[2]**

**(b)** Demand falls to 500 units a week. Method L can be run with 5 workers and equipment costing $150. Method C still needs 2 workers and the machine. Calculate the new cost per unit of each method. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what your answers show about the choice of method. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why a firm in a low-wage country might choose labour-intensive production. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a rise in the availability of bank loans could affect a firm's demand for capital and labour. **[3]**

**10.** Explain the difference between production and productivity, using an example. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse two ways in which a firm could raise the productivity of its workers. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a clothing firm should replace many of its workers with machines. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Output per unit of input [1] in a given period, for example output per worker or per hour worked [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. A, B and D rely mainly on labour. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. Training raises output per worker; A and D raise production but not necessarily productivity, and C affects revenue, not output per worker. **[1]**

**4.** Firms want factors only to produce goods and services that they can sell [1], so demand for factors depends on demand for those products, rising and falling with it [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** A: 20 × 40 = 800 hours; 12,000 ÷ 800 = 15 units per hour [1]. B: 15 × 45 = 675 hours; 10,800 ÷ 675 = 16 units per hour [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Factory B [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* Factory A has higher total production, but productivity compares output with the hours worked.

**6. (a)** Before: 12 ÷ 30 = $0.40 [1]. After: 13.20 ÷ 33 = $0.40 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** A wage rise need not raise cost per unit if productivity rises by the same proportion [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** Method L: (10 × 200 + 300) ÷ 1,000 = $2.30 [1]. Method C: (2 × 200 + 1,400) ÷ 1,000 = $1.80 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Method L: (5 × 200 + 150) ÷ 500 = $2.30 [1]. Method C: (400 + 1,400) ÷ 500 = $3.60 [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Capital-intensive production is cheaper only when output is large enough to keep the machine busy [1]; at low output its fixed machine cost is spread over few units, so the flexible labour-intensive method is cheaper [1]. **[2]**

**8.** First: labour is cheap relative to machinery [1], so using more workers gives a lower cost per unit than buying expensive machines [1]. Second: firms may find it hard to raise finance for machines, or lack technicians to maintain them [1], so a method that relies on workers is more practical [1]. (Credit also: small or variable markets; custom products.) **[4]**

**9.** Easier loans make it possible to finance machinery that the firm could not afford before [1], so its demand for capital rises [1]. Demand for labour may fall if machines replace workers, or rise if the new capital lets the firm expand and it needs more staff to run it [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Production is the total output of a firm or economy in a period [1]; productivity is output per unit of input, such as per worker or per hour [1]. Example: a farm that hires more workers may produce more crops in total while each worker produces less, so production rises but productivity falls [1]. **[3]**

**11.** First: training [1] improves workers' skills, so they work faster and make fewer mistakes, raising output per hour [1]. Second: investing in better equipment [1] gives each worker more capital to work with, so each can produce more in the same time [1]. (Credit also: better management, motivation through pay or conditions, division of labour, improved health and safety.) **[4]**

**12.** For: if the firm makes large, standard orders, machines can lower cost per unit [1], give consistent quality and run for long hours, making the firm more competitive [1]. Against: machines need a large initial investment and possibly borrowing [1]; if demand is small or fashion changes quickly, machines may stand idle, raising cost per unit, while workers can switch between styles [1]; job losses may harm the firm's reputation and the local community [1]. Judgement: the firm should mechanise if its market is large and steady, labour is expensive and it can finance the machines; a firm making small batches of changing designs may do better keeping a labour-intensive method [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total output to compare productivity when hours or workers differ.
- Giving "hire more workers" as a way to raise productivity.
- Assuming high wages always mean high costs per unit.
- Saying capital-intensive production is always cheaper, ignoring the size of the market.
- Explaining factor demand without linking it to demand for the product.
- Ending a discussion without saying what the decision depends on.
