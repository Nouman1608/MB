## Global distribution, explained by margin type

| Setting | Earthquakes | Volcanoes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oceanic-continental subduction | Shallow to very deep, along a sloping Benioff zone; the largest earthquakes | Explosive, andesitic | Andes |
| Oceanic-oceanic subduction | As above | Explosive island arcs | Japan, the Aleutians |
| Divergent (ocean ridge) | Shallow, usually moderate | Effusive, basaltic | Iceland on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge |
| Continental rift | Shallow | Varied | East African Rift |
| Conservative | Shallow, can be large | None | San Andreas Fault, California; North Anatolian Fault, Turkey |
| Continental collision | Shallow, spread across a wide belt | Few or none | Himalaya |
| Hotspot (intraplate) | Mostly small, linked to magma movement | Effusive shield volcanoes | Hawaii |

Two points earn analysis credit. First, **depth of focus** changes the hazard: shallow earthquakes cause stronger shaking at the surface, while the deepest subduction earthquakes, hundreds of kilometres down, are felt over large areas but less violently. Second, not all hazards are on margins: intraplate earthquakes occur on old faults within plates, and hotspot volcanoes lie far from boundaries.

## Earthquakes and their hazards

An earthquake is the sudden release of strain along a fault. Energy travels as **P waves** (compressional and fastest), **S waves** (shear waves, slower, which cannot pass through liquids) and **surface waves**, which arrive last and cause most damage. **Moment magnitude** measures energy: each whole unit is about 32 times more energy, so an Mw 9.0 earthquake releases about 1,000 times the energy of an Mw 7.0.

- **Shaking:** damage depends on magnitude, depth, distance from the epicentre, duration, local geology and building design. Soft sediments amplify ground motion; buildings whose natural frequency matches the shaking resonate and collapse.
- **Landslides:** shaking reduces friction on steep, weathered slopes. Landslides block roads and rivers, cutting off rescue and sometimes forming landslide-dammed lakes that later burst.
- **Soil liquefaction:** saturated, loose sand or silt loses strength as pore-water pressure rises during shaking. Buildings tilt or sink, pipes rupture and sand "boils" erupt at the surface.
- **Tsunami:** sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor at a subduction zone, or a large submarine landslide, lifts the whole water column. In deep water, tsunami have very long wavelengths and low heights, and travel at a speed given approximately by √(gd), where g is gravitational acceleration and d is depth. At 4,000 m depth that is about 200 m per second, or roughly 710 km per hour. As depth falls near the coast, the wave slows, shortens and grows in height. The first sign is often the sea **drawing back**, exposing the sea bed.

The **Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004**, generated by an Mw 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra, killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries. There was no regional tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean; one was established afterwards.

## Volcanoes and their hazards

Eruption style depends on magma **viscosity**, controlled mainly by silica content and temperature, and on **gas content**.

| Eruption type | Magma | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Hawaiian | Basaltic, runny | Lava fountains and long lava flows |
| Strombolian | Basaltic to andesitic | Regular bursts of gas throwing out lava clots and bombs |
| Vulcanian | Viscous | Short, violent explosions clearing a blocked vent; ash clouds |
| Peléan | Viscous, dome-building | Dome collapse generates nuées ardentes |
| Plinian | Very viscous, gas-rich | Columns tens of kilometres high, widespread ash, pyroclastic flows |

**Products and why they are hazardous:**

- **Lava flows:** basaltic flows can be fast on steep slopes, but most move slowly enough to walk away from. They destroy property rather than lives. The 2018 eruption of Kīlauea, Hawaii, destroyed hundreds of homes in the Lower Puna district.
- **Pyroclastic flows and nuées ardentes:** ground-hugging avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock, often hundreds of degrees Celsius, moving faster than people can run. On Montserrat, pyroclastic flows from the Soufrière Hills volcano killed 19 people on 25 June 1997 and later buried much of the abandoned capital, Plymouth.
- **Lahars:** mixtures of volcanic debris and water from melted snow and ice, crater lakes or heavy rain. They follow valleys and can travel tens of kilometres, so they threaten places far from the vent.
- **Volcanic landslides:** collapse of an over-steepened or bulging flank, which can release pressure suddenly and trigger a lateral blast.
- **Ash fallout:** wet ash is heavy enough to collapse roofs; fine ash damages lungs, machinery, crops and jet engines. The 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull in Iceland was small, but its ash closed much of European airspace for several days.

## Primary and secondary impacts

**Primary impacts** come directly from the event: collapsing buildings, burial by ash, people killed by pyroclastic flows. **Secondary impacts** follow from them: fire from broken gas pipes, disease from contaminated water, homelessness, loss of income, lahars in later rainy seasons, and economic disruption. A useful exam habit is to classify impacts in a table by type (social, economic, environmental) and by timing (primary, secondary).

The scale of impact depends on **vulnerability** as much as on the size of the event. The Haiti earthquake of 12 January 2010 (Mw 7.0) near Port-au-Prince killed many more people than earthquakes of similar magnitude in wealthier countries, because of dense informal housing, weak construction and limited emergency capacity. The Turkey–Syria earthquakes of 6 February 2023 (the largest Mw 7.8) killed more than 50,000 people, partly because many buildings did not meet existing building codes.

## Prediction, hazard mapping, preparedness and monitoring

**Volcanoes can often be forecast** because magma movement produces measurable signals: swarms of small earthquakes, ground deformation (tiltmeters, GPS and satellite radar), changes in gas emissions such as sulphur dioxide, and changes in temperature.

**Earthquakes cannot be predicted** to a specific time and place. Forecasts are probabilities over decades. The Parkfield experiment in California expected a moderate earthquake around 1988; it did not come until 2004, showing the limits of prediction from past regularity.

**Hazard mapping** combines the mapped extent of past deposits (lava, pyroclastic flows, lahars), fault locations, ground type and liquefaction susceptibility. It guides land-use zoning and evacuation routes.

**Preparedness** includes building codes and retrofitting (cross-bracing, base isolation, flexible frames), public education and drills, early warning systems, tsunami evacuation routes and vertical evacuation buildings, and emergency supplies.

## Perception of risk

People's perception of risk does not always match the actual risk. People may stay because of fertile volcanic soils, jobs, family ties, cost of moving, past experience of events that caused little harm, or trust in technology. Communication is critical: in L'Aquila, Italy, an earthquake on 6 April 2009 killed over 300 people after public reassurance during a swarm of small tremors. Six scientists and an official were convicted of manslaughter in 2012; the scientists were acquitted on appeal in 2014. The case shows how uncertain science, public messages and responsibility interact.

A useful model is **risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity to cope**, which explains why the same event can be a minor incident in one place and a disaster in another.

## Worked example

*Explain why tsunami can cause deaths far from the earthquake that generated them. [5]*

> A subduction earthquake displaces the whole water column [1]. In deep ocean the wave travels at hundreds of kilometres per hour with little loss of energy [1], so it can cross an ocean in hours [1]. Near the coast it slows and grows in height, flooding low-lying land [1]. Where no warning system exists, people far away receive no warning because they did not feel the earthquake, as around the Indian Ocean in 2004 [1].

## Common mistakes

- Treating magnitude as the only cause of deaths, ignoring vulnerability.
- Confusing lava flows with pyroclastic flows.
- Claiming earthquakes can be predicted.
- Listing impacts without classifying them as primary or secondary.
- Forgetting that lahars and tsunami reach places far from the source.

## Exam technique

**Describe the distribution** wants the margin pattern plus exceptions (hotspots, intraplate). **Explain** wants process chains (shaking, pore-water pressure, liquefaction). **Assess** or **evaluate** prediction and preparedness needs evidence of success and failure, and recognition that monitoring only saves lives if warnings are trusted and acted on.
