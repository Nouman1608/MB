Cambridge O Level Economics (2281) asks a simple question with complicated answers: how do societies decide what to produce, how to produce it and who gets it, when resources are limited? The course follows that question through markets, the behaviour of households, workers and firms, government policy, development and international trade. This page describes the 2026 edition of the syllabus. It is examined through two papers taken by all candidates: Paper 1 Multiple Choice, lasting 45 minutes, marked out of 30 and worth 30% of the grade, and Paper 2 Structured Questions, lasting 135 minutes, marked out of 90 and worth 70%.

## How the course is organised

There are six topics. A useful way to see them is as three pairs.

### Pair one: the problem and the market response

**Topic 1, The basic economic problem** covers the nature of the economic problem, the factors of production, opportunity cost and production possibility curve (PPC) diagrams.

**Topic 2, The allocation of resources** explains how markets answer that problem. It separates microeconomics from macroeconomics, then works through the role of markets, demand, supply, price determination and price changes, price elasticity of demand (PED) and of supply (PES), and the market and mixed economic systems, including market failure.

### Pair two: decision makers and government

**Topic 3, Microeconomic decision makers** looks at the agents inside the economy: money and banking, households, workers, trade unions, firms, firms and production, firms' costs, revenue and objectives, and market structure.

**Topic 4, Government and the macroeconomy** turns to the state: the role of government, its macroeconomic aims, fiscal policy, monetary policy, supply-side policy, economic growth, employment and unemployment, and inflation and deflation.

### Pair three: beyond the national economy

**Topic 5, Economic development** considers living standards, poverty, population and the differences in economic development between countries.

**Topic 6, International trade and globalisation** covers international specialisation; globalisation, free trade and protection; foreign exchange rates; and the current account of the balance of payments.

## How it's assessed

| Paper | Title | Length | Marks | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | Multiple Choice | 45 minutes | 30 | 30% |
| Paper 2 | Structured Questions | 135 minutes | 90 | 70% |

- **Paper 1** contains 30 multiple-choice questions, all compulsory. It rewards wide, accurate knowledge across every topic.
- **Paper 2** begins with one compulsory question and then asks you to answer three more from a choice of four. This is where explanation, analysis and judgement earn marks, and with 70% of the weighting it decides most of the final grade.
- The qualification is **not tiered**: there is one set of papers for all candidates, with grades from A* to E.
- Both papers are **externally assessed**, and assessment is **linear**, so the papers are sat together at the end of the course.

**A note on syllabus editions.** The 2026 edition is a single-year syllabus, so 2026 is both its first and its final examination year. Cambridge has also published a 2027-2029 edition of 2281. If your examinations are in 2027 or later, check that edition for the details that apply to you.

## How the parts fit together

The pairs above are not separate compartments. Some of the strongest links run across them.

- **Demand and supply is everywhere.** The model from Topic 2 explains prices in product markets, but the same reasoning applies to labour in Topic 3 and to currencies in Topic 6. Learn it thoroughly once and you will reuse it constantly.
- **Firms connect micro and macro.** Firms' costs, revenue and objectives in Topic 3 shape how they respond to the fiscal, monetary and supply-side policies in Topic 4.
- **Government objectives can clash.** Growth, employment and stable prices from Topic 4 do not always move together, and Topics 5 and 6 add development and trade to the picture.
- **Market failure links back to government.** Topic 2's market failure is the reason many of Topic 4's interventions exist.

The skills tested do not change from topic to topic: accurate definitions, well-labelled diagrams, logical chains of reasoning, and balanced evaluation that ends in a clear conclusion.

## Planning your study

The syllabus order is a sound sequence for first teaching, since each topic leans on the one before.

1. Spend the opening part of the course on Topics 1 and 2. Make sure you can draw and shift demand and supply curves confidently, and practise PED and PES until they feel routine.
2. Move to Topic 3, keeping the link to Topic 2 in view whenever wages or market structure come up.
3. Study Topic 4 next, building a one-page summary of each policy type and its likely effects.
4. Finish new content with Topics 5 and 6.
5. Use the remaining time for whole-course review and full past papers.

Multiple-choice questions make a good weekly check from the start. Longer structured answers should begin as soon as a topic is complete, rather than waiting for the revision period. For guidance on approaching each paper, use the course's exam-preparation page.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 assessed?**
By two compulsory, externally assessed papers: Paper 1 Multiple Choice (45 minutes, 30 marks, 30%) and Paper 2 Structured Questions (135 minutes, 90 marks, 70%).

**How does the choice in Paper 2 work?**
You answer one compulsory question and then three questions chosen from four.

**What grades are available in O Level Economics 2281?**
Grades run from A* to E. The course is not tiered.

**Does this page apply if I sit the exam in 2027?**
This page describes the 2026 edition. Cambridge has also published a 2027-2029 edition of 2281, so check that syllabus if you sit from 2027.

**How many topics does the syllabus contain?**
Six, running from the basic economic problem to international trade and globalisation.
