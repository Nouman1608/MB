## Questions

**1.** Define the term mixed economic system. **[2]**

**2.** In Country G the market for cooking gas has demand Qd = 90 − 3P and supply Qs = −30 + 7P, where P is the price per cylinder.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The government sets a maximum price of 10. Calculate the shortage. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one consequence of the shortage for consumers. **[2]**

**3.** In Country H the market for a farm crop has demand Qd = 110 − 5P and supply Qs = −10 + 10P. The government sets a minimum price of 10.

**(a)** Calculate the surplus. **[3]**
**(b)** The government buys the whole surplus. Calculate the cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one drawback of this policy for the government. **[2]**

**4.** In Country I the labour market has demand Ld = 180 − 10W and supply Ls = −20 + 15W, where W is the hourly wage and labour is measured in hundreds of workers.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** A minimum wage of 9 is introduced. Calculate the unemployment, in workers. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage fall in employment. **[2]**
**(d)** State one benefit of the minimum wage. **[1]**

**5.** In Country J the demand for and supply of its currency, the kuri, are Qd = 400 − 50P and Qs = 100 + 100P, where P is the price in foreign currency units per kuri and quantities are in millions.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium exchange rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The central bank sets a minimum price of 2.5. Calculate the excess supply of kuri. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the foreign currency the central bank spends to buy this excess. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the central bank cannot keep the minimum price indefinitely. **[2]**

**6.** In Country K, a good has demand Qd = 150 − 6P and supply Qs = −30 + 12P. The government imposes an indirect tax of 3 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity before the tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the new supply equation and calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate how much of the revenue is paid by consumers and how much by producers. **[2]**

**7.** In Country L, a good has demand Qd = 160 − 8P and supply Qs = −20 + 12P. The government pays a subsidy of 2.5 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity before the subsidy. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost of the subsidy. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage increase in quantity, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**8.** The government of Country M is considering privatising its state-owned rail network.

**(a)** Distinguish between privatisation and nationalisation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two possible drawbacks of privatising the rail network. **[4]**

**9.** The government of Country N has a budget of 150 for new health clinics, each costing 3.0.

**(a)** Calculate how many clinics it could build with the whole budget. **[1]**
**(b)** It builds 30 clinics and spends the rest on schools costing 2.5 each. Calculate how many schools it can build. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why a government may provide a public good directly. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one implication for firms of a law limiting factory emissions. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate whether an indirect tax is more effective than regulation in reducing consumption of a demerit good. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the effectiveness of government intervention in overcoming the drawbacks of a market economic system. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An economic system in which resources are allocated partly by the private sector through markets and the price mechanism [1] and partly by the public sector, meaning government decisions [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns credit when it names both sectors; naming only the market, or only the government, describes a different system.

**2. (a)** 90 − 3P = −30 + 7P, so 120 = 10P [1]. **P = 12, Q = 54** [1].
**(b)** At 10: Qd = 90 − 30 = 60 [1]; Qs = −30 + 70 = 40 [1]; **shortage = 20** [1].
**(c)** Only 40 can be supplied, so some buyers cannot get a cylinder [1]. Some may pay above 10 on a black market, or queue [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Checking that 10 is below the equilibrium of 12 before calculating shows you know a maximum price only binds below equilibrium.

**3. (a)** At 10: Qd = 110 − 50 = 60 [1]; Qs = −10 + 100 = 90 [1]; **surplus = 30** [1].
**(b)** 30 × 10 = **300** [1].
**(c)** The 300 has an opportunity cost, since it could have funded other services [1]. The surplus must also be stored or disposed of, which adds cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A drawback is stronger when you name the cost and what it displaces, rather than saying only that it is expensive.

**4. (a)** 180 − 10W = −20 + 15W, so 200 = 25W [1]. **W = 8, L = 100 hundred (10,000 workers)** [1].
**(b)** Ld = 180 − 90 = 90 [1]; Ls = −20 + 135 = 115 [1]; unemployment = 25 hundred = **2,500 workers** [1].
**(c)** Change = 90 − 100 = −10, divided by the original 100 [1]; **10% fall** [1].
**(d)** Workers who keep their jobs earn a higher wage (9 instead of 8) [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Labour is in hundreds, so quoting 25 rather than 2,500 workers is a typical slip; show the unit conversion.

**5. (a)** 400 − 50P = 100 + 100P, so 300 = 150P [1]. **P = 2, Q = 300 million** [1].
**(b)** At 2.5: Qd = 400 − 125 = 275 [1]; Qs = 100 + 250 = 350 [1]; **excess supply = 75 million** [1].
**(c)** 75 × 2.5 = **187.5 million foreign currency units** [1].
**(d)** The central bank pays for the excess from its reserves of foreign currency [1]. Reserves are limited, so they run out and the minimum price cannot be defended [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whose reserves are used; "the government runs out of money" is vaguer than foreign currency reserves.

**6. (a)** 150 − 6P = −30 + 12P, so 180 = 18P [1]. **P = 10, Q = 90** [1].
**(b)** New supply Qs = −30 + 12(P − 3) = −66 + 12P [1]. 150 − 6P = −66 + 12P, so 216 = 18P [1]. **P = 12, Q = 78** [1].
**(c)** 3 × 78 = **234** [1].
**(d)** Consumers: (12 − 10) × 78 = **156** [1]. Producers: (3 − 2) × 78 = **78** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Producers receive the new price minus the tax (9), so their loss is 1 per unit, not 3; the two shares must add to the revenue.

**7. (a)** 160 − 8P = −20 + 12P, so 180 = 20P [1]. **P = 9, Q = 88** [1].
**(b)** New supply Qs = −20 + 12(P + 2.5) = 10 + 12P [1]. 150 = 20P, so **P = 7.5** [1] and **Q = 100** [1].
**(c)** 2.5 × 100 = **250** [1].
**(d)** (100 − 88) ÷ 88 [1] = 0.136, so **13.6%** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The subsidy is paid on the new quantity of 100, not the old quantity of 88.

**8. (a)** Privatisation is the transfer of ownership from the public sector to the private sector [1]; nationalisation is the transfer from the private sector to the public sector [1].
**(b)** Drawback one: unprofitable lines may be closed [1], leaving some communities with no service [1]. Drawback two: fares may rise [1], if a single private operator has monopoly power and no competitor [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each drawback needs a reason; naming "higher prices" alone, without why the private owner would raise them, earns less than a developed point.

**9. (a)** 150 ÷ 3.0 = **50** [1].
**(b)** 30 × 3.0 = 90 [1]; 150 − 90 = 60 [1]; 60 ÷ 2.5 = **24 schools** [1].
**(c)** Public goods are non-excludable, so people can use them without paying [1]; private firms cannot make a profit and so will not supply them [1].
**(d)** Firms face compliance costs, such as new equipment [1], which may raise their costs and prices or cut profits [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An implication is a consequence, so link the rule to what happens to the firm, not merely to what the rule says.

**10.** Tax: raises the price of the good, so quantity demanded falls [1]. The fall depends on elasticity: if demand is inelastic, consumption barely changes [1]. It raises revenue which can fund health care or education [1]. Consumers of lower income bear more of the burden [1]. Regulation: a limit or ban directly cuts consumption [1]. It needs inspection and penalties, which cost money [1], and a ban can create an illegal market [1]. Judgement: a tax is better where demand is elastic and revenue is wanted; regulation is better where the harm is severe and enforcement is possible [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that reach a conclusion tied to a condition, such as elasticity or enforcement ability, read as evaluation rather than a list of features.

**11.** Maximum price makes essentials affordable but causes a shortage [1]. Minimum price or wage helps producers or workers but creates a surplus or unemployment [1]. Taxes raise the price of demerit goods and the revenue [1], but are less effective if demand is inelastic [1]. Subsidies raise consumption of merit goods [1] but have an opportunity cost [1]. Direct provision secures public goods [1], but the government may lack information, so intervention can misallocate resources (government failure) [1]. Judgement: intervention is effective where it targets the specific failure, is set at the right level and its costs are lower than the benefit [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use named measures with specific consequences; a general claim that government "helps" or "hurts" does not show analysis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Setting a maximum price above equilibrium, or a minimum below it.
- Reporting the wrong gap: shortage is Qd − Qs, surplus is Qs − Qd.
- Using the original quantity instead of the new quantity for tax revenue or subsidy cost.
- Moving the demand curve for a tax or subsidy.
- Forgetting units, such as hundreds of workers or millions of currency units.
- Describing a policy without a consequence or a reason.
- Evaluation that stops at a list of points, or a verdict that names no condition.
- Confusing privatisation and nationalisation.
