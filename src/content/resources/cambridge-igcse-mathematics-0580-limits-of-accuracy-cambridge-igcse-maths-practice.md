These questions cover subtopic 1.10 Limits of accuracy of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. Section A uses Core content (bounds of a single rounded value) and suits every candidate. Section B is **(Extended)**: it asks for bounds of the results of calculations, which the syllabus does not expect of Core candidates.

Related: [limits of accuracy study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-limits-of-accuracy-cambridge-igcse-maths/) | [limits of accuracy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-limits-of-accuracy-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/)

## Section A

**1.** (non-calculator)

**(a)** A pencil is 7.6 cm long, correct to 1 decimal place. Write down the lower bound of its length. **[1]**

**(b)** A town has an area of 2300 hectares, correct to the nearest 100 hectares. Write down the upper bound of the area. **[1]**

**2.** (non-calculator) The mass of a parcel is 0.48 kg, correct to 2 significant figures. Complete the error interval for the mass, m kg. **[2]**

**3.** (non-calculator) A sprinter's time is 14.2 seconds, correct to the nearest tenth of a second.

**(a)** Write down the upper bound of the time. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the time cannot actually be equal to this upper bound. **[1]**

**4.** (non-calculator) The height of a tree is 35 m, correct to the nearest 5 m. Write down the lower bound and the upper bound of the height. **[2]**

**5.** (non-calculator) The number of people at a concert is 18 000, correct to 2 significant figures. Find the smallest and the largest possible number of people. **[2]**

**6.** (non-calculator) Lena's height is 162 cm, correct to the nearest centimetre. Ana's height is 1.6 m, correct to 1 decimal place. Ana says she cannot be taller than Lena. Use bounds to show that Ana is wrong. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Extended) (calculator) A rectangle measures 9.4 cm by 5.7 cm, each correct to 1 decimal place.

**(a)** Calculate the lower bound of the perimeter. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the upper bound of the area. **[2]**

**8.** (Extended) (calculator) An athlete runs 1500 m, correct to the nearest 100 m, in 252 seconds, correct to the nearest second.

**(a)** Calculate the lower bound of the average speed. Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the upper bound of the average speed. Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**9.** (Extended) (non-calculator) A rope is 50 m long, correct to the nearest metre. Three pieces are cut from it. Each piece is 12.4 m long, correct to 1 decimal place. Calculate the lower bound of the length of rope left. **[3]**

**10.** (Extended) (calculator) A block has a mass of 840 g, correct to the nearest 10 g, and a volume of 310 cm³, correct to the nearest 10 cm³. Density = mass ÷ volume.

**(a)** Calculate the upper bound of the density. Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the lower bound of the density uses the upper bound of the volume. **[1]**

**11.** (Extended) (non-calculator) A shelf is 30 cm long, correct to the nearest centimetre. Six books, each 4.9 cm thick correct to 1 decimal place, are to stand side by side on the shelf. Use bounds to decide whether the books will definitely fit. Show your working. **[3]**

**12.** (Extended) (calculator) a = 5.6, b = 3.2 and c = 1.6, each correct to 1 decimal place. Calculate

**(a)** the upper bound of (a + b) ÷ c, **[2]**

**(b)** the lower bound of (a + b) ÷ c. **[2]**

Give each answer correct to 3 significant figures.

## Answers

**1. (a)** **7.55 cm** [1]

**(b)** **2350 hectares** [1] **[2]**

**2.** One correct bound, 0.475 or 0.485 [1]; **0.475 ⩽ m < 0.485** (in kg) [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* 0.48 to 2 s.f. is rounded to the nearest 0.01, so the half-unit is 0.005. Using 0.05 as the half-unit gives 0.43 to 0.53 (0.48 ± 0.05), which would be the interval only for a value rounded to the nearest 0.1.

**3. (a)** **14.25 s** [1]

**(b)** A time of exactly 14.25 s would round up to **14.3 s**, not 14.2 s, so the time is less than 14.25 s [1] **[2]**

**4.** Lower bound **32.5 m** [1]; upper bound **37.5 m** [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* the unit of rounding is 5 m, so the half-unit is 2.5 m. Answers of 34.5 and 35.5 come from treating the value as rounded to the nearest metre.

**5.** The unit is 1000, so 17 500 ⩽ n < 18 500 [1]; smallest **17 500**, largest **18 499** [1] **[2]**

**6.** Ana's upper bound is 1.65 m = 165 cm [1]; Lena's lower bound is 161.5 cm, and 165 > 161.5, so Ana **could** be taller than Lena [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* compare in the same unit. The largest possible height for Ana and the smallest possible height for Lena are the two values that matter.

**7. (a)** 2 × (9.35 + 5.65) [1] = **30 cm** [1]

**(b)** 9.45 × 5.75 [1] = **54.3375 cm²** [1] **[4]**

**8. (a)** 1450 ÷ 252.5 [1] = 5.7425... = **5.74 m/s** [1]

**(b)** 1550 ÷ 251.5 [1] = 6.1630... = **6.16 m/s** [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* the lower bound of a speed uses the smallest distance and the largest time. Using 1450 ÷ 251.5 gives 5.77 m/s and earns no accuracy mark.

**9.** Lower bound of the rope 49.5 m [1]; upper bound of the three pieces 3 × 12.45 = 37.35 m [1]; 49.5 − 37.35 = **12.15 m** [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* the shortest possible remainder comes from the shortest rope minus the longest pieces. The 3 is an exact count, so it has no bounds.

**10. (a)** 845 ÷ 305 [1] = 2.7704... = **2.77 g/cm³** [1]

**(b)** Dividing by a larger number gives a smaller answer, so the largest possible volume gives the smallest possible density [1] **[3]**

**11.** Upper bound of the books 6 × 4.95 = 29.7 cm [1]; lower bound of the shelf 29.5 cm [1]; 29.7 > 29.5, so the books **might not fit**: they will not definitely fit [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* "definitely fit" needs the worst case, which is the thickest books on the shortest shelf. Comparing 6 × 4.9 = 29.4 with 30 gives the wrong conclusion.

**12. (a)** (5.65 + 3.25) ÷ 1.55 [1] = 5.7419... = **5.74** [1]

**(b)** (5.55 + 3.15) ÷ 1.65 [1] = 5.2727... = **5.27** [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a whole unit instead of half a unit.
- Reading the number of digits rather than the stated accuracy, as in Questions 4 and 5.
- Giving 14.249 or 14.2499 as an upper bound.
- (Extended) Pairing the bounds wrongly in a division or subtraction, as in Questions 8 and 9.
- (Extended) Applying bounds to exact numbers such as the count of books or pieces.
- (Extended) Concluding "it fits" from the rounded values instead of the worst-case bounds.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do Core candidates need bounds of calculations?**
No. The 0580 Core syllabus notes say Core candidates are not expected to find bounds of the results of calculations. They must still give bounds of a single rounded value.

**Should I round the answer to a bounds calculation?**
Only if the question asks, as Questions 8, 10 and 12 do. Otherwise give the exact result, such as 54.3375 cm².

**How do I spot which bound to use in a "will it fit" question?**
Take the worst case: the largest possible size of the objects and the smallest possible size of the space.
