## Section A: dramatic methods (original scene)

Read the scene, then answer questions 1 to 6.

> *A small kitchen. Evening. A clock ticks loudly. RUTH, in her sixties, sits at the table with an
> unopened letter in front of her. Her son DANIEL, in his thirties, enters in his coat and stops in
> the doorway.*
>
> **DANIEL:** You didn't answer the phone.
>
> **RUTH:** *(not looking up)* It rang.
>
> **DANIEL:** I know it rang. I was the one ringing it.
>
> *(Pause. He sees the letter.)*
>
> **DANIEL:** Is that from the hospital?
>
> **RUTH:** Sit down. Your tea's gone cold.
>
> **DANIEL:** Mum.
>
> **RUTH:** *(She pushes the letter an inch towards him, then pulls it back.)* I'll open it when I'm
> ready.
>
> *(The clock ticks. DANIEL takes off his coat, slowly, and sits. Neither of them touches the letter.
> The lights fade until only the table is lit.)*
>
> *(An original scene written for this practice page.)*

**1.** What does the opening stage direction suggest about the mood of the scene? Refer to two
details. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the effect of Ruth's reply "It rang." **[2]**

**3.** How does the playwright use the letter as a prop? **[3]**

**4.** Comment on the effect of the pause and of the direction that Daniel takes off his coat
"slowly". **[3]**

**5.** How might the final lighting direction affect an audience? **[2]**

**6.** How does the dialogue show the balance of power between Ruth and Daniel? **[3]**

## Section B: essay questions on your set play

**7.** Explore how the playwright presents a relationship between two characters in your studied

**8.** How does the playwright create tension in the final act or final scenes of your studied play?
**[30]**

**9.** Answer the option for your studied play. **[30]**

- **(a)** *An Inspector Calls*: How does Priestley present ideas about responsibility?
- **(b)** *A View from the Bridge*: Explore how Miller presents Eddie as a tragic figure.
- **(c)** *Kindertransport*: How does Samuels use the relationship between Eva and Evelyn to explore

- **(e)** *Death and the King's Horseman*: How does Soyinka present Elesin's failure to carry out his

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The kitchen setting is domestic and ordinary (1), but the loudly ticking clock creates tension
and a sense of waiting or time running out (1); the unopened letter in front of Ruth suggests
something feared that has not yet been faced (1).

**2.** The minimal, literal reply avoids Daniel's real point (1); its shortness, with Ruth "not
looking up", suggests evasion or fear, and creates dry, uncomfortable humour (1).

**3.** The letter is the focus of the scene's unspoken subject (1). Ruth pushing it towards Daniel and
pulling it back shows her conflict between wanting to share the news and wanting to control it (1).
Leaving it untouched at the end keeps the audience in suspense, since we never learn what it says (1).

**4.** The pause gives the audience time to register Daniel's frustration and to notice the letter
with him (1). Taking off his coat signals that he has decided to stay (1); "slowly" suggests reluctance
or a recognition that he must wait on his mother's terms (1).

**5.** Narrowing the light to the table isolates the characters and the letter (1), so the audience
focuses on what is unsaid and the scene ends unresolved and intimate (1).

**6.** Daniel asks questions and makes a plea ("Mum."), but Ruth uses commands ("Sit down") and
changes the subject (1); she decides when the letter will be opened (1); so although she is the
vulnerable one, she controls the conversation, and Daniel's power is limited to waiting (1).

### Section B: how to mark questions 7 to 9

Essays are marked holistically on AO1 (15) and AO2 (15). The bands below are Marlbridge's
illustrative guidance, not Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 25-30 | Perceptive, sustained argument; precise analysis of dramatic methods and their effect on an audience |
| 19-24 | Thorough, well-supported argument; clear analysis of language, form and structure |
| 13-18 | Clear response with relevant references; some analysis of methods |
| 7-12 | Some understanding, largely narrative; limited comment on methods |
| 1-6 | Simple narrative with little reference to the playwright's choices |

**7 and 8. What to credit (any play):** an argument across several moments; analysis of dialogue
patterns, stage directions, entrances and exits, structure and staging; a consistent focus on the
audience's response. For question 8, look for how the ending resolves or withholds resolution, use of
dramatic irony, pace and silence, and how earlier scenes have built expectation. Context is not
assessed here; brief references that support interpretation are acceptable.

**9. Indicative content:**

- **(a)** Birling's speeches rejecting shared responsibility contrast with the Inspector's insistence
  that people are connected. The structure of one-by-one questioning reveals each character's part in
  Eva Smith's story. Sheila and Eric accept responsibility while their parents resist, dividing the
  generations. Dramatic irony undermines Birling's confident predictions. The Inspector's final warning
  and the closing telephone call leave the audience with the question of responsibility.
- **(b)** Alfieri's direct address frames the story as a tragedy whose ending he cannot prevent.
  Eddie's feelings for Catherine and his hostility to Rodolpho drive the action; his call to the
  Immigration Bureau breaks the community's code of loyalty. Tension builds through staged
  confrontations such as the chair-lifting at the end of Act One, and Eddie's death in the final
  confrontation with Marco completes the tragic pattern. Alfieri's closing words invite mixed sympathy
  and judgement.
- **(c)** Eva, the child sent from Germany to England, and Evelyn, the adult she becomes, are on stage
  together, so the audience sees the cost of her rejecting her past. Her relationships with her mother
  Helga, her foster mother Lil and her daughter Faith, whose discoveries in the attic reopen the past,
  show identity as divided by trauma. The recurring Ratcatcher figure embodies fear.
- **(d)** Ed's care for Christopher contrasts with his lies about Christopher's mother and his killing of
  Wellington. Christopher's discovery breaks his trust and drives his journey to London. Narration
  drawn from Christopher's writing, and physical staging, show the relationship from his perspective;
  the ending suggests trust rebuilt slowly.
- **(e)** Elesin's poetic, celebratory language early in the play shows his status and appetites; his
  delay, including his marriage to a young bride, weakens his resolve. Pilkings's intervention prevents
  the ritual, and Olunde's return and death in his father's place shame Elesin. Iyaloja's judgement and
  Elesin's death at the end complete the failure. Credit answers that consider Soyinka's note warning
  against a simple "clash of cultures" reading.
