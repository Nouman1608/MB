## Key definitions (6.1.1–6.1.3)

- **Photosynthesis:** the process by which plants make carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light.
- **Chlorophyll:** a green pigment found in chloroplasts. It transfers light energy into chemical energy for the formation of glucose and other carbohydrates.
- **Limiting factor:** the factor in shortest supply that keeps the rate of photosynthesis down. Only increasing this factor raises the rate.
- **Control:** a set-up identical to the test except for the one factor being investigated.
- **Destarching:** keeping a plant in the dark so its leaves use up stored starch before an experiment.

## Equations (6.1.5)

| Type | Equation |
|---|---|
| Word | carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen |
| Balanced | 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ |
| Conditions (by the arrow) | light energy, chlorophyll |

Atom check: C 6 = 6; H 12 = 12; O 18 = 18.

## Showing what photosynthesis needs (6.1.6)

**Method in steps: testing a leaf for starch**

1. Boiling water, about 1 minute: kills cells, breaks membranes.
2. Hot ethanol in a water bath (no naked flame, ethanol is flammable): removes chlorophyll.
3. Warm water: softens the brittle leaf.
4. Iodine solution on a white tile: blue-black = starch; orange-brown = no starch.

**The three experiments at a glance**

| Testing for | Treatment | Control | Starch result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll | Variegated leaf in light | Green areas of the same leaf | Green areas only |
| Light | Foil strip over part of a leaf | Uncovered part | Uncovered part only |
| Carbon dioxide | Soda lime or sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide around the leaf | Same set-up with water instead | Control leaf only |

Every one starts with a **destarched** plant and ends with the **starch test**.

## Rate of photosynthesis (6.1.7–6.1.9)

**Effect of each factor**

| Factor | Shape of graph | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Rises, then levels off | More light energy transferred by chlorophyll; plateau when another factor limits |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | Rises, then levels off | More raw material; plateau when another factor limits |
| Temperature | Rises to an optimum, then falls sharply | Up to the optimum: more kinetic energy, more effective collisions between enzymes and substrates. Above it: enzymes denature, active site changes shape, substrate no longer fits |

The enzyme model is the same one used in the [Enzymes revision notes](/resources/enzymes-revision-notes/).

**Method in steps: naming the limiting factor from a graph**

1. Find the part of the curve you are asked about.
2. Rising? The factor on the x-axis is limiting.
3. Flat? The x-axis factor is not limiting; name another factor from the conditions given.
4. Two curves? The factor that differs between them was limiting the lower curve's plateau.

**Typical conditions**

| Situation | Usually limiting |
|---|---|
| Early morning, late evening, deep shade | Light intensity |
| Cold but bright winter day | Temperature |
| Bright, warm day | Carbon dioxide concentration |
| Sealed greenhouse in sunshine | Carbon dioxide concentration |

## Investigating the rate (6.1.8)

**Pondweed (submerged aquatic plant)**

- Measure: bubbles counted in a set time, or volume of gas collected (more accurate, as bubbles vary in size).
- Rate = amount ÷ time, for example cm³ per minute or bubbles per minute.
- Vary light by lamp distance, carbon dioxide by concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, temperature by water bath.
- Control variables: same plant piece, same solution volume, same temperature (use a heat shield of water), acclimatisation time at each condition, repeats with a mean.

**Hydrogencarbonate indicator**

| Indicator colour | Carbon dioxide compared with air | Meaning with a plant |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow | Higher | Respiration greater than photosynthesis (dark) |
| Red | Same | Photosynthesis and respiration balance |
| Magenta to purple | Lower | Photosynthesis greater than respiration (bright light) |

Always include a tube with no plant as a control.

**Worked reminder: turning gas volumes into rates**

A syringe collects 2.4 cm³ of gas from pondweed in 8 minutes at one temperature, and 1.2 cm³ in 8 minutes at a lower temperature.

- Rate at the higher temperature = 2.4 ÷ 8 = **0.3 cm³ per minute**.
- Rate at the lower temperature = 1.2 ÷ 8 = **0.15 cm³ per minute**.
- Units matter: give cm³ per minute (or cm³ min⁻¹), not just cm³.

**Worked reminder: explaining an indicator result**

Three sealed tubes of hydrogencarbonate indicator each contain pondweed. After two hours tube P (dark) is yellow, tube Q (near a lamp) is purple, and tube R (no plant, near the lamp) is still red.

- P: no light, so no photosynthesis; respiration releases carbon dioxide, so its concentration rises above air.
- Q: photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide faster than respiration releases it, so its concentration falls below air.
- R: stays red, showing that the lamp alone does not change the indicator and the plant caused the changes in P and Q.

A full answer names the process **and** the change in carbon dioxide concentration for each tube.

## Leaf structure (6.2.1–6.2.2)

**Large surface area:** absorbs more light; more area for carbon dioxide to enter. **Thin:** short diffusion distance for gases; light reaches all mesophyll cells.

| Structure (top to bottom) | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Cuticle | Waxy, transparent: cuts water loss, lets light through |
| Upper epidermis | Transparent, no chloroplasts: light passes to mesophyll |
| Palisade mesophyll | Tightly packed, most chloroplasts, near the light |
| Spongy mesophyll | Loosely packed, fewer chloroplasts, large surface for gas exchange |
| Air spaces | Carbon dioxide diffuses in to cells, oxygen out |
| Vascular bundle | Xylem supplies water and mineral ions; phloem removes sucrose and amino acids |
| Lower epidermis | Most stomata found here |
| Stomata and guard cells | Guard cells open and close the pore for gas exchange |

**Distribution of chloroplasts:** most in palisade, fewer in spongy, none in epidermal cells except guard cells.

## Mineral nutrition (6.3.1–6.3.2)

|---|---|---|

| Magnesium | Making chlorophyll | Less chlorophyll means less light absorbed, less photosynthesis |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Raw materials vs conditions.** Carbon dioxide and water are raw materials. Light and chlorophyll are conditions.
- **Starch vs sucrose.** Starch is stored; sucrose is transported.
- **Glucose vs starch in tests.** You test leaves for starch, not glucose, because glucose is quickly converted.
- **Rate levels off vs rate falls.** Levelling off means another factor is limiting. A fall with temperature means enzymes are denaturing.
- **Palisade vs spongy.** Palisade: photosynthesis (most chloroplasts). Spongy: gas exchange (air spaces).
- **Nitrate vs magnesium.** Nitrate: amino acids and proteins. Magnesium: chlorophyll.

### Answers

1. 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light and chlorophyll by the arrow.
2. It transfers light energy into chemical energy, used to form glucose and other carbohydrates.
3. Cellulose.
4. To destarch it, so any starch found later was made during the experiment.
5. To remove the chlorophyll, so the iodine colour change can be seen.
6. Yellow: only respiration happens, so carbon dioxide concentration rises.
7. 54 ÷ 3 = **18 bubbles per minute**.
8. Temperature: enzymes work slowly in the cold.
9. Short diffusion distance for carbon dioxide to reach mesophyll cells; light can reach all the photosynthesising cells.
10. Palisade mesophyll.
11. Nitrate supplies nitrogen to make amino acids; without amino acids it cannot make proteins needed for growth.
12. Magnesium.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "energy is made" or "chlorophyll produces energy". Energy is transferred.
- Writing glucose as C₆H₁₂O₆ but forgetting to balance with 6 on carbon dioxide, water and oxygen.
- Describing the starch test without saying **why** each step is done; "explain" questions need the reason.
- Forgetting the safety point about ethanol and flames.
- Naming the factor on the x-axis as limiting when the curve is flat.
- Describing a hydrogencarbonate colour without linking it to carbon dioxide concentration.
- Saying palisade cells have chloroplasts "for photosynthesis" without their position near the upper surface, where light is strongest.
- Stating that stomata "let air in": name the gases (carbon dioxide in, oxygen out).
- Writing that magnesium "makes the leaf green" without naming chlorophyll.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between raw materials and conditions for photosynthesis?**
Carbon dioxide and water are the raw materials. Light and chlorophyll are conditions, which is why they are written by the arrow in the equation.

**Why do you test a leaf for starch rather than glucose?**
Glucose made in photosynthesis is quickly converted to other substances, so leaves are tested for starch, which is stored.

**Why do you put a leaf in boiling water and then hot ethanol in the starch test?**
Boiling water for about a minute kills the cells and breaks the membranes. Hot ethanol in a water bath removes the chlorophyll, and no naked flame is used because ethanol is flammable.

**What does it mean when a rate graph levels off?**
A level part of the curve means another factor is limiting the rate. A fall in rate with rising temperature, by contrast, means enzymes are being denatured.
