## Questions

**1.** Define derived demand and give one example from the market for labour. **[2]**

**2.** An orchard sells fruit at 20 per crate. Total daily output with 1 to 5 pickers is 9, 17, 23, 27 and 29 crates.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal physical product of each picker. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the marginal revenue product of each picker. **[2]**
**(c)** The daily wage is 100. State how many pickers the orchard hires and justify your answer. **[3]**
**(d)** The wage falls to 70. State the new number hired. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the demand curve for labour slopes downward. **[3]**

**4.** A furniture maker has a worker whose marginal physical product is 8 chairs a week. The price is 90, but the marginal revenue from the extra chairs is 70.

**(a)** Calculate the worker's marginal revenue product. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why marginal revenue is below price for this firm. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how much the marginal revenue product would be overstated if price were used. **[1]**

**5.** Explain two factors that could cause a rightward shift in the demand for delivery riders in a city. **[4]**

**6.** A warehouse raises its hourly wage from 15.00 to 16.50. Employment falls from 600 to 570.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for labour. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the wage bill before and after the change, and state the percentage change. **[2]**

**7.** In firm X labour is 80% of total costs. In firm Y it is 8%. Each firm faces a 10% rise in the wage rate, with other costs unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in total costs for each firm. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain which firm is likely to have the less elastic demand for labour. **[2]**

**8.** A call centre can now replace many staff with an automated system. Evaluate whether this makes its demand for labour more elastic. **[9]**

**9.** Explain one monetary and one non-monetary consideration that influences the supply of labour to night-shift security work. **[4]**

**10.** A wage rise of 10% (18.00 to 19.80) is offered in two invented labour markets. In data entry, willing workers rise from 2,000 to 2,300. For specialist surveyors, they rise from 400 to 408.

**(a)** Calculate the elasticity of supply of labour in each market. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between the two results. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse the effect on the supply of labour in one occupation of (i) a pay rise in a rival occupation and (ii) a new training scheme that doubles the number of qualified people. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Derived demand is demand for a factor of production that exists only because customers want the final output [1]. For example, the demand for crop pickers is derived from the demand for fruit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good definition says where the demand comes from. Name the final product in your example as well as the worker.

**2. (a)** MPP is the difference between successive outputs [1]: 9, 8, 6, 4 and 2 crates [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MRP = MPP × 20 [1]: 180, 160, 120, 80 and 40 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The orchard hires **3 pickers** [1]. The third picker has MRP of 120, above the wage of 100 [1]. The fourth has MRP of 80, below the wage, so hiring that picker would reduce profit [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** At a wage of 70 the fourth picker (MRP 80) is worth hiring and the fifth (40) is not, so **4 pickers** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A hiring answer is strongest when it compares the last worker hired and the first one rejected with the wage, rather than just quoting a number.

**3.** Marginal physical product eventually falls as more workers are added, because of diminishing marginal returns [1]. So marginal revenue product falls with each extra worker [1]. A firm will only employ workers with lower MRP if the wage is lower, so the curve slopes down [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for a connected chain, from productivity to MRP to the wage. A bare statement that firms hire more at lower wages explains little.

**4. (a)** MRP = MPP × MR [1] = 8 × 70 = **560** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The firm has some control over price, so its demand curve slopes down [1]. To sell extra chairs it must lower the price on all of them, so the extra revenue is less than the price [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Using price gives 8 × 90 = 720, an overstatement of **160** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the right formula and show the substitution. In a context question, the reason MR falls should refer to the firm's own demand curve.

**5.** Higher demand for deliveries, for example more online shopping, raises the revenue from each rider [1], so MRP rises at each wage and demand shifts right [1]. A rise in rider productivity, for example better routing software, raises output per rider [1], so MPP and MRP rise and more riders are demanded at each wage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A factor needs a link to MRP to earn the explanation point. Naming a change in the wage is a different thing, because that causes a movement along the curve.

**6. (a)** Wage change = 1.50 ÷ 15.00 × 100 = 10% [1]. Employment change = −30 ÷ 600 × 100 = −5% [1]. PEDL = −5 ÷ 10 = **−0.5** (inelastic) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Before: 15.00 × 600 = 9,000. After: 16.50 × 570 = 9,405 [1]. The wage bill rises by 405, which is **4.5%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both percentage changes before dividing. A reader can only judge the method from the working, and the sign should be explained.

**7. (a)** Firm X: 80% × 10% = **8%** [1]. Firm Y: 8% × 10% = **0.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Firm Y is likely to have the less elastic demand [1]. The wage rise barely affects its total costs, so it has little reason to raise prices or cut output and hiring, whereas firm X faces a large cost increase [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion about elasticity should be tied to the calculation. Add "other things equal", because substitution and the product's elasticity also matter.

**8.** *Analysis (up to 5 points):* Automation is a closer substitute for workers [1]. If wages rise, the call centre can switch to the automated system [1], so the substitution effect is larger and employment falls by a bigger percentage than the wage rise [1]. Elasticity therefore rises [1]. The effect also works through the output effect: a higher wage lifts costs and prices, and if customers can switch provider, sales and labour demand fall [1].
*Evaluation (up to 4 points):* The effect depends on how much of the work the system can do. Complex calls may still need staff, so demand stays inelastic for them [1]. The share of labour in costs matters: if labour is a small share, the response will be small [1]. Time matters, because installing a system takes time, so it is more elastic in the long run than the short run [1]. Judgement: demand is likely to become more elastic over time, but by how much depends on the share of calls that can be automated [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation answers tend to gain credit when the judgement is justified by named conditions such as time, cost share or task complexity. Repeating the analysis does not count as evaluation.

**9.** A monetary consideration is the wage, including a night-shift premium [1]. A higher payment compensates workers for disruption, so more are willing to work at that wage [1]. A non-monetary consideration is unsocial hours and personal safety [1]. If hours are disruptive or the work is risky, fewer people will accept the job at a given wage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each consideration needs a reason why it changes the number willing to work. A list of words gains little.

**10. (a)** Data entry: (2,300 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 15%, and 15 ÷ 10 = **1.5** [2]. Surveyors: (408 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = 2%, and 2 ÷ 10 = **0.2** [2]. **[4]**
**(b)** Data entry is elastic and surveyor supply is inelastic [1]. Surveying needs long training and qualifications, so few extra people can enter quickly [1]. Data entry needs few specific skills, so workers can move in from other jobs easily [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two markets directly, with the figures, and give reasons rooted in training, skills and mobility.

**11.** (i) A pay rise in a rival occupation makes it more attractive [1], so some workers move there and fewer are willing to work in this occupation at each wage [1]. The supply curve shifts left [1]. (ii) A training scheme that doubles qualified people raises the number able to enter [1], so more are willing to work at each wage [1]. The supply curve shifts right [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe each shift in words, naming what the two axes show and which way the curve moves.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "movement" for a shift in the curve, or the reverse.
- Quoting a hiring number without comparing MRP with the wage.
- Using price where the firm needs marginal revenue.
- Taking a percentage change from the wrong base.
- Leaving out the sign or the meaning of an elasticity result.
- Listing determinants without a chain of reasoning.
- Treating supply of labour as about pay only.
- Evaluating with a single caveat and no overall judgement.
