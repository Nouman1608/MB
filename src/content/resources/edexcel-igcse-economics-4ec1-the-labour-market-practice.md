These questions cover section 1.2.5, The labour market, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Read the [labour market study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-labour-market/) and the [labour market revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-labour-market-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by derived demand for labour? **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following would increase the supply of labour?

A: raising the school-leaving age

B: lowering the retirement age

C: an increase in female participation in the workforce

D: emigration of young workers

**[1]**

**3.** State **two** factors that affect the demand for labour. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by human capital? **[2]**

**5.** State **one** way a trade union may try to improve working conditions. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** The table shows the invented labour market for software testers in Country J (wage per hour).

| Wage | Testers demanded | Testers supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | 5 200 | 3 600 |
| 16 | 4 800 | 4 000 |
| 18 | 4 400 | 4 400 |
| 20 | 4 000 | 4 800 |
| 22 | 3 600 | 5 200 |

**(a)** Identify the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the shortage of testers at a wage of 14. **[1]**

**(c)** A union negotiates a wage of 22. Calculate the change in employment compared with the equilibrium, and the percentage change. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** benefit and **one** cost of the union's action for testers. **[4]**

**7.** Draw a labour market diagram showing the effect on the wage and employment of bus drivers when many drivers retire at the same time. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the introduction of self-driving lorries could affect the demand for lorry drivers. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why a rise in the productivity of workers may increase the demand for labour. **[3]**

**10.** A city has many vacancies for builders, while a town 300 km away has high unemployment among builders. Explain **one** reason why the vacancies may remain unfilled. **[3]**

**11.** A clothing firm pays machinists 12 an hour. Before training, each machinist sews 15 garments an hour; after training, 20.

**(a)** Calculate the labour cost per garment before and after training. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the quality of labour matters to the firm. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that trade unions are always good for workers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The demand for workers depends on [1]; the demand for the final product they produce [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A, B and D all reduce the number of people available for work.

**3.** Any two: demand for the final product [1]; availability of substitutes, including machines [1]; (also accept productivity of the workforce). **[2]**

**4.** The knowledge, skills and experience [1]; of workers, which affect their productivity [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Negotiating for safer equipment or working practices (accept: limits on working hours, paid holidays, protection from unfair treatment) [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Wage **18** [1]; employment **4 400** [1].

**(b)** 5 200 − 3 600 = **1 600** [1].

**(c)** 3 600 − 4 400 = **−800** testers [1]; −800 ÷ 4 400 × 100 = **−18.2%** [1].

**(d)** Benefit: testers who keep their jobs earn 4 more an hour [1]; raising their income and living standards [1]. Cost: firms employ 800 fewer testers [1]; so some testers lose their jobs or cannot find work [1]. **[9]**

**7.** Axes labelled wage rate (vertical) and quantity of labour or employment (horizontal), with D and S [1]; supply curve shifted left to S1 [1]; new equilibrium showing a higher wage and lower employment, labelled W1 and Q1 [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Self-driving lorries are a substitute for drivers [1]; firms can replace drivers with machines [1]; so the demand for lorry drivers shifts left, lowering their wage and employment [1]. **[3]**

**9.** More productive workers produce more output per hour [1]; so each worker adds more to the firm's revenue for the same wage [1]; so firms are willing to employ more workers at each wage and demand shifts right [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Unemployed builders may lack geographical mobility [1]; for example, housing in the city may be too expensive, or they have family ties in the town [1]; so they cannot move to where the jobs are, and the vacancies remain [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Before: 12 ÷ 15 = **0.80** [1]; after: 12 ÷ 20 = **0.60** [1].

**(b)** Higher-quality labour produces more garments per hour [1]; lowering the labour cost of each garment [1]; so the firm can cut prices or raise profit, and may produce better-quality garments with fewer faults [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For: unions bargain collectively, so workers gain more power than negotiating alone [1]; they can win higher wages [1]; and better working conditions such as safer equipment [1]; giving workers a voice in decisions [1]. Against: wages above equilibrium may reduce the number of jobs [1]; strikes mean workers lose pay [1]; higher costs may lead firms to replace workers with machines [1]. Judgement: unions are good for workers who keep their jobs, but "always" is too strong; the effect depends on whether productivity rises with pay [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The word "always" invites a judgement that names when the claim fails.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting demand when supply has changed, or the reverse.
- Unlabelled axes and curves on the diagram.
- Calculating a percentage change from the new value instead of the equilibrium value.
- Union evaluations without a judgement.
- Mobility answers that do not say which kind of mobility is missing.
