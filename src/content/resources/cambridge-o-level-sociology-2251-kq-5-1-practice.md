## Set A — Education and schooling

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *formal education* **[2]**
- (ii) *official curriculum* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** ways the hidden curriculum may teach conformity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways the hidden curriculum may teach gender roles. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** strengths of single-sex schools. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the effectiveness of faith schools. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that alternative approaches to education are more effective than
traditional schooling. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** limitations of unschooling. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** strengths of progressive schooling. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** limitations of international schools. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** strengths of co-educational schools. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways testing in the official curriculum may affect pupils. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss the effectiveness of state schools. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Planned learning that takes place in institutions such as schools and colleges (1),
with a set curriculum, trained teachers and formal assessment (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** What schools openly and deliberately teach (1): the subjects, syllabus content and the
testing used to assess pupils (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: wearing uniform; following school rules; lining up or queuing;
standing when a teacher enters; attending assemblies; asking permission to speak or leave the room.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Subject and career guidance** may steer girls towards caring or arts subjects and boys towards
  science and technology.
- **Sports** may be separated by gender, with different sports seen as suitable for boys and girls.
- **Teacher expectations**: girls praised for neatness and quietness, boys tolerated for being
  boisterous.
- **Tasks and roles**: girls asked to tidy or help, boys to carry equipment; leadership roles given
  unevenly.
- **Staffing**: senior posts held mainly by men can suggest that authority is male.
- **Textbooks and examples** that show men and women in traditional roles.

**(d) [6]** Three strengths, 2 marks each:
- Pupils may choose subjects with less gender stereotyping, for example girls taking physics.
- Fewer distractions in class, which some parents and schools believe helps concentration.
- Girls may gain confidence and leadership roles without boys dominating attention.
- Teaching can be adapted to pupils' needs, for example in boys' schools to address lower motivation.
- In some societies, single-sex schools allow girls to attend school where mixed schooling would not
  be accepted, increasing access.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- *Effective:* shared values and strong discipline; a supportive community of families; often
  popular with parents and may achieve good results; teach religious and moral education that parents
  want.
- *Less effective:* may separate pupils by religion and reduce social mixing; selective admissions
  may give them more advantaged pupils, so results may reflect intake; may teach a narrower view of
  values; may disadvantage pupils who do not share the faith.
- *Evaluation:* effectiveness depends on the measure used: results, values or social cohesion.
  Functionalists may value their role in transmitting shared values; critics question whether they
  promote consensus across a whole society.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For alternative approaches:*
- Online learning gives flexible access to specialist courses and teachers anywhere.
- Homeschooling allows a tailored pace and protects some children from bullying.
- Unschooling and progressive schooling build curiosity and independence (Holt; Neill's Summerhill).
- Vocational learning gives practical skills and links to employment.
- Illich (1971) argued traditional schooling teaches dependence on institutions.

*For traditional schooling:*
- Recognised qualifications and a structured official curriculum.
- Social contact and secondary socialisation with peers from different backgrounds.
- Trained teachers and specialist facilities such as laboratories.
- Alternatives depend on parents' income, time and digital access, so they may widen inequality.
- Functionalists stress schools' role in value consensus and role allocation.

*Conclusion:* alternatives can be highly effective for some children, but traditional schooling
remains more effective for most at providing qualifications and equal access. Credit a reasoned
judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: gaps in core knowledge such as mathematics; harder to gain
recognised qualifications; depends heavily on parents' time and resources; fewer chances to learn to
work in groups; may make later entry to formal education difficult.

**(h) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each: pupils are motivated because they follow their interests;
they learn democratic values by having a say in school decisions; less pressure from testing may
reduce anxiety; encourages creativity and independent learning.

**(i) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: high fees limit access to wealthy or expatriate families;
pupils may become distant from local language and culture; staff and pupils may move often, reducing
continuity; curricula may not fit local university or employment routes.

**(j) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each: reflects the mixed nature of wider society and work; pupils
learn to cooperate and communicate across genders; may reduce stereotypes through everyday contact;
wider choice of local schools for families.

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: examination pressure may cause stress and anxiety; teaching may
narrow to what is tested; results are used to set or stream pupils, which can lead to labelling;
success brings qualifications that open further education and jobs.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Effective:* free or low-cost access for all; a national curriculum and standards; mixed intakes
  encourage social mixing and equal opportunity; trained teachers.
- *Less effective:* large classes and limited resources in many countries; quality varies between
  areas, so richer neighbourhoods may have better state schools; wealthier families may leave for
  private schools.
- *Evaluation:* effectiveness depends on government funding and on the measure used; Marxists argue
  state schools still reproduce class inequality through the hidden curriculum.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know examples of real schools?** Not necessarily, but one accurate example, such as
Summerhill for progressive schooling, strengthens an answer.

**How do I judge "effectiveness"?** State the measure you are using: results, equality, social mixing
or values. Different measures can give different answers.

**Can I write about schools in my own country?** Yes. Applying the school types to a society you know
is good application.
