Two elements in the 9675 Unit 3 content belong together: "punishment, justice, retribution, the
legal system" and "the sense that there will be a resolution and the criminal will be punished".
This guide develops them for the Unit 3 essay. For the unit overview, set texts and exam format,
use the [Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-3-elements-of-crime-and-mystery-study-guide/);
this page focuses on what happens after the crime is known, and on who, if anyone, delivers
justice.

## Four kinds of justice

Crime texts distribute punishment in different ways. Naming the kind helps you argue rather than
summarise.

| Kind | Who punishes | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal justice** | Courts, police, magistrates, the state | Society's order is restored, or exposed as flawed |
| **Divine or supernatural justice** | God, spirits, fate, nature | Moral law exists beyond human institutions |
| **Private justice or retribution** | An avenger, detective or victim | The law is absent, too slow or unfit |
| **Internal punishment** | The criminal's own conscience | Guilt is the real sentence |

The terms overlap. Retribution means punishment as repayment for a wrong; it can be legal, divine or
private. Justice is broader, including fairness to victims and the innocent as well as punishment of
the guilty.

## Key questions

- Is the criminal punished, and by whom?
- Does the legal system appear, and is it just, corrupt, comic or powerless?
- Is the innocent wrongly accused or punished?
- Does punishment restore order, or does disorder remain?
- Is the reader satisfied, unsettled or made to question the idea of punishment?

## The set texts

**Macbeth.** There is no court in the play. The opening reports the execution of the traitor Cawdor,
whose dignified death sets a standard; Macbeth himself fears an "even-handed justice" that will turn
his crime back on him, and the play bears this out. Retribution comes through Macduff, a private
avenger whose cause is also Scotland's, and through the army that restores Malcolm. The display of
Macbeth's head is punishment as public spectacle.

**The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.** Punishment is supernatural and disproportionate: spirits pursue
the ship, the crew die and the Mariner is left in a living death. His partial release follows a moment
of spontaneous love for living creatures, and he is shriven by the Hermit, but his penance never
ends, since he must keep telling his tale. Justice here is moral and religious, not legal.

**Browning, Selected Poems.** The monologues often show criminals who escape human law. The speaker
of "Porphyria's Lover" notes that God has said nothing, which leaves judgement to the reader. "The
Laboratory" is private revenge by poison. "The Patriot", by contrast, is spoken by a man going to
execution by the people who once cheered him, who looks to God's judgement over theirs. In "The
Confessional" a woman's private words to a priest are used against her lover, who is executed, so
the institutions of church and state become the betrayers.

**Oliver Twist.** The legal system is a target of Dickens's satire. The magistrate Mr Fang judges
Oliver without listening; Mr Bumble, told that the law assumes a wife acts under her husband's
direction, declares the law "a ass". Yet the novel also uses the law to end the criminal world:
Fagin is tried and condemned and spends his last night in the condemned cell, the Artful Dodger is
transported, and Sikes dies in flight, hanged by his own rope as a crowd closes in. Legal justice and
something like providence work together.

**Poe, Collected Stories.** The narrators of "The Tell-Tale Heart" and "The Black Cat" are caught
by their own compulsion: one shrieks a confession to the police, the other betrays the hiding place
and awaits execution. "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" ends with no one to punish, since the killer is
an animal, and an innocent man is released. In "The Pit and the Pendulum" the legal system is the
Inquisition, an unjust court whose punishments are torture, and rescue comes from outside.

**Stevenson, Collected Stories.** Utterson is a lawyer, but the law never reaches Hyde, who dies
before he can be taken. In "Markheim" the murderer chooses to give himself up, so justice begins in
his conscience. In "The Body Snatcher" the anatomists escape the law, and punishment arrives as a
supernatural horror.

**The Murder of Roger Ackroyd.** Poirot solves the crime but does not hand the murderer to the
police at once; he offers him the chance to spare his sister the scandal by ending his own life. The
novel ends with private justice arranged by the detective, outside the courts.

**Death of a Red Heroine.** Chief Inspector Chen's investigation tests whether the legal system can
reach a suspect with political protection. Justice depends on navigating the Party as much as on
evidence, so the novel uses crime to comment on power in a particular historical period.

**When Will There Be Good News?** Its investigators include a former police officer working
outside the force and a serving detective, so the novel explores the limits of official justice and
the long aftermath that punishment cannot repair.

## Building an essay

1. **Name the kind of justice** the text relies on, and whether it shifts.
2. **Examine the ending.** Is resolution complete, partial or ironic?
3. **Look at the innocent.** Wrongful accusation reveals what a text thinks of the law.
4. **Weigh satisfaction against unease.** The specification names "relief" as a reader response;
   ask whether the text grants it.
5. **Use context precisely.** Victorian prisons and transportation, Jacobean ideas of kingship and
   divine order, or China after the Cultural Revolution, each tied to a moment.

## Worked paragraph

This model uses *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*, a set text, with references suited to a closed-book
answer.

> Christie resolves the puzzle completely but leaves justice deliberately incomplete. Poirot's
> unmasking of the murderer in the final chapters gives the reader the relief of knowing, yet he
> stops short of the legal process the genre usually promises: he lets the murderer choose a private
> death so that his sister will be spared a public trial. The detective therefore becomes judge as
> well as investigator, and the novel's order is restored by discretion rather than by the courts.
> Because the murderer has narrated the story, his final pages read as a confession written under
> sentence, and the reader's satisfaction at the solution is shadowed by discomfort at having trusted
> him. The ending suggests that in the closed world of the village, reputation matters as much as
> law.

## Common mistakes

- **Equating justice with arrest.** Many texts end without a court; analyse what replaces it.
- **Ignoring satire of the law.** Dickens attacks the system he also relies on.
- **Forgetting the innocent.** Wrongful accusations are evidence about justice.
- **Treating endings as neutral.** Ask what feeling the ending leaves.

## Exam technique

Unit 3 is closed book: two essays on two set texts, 25 marks each, in 2 hours. For this element,
prepare for each text: the moment the crime is exposed, the moment of punishment (or its absence),
and one scene involving an institution of law or religion. A view-based question, for example that
the criminal is always punished, should be tested against all three.
