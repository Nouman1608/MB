## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.6.1 a | Represent reactions by word equations and balanced symbol equations |
| 3.6.1 b | Use the state symbols (g), (l), (s) and (aq) |
| 3.6.1 c | No atoms are lost or made, so the mass of products equals the mass of reactants |
| 3.6.1 d | Calculate the mass of a reactant or product from the masses of the others and from balanced equations |
| 3.6.1 e | Explain why the calculated amount of product is not always obtained |

## Writing and balancing equations (3.6.1 a)

A word equation names the substances. A symbol equation uses formulae, and is **balanced** when every element has the same number of atoms on each side.

**Rules for balancing**

1. Write correct formulae first. Never change a formula to make it balance.
2. Balance by putting numbers **in front of** formulae.
3. Balance elements that appear in only one substance on each side first; leave elements on their own (such as O₂ or H₂) until last.
4. Recount every element at the end.

### Worked example 1: burning propane

propane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

- Carbon: 3 on the left, so 3CO₂.
- Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so 4H₂O.
- Oxygen on the right: 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 atoms, so 5O₂.

**C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O**

Check: C 3 = 3; H 8 = 8; O 10 = 10.

### Worked example 2: sodium and water

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

With one of each, Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂ has 2 hydrogen atoms on the left but 3 on the right (1 in NaOH, 2 in H₂). Doubling sodium, water and sodium hydroxide gives 4 hydrogen atoms on each side, and sodium and oxygen balance too.

## State symbols (3.6.1 b)

| Symbol | Meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| (s) | solid | metals, precipitates, insoluble oxides |
| (l) | liquid | water, molten substances |
| (g) | gas | oxygen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen |
| (aq) | aqueous: dissolved in water | acids, alkalis, soluble salts in solution |

Two common slips: water made in a reaction is H₂O(l), not (aq); and a precipitate is (s) even though it forms in a solution.

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)

## Conservation of mass (3.6.1 c)

Atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. So in a **closed system**, where nothing can get in or out, the total mass does not change.

When measured mass **does** change, a gas has crossed the boundary:

| Situation | Measured mass | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Two solutions form a precipitate in a beaker | Stays the same | Nothing enters or leaves |
| A carbonate reacts with acid in an open flask | Falls | Carbon dioxide escapes into the air |
| A metal is heated in air in an open crucible | Rises | Oxygen from the air joins the metal |
| The carbonate and acid react in a sealed flask | Stays the same | The carbon dioxide is trapped |

### Worked example 3: using a mass change

Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with excess hydrochloric acid in an open flask on a balance. The total mass falls from 85.62 g to 84.74 g.

- Mass of carbon dioxide lost = 85.62 − 84.74 = **0.88 g**.
- The atoms in the carbon dioxide still exist; they are in the air instead of the flask.

## Calculating masses (3.6.1 d)

There are two types of question.

### Type 1: from the other masses

If you know all masses but one, use *total mass of reactants = total mass of products*.

**Worked example 4.** 12.35 g of copper(II) carbonate is heated until it has all decomposed. 7.95 g of copper(II) oxide remains. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide given off.

CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂

Mass of CO₂ = 12.35 − 7.95 = **4.40 g**.

### Type 2: from a balanced equation

Use the equation's numbers to link moles of one substance to another. Relative atomic masses are given in the question.

1. Moles of the known substance = mass ÷ Mᵣ.
2. Use the ratio from the balanced equation.
3. Mass of the unknown = moles × Mᵣ.

**Worked example 5: copper heated in air.** What mass of copper(II) oxide forms when 2.54 g of copper is heated in air until it has all reacted? (Aᵣ: Cu = 63.5, O = 16)

2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO

1. Moles of Cu = 2.54 ÷ 63.5 = 0.0400 mol.
2. Ratio Cu : CuO = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1, so 0.0400 mol CuO.
3. Mᵣ CuO = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5; mass = 0.0400 × 79.5 = **3.18 g**.

The crucible gains 3.18 − 2.54 = 0.64 g, which is the mass of oxygen that combined.

**Worked example 6: mass of a reactant needed.** What mass of iron(III) oxide is needed to make 5.6 tonnes of iron in a blast furnace? (Aᵣ: Fe = 56, O = 16)

Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

- Mᵣ Fe₂O₃ = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160. The equation shows 160 parts by mass of Fe₂O₃ give 2 × 56 = 112 parts of Fe.
- Scale factor = 5.6 ÷ 112 = 0.05.
- Mass of Fe₂O₃ = 160 × 0.05 = **8.0 tonnes**.

The mass-ratio method works in any unit (g, kg, tonnes) as long as you use the same unit throughout.

## Why you do not always get the calculated amount (3.6.1 e)

The specification gives three reasons. Learn them with an example of each:

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| The reaction may not go to completion because it is **reversible** | Ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen: some ammonia breaks back down |
| Some product is **lost when it is separated** from the reaction mixture | Crystals left on filter paper or in the evaporating basin |
| Some reactants **react in ways different from the expected reaction** | A fuel burning partly to carbon monoxide instead of carbon dioxide |

None of these breaks the law of conservation of mass: the "missing" atoms are still somewhere, just not in the product you collected.

## Common mistakes

- Changing subscripts (H₂O to H₂O₂) to balance an equation.
- Using the mole ratio from the subscripts instead of the numbers in front.
- Writing (aq) for water or for a precipitate.
- Saying mass "is lost" in a reaction. Mass is conserved; a gas escapes.
- Mixing grams and tonnes in one calculation.
- Rounding moles too early, which shifts the final answer.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Write a balanced symbol equation:** check every element, and add state symbols only if asked, or if the question says "include state symbols".
- **Calculate:** show the three steps (moles, ratio, mass) so method marks are available even if one number is wrong.
- **Explain why the mass changes:** name the gas and say whether it left or joined the system.
- **Suggest why the yield is lower:** use one of the three specification reasons, applied to the reaction in the question.
