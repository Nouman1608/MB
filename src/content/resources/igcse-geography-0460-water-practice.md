These questions cover Topic 3.6 Water in the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). The topic is examined in Paper 1 Geographical Themes. Questions 3, 4 and 7 practise the data and calculation skills tested in Paper 2, and question 9 practises enquiry planning linked to the water-supply enquiry the syllabus suggests for Component 3 Coursework. All places and data in the questions are fictional unless a question asks for your own case study.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "desalination". **[2]**

**2.** Methods of water supply.

**(a)** Describe two differences between a well and a borehole. **[2]**
**(b)** State two disadvantages of building a large dam and reservoir. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows the percentage of water withdrawn for each use in three fictional countries.

| Country | Agriculture (%) | Industry (%) | Domestic (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Altera (high income) | 31 | 52 | 17 |
| Bremia (middle income) | 64 | 21 | 15 |
| Corvane (low income) | 88 | 4 | 8 |

**(a)** Identify the country that uses the largest percentage of its water for industry. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference, in percentage points, between the industrial share in Altera and in Corvane. **[1]**
**(c)** Corvane withdraws 12 km³ of water a year. Calculate the volume used for agriculture. Show your working. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest three reasons why Corvane uses most of its water for agriculture. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest why the domestic share in Altera is higher than in Corvane. **[2]**

**4.** A water company records how full the fictional Teswick Reservoir is at the start of each month. It holds 30 million m³ when full. The percentages are: January 92, February 95, March 90, April 81, May 70, June 58, July 47, August 40, September 44, October 60, November 78, December 88.

**(a)** Identify the month when the reservoir was lowest. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the fall in stored volume, in million m³, between the start of March and the start of August. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why storage fell between March and August. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest two ways the company could reduce the risk of a shortage next summer. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why desalination is more widely used in wealthy, dry coastal countries than in low-income inland countries. **[4]**

**6.** Read the description of a fictional village.

> Kanoro is a village of 900 people in a semi-arid region. Its only shallow well dries up for four months each year. In those months, women and girls walk to a river and back; each round trip takes 75 minutes and each household makes two trips a day. The river is also used by cattle. A clinic 10 km away reports many cases of diarrhoea in young children during the dry season.

**(a)** Calculate the number of hours a household spends collecting water each week in the dry season. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the lack of clean water affects the people of Kanoro and the village's potential for economic development. **[6]**

**7.** The fictional country Dravia has 42 000 million m³ of renewable fresh water each year. Its population is 30 million and is forecast to reach 40 million. One widely used measure says a country under 1 700 m³ per person per year has water stress, and under 1 000 m³ per person has water scarcity.

**(a)** Calculate the water available per person per year now. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the water available per person per year when the population reaches 40 million. **[1]**
**(c)** Using the measure, state Dravia's category now and in the future. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest two reasons why water use per person in Dravia may rise as it develops. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why some areas with high annual rainfall still suffer water shortages. **[4]**

**9.** A student wants to investigate whether problems of an unreliable water supply are being solved in a fictional town, Pell Rock.

**(a)** Suggest three questions the student could include in a questionnaire for households. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how the student could choose a sample of households. **[2]**

**10.** For a named country or area you have studied, describe and explain how water is supplied to meet demand. **[8]**

**11.** "Large dams are the best way to supply water to rural communities in low-income countries." How far do you agree with this view? **[9]**

**12.** To what extent are water shortages caused by human factors rather than physical factors? Refer to examples. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Removing salt from seawater (or brackish water) [1] to make fresh water fit for drinking or other uses [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both parts are needed: the process (removing salt) and the purpose or result (fresh water).

**2. (a)** A well is usually dug by hand whereas a borehole is drilled by machine [1]; a well is shallow while a borehole is deeper, so it reaches more reliable groundwater [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Farmland, homes or villages are flooded and people are displaced [1]; very high construction cost [1]. Also accept: sediment trapped behind the dam, so farmland downstream loses fertile silt; high evaporation losses in hot climates; disrupted habitats and fish migration. Maximum two points. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe two differences" needs both sides of each comparison; a statement about wells alone does not score.

**3. (a)** Altera [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 52 − 4 = 48 percentage points [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 88% of 12 km³ = 0.88 × 12 [1] = 10.56 km³ [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Most people work in farming, so irrigation takes most water [1]; there are few factories, so industrial demand is small [1]; many homes lack piped water, so domestic use is low [1]. **[3]**
**(e)** More homes in Altera have piped water [1] and higher living standards mean showers, washing machines and gardens that use more water [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In calculations, show the working and give the unit; a bare figure loses the working mark if it is wrong.

**4. (a)** August [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 90 − 40 = 50 percentage points; 50% of 30 million m³ [1] = 15 million m³ [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Lower rainfall in the summer months, so less water flows in [1]; higher temperatures increase evaporation and demand for watering gardens and crops [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Introduce a hosepipe or sprinkler ban in summer [1]; repair leaking pipes so less treated water is lost [1]. Also accept: water meters so people pay for what they use; transfer from another reservoir; new boreholes. Maximum two points. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For "suggest" answers on a resource, tie each reason to the months shown, not to water problems in general.

**5.** Desalination uses large amounts of energy, so it is expensive [1]; wealthy countries can afford the plants and energy costs [1]; it needs seawater, so it suits coastal countries but not inland ones without long pipelines [1]; dry countries have few rivers or aquifers, so they have fewer cheaper alternatives [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names three conditions (wealthy, dry, coastal); a strong answer explains each one.

**6. (a)** 75 × 2 = 150 minutes a day = 2.5 hours; 2.5 × 7 = 17.5 hours a week [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** People: river water shared with cattle is likely to be contaminated [1], so children get diarrhoea, as the clinic reports [1]; women and girls spend 17.5 hours a week collecting water, so girls may miss school [1]. Development: time spent carrying water cannot be spent farming or earning income [1]; sick adults and carers lose working days and families pay for travel to a clinic 10 km away [1]; without a reliable supply, crops cannot be irrigated and businesses will not set up in the village [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the resource's details (cattle, 10 km, four dry months); answers that only list general impacts of dirty water score poorly.

**7. (a)** 42 000 million ÷ 30 million [1] = 1 400 m³ per person per year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 42 000 million ÷ 40 million = 1 050 m³ per person per year [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Now: water stress, because 1 400 is below 1 700 [1]. Future: still water stress but close to scarcity, because 1 050 is just above 1 000 [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Rising incomes bring more appliances, showers and flush toilets [1]; growth of industry and irrigated farming raises demand [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify using the numbers given in the question and state them; "it gets worse" without figures does not earn the mark.

**8.** Rainfall may be seasonal, so rivers dry up in the dry season [1]; there may be no money to build dams, pipes and treatment works, so water cannot be stored or supplied (economic scarcity) [1]; water may be polluted by sewage or industry, making it unsafe [1]; rapid population growth in cities can outstrip the supply system [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* High-rainfall questions reward economic scarcity; answers that only mention drought miss the point of the question.

**9. (a)** How many hours a day do you have running water? [1]; Has your supply become more reliable in the last five years? [1]; Which organisation, if any, has improved your supply? [1]. Also accept other closed or clear questions linked to the aim, such as where the household gets water when the tap fails. Maximum three questions. **[3]**
**(b)** Use systematic sampling, for example every tenth house along each street [1], covering different parts of the town so the sample is representative [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Questionnaire questions must link to the enquiry aim and be answerable; vague opinion questions rarely score.

**10.** Indicative answer using Singapore; the real exam marks this with levels of response, not point by point.
Singapore is a small, densely populated island with little land to store rain [1]. About two-thirds of the land is catchment feeding 17 reservoirs [1], including Marina Reservoir behind Marina Barrage, opened in 2008 [1]. It imports water from Malaysia's Johor River under a 1962 agreement lasting until 2061 [1], because local catchment cannot meet demand of about 430 million gallons a day [1]. Used water is purified by microfiltration, reverse osmosis and ultraviolet light into NEWater, launched in 2002 [1], which can meet up to 40% of current needs, mainly for industry [1]. Five desalination plants turn seawater into fresh water, so supply does not depend on rainfall [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Top-level answers combine named methods, place-specific figures and a reason for each method.

**11.** Indicative points; the real exam marks this with levels of response, not point by point.
For: a large dam stores wet-season water, so supply is reliable in the dry season [1]; it can also provide hydroelectric power and irrigation, which supports development [1]; Lesotho's Katse Dam earns royalties from water transferred to South Africa [1].
Against: large dams cost a great deal, which low-income governments struggle to fund [1]; they often supply cities and industry rather than scattered rural villages [1]; people lose land and homes, as in the Lesotho Highlands Water Project [1].
Alternatives: boreholes with hand pumps are cheap and close to where rural people live [1]; but they need maintenance and can lower the water table if over-used [1].
Judgement: for scattered rural communities, small-scale boreholes or rainwater tanks run by the community are usually more suitable than large dams, though dams may help regional supply [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "How far do you agree" needs both sides and a reasoned judgement; a one-sided list stays in the lower levels.

**12.** Indicative points; the real exam marks this with levels of response, not point by point.
Physical: low annual rainfall in deserts limits supply [1]; seasonal or unreliable rainfall means rivers and reservoirs dry out [1]; high temperatures increase evaporation [1].
Human: rapid population growth increases demand [1]; irrigation uses most water, especially in low-income countries [1]; over-abstraction lowers the water table so wells dry up [1]; pollution from sewage and industry makes water unusable [1]; lack of investment causes economic scarcity even where rain is plentiful [1].
Example: Singapore has heavy rainfall but too little land to store it, yet human solutions such as NEWater and desalination largely overcome this [1].
Judgement: physical factors set the limits, but human factors decide whether shortages actually happen, so human factors are usually more important [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Questions saying "to what extent" need a clear overall judgement supported by a named example.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving one side of a comparison, such as describing only wells when asked to compare wells and boreholes.
- Missing units or working in calculations such as volumes and water per person.
- Not quoting figures or months from a graph or table.
- Giving general impacts of dirty water without the scenario's details.
- Ignoring economic scarcity in questions about high-rainfall areas.
- Case study answers without a named place, dates or figures.
- Extended answers with no judgement at the end.
- Writing "manage water better" without naming a method.
