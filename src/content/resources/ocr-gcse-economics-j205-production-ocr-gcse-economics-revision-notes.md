These notes condense section 2.6, Production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The bike workshop workout and the productivity example are in the [2.6 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.6 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-production-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Production | Combining factors to make goods and services; also the total output |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input, such as output per worker or per hour |
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not change with output in the short run |
| Variable cost | A cost that rises as output rises |
| Total cost | Fixed cost + variable cost |
| Average cost | Total cost ÷ output |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity sold |
| Average revenue | Total revenue ÷ quantity sold (the price) |
| Profit | Total revenue − total cost, when revenue is greater |
| Loss | Total cost − total revenue, when cost is greater |
| Economies of scale | Falls in average cost from increasing the scale of production |

## Producers

- **Individuals:** self-employed people and sole traders.
- **Firms:** private businesses, usually aiming for profit.
- **Government:** public-sector services such as schools, hospitals and roads.

## Productivity

```
Labour productivity = output ÷ number of workers   (or ÷ hours worked)
Labour cost per unit = wage bill ÷ output
```

**Worked reminder.** A team of 5 makes 600 units a week (120 each). After training, 5 make 690 (138 each). Productivity rises by (138 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **15%**.

**Why it matters:** higher living standards, lower cost per unit, competitiveness, more tax revenue.
**Limits:** fewer jobs if machines replace workers; environmental cost of more output; gains may go mainly to owners.

## Cost and revenue: method

```
1. Variable cost = cost per unit × output
2. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost
3. Average cost = total cost ÷ output
4. Total revenue = price × quantity
5. Profit = total revenue − total cost (negative → state "loss")
Check: profit = (AR − AC) × output
```

**Worked reminder.** Fixed cost £900; variable cost £6 a unit; price £15; output 200.
TC = 900 + 1,200 = £2,100. AC = £10.50. TR = £3,000. Profit = **£900**. Check: (15 − 10.50) × 200 = £900.

## Cost, revenue, profit and supply

| Change | Effect on profit | Effect on supply |
|---|---|---|
| Costs rise | Falls at every price | Shifts left |
| Costs fall | Rises at every price | Shifts right |
| Price rises | Rises per unit | Movement along (extension); new firms may enter |
| Persistent losses | Firms leave | Market supply shifts left |

## Why profit and loss matter

- Profit rewards risk, funds investment and signals that a market is worth entering.
- Loss signals the need to cut costs, raise revenue or leave.
- Revenue is not profit: sales can rise while profit falls.

## Economies of scale

| Type | Why average cost falls |
|---|---|
| Technical | Larger, more efficient machines; specialised workers |
| Purchasing | Bulk-buying discounts |
| Financial | Cheaper borrowing for large firms |
| Managerial | Specialist managers raise efficiency |
| Marketing | Advertising cost spread over more sales |
| Risk-bearing | Wide product range reduces the impact of one failure |

**Distinction:** AC falling because the same fixed cost is spread over more output in one workshop is not an economy of scale; economies of scale come from growing the size of the business.

## Exam traps

- Profit = TR − AC (wrong): use total cost.
- Production and productivity used as if they mean the same.
- No £ sign, or a loss written as a positive "profit".
- Supply shifted for a price change.
- Naming a type of economy of scale without saying why cost per unit falls.

## Quick self-test

1. Define productivity.
2. Give one example each of a fixed cost and a variable cost for a café.
3. A firm has fixed cost £500, variable cost £4 a unit, and makes 250 units. Calculate total cost and average cost.
4. It sells the 250 units at £7. Calculate total revenue and profit.
5. Calculate average revenue in question 4.
6. A firm's output per worker rises from 40 to 46. Calculate the percentage change.
7. Explain how a rise in energy costs affects a market's supply curve.
8. Explain why higher productivity can lead to higher wages.
9. Name and explain one purchasing economy of scale.
10. A firm's revenue rises by 10% but its profit falls. Explain how this is possible.
11. Give one reason the government acts as a producer.
12. Give one cost to the economy of higher productivity.

### Answers

1. Output per unit of input, such as output per worker or per hour worked.
2. Fixed: rent or insurance. Variable: coffee beans, milk or cups.
3. TC = 500 + 4 × 250 = **£1,500**; AC = 1,500 ÷ 250 = **£6**.
4. TR = 7 × 250 = **£1,750**; profit = 1,750 − 1,500 = **£250**.
5. AR = 1,750 ÷ 250 = **£7**, the same as the price.
6. (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **15%**.
7. Higher costs reduce profit at every price, so firms supply less and the supply curve shifts left.
8. Each worker produces more value, so the firm can pay more without raising cost per unit.
9. Bulk buying: a large firm orders in large quantities and receives a lower price per unit, so average cost falls.
10. Costs rose faster than revenue, so the gap between them narrowed.
11. To provide services that people need, such as education or health care, that the market might not provide to everyone.
12. Job losses where machines replace workers, or more pollution and use of resources.
