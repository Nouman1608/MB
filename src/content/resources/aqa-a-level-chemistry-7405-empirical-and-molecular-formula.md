## The two definitions and how they relate

The **empirical formula** is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element. The **molecular formula** is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula:

```
molecular formula = (empirical formula) × n
n = Mᵣ ÷ empirical formula mass
```

Several compounds can share one empirical formula. CH₂O is the empirical formula of methanal (CH₂O, n = 1), ethanoic acid (C₂H₄O₂, n = 2) and glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆, n = 6). Ethyne (C₂H₂) and benzene (C₆H₆) are both CH. Only the Mᵣ tells them apart.

For ionic compounds and giant structures, which have no molecules, only an empirical formula is meaningful: the formula of sodium chloride is NaCl, an empirical formula.

## The method, with the judgement made explicit

1. Write the mass (or percentage) of each element. If one element is not given, find it **by difference**.
2. Divide each by its Aᵣ to get moles of atoms.
3. Divide every value by the **smallest** of them.
4. Decide: is each result within about 0.1 of a whole number? If so, round it.
5. If not, look for a familiar decimal and multiply **every** value by the same factor.
6. Write the formula, usually with C first, then H, then other elements for organic compounds.

### Recognising the decimals

| Decimal ending | Fraction | Multiply by |
|---|---|---|
| .5 | 1/2 | 2 |
| .33 or .67 | 1/3 or 2/3 | 3 |
| .25 or .75 | 1/4 or 3/4 | 4 |
| .2, .4, .6, .8 | fifths | 5 |

The rule is: never round 1.5 to 2 or 1.33 to 1. Rounding only the values that are already within a few hundredths of a whole number protects you from experimental scatter without hiding a real fraction.

## Worked example 1: a ratio of 1 : 1.5

An oxide of iron contains 69.9% iron by mass. (Aᵣ: Fe = 55.8, O = 16.0)

```
O by difference = 100 − 69.9 = 30.1 %
moles:  Fe 69.9 ÷ 55.8 = 1.253     O 30.1 ÷ 16.0 = 1.881
÷ smallest:  Fe 1.000              O 1.501
× 2:         Fe 2                  O 3
empirical formula: Fe₂O₃
```

## Worked example 2: a ratio of 1 : 1.33

A different iron oxide contains 72.4% iron.

```
O = 27.6 %
moles:  Fe 72.4 ÷ 55.8 = 1.297     O 27.6 ÷ 16.0 = 1.725
÷ smallest:  Fe 1.000              O 1.329
× 3:         Fe 3                  O 3.99 ≈ 4
empirical formula: Fe₃O₄
```

Rounding 1.33 to 1 would give FeO, a different compound with 77.7% iron. The decimal is real information.

## Worked example 3: a ratio of 1 : 2.5, then the molecular formula

An oxide of phosphorus contains 43.7% phosphorus. Its Mᵣ is 284. (Aᵣ P = 31.0)

```
O = 56.3 %
moles:  P 43.7 ÷ 31.0 = 1.410      O 56.3 ÷ 16.0 = 3.519
÷ smallest:  P 1.000               O 2.496
× 2:         P 2                   O 5
empirical formula: P₂O₅      empirical formula mass = 62.0 + 80.0 = 142.0
n = 284 ÷ 142.0 = 2
molecular formula: P₄O₁₀
```

## Worked example 4: masses, not percentages

2.40 g of carbon combines completely with hydrogen to give 3.00 g of a hydrocarbon with Mᵣ 30.0.

```
mass of H = 3.00 − 2.40 = 0.60 g
moles:  C 2.40 ÷ 12.0 = 0.200      H 0.60 ÷ 1.0 = 0.60
÷ smallest:  C 1                   H 3
empirical formula: CH₃ (mass 15.0)
n = 30.0 ÷ 15.0 = 2, so molecular formula C₂H₆
```

CH₃ cannot exist as a stable molecule on its own, which is a useful check that the empirical formula here is not also the molecular formula.

## Where the Mᵣ comes from

A question may give Mᵣ directly or expect you to find it from other data on the paper:

- from the **molecular ion peak** in a mass spectrum (3.1.1.2);
- from gas data, using **Mᵣ = mRT ÷ pV** (3.1.2.3).

Linking the sub-topics in one question is common, so be ready to find Mᵣ first and then the molecular formula.

## The metal oxide practical: evaluation points

When magnesium is heated in a crucible to form magnesium oxide, the mass of oxygen is found by difference. Typical evaluation points:

- **Incomplete reaction** leaves unreacted metal, so the oxygen mass is too low and the ratio of O to metal comes out too small. Heat to constant mass to check.
- **Loss of white smoke** (oxide particles) when the lid is lifted lowers the final mass. Lift the lid only slightly and briefly.
- **Reaction with nitrogen** in the air can form some magnesium nitride, Mg₃N₂. Nitrogen adds less mass per magnesium atom than oxygen does, so the mass gained is too low.

Each point should state the direction of the error, not only that "it affects the result".

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the percentage by the atomic number, or by the Mᵣ of a diatomic element (O₂ = 32) instead of the Aᵣ of the atom.
- Forgetting the element found by difference.
- Rounding 1.5, 1.33 or 2.5 to the nearest whole number.
- Multiplying only the decimal value, not every value, by the factor.
- Rounding moles too early: 1.25 and 1.3 lead to different formulae.
- Giving the empirical formula when the question asks for the molecular formula, or vice versa.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate** an empirical formula: set out a table with a column per element and a row per step, so each stage can earn credit.
- **Deduce** a molecular formula: show the empirical formula mass and the division Mᵣ ÷ empirical formula mass.
- **Show that** a compound has a given formula: work forwards from the data; do not start from the answer.
- **Explain** why two compounds have the same empirical formula but different molecular formulae: refer to the ratio being the same and the actual numbers of atoms being different multiples of it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know when to round a ratio?**
Round only when a value is within about 0.1 of a whole number. Values such as 1.5, 1.33 or 1.25 are real fractions, so multiply every value by 2, 3 or 4.

**Can the empirical formula be the same as the molecular formula?**
Yes. For water (H₂O) and methane (CH₄), Mᵣ ÷ empirical formula mass equals 1, so the formulae are the same.

**What do I do if one element's percentage is missing?**
Find it by difference: subtract the total of the other percentages from 100. Oxygen is the element most often left out in this way.
