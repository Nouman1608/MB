## Commercial and investment banks

A **commercial bank** takes deposits from households and firms and lends to them. Its customers are mostly ordinary savers and borrowers, and it runs the payment system they use. An **investment bank** serves large firms, governments and financial institutions. It advises on mergers and takeovers, helps firms raise money by issuing shares or bonds, and trades financial assets. It earns fees and trading profits, not mainly interest from retail lending.

| | Commercial bank | Investment bank |
|---|---|---|
| Main customers | Households and firms | Large firms, governments, institutions |
| Main income | Interest margin, charges | Fees, trading gains |
| Liabilities | Mostly deposits | Mostly market borrowing |

Many banks do both. Other institutions also operate in financial markets, but you do not need to know their activities or functions.

## Functions of a commercial bank

- **Accepting deposits.** A safe place to hold money, with easy access.
- **Lending.** Overdrafts, loans and mortgages to households and firms.
- **Operating payments.** Transfers, cards and cheques settle debts between customers.
- **Creating credit.** Lending creates new deposits, so it adds to the money available to spend.
- **Maturity transformation.** Short-term deposits fund longer-term loans. This is useful and also the main source of risk.

## The balance sheet

A balance sheet lists what a bank owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities), plus the owners' stake (capital). It always balances: assets = liabilities + capital. Capital is the cushion that absorbs losses before depositors are hurt.

**Worked example.** Bank J is an invented commercial bank. Figures are in £m.

| Assets | £m | Liabilities and capital | £m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and central bank balances | 25 | Current accounts | 210 |
| Short-term loans to other banks | 45 | Savings accounts | 170 |
| Government bonds | 70 | Short-term wholesale borrowing | 60 |
| Mortgages | 240 | Bonds issued by Bank J | 20 |
| Loans to firms | 110 | Capital | 40 |
| Other assets | 10 | | |
| **Total** | **500** | **Total** | **500** |

Liabilities are 210 + 170 + 60 + 20 = 460. With capital of 40, the total is 500, which equals total assets. A loan is an asset to the bank, because borrowers owe it money. A deposit is a liability, because the bank owes it to the depositor. Do not reverse them.

## Objectives and conflicts

- **Liquidity:** being able to meet withdrawals as they fall due. Cash is the most liquid asset.
- **Profitability:** earning more on assets than the bank pays on liabilities.
- **Security:** keeping the risk of loss low, so loans are repaid and the bank survives.

The conflicts arise because liquid, safe assets earn little, and high-yielding assets are illiquid or risky.

**Worked example.** Bank J earns these illustrative annual rates: cash 0%, loans to other banks 1%, government bonds 2%, mortgages 4%, loans to firms 6%. Its interest income is 45 × 0.01 + 70 × 0.02 + 240 × 0.04 + 110 × 0.06 = 0.45 + 1.40 + 9.60 + 6.60 = £18.05m. It pays 0.5% on current accounts, 2.5% on savings, 3% on wholesale borrowing and 4% on its bonds: 1.05 + 4.25 + 1.80 + 0.80 = £7.90m. Net interest is £10.15m.

Now suppose a regulator wants more liquidity, and Bank J moves £40m from firm loans into government bonds. Income falls by 40 × (0.06 − 0.02) = £1.6m, so net interest is £8.55m. Liquidity and security improve while profitability falls. Another conflict: lending to a risky firm at a high rate raises expected profit but threatens security.

## How banks create credit

Banks lend more than the cash they hold because depositors rarely all withdraw at once. When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account, which creates a new deposit. The borrower spends it, the payee's bank receives it as a new deposit, and that bank lends part of it. The chain continues.

**Illustration.** Suppose a bank wants to keep 25% of any deposit liquid and lends the rest. A new deposit of £600m arrives.

| Round | New deposit | Kept liquid (25%) | Lent on (75%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 600.0 | 150.0 | 450.0 |
| 2 | 450.0 | 112.5 | 337.5 |
| 3 | 337.5 | 84.4 | 253.1 |
| 4 | 253.1 | 63.3 | 189.8 |

After four rounds total deposits are 600 + 450 + 337.5 + 253.1 = £1,640.6m, from an initial £600m. Each round is smaller, so the chain converges towards a limit (here 2,400). You are not asked to calculate the credit multiplier. You need to explain why the process happens and what limits it.

**What limits credit creation:**

- Demand for loans: borrowers must want and qualify for credit.
- Liquidity: banks keep assets that can meet withdrawals.
- Capital: each extra loan is an asset that must be backed by capital if it goes wrong.
- Leakage: money held as cash leaves the chain.
- Regulation and monetary policy: both affect the cost and amount of credit.

## Combined banks and systemic risk

**Systemic risk** is the risk that the failure of one institution, or a shock to the system, spreads and threatens the whole financial system. A bank that combines retail and investment banking has a larger and more complex balance sheet. Trading losses can threaten the deposits of ordinary savers. Governments may then feel obliged to rescue it, which links to moral hazard below. Against this, combined banks are more diversified.

## UK regulation

You need to appreciate the role of each body, not know their detail.

| Body | Role in financial stability |
|---|---|
| Bank of England | Responsible for financial stability in the UK. It houses the PRA and the FPC |
| Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) | Supervises individual firms such as banks, building societies and insurers, setting standards on capital and liquidity |
| Financial Policy Committee (FPC) | Monitors the system as a whole and acts to reduce systemic risks |
| Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) | Regulates how financial firms treat customers and behave in markets |

The PRA looks at whether each firm is safe. The FPC looks at the whole system. The FCA looks at conduct.

## Why banks fail

A bank can fail in two ways. It may be **illiquid**: unable to meet payments as they fall due even though its assets are worth enough in the long run. Or it may be **insolvent**: its assets are worth less than its liabilities, so its capital is wiped out.

**Borrowing short and lending long.** Deposits can be withdrawn at short notice, but mortgages and business loans are repaid over years and cannot be sold quickly without a loss. If depositors lose confidence, they withdraw together. This is a bank run, and it can feed itself: fear of failure causes the failure.

**Worked example (a run on Bank J).** Bank J's liquid assets are 25 + 45 + 70 = £140m. Depositors withdraw 25% of current and savings accounts: 0.25 × 380 = £95m. Wholesale lenders also refuse to renew £60m. Demands are 95 + 60 = £155m, so the shortfall is £15m. Bank J must sell mortgages. In a hurry it sells £40m of them for 85% of their value, receiving £34m and losing £6m.

**Insolvency.** Suppose a recession causes 10% of loans to firms (£11m) and 2% of mortgages (£4.8m) to default: total £15.8m. Capital of 40 absorbs this and leaves £24.2m. A similar bank with capital of only 10 would end at −£5.8m. It is insolvent.

## Liquidity and capital ratios

A **liquidity ratio** compares liquid assets with liabilities that may be demanded soon. A higher ratio means the bank can survive withdrawals without selling assets at a loss. A **capital ratio** compares capital with assets, often adjusted for risk. A higher ratio means more cushion against losses. You will not calculate either ratio. You need to explain how they affect stability.

Higher required ratios make failure less likely, but they cost the bank profit and restrict lending: this is the security versus profitability conflict again.

## Moral hazard

**Moral hazard** exists when a party is protected from the full cost of its own risks, so it gambles more freely. In banking, deposit protection, limited liability or an expectation of a government rescue can encourage risky lending, because gains go to the bank and losses partly go elsewhere.

**Worked example.** A bank can invest £100m safely for a 3% return (£3m). Or it can take a risky investment: a 60% chance of a 12% gain and a 40% chance of a 15% loss.

- Expected return on the risky option, with no rescue: 0.6 × 12 + 0.4 × (−15) = 7.2 − 6.0 = £1.2m. The safe option is better, so a bank bearing its own risk avoids it.
- If a rescue means the bank never loses, its expected return is 0.6 × 12 + 0.4 × 0 = £7.2m. The risky option now wins.
- The taxpayer's expected cost is 0.4 × 15 = £6m.

The bank's private incentive is to gamble, though society's expected return is still low. Regulators respond with capital requirements, supervision and rules that let failing banks go under without ruining the system.

## Systemic risk and the real economy

Banks are linked by lending to each other. If Bank K fails and repays only 40p in the pound on a £30m loan from Bank J, Bank J loses 30 × 0.6 = £18m. Added to the £15.8m loan losses above, losses reach £33.8m, leaving capital of £6.2m. One failure has nearly destroyed another bank.

The real economy suffers through these links:

1. Banks lose capital and confidence, so they cut lending (a credit crunch).
2. Firms cannot finance investment or working capital, so investment falls.
3. Households cut spending on credit-dependent goods.
4. Aggregate demand falls, output falls and unemployment rises.
5. Lower incomes cause more loan defaults, which weakens banks further.

**Illustration.** In Country A, bank lending to firms falls £8bn. Investment falls £8bn. With a multiplier of 1.5, aggregate demand falls 8 × 1.5 = £12bn, which is 12 ÷ 400 = 3.0% of a £400bn economy. This is an invented example.

**Evaluation.** The effect depends on how well capitalised banks are, how fast regulators respond, whether firms can borrow elsewhere, and whether confidence returns.

## Common errors

- Putting deposits on the asset side of a bank balance sheet.
- Treating illiquid and insolvent as the same.
- Describing moral hazard as "risky behaviour" without saying who bears the cost.
- Saying the FPC supervises individual banks. That is the PRA.
