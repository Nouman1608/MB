## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Geographical immobility | Workers unable or unwilling to move to where jobs are |
| Occupational immobility | Workers unable to switch easily between jobs or industries |
| Structural unemployment | Unemployment from a mismatch of skills or location with vacancies |
| Monopsony | A single (or dominant) buyer of labour, able to pay below MRP |
| Minimum wage | Legal floor on hourly pay |
| Maximum wage | Legal ceiling on pay |
| Direct tax | Tax on income or profit, paid by the person or firm it is aimed at |
| NICs | National insurance contributions: payroll taxes on earnings, paid by employees and employers |
| Corporation tax | Tax on company profits |
| Pay gap | (Pay of group A - pay of group B) / pay of group A x 100 |

## Causes and consequences at a glance

| | Geographical | Occupational |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | Housing cost, family ties, removal costs, poor information, weak transport | Skills mismatch, licences, retraining cost, age, little information |
| Consequences | Regional unemployment and shortages, persistent wage gaps | Structural unemployment, skill shortages, lost output and tax |
| Policy | Housing, relocation grants, transport, vacancy information | Training, apprenticeships, careers advice, retraining schemes |

Keep them apart: geographical is about place, occupational is about skill.

## The case for intervention

Say which failure you mean and name it.
1. Immobility: resources idle or mismatched.
2. Monopsony: wages below MRP.
3. Discrimination: pay or hiring unrelated to productivity.
4. Under-provision of training (positive externality, poaching fear).
5. Information gaps.
6. Equity and poverty.

Against: government failure, cost, lower employment, avoidance.

## Wage controls: method

**Minimum wage, competitive market**
1. Find equilibrium (demand = supply).
2. Check the floor is above it; if not, nothing happens.
3. Read employment from the demand curve and supply from the supply curve.
4. Unemployment = supply minus demand at the floor.
5. Use elasticity of demand for labour to judge the wage bill.

**Monopsony**
1. Equate MRP = marginal cost of labour (MC).
2. Read the wage from the supply curve at that quantity.
3. A floor between that wage and the competitive wage raises employment.

**Maximum wage:** a cap below equilibrium creates a shortage of labour (supply below demand).

**Worked reminder.** A floor raises pay from 8.00 to 8.80 (+10.0%) and employment falls from 500 to 470 (-6.0%).
```
Elasticity of demand for labour = -6.0 / 10.0 = -0.6  (inelastic)
Wage bill: 8.00 x 500 = 4,000 rises to 8.80 x 470 = 4,136 (+3.4%)
```
Inelastic demand means the wage bill rises; elastic demand means it falls.

## Monopsony reminder

A small fictional town has one large employer. Marginal revenue product is w = 14 - L and supply is w = 2 + L (L in hundreds of workers, w per hour).
```
MC of labour = 2 + 2L
MRP = MC: 14 - L = 2 + 2L -> L = 4, wage = 2 + 4 = 6
Competitive outcome: 14 - L = 2 + L -> L = 6, wage = 8
A floor of 7: supply L = 5, demand L = 7 -> employment 5 (up from 4)
```
The firm pays 6 against an MRP of 10 at L = 4, so the gap is 4 per hour. A floor of 9 would sit above the competitive wage: demand L = 5, supply L = 7, so 2 hundred are unemployed.

## Building an evaluation paragraph

Use this chain for any policy question.
1. **Point:** the policy and the failure it targets.
2. **Mechanism:** which curve moves, and why.
3. **Size:** elasticity, cost or the scale of the gap.
4. **Counter-argument:** who loses, or what else changes.
5. **Time:** short run against long run.
6. **Judgement:** which factor decides the outcome, stated plainly.

For example: a floor raises pay for those in work (mechanism), but if demand for labour is elastic, jobs fall by a larger percentage than pay rises (size). Young and low-skilled workers may be hit hardest (counter-argument). Firms may adapt over time through productivity gains (time). So the verdict depends on the size of the floor relative to the market wage.

## Direct taxes

| Tax | Curve affected | Likely effect |
|---|---|---|
| Employer NICs | Labour demand shifts left | Fewer jobs or lower wages |
| Employee NICs and income tax | Labour supply shifts left | Weaker incentive to work |
| Corporation tax cut | More retained profit | May raise investment and labour demand |

**Worked reminder.** Employer NIC of 13% on pay of 10.00 gives labour cost 10.00 x 1.13 = 11.30. Corporation tax of 19% on 250,000 profit is 47,500, leaving 202,500.

Evaluate through elasticities, the share of the tax passed to workers, the time lag, and what the government does with the revenue.

## Measures to reduce immobility: method

1. Name the measure (housing, grants, transport, training, apprenticeships, information).
2. Link it to the cause it targets.
3. Quantify cost and benefit if data are given.
4. Evaluate: cost, time lag, deadweight, whether training suits employers' needs.

**Worked reminder.** Retrain 500 people at 4,000 each (2.0 million). 70% succeed (350), each gaining 5,000 a year in saved benefits and tax.
```
Annual fiscal gain = 350 x 5,000 = 1.75 million
Payback = 2.0 / 1.75 = 1.14 years
```
Geographical check: pay gap 22,500 - 18,000 = 4,500 (25.0%); less extra housing 1,500 = 3,000 net; moving cost 6,000 / 3,000 = 2.0 years.

## Discrimination and exploitation

- Anti-discrimination and equal-pay law, with penalties.
- Pay-gap reporting.
- Minimum wages, limits on hours, enforcement bodies.
- Evaluation: hard to enforce, hidden discrimination, compliance cost; differences in hours or experience can explain part of a gap.

**Worked reminder.** Mean pay 22.00 against 19.80: gap = 2.20 / 22.00 = 10.0%.

## Must-know distinctions

- Floor in competitive market: unemployment. Floor in monopsony: employment may rise.
- Employer NICs shift demand; employee NICs shift supply.
- Training that raises a firm's own productivity differs from training that benefits the whole economy.
- A pay gap is a measure; discrimination is a cause.

## Quick self-test

1. Name two causes of geographical immobility.
2. Name two causes of occupational immobility.
3. Employer NIC of 12% is charged on pay of 15.00. What is the labour cost?
4. 18,000 are unemployed in a labour force of 300,000. Find the unemployment rate.
5. A retraining scheme costs 8,000 per person and saves 5,000 in benefits and tax each year, with 1,000 in running costs. Find the payback.
6. Elasticity of demand for labour is -1.5. Pay rises 4%. What is the change in employment?
7. Why might a minimum wage raise employment?
8. Why might a maximum wage cause a shortage?
9. Firm profit is 250,000 and the tax rate is 40%. Find the tax.
10. Give one limit of anti-discrimination laws.
11. State the formula for the pay gap.
12. Why might firms under-provide training?

### Answers

1. Any two of: housing cost, family ties, removal costs, poor information, weak transport.
2. Any two of: skills mismatch, licences, retraining cost, age, little information.
3. 15.00 x 1.12 = 16.80.
4. 18,000 / 300,000 x 100 = 6.0%.
5. Net saving 5,000 - 1,000 = 4,000; 8,000 / 4,000 = 2.0 years.
6. -1.5 x 4 = -6%.
7. In monopsony, the floor can lie between the monopsony wage and MRP, so firms hire more workers.
8. A cap below equilibrium lowers supply of labour below demand.
9. 250,000 x 0.40 = 100,000.
10. Enforcement is costly and discrimination can be hidden.
11. (Pay of one group - pay of the other) / pay of the first group x 100.
12. Trained workers can be poached by rivals, so the firm cannot capture all the benefit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Predicting unemployment from a minimum wage without checking the structure of the market.
- Drawing the floor below equilibrium and then describing an effect.
- Mixing up the curves a tax shifts.
- Using "immobility" without saying which type.
- Listing measures with no link to the cause they address.
- Quoting a pay gap as proof of discrimination.
- Giving a calculation with no working or units.
- A conclusion that repeats points without a judgement.
- Treating a training subsidy as free: it has an opportunity cost, and the benefit arrives later.
- Ignoring who bears an employer tax: part may fall on workers through lower pay, so say which group loses.
- Forgetting to separate short-run and long-run effects when judging a policy.
