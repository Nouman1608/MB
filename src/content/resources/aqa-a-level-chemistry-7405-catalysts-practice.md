These questions cover section **3.1.5.5 Catalysts** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Catalysts study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-catalysts/) and the [Catalysts revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-catalysts-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Define the term catalyst. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction. **[2]**

**3.** Sketch a reaction profile for an exothermic reaction, with and without a catalyst. Label the activation energies and the enthalpy change. **[4]**

**4.** State the effect of a catalyst on the enthalpy change of a reaction, and explain your answer by reference to your profile. **[2]**

**5.** Explain, with a labelled Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction between gases. **[4]**

**6.** In the Haber process, nitrogen and hydrogen react in the presence of iron: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g).

**(a)** State the effect of the catalyst on the position of equilibrium. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest two ways in which the use of a catalyst benefits the manufacturer. **[2]**

**7.** A student says: "A catalyst gives the molecules more energy so more of them can react." Identify the error and correct it. **[2]**

**8.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes slowly: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). When manganese(IV) oxide is added, oxygen is produced rapidly. After the reaction the manganese(IV) oxide is filtered off, dried and weighed.

**(a)** Predict the mass of manganese(IV) oxide recovered compared with the mass added, and explain. **[2]**

**(b)** State what this shows about the nature of a catalyst. **[1]**

**9.** The activation energy of a gas-phase reaction is 120 kJ mol⁻¹ without a catalyst and 80 kJ mol⁻¹ with a catalyst. At 400 K the reaction mixture is the same in both cases.

**(a)** State whether the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular energies is different in the two cases. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the reaction is faster with the catalyst. **[2]**

**10.** Platinum and rhodium are used in catalytic converters.

**(a)** State one function of the catalyst in the converter. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why the surface area of the catalyst is made as large as possible. **[2]**

**11.** Explain why using a catalyst can reduce the carbon dioxide emissions of a chemical process, even though the catalyst does not itself change the reaction. **[2]**

**12.** Explain why a catalyst does not change the amount of product formed at equilibrium. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A substance that increases the rate of a reaction [1] without being changed in chemical composition or amount [1]. **[2]**

**2.** It provides an alternative reaction route [1] with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Axes labelled enthalpy and progress of reaction, with reactants higher than products [1]; an uncatalysed curve with a high peak, labelled Eₐ [1]; a catalysed curve with a lower peak, labelled Eₐ(catalysed), starting and ending at the same levels [1]; ΔH shown as a downward arrow between the reactant and product levels [1]. **[4]**

**4.** No effect on ΔH [1]; the reactants and products are at the same enthalpy levels with and without the catalyst [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A single distribution curve, with axes labelled energy and number of molecules [1]; Eₐ marked, and a lower Eₐ(catalysed) marked to the left [1]; a larger area beyond the lower Eₐ shaded [1]; a greater proportion of molecules have energy at least equal to Eₐ, so more collisions are successful and the rate is higher [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** No effect [1]

**(b)** The catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions equally [1]; so equilibrium is reached sooner but the composition at equilibrium is unchanged [1]

**(c)** Any two of: a lower temperature can be used, which saves energy; the reaction is faster, so more ammonia is made per hour; lower energy use means lower costs and lower carbon dioxide emissions [1 each]. **[5]**

**7.** The catalyst does not give the molecules more energy [1]; it provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of the molecules has enough energy [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** The mass is the same as the mass added [1]; a catalyst is not used up, or changed in amount [1]

**(b)** It is unchanged in chemical composition and amount at the end of the reaction [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** No, the distribution is the same, because the temperature is unchanged [1]

**(b)** The catalyst provides a route with lower activation energy [1]; so a greater proportion of molecules has energy of at least the activation energy, and there are more successful collisions per second [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** It speeds up the reactions that convert pollutant gases (for example carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides) to less harmful gases [1]

**(b)** Reactions take place on the surface of the catalyst [1]; a larger surface area means more reactant molecules can be adsorbed at the same time, so the rate is greater [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The catalyst lets the process run at a lower temperature [1]; less fuel is burned to heat the reactor, so less carbon dioxide is released [1]. **[2]**

**12.** A catalyst lowers the activation energy for the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount [1]; both rates increase equally, so the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium is unchanged [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "alternative route".
- Saying the catalyst gives particles more energy.
- Drawing catalysed profiles with different start or end levels.
- Moving the distribution curve instead of the activation energy.
- Saying that a catalyst increases the equilibrium yield.
