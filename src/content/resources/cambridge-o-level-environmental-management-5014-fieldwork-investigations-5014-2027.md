Sub-topic 5.4, Fieldwork investigations, is new in the 2027-2029 Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus. It sets out the scientific method for collecting reliable data (5.4.1), two sampling strategies (5.4.2 and 5.4.3) and seven sampling techniques for measuring the populations of species (5.4.4 and 5.4.5). The syllabus also has a separate "Fieldwork requirements" section listing the investigation skills candidates should have and the apparatus they should know. Paper 2 uses source material on fieldwork investigations, so expect questions that give you an unfamiliar method or a table of results and ask you to plan, process or evaluate.

For the whole of Topic 5, see the [ecosystems, biodiversity and fieldwork study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study/).

## The scientific method in the field (5.4.1)

The syllabus lists nine steps. Treat them as a checklist for any planning question.

1. **Aims and hypotheses.** An aim says what you want to find out; a hypothesis is a testable prediction, for example "Percentage cover of grass increases with distance from the woodland edge."
2. **Plan methods.** Choose a sampling strategy, a sampling technique and, where people are involved, a questionnaire or survey. A **pilot study** is a small trial run that tests the method before the main data collection.
3. **Measured and control variables.** Control variables are the factors you keep the same so they do not affect the result, such as quadrat size or time of day.
4. **Independent and dependent variables.** The independent variable is the one you change or select (distance from the woodland edge); the dependent variable is the one you measure (percentage cover).
5. **Repeats and replicates.** Repeats measure the same sample point again; replicates take more samples at the same level of the independent variable. Both allow a mean and make results more reliable.
6. **Record data** in a table with the quantity and unit in the heading, for example "light intensity / lux".
7. **Analyse data**: calculate means and ranges, draw graphs, describe patterns.
8. **Identify and process anomalies**: spot results that do not fit the pattern, give a likely reason, and leave them out of the mean or repeat the reading.
9. **Form conclusions** that answer the aim, using data, and say whether the hypothesis is supported.

The fieldwork requirements section adds three expectations that are easy to forget: a suitable **control** where appropriate, a **risk assessment** with safety precautions, and an appropriate **number and range** of values for the independent variable.

## Sampling strategies (5.4.2 and 5.4.3)

You sample because counting every organism in a habitat is impossible.

**Random sampling.** Sample positions are chosen by chance, for example by laying two tapes at right angles to make a grid and using random numbers as co-ordinates.
- *Benefits:* avoids bias, because the investigator does not choose where to sample; suitable when the habitat looks uniform.
- *Limitations:* may miss small areas or rare species by chance; can leave parts of the site unsampled; does not show how a population changes across the site.

**Systematic sampling.** Samples are taken at regular intervals, usually along a transect line.
- *Benefits:* shows change along an environmental gradient, such as from shade into light or from a river bank upslope; ensures the whole length is covered.
- *Limitations:* can be biased if the interval matches a pattern in the habitat; organisms between sample points are missed.

The choice depends on the aim. "Is there a difference between two fields?" suits random sampling in each field. "How does cover change with distance?" suits systematic sampling along a transect.

## Sampling techniques (5.4.4 and 5.4.5)

| Technique | What it is and how it is used | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pitfall trap | A container sunk so its rim is level with the soil, covered with a raised lid; catches ground-dwelling invertebrates overnight | Cheap; samples animals active when no one is watching | Animals may eat each other; must be checked regularly; catch depends on activity |
| Pooter | A jar with two tubes: suck on one and small insects are drawn in through the other | Catches small animals without harm | Only very small animals; slow |
| Sweep net | A strong net swept through long grass or low plants in a set number of sweeps | Quick way to sample insects in vegetation | Misses animals near the ground; hard to standardise sweeps |
| Quadrat | A square frame (open frame or grid) placed on the ground to count individuals or estimate percentage cover | Simple and cheap; allows density and cover estimates | Small area per sample; percentage cover is an estimate; slow for large sites |
| Transect | A long tape measure or string laid across a habitat; samples taken along it at intervals | Shows change across a gradient | Time-consuming; covers one line only |
| Aerial photography and drones | Images taken from above to map vegetation or count large animals | Covers large or inaccessible areas quickly | Cost; cannot see under a canopy or identify small species |
| Automated sampling | Equipment that records without a person present, such as camera traps or data loggers | Continuous data, day and night; less disturbance | Equipment cost; can fail or be stolen; large amounts of data to process |

### Apparatus to recognise

The syllabus lists apparatus that you should be able to identify from a diagram or description: hand lens, humidity or moisture meter, light meter, metre ruler, pH meter, pitfall trap, pooter, quadrat (open frame and grid), 30 cm ruler, sweep net, transect (long tape measure or string) and tray for hand-sorting. Questions may ask you to choose apparatus for an abiotic measurement: a light meter for light intensity, a pH meter for soil or water pH, a moisture meter for soil water.

## Worked example 1: estimating a plant population with quadrats

A school field measures 30 m by 20 m. A student places ten 0.25 m² quadrats (50 cm x 50 cm) at random and counts dandelions. The mean is 3.0 plants per quadrat.

- Area of the field = 30 x 20 = **600 m²**.
- Density = 3.0 / 0.25 = **12 plants per m²**.
- Estimated population = 12 x 600 = **7 200 dandelions**.

To evaluate: ten quadrats cover only 2.5 m², which is about 0.4% of the field (2.5 / 600 x 100 = 0.42%), so more quadrats would improve reliability.

## Worked example 2: transect data with an anomaly

A student uses systematic sampling along a transect from a woodland edge into a field. At each distance she takes three light readings and estimates grass cover with a grid quadrat.

| Distance / m | Light 1 / lux | Light 2 / lux | Light 3 / lux | Mean grass cover / % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 200 | 1 150 | 1 250 | 10 |
| 5 | 2 400 | 2 600 | 2 500 | 30 |
| 10 | 4 100 | 3 900 | 4 000 | 55 |
| 15 | 5 200 | 5 000 | 1 100 | 70 |
| 20 | 6 100 | 5 900 | 6 000 | 85 |

*The data are invented for practice.*

- The 1 100 lux reading at 15 m is **anomalous**: it is far below the other two readings and the trend, perhaps because a cloud covered the Sun.
- Including it, the mean is 11 300 / 3 = 3 766.7 lux, which wrongly suggests less light than at 10 m.
- Excluding it, the mean is (5 200 + 5 000) / 2 = **5 100 lux**.

Conclusion: as distance from the woodland increases, mean light intensity increases (1 200 to 6 000 lux) and grass cover increases (10% to 85%), a positive correlation. This supports the hypothesis, but it does not prove that light is the cause, because soil moisture or trampling may also change along the transect. Improvements: take replicate transects, measure soil moisture as well, and take all light readings within a short time.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "repeat the experiment" without saying why: repeats allow a mean and show whether results are reliable.
- Confusing a control variable with a control. A control variable is kept constant; a control is a comparison sample without the factor being tested.
- Putting units inside table cells. Units go in the column heading after a solidus.
- Plotting the dependent variable on the x-axis. The independent variable goes on the x-axis unless told otherwise.
- Claiming a correlation proves cause.
- Forgetting safety: risk assessment, working in groups, care near water, gloves when handling soil or animals, washing hands.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a method: give the apparatus, the sampling strategy, the number of samples, what is measured and how it is recorded, in order. **Explain** a choice: give the reason, such as "systematic sampling, because the aim is to show change along a gradient". **Suggest** improvements: name a specific change (more quadrats, replicate transects, a control site) and say what it improves. **Discuss** benefits and limitations: give both for each technique or strategy named and finish with which is more suitable for the stated aim. **Calculate**: show working, give units and round sensibly.

## Check your understanding

1. What is a pilot study for? *To test the method and fix problems before the main data collection.*
2. Which sampling strategy suits a hypothesis about change with distance? *Systematic sampling along a transect.*
3. Name the technique for catching insects in long grass. *A sweep net.*
4. Mean count 6 per 0.5 m² quadrat; site 80 m². Estimate the population. *12 per m² x 80 = 960.*
5. Why should anomalous results be excluded from a mean? *They distort the mean and do not represent the pattern.*
