These notes condense **Aggregate demand and supply** from Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification: the assumptions underlying the AD and AS models and how to analyse changes using both curves. The [aggregate demand and supply study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-and-supply-ocr-aslevel-economics/) has the full worked examples, and the [aggregate demand and supply practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-and-supply-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/) give exam-style work. Equilibrium and its effects on indicators are in the [macroeconomic equilibrium revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-macroeconomic-equilibrium-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/). All figures are invented.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification wording | In one line |
|---|---|
| Explain the assumptions underlying the AD and AS models | Aggregation, ceteris paribus, independent curves, sticky short-run costs, flexible long-run prices, known capacity, given expectations, comparative statics |
| Use AD and AS together to analyse change | Identify the curve, the direction, draw it, read off P and Y |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aggregation | Treating the whole economy as one market with one price level and one output |
| Ceteris paribus | All other influences held constant while one changes |
| Independence of the curves | The assumption that a shift in AD does not shift AS, and the other way round |
| Comparative statics | Comparing two equilibria without modelling the path between them |
| Demand shock | An unexpected change in AD |
| Supply shock | An unexpected change in costs or capacity, shifting AS |
| Simultaneous shift | Both AD and AS shift in the same period |
| Spare capacity | Unused labour and capital, shown by the flat part of a Keynesian AS curve |
| Stagflation | Rising price level with falling or stagnant output |

## Assumptions and when they fail

| Assumption | Fails when... |
|---|---|
| Aggregation | Sectors or regions move in opposite directions |
| Ceteris paribus | Several shocks hit at once |
| Independence | Investment, wage changes or exchange rate changes affect both demand and costs |
| Sticky short-run costs | Wage contracts are short or indexed to inflation |
| Flexible long-run prices | Wages resist cuts, so unemployment persists |
| Known capacity | Estimates of potential output are wrong or revised |
| Given expectations | Confidence or expected inflation changes suddenly |
| Comparative statics | The question is about how long adjustment takes |

## Single-shift patterns

| Shift | Price level | Real output |
|---|---|---|
| AD right | Up | Up |
| AD left | Down | Down |
| SRAS right | Down | Up |
| SRAS left | Up | Down |

**Demand shocks move P and Y the same way; supply shocks move them opposite ways.**

## Simultaneous-shift patterns

| Shifts | Certain | Depends on relative size |
|---|---|---|
| AD right + SRAS right | Y up | P |
| AD right + SRAS left | P up | Y |
| AD left + SRAS left | Y down | P |
| AD left + SRAS right | P down | Y |

### Worked reminder: reading a schedule

| Price level | AD1 | SRAS1 | AD2 | SRAS2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90 | 450 | 390 | 420 | 420 |
| 100 | 420 | 420 | 390 | 450 |
| 110 | 390 | 450 | 360 | 480 |

- Start: AD1 = SRAS1 at P = 100, Y = 420.
- AD shifts left by 30 to AD2 and SRAS shifts right by 30 to SRAS2. New equilibrium: AD2 = SRAS2 at **P = 90, Y = 420**. The price level falls 10% and output is unchanged: the two shifts on output cancel exactly.

### Worked reminder: inferring the cause

Data show inflation up from 2% to 5% while real output falls 1%. P and Y move in opposite directions, so the likely cause is an SRAS shift left, such as an energy price shock.

### Worked reminder: slope

AD shifts right by 30. On a flat AS section, output rises by nearly 30 with prices steady. On a vertical section, output is unchanged and only prices rise.

## Applying the four steps

1. Identify: spending (AD), costs (SRAS), capacity (LRAS).
2. Direction: right or left, with a one-line reason.
3. Draw: label axes, curves, P1, Y1, P2, Y2 and arrows.
4. Read off and translate: growth, unemployment, inflation, current account.

## Exam traps

- Do not shift AD for a cost shock: higher oil prices are an SRAS shift first.
- A depreciation shifts **both** AD (right, via net exports) and SRAS (left, via import costs).
- With two shifts, never give a definite answer for the uncertain variable without data.
- A rise in AD on a vertical LRAS raises only the price level.
- Name the assumption that matters for the case; a list of all eight earns little.
- "Stagflation" needs a supply-side cause, not a fall in AD.

## Self-test

1. State three assumptions of the AD/AS model.
2. Why is the independence of the curves a weak assumption for an investment boom?
3. What happens to P and Y when SRAS shifts left?
4. AD and SRAS both shift right. Which variable certainly rises?
5. AD shifts right and SRAS shifts left. Which variable certainly rises?
6. Inflation and output both rise. Is the cause more likely on the demand or supply side?
7. Explain why a depreciation can shift both AD and SRAS.
8. What does comparative statics leave out?
9. Why does the slope of AS matter for the effect of a rise in AD?
10. Give an example of a situation where the aggregation assumption hides important information.
11. AD shifts left by 40 and SRAS shifts right by 40, with equal slopes. What happens to output?
12. Define stagflation and state which curve shift causes it.

### Answers

1. Any three: aggregation; ceteris paribus; independence of the curves; sticky short-run costs; flexible long-run prices; known capacity; given expectations; comparative statics.
2. Investment raises AD now and adds to capacity later, so both curves shift.
3. P rises and Y falls.
4. Real output.
5. The price level.
6. Demand side: P and Y moving together points to an AD shift.
7. Exports become cheaper abroad (AD right) while imported inputs become dearer (SRAS left).
8. The time taken to adjust and the path between equilibria.
9. On a flat section extra demand raises output; on a steep or vertical section it mainly raises prices.
10. Any suitable case, such as a boom in services in one region alongside factory closures in another.
11. With equal slopes and equal shifts, output is unchanged and the price level falls.
12. Rising price level with falling or stagnant output, caused by SRAS shifting left.
