This guide covers **3.1.6.1 Collision theory** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. The specification states that **reactions can only occur when collisions take place between particles having sufficient energy**, and that **this energy is called the activation energy**. You must be able to:

- define the term activation energy;
- explain why most collisions do not lead to a reaction.

## The central idea

For particles to react they must first **collide**. A collision alone is not enough: the colliding particles must have, between them, at least a certain **minimum energy**. Only then can bonds in the reactants begin to break so that new bonds can form.

**Activation energy, Ea**: the **minimum energy** that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur.

Ea is measured in kJ mol⁻¹. It is always positive: every reaction, exothermic or endothermic, has an energy barrier.

## Why most collisions do not lead to a reaction

In a gas or solution, particles collide extremely often: each one collides many millions of times every second. If every collision led to reaction, almost every reaction would be over in a fraction of a second. Most reactions are much slower than this, because:

- at a given temperature, particles have a **spread of energies**, and for most reactions **only a small fraction** of collisions involve energy equal to or greater than Ea;
- collisions with less energy than Ea simply cause the particles to bounce apart unchanged.

Collisions that lead to reaction are **successful** (or effective) collisions. The rate of reaction depends on how many successful collisions happen each second, which depends on:

1. how often particles collide (the collision **frequency**);
2. what **fraction** of those collisions have energy ≥ Ea.

Collision theory also recognises that colliding particles must meet in a suitable orientation; even an energetic collision can fail if the reacting parts of the particles do not meet. The specification's emphasis, and the focus of most exam answers, is the energy condition.

## Enthalpy profiles

An **enthalpy profile** (reaction profile) plots enthalpy against the progress of the reaction. It shows:

- the enthalpy of the reactants (start) and products (end);
- a hump between them, whose peak represents the highest-energy arrangement on the way from reactants to products;
- **Ea**: the height from the **reactants** up to the peak;
- **ΔH**: the difference between reactants and products (negative if products are lower).

For an **exothermic** reaction the products end below the reactants. For an **endothermic** reaction they end above, and Ea must be at least as large as ΔH.

### Worked example 1: reading a profile in both directions

A reaction has Ea = +75 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −40 kJ mol⁻¹. What is the activation energy of the **reverse** reaction?

The products are 40 kJ mol⁻¹ below the reactants, and the peak is 75 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants. From the products, the climb to the peak is:

```
Ea(reverse) = 75 + 40 = 115 kJ mol⁻¹
```

In general, Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH(forward). For an endothermic forward reaction, the reverse barrier is smaller than the forward one.

### Worked example 2: an endothermic case

The decomposition X → Y + Z has ΔH = +30 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea(reverse) = 50 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Ea(forward).

```
Ea(forward) = Ea(reverse) + ΔH = 50 + 30 = 80 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Sketching the profile first, with numbers on each arrow, avoids sign errors.

## Fast or exothermic? Two separate questions

ΔH tells you **how much** energy is released or absorbed overall. Ea tells you **how easily** the reaction starts. They are independent:

- Hydrogen peroxide decomposes very exothermically into water and oxygen, but at room temperature without a catalyst the reaction is very slow. Its activation energy is high, so few collisions succeed.
- Paper is stable in air for years although burning it is strongly exothermic. A flame supplies enough energy for some molecules to exceed Ea; the heat then released keeps the reaction going.

A mixture that is energetically able to react but does not do so at a noticeable rate is held back by its activation energy.

## Worked example 3: an annotated explanation

**Question.** A sample of hydrogen and nitrogen is stored at room temperature for a month. No ammonia forms, even though the gas molecules collide constantly. Explain why.

**Annotated answer.**

- The molecules are colliding all the time, so collisions are not the problem [shows the collision condition is met].
- For reaction, colliding molecules need at least the activation energy, the minimum energy to start breaking bonds [uses the definition].
- At room temperature almost none of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than Ea [the key reason].
- So almost no collisions are successful and the rate is negligible [conclusion].

## Common mistakes

- Defining activation energy as "the energy needed for a reaction" without the word **minimum**, or without "when particles collide".
- Measuring Ea from the products instead of from the reactants.
- Saying exothermic reactions have no activation energy.
- Writing "the particles do not collide" to explain a slow reaction; they do collide, but without enough energy.
- Linking a large negative ΔH with a fast reaction.
- Drawing an endothermic profile with Ea smaller than ΔH.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: "the minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur".
- **Explain** why most collisions are unsuccessful: refer to the spread of energies and the small fraction with E ≥ Ea.
- **Draw** a profile: label the axes (enthalpy; progress of reaction), reactants, products, Ea from reactants to peak, and ΔH.
- **Calculate** reverse barriers: sketch first, then add or subtract.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is activation energy?**
Activation energy is the minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur. It is shown on an enthalpy profile as the height from the reactants to the peak.

**Why do most collisions not lead to a reaction?**
Particles have a spread of energies, and in most reactions only a small fraction of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. The rest are unsuccessful, and the particles separate unchanged.

**Can a very exothermic reaction be slow?**
Yes. ΔH and activation energy are independent. A reaction with a large negative ΔH can still be very slow if its activation energy is high, as with hydrogen peroxide decomposing at room temperature without a catalyst.
