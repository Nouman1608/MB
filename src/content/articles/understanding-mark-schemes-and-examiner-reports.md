Two documents are published alongside every past paper and most students never
open either. The mark scheme states precisely what earns a mark. The examiner
report describes what candidates actually wrote, and where the cohort lost
marks. Together they turn practice from guesswork into a repeatable method.

## What a mark scheme actually tells you

Read a few and the grammar of examining becomes visible.

- **Marks are discrete.** A four-mark question wants four separate creditable
  points, not one point expressed four ways.
- **Command words are contractual.** *State* wants a sentence. *Explain* wants a
  causal link, usually signalled by "because" or "therefore". *Evaluate* wants
  both sides and a judgement. Answering an *explain* question with a *state*
  answer caps the mark however correct the content.
- **Some words are required.** Where a scheme lists an accepted term with
  alternatives in brackets, those alternatives are the only paraphrases that
  score. Elsewhere, the specific term is mandatory.
- **Method carries credit.** In quantitative subjects, marks are often
  available for the correct method even when the final figure is wrong — which
  is why unexplained answers are expensive.

## What an examiner report adds

The mark scheme is the standard; the report is the diagnosis. Reports are
strikingly consistent year to year, and the recurring themes are rarely about
knowledge. They are about answering the question asked, using data given in the
stem, showing working, and writing legibly and concisely. When a report says
candidates "described rather than explained", it is naming the single most
common way marks are lost across almost every subject.

## How to use them together

After a timed past paper, mark your own script against the scheme without
mercy. For every mark lost, write one of three causes: *did not know it*,
*knew it but did not say it precisely*, or *misread the question*. Only the
first is a content gap. The second is a writing habit, and the third is a
reading habit — both improve quickly with deliberate practice, and both are
what the examiner report is describing.

Then read the report for that paper and note anything that matches your own
errors. Where they overlap, you have found a fault worth an hour of work.

## A caution

Mark schemes teach you the shape of a good answer. They do not teach the
subject, and a student who studies schemes instead of content will write
confident, well-structured answers about the wrong thing. Content first, then
technique — technique is the multiplier, not the foundation.
