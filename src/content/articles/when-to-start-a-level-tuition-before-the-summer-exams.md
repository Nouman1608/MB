Most AS and A Level written exams are sat in May and June. Families usually think about
tuition at one of three moments: early in the school year, after mock results, or in the last
weeks before the exams. Each is a reasonable time to start, but each can achieve different
things, and it helps to be realistic about which one you are in.

## Starting in the autumn (September to December)

This is the best time to fix understanding rather than technique.

- There is time to rebuild a topic that never made sense, properly, before moving on.
- A weekly class can run alongside school teaching, so new topics are learnt once, well.
- For a two-year A Level, an autumn start in the first year stops gaps compounding: in
  Mathematics, Physics and Chemistry, later topics are built on earlier ones.

**Use it for:** the topics your child finds hardest, in the order the school teaches them.

## Starting after mocks (January to March)

Mock results are the most useful information a family gets all year. They show which topics
lose marks and whether the problem is knowledge or exam technique.

- Bring the marked mock to the first class. A teacher can usually tell within one lesson
  whether marks were lost on content, on the command word, or on method and units.
- There is still time for both: reteaching the weak topics, then timed past-paper practice.

**Use it for:** a short list of weak topics, each closed off before moving to the next, with
past-paper questions on those topics as soon as the content is secure.

## Starting in spring (April to the exams)

Late tuition can still help a lot, but it should be focused.

- There is rarely time to relearn a whole unit. The best gains come from exam technique,
  mark-scheme language and the most heavily weighted topics.
- One-to-one classes usually suit this stage better than joining a group partway through a
  course, because every minute can go on that student's weak points.

**Use it for:** past papers under timed conditions, worked through with a teacher, and the
topics that carry the most marks.

## Whenever you start: the first two weeks

1. **Find the gaps.** Use the course's [printable topic checklist](/checklists/) to mark each
   topic secure, shaky or not started. A 10-minute [diagnostic](/practice/) shows the same
   for the subjects that have one.
2. **Plan the weeks left.** The free [revision planner](/revision-planner/) builds a weekly
   plan from your exam dates and the topics you marked.
3. **Learn what the questions ask.** The [command words guide](/command-words/) explains what
   "explain", "evaluate" or "deduce" require; many lost marks come from answering a different
   question.
4. **Know the targets.** The [grade threshold explorer](/grade-thresholds/) shows the marks
   needed for each grade in recent Cambridge series, and the [exam calendar](/exam-calendar/)
   lists entry deadlines and results days.

## Group or one-to-one?

An autumn start suits group classes well: they follow the course steadily, three times a week
per subject. After mocks and in spring, one-to-one usually works better. Our guide to
