## 0580 Mathematics — patterns found, mapped to topic (June 2024 series)

Written in this document's own words, not copied from the source. Each maps to
an existing syllabus topic already covered in `src/content/` per the D-113
72-subtopic taxonomy.

- **Order of operations (BIDMAS/PENDMAS)**: a recurring error pattern was
  applying operations left-to-right instead of respecting the convention —
  e.g. treating `28 − 8 ÷ 2` as `(28 − 8) ÷ 2`.
- **Unit conversion, especially length**: candidates frequently gave answers
  in the wrong unit scale (mm vs cm; the 100,000 cm = 1 km conversion was a
  specifically flagged weak spot).
- **Rounding discipline mid-calculation**: a standing key message across
  multiple papers — candidates truncate or round intermediate results instead
  of carrying full precision to the final step, then round only once at the
  end.
- **Position vectors / vector notation**: candidates confused a position
  vector with a displacement vector between two named points, and some gave
  vector answers as a combination of both basis vectors when the geometry
  required the answer to be a scalar multiple of only one (i.e. failing to
  use a known collinearity/straight-line constraint).
- **Recurring decimal → fraction conversion**: a large minority could not
  correctly set up the standard "multiply by 10ⁿ, subtract" method, most
  commonly by misreading which digits recur.
- **Set notation `n(...)` and Venn diagram regions**: consistently one of the
  weaker-answered items — many candidates could shade a described region but
  could not correctly evaluate a numerical set-cardinality expression built
  from union/intersection/complement.
- **Circle theorems (cyclic quadrilateral angle sum)**: a common wrong
  strategy was applying the general quadrilateral angle sum (360°) instead of
  the cyclic-quadrilateral-specific opposite-angles-sum-to-180° rule.
- **Graphical solution of equations**: candidates who were told to solve an
  equation by drawing a line on a given graph frequently attempted an
  algebraic solution instead, or drew a line with the wrong gradient/intercept
  when they did attempt the graphical method.
- **Factorising (difference of two squares; incomplete factorisation)**:
  candidates often stopped after taking out a common numeric factor without
  spotting the a²−b² structure needed for full marks.

## 0625 Physics — patterns found, mapped to topic (June 2024 series)

- **Total internal reflection / critical angle**: a specifically flagged
  misconception — believing a refracted ray still exists once the angle of
  incidence exceeds the critical angle (it doesn't; total internal reflection
  occurs instead).
- **Radioactive decay (alpha particle emission; proton vs. nucleon number)**:
  both a multiple-choice topic candidates scored near chance on (evidence of
  guessing across all four options) and, separately in the extended paper, a
  recurring confusion between proton number and nucleon number.
- **Beta particle identity**: only a minority of candidates correctly
  identified beta particles as electrons; "negative charge" (a description,
  not an identification) was a common incomplete answer.
- **Electromagnetic induction (induced e.m.f.)**: a persistent misconception
  that the *speed* of relative motion between magnet and coil does not affect
  the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. (it does).
- **Thermal radiation through a vacuum**: candidates commonly believed thermal
  radiation cannot travel through a vacuum (it can — this is precisely why it
  differs from conduction/convection).
- **Kinematics — tangent-to-curve technique for instantaneous acceleration**:
  a specific, teachable technique gap — candidates used `Δv/Δt` between two
  arbitrary points instead of correctly drawing and measuring the gradient of
  a tangent at the exact instant required.
- **Conservation of momentum with zero total initial momentum**: candidates
  solving a "two trolleys initially at rest, then moving apart" problem
  commonly assumed the trolleys stuck together (a collision framing) rather
  than recognising a "starting at rest, momentum must sum to zero" framing.
- **Energy store transfer language**: candidates could often name the correct
  before/after energy stores but reversed the direction of transfer (e.g.
  writing "kinetic to strain" for a process that transfers strain to kinetic).
- **Command-word/precision discipline**: a repeated key message independent of
  topic — final numeric answers without units, wrong significant-figure count
  relative to the given data, and definitions stated imprecisely (e.g.
  "acceleration" defined without the required "rate of" or "per unit time"
  framing) cost marks across multiple questions.
